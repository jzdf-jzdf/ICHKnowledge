# ABSTRACT  

Background: The surgical trial of lobar intracerebral haemorrhage (STICH II) was a randomised controlled trial evaluating early surgical removal of a clot. This paper investigates volume change in both arms of the trial with respect to Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOSE) groups.  

Methods: Patients randomised into STICH II had an initial diagnostic CT and a second CT 5 days after randomisation. Each scan was anonymously assessed by at least two central readers. An analysis of agreement between the two readers was conducted using kappa tests and intraclass correlation. The change in volume in both the early surgery (ES) and the initial conservative treatment (ICT) arms were analysed with respect to the six-month GOSE outcome.  

Results: Of the 597 patients randomised in the trial there were 582 pre-randomisation scans and 566 5-day scans available for analysis of agreement. There was good agreement between the assessors for the radiological inclusion criteria including volume $(|{\mathsf{CC}}=0.87.$ ) and this was better than the agreement between the assessor and local investigator $\begin{array}{r}{\langle\vert{\mathsf{CC}}=0.73.}\end{array}$ ). There were 526 patients with two scans available for analysis of change in volume measurement. The median percentage change in volume for the ES group was a reduction of $92.4\%$ (IQR $75.6\%$ , $99.0\%$ ) while for the ICT group, in which some cases crossed over to delayed surgery, it was only $5.7\%$ (IQR $16.4\%$ increase, $29.5\%$ reduction). ES patients with almost complete removal (99- $100\%$ ) had the best outcome with only $30\%$ dead or lower severely disabled. For the ICT group outcome was related to the final volume: the smaller the final volume the better the outcome.  

Conclusions: This analysis provides evidence for central assessments of scans in exploratory analyses and further information regarding the potential advantage of early and more complete clot removal on outcome in ICH and should inform the planning of future trials.

# Background  

Spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage remains a disease with high morbidity and mortality for which surgery may have two benefits: (i) reduction in mass effect and/or (ii) removal of toxic blood breakdown products.1–5 Several trials have investigated surgical clot removal. One of the largest was the surgical trial of lobar intracerebral haemorrhage (STICH II).6 This focussed on the removal by craniotomy of clots close to the surface of the brain implying less invasive approach paths than with deep-seated clots in the basal ganglia or thalamus.  

STICH II was a pragmatic randomised controlled trial comparing early surgery (ES) as defined in the protocol with initial conservative treatment (ICT).7 Patients had to have had a diagnostic CT scan showing a haemorrhage with a volume between 10 and $100\mathrm{ml}$ and be located within 1 cm of the cortical surface, a Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) of 8 or more and be within 48 hours of ictus. Patients randomised for surgery were expected to have a craniotomy within 12 hours of randomisation. Patients randomised to initial conservative treatment could have surgery if they deteriorated and the neurosurgeon decided it was necessary. Patients were followed up at six months with a postal questionnaire. The primary outcome was the extended Glasgow Outcome Scale; secondary outcomes included the Modified Rankin Scale. STICH II failed to demonstrate a statistically significant functional benefit for open surgical intervention of intracerebral haemorrhage.6  

Patients in STICH II had two CT scans: the diagnostic CT scan and a further scan at 5 $(\pm\nobreakspace2)$ days after randomisation. The purpose of the second scan was to assess the change in the haematoma size in comparison with the initial scan. Polster et al.10 have demonstrated these changes in MISTIE III and in STICH II, showing that greater clot reduction was associated with a better outcome using the Modified Rankin Scale. This analysis by Polster et al. was restricted to those patients randomised to surgery who received surgery.  

Our paper reports the level of agreement on the CT scan characteristics between readers and investigates the relationship between surgery and volume change with respect to GOSE outcome at six months for all patients whether randomised to receive early surgery (ES) or initial conservative treatment (ICT). There may be some patients with ICH who do benefit from surgical evacuation: this study helps to identify them.

# Methods  

For each patient recruited into STICH II, investigators were required to submit to the coordinating centre two anonymised DICOM files on separate CDs of the diagnostic and 5-day CT scans. If DICOM images were not available, scans were submitted on film, photographed or otherwise copied. Sometimes an MRI was undertaken instead and this was submitted. All scans were completely anonymised to all readers at the coordinating centre. All coded scans were then uploaded into Digital Jacket software for analysis in random order.  

The scans were read by at least two readers (mainly AI, SM; some MP, PB; queries undertaken by ADM, MH). They completed a proforma to record the location and size of the haematoma (size being measured by the ABC/2 method), and the presence of any other artefacts. These details were entered into an ACCESS database and analysed using SPSS version 21. Agreement between the readers was assessed and for continuous variables, the intraclass correlation (with $95\%$ confidence intervals) was measured.  

We then derived a volume metric which was the average of the two readers’ volumes if they differed by less than $40\mathrm{ml}.$ . In cases where they differed by more than $40\mathrm{ml}$ a third independent reader was used, and then an average was taken between the two closest readers. For discrepancies in the side of the haematoma a third reader checked the scans. For the binary variables showing the presence or absence of an extension into a deeper region (basal ganglia, thalamus etc), shift, IVH, hydrocephalus and other bleeds new variables were constructed indicating how many readers said the aspect was present.  

Two data sets were constructed: one containing all patients for whom there were two assessable scans (the ITTA population – intention to treat for available cases) and one excluding cases where both readers identified extension into a deeper region, IVH, hydrocephalus or volume outside the range of $10{-}100\mathrm{ml}$ (the exclusion criteria in STICH II) (the PPCA population – per protocol according to central assessment). Reasons for missing data from either of these datasets are described and the relationship between change in volume and randomisation group is compared using Mann-Whitney tests. An analysis of clot expansion in the initial conservative group was also undertaken to compare those where the clot expanded with those where it didn’t for differences in age, initial GCS, gender, initial volume of haematoma, and use of antithrombolytics/anticoagulants.  

The relation between the randomisation group and outcome (using the Glasgow Outcome Scale) after adjusting for volume is investigated using proportional odds models. For these analyses, the extended Glasgow Outcome Scale was used but for both the moderate disability and good recovery categories the upper and lower sub-categories are combined; thus producing five categories of a similar size (Dead, Lower severe disability, upper severe disability, moderate disability and good recovery.) (There were no cases of vegetative survival in STICH II.)

# Results  

# Scans available for analysis  

Of the 597 patients randomised correctly 589 had at least one scan. There were 582 pre-randomisation scans analysed and 566 5-day scans. Randomisation scans were not available where sites were unable to obtain them from the first hospital (before the patient was transferred to the neurosurgical centre). The reasons for no 5-day scan were that the patient had died before 5 days (12 patients: 3 ES and 9 ICT), were discharged or transferred before 7 days (4: 2 ES; 2 ICT), withdrew (2) or were missed (5: 3 ES; 2 ICT) (See Figure 1 and 2).  

Results of the analysis of agreement between the two readers are reported in the supplemental material. On the whole, the level of agreement between the two readers was high. There was agreement in 14 cases that the pre-randomisation scan suggested an extension into a deeper region. The readers agree that shift was present on almost a third but there was less agreement on whether it was mild, moderate or severe. There was good agreement on the presence of IVH and hydrocephalus. As expected from the inclusion criteria (which excluded patients with hydrocephalus or IVH) the presence of hydrocephalus was very low, however, readers agreed it was present on 3 scans. Readers agreed that 40 scans showed the presence of IVH.  

Intraclass correlation was high for volume measurement (prerandomisation $\mathrm{ICC}=0.87$ $95\%$ CI 0.82, 0.90; 5-day $\mathrm{ICC}=0.92$ $95\%$ CI 091, 0.93) but lower for depth from the cortical surface. However, an Altman- Bland analysis shows that the mean difference between the two readers at randomisation is $4.1\mathrm{ml}$ (appendix Figure S1) and at 5 days is $1.6\mathrm{ml}$ (appendix Figure S2).  

The comparison of the site investigator reports and the central readers showed that the local site investigator underestimated haematoma volume on average by $7.1\textrmml\textrm{ml}$ compared to the central readers estimate (see appendix Figure S3).  

Of the 582 patients with a pre-randomisation scan 74 were identified as having been ineligible for the study due to the agreed presence of extension (6 ES; 8 ICT), IVH (18 ES; 22 ICT), hydrocephalus (0 ES; 3 ICT) and volume (10 ES; 13 ICT) (see Table 1 and Figure 2). Plus the readers were unable to estimate volume on a further 20 scans (11 ES; 8 ICT). Thus for the eligible patient scan analysis (PPCA) there are a total of 238 ES patients with outcomes available and 222 ICT patients. While for the intention to treat scan analysis (ITTA) there are 267 ES patients and 259 ICT patients.  

# Results of change in volume  

The pyramid plots (Figure 3(a and b)) show the change in volume of the haematoma between the two scans for all 526 available patients. Patients randomised to early surgery were more likely to have a reduction in volume $(\boldsymbol{p}<0.0001)$ and more likely to have a higher percentage of their haematoma removed $(p<0.0001)$ . The median change in volume for patients in the early surgery group was a reduction of $35.0\mathrm{ml}$ (IQR 20.2, 54.8) with a median of $92.4\%$ (IQR $75.7\%$ , $99.0\%$ ) of the haematoma removed. For patients in the initial conservative group median percentage change was $2.4\mathrm{ml}$ reduction (IQR $14.3\mathrm{ml}$ reduction to $5.1\mathrm{ml}$ increase) with a median reduction of $5.7\%$ (IQR $29.5\%$ reduction, $16.4\%$ increase).

As reported in our original paper (6) there were some crossovers in this study, most commonly in the initial conservative group, where some patients had delayed surgery following neurological deterioration. Considering only the 257 ES patients who had surgery a median reduction of $36.6\mathrm{ml}$ (IQR $21.7\mathrm{ml}$ , $56.6\mathrm{ml}.$ ) was observed with a $93.0\%$ (IQR $80.0\%$ , $99.3\%$ ) reduction. Whilst for the 55 patients in the ICT group who had delayed surgery the reduction had a median of $36.9\mathrm{mls}$ removed, a median percentage reduction of $77.1\%$ (IQR $28.0\%$ , $96.4\%$ ). Looking at the ICT group alone clot expansion was related to the initial volume of the haematoma: the smaller the volume, the more likely the clot to expand. The median initial volume in the expansion group was $31.7\mathrm{ml}$ compared to $47.3\mathrm{ml}$ in the non-expansion group (Mann–Whitney $p<0.0001$ ).  

Restricting the analysis to the eligible population (460 patients), the median reduction in volume for the early surgery patients was $33.4\mathrm{~ml}$ (IQR 19.3, 52.3) and a percentage reduction of $92.8\%$ (IQR $76.5\%$ , $99.3\%\$ ); while for the initial conservative patients the change was $1.5~\mathrm{ml}$ reduction (IQR $13.7\mathrm{~ml}$ reduction, $5.9~\mathrm{ml}$ increase) and a percentage change of $4.7\%$ reduction (IQR $30.4\%$ reduction, $17.8\%$ increase). In the 228 ES patients who had surgery $34.1\mathrm{ml}$ (IQR 20.7, 53.3) was removed, a reduction of $93.6\%$ (IQR 81.6, 99.4); while of the 44 ICT patients who had surgery $37.3\mathrm{ml}$ (IQR 19.2, 57.3) was removed, a reduction of $84.7\%$ (IQR $36.1\%$ , $99.2\%$ ).

The bar charts (Figure 3(c and d)) show the frequency and percentage of outcome category by change in volume demonstrating that any increase has an effect on outcome and also that the larger the increase the greater the adverse effect. However, they also show that those with a large reduction also have a worse outcome because they were larger initially.  

Figures 4(a) and 4(b) demonstrate the change in volume by the initial volume and the effect on the outcome according to the randomisation group. Large increases in volume have a negative impact on outcomes. A good outcome from conservative treatment is rare in patients with an initial haematoma volume above $60\mathrm{ml}$ but can be achieved with complete removal for volumes up to $90~\mathrm{ml}$ . Tables S2 to S4 show the analysis of outcome by initial volume using these cutpoints of $60\mathrm{ml}$ and $90~\mathrm{ml}$ . If the initial volume of the haematoma was greater than $60\mathrm{ml}$ then $61\%$ died or were lower severely disabled at 6 months compared to $35\%$ if the haematoma was smaller $\mathbf{\tilde{\rho}}<0.00001)$ . If the initial volume was greater than $90~\mathrm{ml}$ then $77\%$ were dead or lower severely disabled at 6 months. If we assume that complete removal is $95\%$ or more then for those cases with an initial volume of between 60 and $90\mathrm{ml}$ complete removal was associated with $39\%$ poor outcome (death or lower severely disabled) at 6 months while not completely removing the haematoma was associated with $63\%$ poor outcome $\'P=0.022\rangle$ ). In those who had rescue surgery (the patients in the ICT group who crossed over to surgery) $73\%$ had died or were disabled at 6 months compared with $39\%$ in the ES group who had surgery. However, we know that the patients who had rescue surgery may have deteriorated and their haematoma may have increased in size.

In particular Figure S6 demonstrates that the best outcome is achieved when $99\small{-}100\%$ of the haematoma was removed with only $30\%$ of such patients dead or lower severely disabled at six months. The results of the proportional odds model analysis are shown in Table 2. The first line is the proportional odds model showing the odds ratio and $95\%$ confidence interval for any differences between the two treatment groups of 1.317 $(p=0.061)$ ) when all patients randomised into the trial are included using this categorisation of GOSE. For each population of cases (all those with both scans, all those with just the randomisation scan, and also when cases found to not fulfil eligibility criteria are excluded) age, GCS and volume at randomisation are all highly significantly related to outcome in all models. Age and volume are negatively associated – as age and volume increase, the outcome is worse while as GCS increases outcome is better. In population A Model 2 shows the odds ratio for age is 0.958 $(95\%\mathrm{CI}0.946,0.969)$ , for volume is 0.982 $95\%$ CI 0.976, 0.989) and GCS is 1.317 ( $95\%$ CI 1.207, 1.438). Including volume change in the model shows that it is also significantly related to outcome, (odds ratio $=0.990$ ; $95\%$ CI 0.983, 0.997) decreasing the volume improves outcome. However, adding this variable into the model reduces the relationship of the treatment group with the outcome because they are so highly correlated.  

The results in terms of odds ratios for age and GCS remain stable whichever population we use. Comparing Model 1 between All cases and Population A shows that excluding patients who did not have both scans available for analysis decreases the probability of a difference between the two treatment groups. Comparing models between Population A and Population C which includes those cases who had the first scan but not the second demonstrates the change due to patients not surviving to their second scan.  

# Discussion  

Investigation of the effects of surgical clot removal relies on accurate analysis of the volume change. This relies on quality assessment of the CT scans. There was a variable degree of agreement between the central scan readers for different variables but the percentage agreement is similar for the same variables on the pre-randomisation scans and for the 5-day scans suggesting that the differences are a feature of the variables being measured and the readers rather than the time of the scan or quality of the scan. Very few scans showed an extension into a deeper region, which was an exclusion criterion, and so it is not surprising that there is a high agreement because there was no extension visible particularly at randomisation. Side of a haematoma should not be an area of judgement, but scans were produced using different software at different sites and it was not always clear as to the side of the haematoma without checking for labelling and other clues. There is reasonable agreement on the presence of shift given that this is a subjective observation with agreement that it was present on almost a third but there was less agreement on whether it was mild, moderate or severe. There was good agreement on the presence of IVH and hydrocephalus but since these were exclusion criteria there should have been no patients with these characteristics on their pre-randomisation scans. The finding that standardised central ABC/2 measurements have substantially less variation than that between local site investigators and central readers was also found by Webb et al.11 This confirms the importance of central assessment in explanatory volumetric analysis.

For the initial report of the study, the volume of haematoma as reported by the local site investigator was used as this was the volume used for treatment decisions. There was a greater difference between the volume as read by the local site investigator and the average volume of the two central readers $(7.1\mathrm{ml})$ than between the two central readers themselves $\mathrm{4.1ml}$ at randomisation, $1.6\mathrm{ml}$ at 3–7 days). On average the local site investigators reported a smaller volume than the central readers. There may be several reasons for this. Although local site investigators were asked to provide the ABC measures they may have used a different slice to do the calculation (the assessor must choose the slice they consider to have the largest area of haematoma and this is subjective). Our own separate analysis of volume in STICH $\boldsymbol{\mathrm{II}^{12}}$ showed that ABC/2.4 gives a better true estimate of volume than ABC/2 for selected groups particularly those with irregularly shaped ICH or heterogenous density.  

One of the criticisms of the earlier STICH trial13 was that there was no indication of the volume and percentage of the haematoma removed. In this study from STICH II we can report that $10\%$ of the early surgery group had $100\%$ removed; $16.0\%$ had $99\%$ removed, $16\%$ had between $95\%$ and $99\%$ ; $18\%$ between 90 and $95\%$ so nearly $60\%$ of the patients had more than $90\%$ removed. Furthermore, if we use the target removal for MISTIE III of a reduction to $15~\mathrm{ml}$ or less then this was achieved in STICH II for $81\%$ of the patients in the Early Surgery group compared with MISTIE III where it was achieved for only $58\%$ 14 .

Outcome of GOSE had 5 categories: dead, lower severe disability, upper severe disability, moderate disability, good recovery (no patients were vegetative at outcome). Population A includes those cases with both ran-domisation and 5 day scans (ITTA), population B includes those cases with both scans that were eligible according to the central assessment of their randomisation scan (PPCA), population C does not take into accountthe 5 day scan so includes all patients with a centrally assessed randomisation scan (in particular it includes anyone who could not have a second scan because they had already died or had been discharged) andpopulation D includes only those with a centrally assessed randomisation scan that were eligible. Model 1 does not include any co-factors; model 2 includes the co-factors: initial volume of haematoma as centrallyassessed, age and GCS; model 3 includes the same cofactors as model 2 plus volume change.  

One of the problems with any analysis that looks at the change in volume of the haematoma between two time points particularly in the first couple of days after the ictus is the early mortality rate leading to the loss of those cases from the analysis. The main reasons for not having a five-day scan were “death” or “not done” due to already being transferred or missed. Of the early surgery patients 3 had died and of the initial conservative 9 had died. The effect of this can be seen in the proportional odds analysis. The different populations do give different results because they include not only different numbers of patients but also different types of patients. So populations A and B exclude all patients who die within the first few days, are transferred to other care options or are possibly too ill to undergo a 5-day scan whilst populations C and D include these patients.  

The STICH II data also show that craniotomy was a highly effective method for reducing the volume of haematoma. Awad et al.15 have shown that greater ICH volume reductions with MISTIE procedures improved outcomes, as measured by the modified Rankin Scale. Our data from STICH II confirms that the outcome, as measured by GOSE, is also improved with craniotomy. So the “change in volume” reported here is new information and successful surgery is advantageous for these patients with lobar ICH.  

A previous meta-analysis has suggested that patients with a GCS of between 10 to 13 benefitted from craniotomy in spontaneous lobar ICH (OR 0.71 with $n=485$ and $95\%$ $\operatorname{CI}0.51$ to 1.00; $P<0.05\$ ).16 There is no evidence in the current paper to refute this suggestion. Patients with this intermediate level of consciousness (GCS 10 to 13) at presentation should still be considered for craniotomy before they deteriorate further.  

This analysis shows how stable age, GCS and initial ICH volume are in predicting outcomes even when we exclude some cases for not having readable scans or dying before the second scan. Also, the final ICH volume does appear to be important (either by reduction with surgery or prevention of enlargement of the ICH). Better outcomes were achieved in STICH II in those patients undergoing Early Surgery and with more complete removal of the clot. There are two conclusions that can be drawn from this study: firstly allowing a clot to enlarge before surgery and secondly less complete clot removal each resulting in poorer outcomes as measured with the GOSE. Furthermore, clots expanded by more than $10\%$ in $31\%$ of initial conservative patients. With conservative treatment, every effort should be made to prevent such expansion with rapid moderate blood pressure control, rapid reversal of anticoagulation and early involvement of neurosurgery for selected cases.17
