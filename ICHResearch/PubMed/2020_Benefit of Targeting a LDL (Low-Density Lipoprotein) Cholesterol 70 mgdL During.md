# Benefit of Targeting a LDL (Low-Density Lipoprotein) Cholesterol <70 mg/dL During 5 Years After Ischemic Stroke  

Pierre Amarenco , MD; Jong S. Kim, MD; Julien Labreuche, BST; Hugo Charles, BST; Maurice Giroud, MD; Byung-Chul Lee, MD; Marie-Hélène Mahagne, MD; Norbert Nighoghossian, MD; Philippe Gabriel Steg, MD; Éric Vicaut, MD; Eric Bruckert, MD; on behalf of the Treat Stroke to Target Investigators\*  

Background and Purpose—The TST trial (Treat Stroke to Target) evaluated the benefit of targeting a LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol of $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ to reduce the risk of cardiovascular events in 2860 patients with ischemic stroke with atherosclerotic stenosis of cerebral vasculature or aortic arch plaque ${>}4~\mathrm{mm}$ , in a French and Korean population. The follow-up lasted a median of 5.3 years in French patients (similar to the median follow-up time in the SPARCL trial [Stroke Prevention by Aggressive Reduction in Cholesterol Level]) and 2.0 years in Korean patients. Exposure duration to statin is a well-known driver for cardiovascular risk reduction. We report here the TST results in the French cohort.   
Methods—One thousand seventy-three French patients were assigned to $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ $1.8\mathrm{mmol/L}$ ) and 1075 to $100{\pm}10\mathrm{mg/}$ dL $(90{-}110\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , 2.3–2.8 mmol/L). To achieve these goals, investigators used the statin and dosage of their choice and added ezetimibe on top if needed. The primary outcome was the composite of ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, new symptoms requiring urgent coronary or carotid revascularization and vascular death.   
Results—After a median follow-up of 5.3 years, the achieved LDL cholesterol was 66 $(1.69\mathrm{mmol/L})$ ) and $96\mathrm{mg/dL}$ (2.46 mmol/L) on average, respectively. The primary end point occurred in $9.6\%$ and $12.9\%$ of patients, respectively (HR, 0.74 $[95\%$ CI, 0.57–0.94]; $P{=}0.019$ ). Cerebral infarction or urgent carotid revascularization following transient ischemic attack was reduced by $27\%$ $(P{=}0.046.$ ). Cerebral infarction or intracranial hemorrhage was reduced by $28\%$ $(P{=}0.023)$ . The primary outcome or intracranial hemorrhage was reduced by $25\%$ $scriptstyle{\calP}=0.021$ ). Intracranial hemorrhages occurred in 13 and 11 patients, respectively (HR, 1.17 $[95\%$ CI, 0.53–2.62]; $P{=}0.70\$ ).   
Conclusions—After an ischemic stroke of documented atherosclerotic origin, targeting a LDL cholesterol of $<70~\mathrm{mg/}$ dL during 5.3 years avoided 1 subsequent major vascular event in 4 (number needed to treat of 30) and no increase in intracranial hemorrhage.

The TST trial (Treat Stroke to Target) trial evaluated the benefit of targeting a LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol of $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ to reduce the risk of cardiovascular events in 2860 patients with ischemic stroke with atherosclerotic stenosis of cerebral vasculature, in a French and Korean population.5 Patients were followed a median of 5.3 years in France and 2.0 years in Korea, since Korea joined the trial late.6 Since exposure time to statin treatment drives the reduction in cardiovascular events,7 we conducted an exploratory analysis to evaluate the benefit of targeting a LDL cholesterol of ${<}70\mathrm{mg/}$ dL during 5.3 years in the French cohort of the TST trial.  

# Methods  

# Trial Design  

This was a randomized, event driven trial. The methods of patient recruitment, evaluation, and statistical assumptions have been published.6 The protocol was approved by local institutional review boards. All patients gave written informed consent. The first author and independent academic statisticians at Bichat hospital, CHRU of Lille and Fernand Widal Hospital had full access to the trial databases, analyzed the data, prepared the first draft of the manuscript, and made decision to submit the manuscript for publication. Data are available to other researcher based on reasonable request. There were unrestricted grants from Pfizer, AstraZeneca, and Merck for the support of the trial, but there was no industry involvement in the conduct of the trial or data gathering or analysis. All authors vouch for the accuracy of the data and all analyses and for the fidelity of the trial to the protocol and reporting of adverse events. There was no data safety monitoring board appointed as there was no expectation of adverse events related to the LDL target strategies evaluated in the trial.  

# Trial Participants  

Patients were eligible for enrollment if they were 18 years or older, had an ischemic stroke ${<}3$ months previously, and a modified Rankin Scale after stroke of 0 to 3 (modified Rankin Scale scores of 0 to 6, 0 indicating no symptoms, 1 no disability, 2 to 3 needing some help, 4 to 5 dependent or bedridden, and 6 death) at randomization, once investigators determined the neurological deficit was stable, or a TIA within the previous 15 days that included at least arm and leg motor deficit or speech disturbance lasting ${>}10$ minutes. Transient ischemic symptoms with a documented ischemic lesion on computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging in the cerebral regions corresponding to the symptoms were defined as ischemic strokes.8 As recommended by the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines,9 all patients were screened using noninvasive imaging of the cervical vessels (carotid duplex, computed tomography angiography, MR angiography) as part of the routine evaluation of patients with suspected TIA or ischemic stroke, as well as computed tomography angiography or MR angiography of the intracranial vasculature to exclude a proximal intracranial stenosis and/ or occlusion, and transesophageal echocardiography or computed tomography angiography of the aorta to detect aortic atheroma, which were obtained when the responsible clinician determined that knowledge of intracranial steno-occlusive disease or severe aortic atheroma would alter management.9 The choice of vascular tests and the diagnosis of atherosclerotic stenosis was made and judged by the investigators and was not standardized or adjudicated. To be enrolled in the trial, patients had to have atherosclerotic disease including stenosis of an extra or intracranial cerebral artery, ipsilateral or contralateral to the region of imputed brain ischemia, or aortic arch atherosclerotic plaques ${\ge}4~\mathrm{mm}$ in thickness, or a known history of coronary artery disease. Patients also had to have an indication for statin treatment based on stroke American Heart Association/ American Stroke Association, French Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament, or South Korean recommendations.1,10,11 According to these recommendations, patients with ischemic stroke presumed to be of atherosclerotic origin should receive statin therapy,1 and for the French and Korean recommendations should be treated to a target LDL cholesterol of $100\mathrm{mg/dL}$ . Patients were required to have a directly measured LDL cholesterol of at least $70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ $1.8\mathrm{mmol/L}$ ) if they were on statin before randomization, or at least $100\mathrm{mg/dL}$ (2.4 mmol/L) if they had not previously received statins.  

# Trial Design  

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to a target LDL cholesterol of $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ or a target LDL cholesterol of $100{\pm}10\$ $\mathrm{mg/dL}$ . Investigators could use any type and any dose of statin to reach this target. Investigators were asked to perform a determination of LDL cholesterol 3 weeks after randomization (as recommended in country guidelines) to adjust the statin dose, or to add other lipidlowering agents including ezetimibe, to achieve the assigned LDL cholesterol target. Patients were followed every 6-month after randomization with measurement of LDL cholesterol. In addition to face-to-face visits with the investigators to collect trial outcomes since the previous visit, a central core of clinical research assistants based at Bichat Hospital called patients or their relatives every 6 months to acquire the results of LDL measurement at the preceding visit and to collect potential trial end points using a structured questionnaire. If the LDL cholesterol level was above or below the range assigned by randomization, the investigator was contacted to adjust the lipidlowering treatment to the target range. If a potential trial outcome was collected, the local investigator was contacted to confirm the event clinically and activate the adjudication process. Triglyceride, HDL cholesterol, blood pressure in the sitting position, fasting glucose, and HbA1C were collected at 6-month visits.  

# End Points  

The primary end point was a composite of adjudicated nonfatal cerebral infarction or stroke of undetermined source, nonfatal myocardial infarction, hospitalization for unstable angina followed by urgent coronary artery revascularization, TIA requiring urgent carotid revascularization, or cardiovascular death including unexplained sudden death. The prespecified secondary composite end points were myocardial infarction or urgent coronary revascularization following new symptoms; cerebral infarction or urgent carotid or cerebral artery revascularization following a TIA; cerebral infarction or TIA; any revascularization procedures both urgent and elective (coronary, cerebral, or peripheral artery); vascular death; all cause death; cerebral infarction or intracranial hemorrhage; intracranial hemorrhage; newly diagnosed diabetes mellitus; composite of primary end point and intracranial hemorrhage. All incident events that were components of these end points were adjudicated by a committee in which the members were unaware of LDL cholesterol group assignments or LDL levels achieved.  

# Trial Hypothesis and Conduct  

The trial sample size was originally planned to be 3786 patients for an anticipated 385 primary outcome events to provide ${\approx}80\%$ power to detect a $25\%$ relative risk reduction in the trial primary end-point detected over a period of a 3-year follow-up, an enrollment period of 1 year, and with an attrition rate of $20\%$ .6 Given the slower than expected enrollment rate, the steering committee amended the protocol to allow the follow-up of all patient beyond 36 month up until the end of the trial, with the last patient being included December 31, 2018, and extending the trial outside France, with 16 centers from South Korea joining the trial in late 2015.6  

# Statistical Analysis  

Analyses were performed on all randomized patients in their assigned group according to the intention-to-treat principle. The cumulative incidence of primary composite outcome of major cardiovascular events was estimated using Kaplan-Meier method by censoring withdrawals or lost to follow-up at the last available follow-up; patients who died from causes other than vascular disease were censored at time of death. Primary efficacy analysis was conducted using a Cox’s proportional hazard regression model including the following covariates as done in the SPARCL trial3: age, sex, entry event (stroke or TIA), and time since entry event. Missing values for covariates included in the Cox model were imputed by multiple imputation method. Adjusted hazard ratio (HR) for target LDL cholesterol of $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ relative to target LDL cholesterol of $100{\pm}10\mathrm{mg/dL}$ and its $95\%$ CI was derived from this model as the relative effect size measure. Unadjusted effect size (unadjusted HR) and log-rank test were also reported. The proportional hazard assumption was checked by examining the plot of scaled Schoenfeld residuals against time and by adding time-dependent covariates in Cox’s model. The absolute reduction in risk was determined from rate of events per 100 person-years. Several sensitivity analyses were conducted: by treating nonvascular death as competing risk using the Fine and Gray model, and we used the Inverse Probability Censoring Weight method12 and applied it to all censored patients in Cox analysis with and without adjustment for covariates to take into account potential information resulting from the censoring process and avoid potential bias. Regression model analyses of prespecified subgroups were conducted. Secondary outcomes were analysed by log-rank tests. Statistical testing was conducted at the 2-tailed $\upalpha$ -level of 0.05. Data were analyzed using SAS software version 9.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

# Results  

Between March 2010 and December 2018, 2148 patients were enrolled in France and 712 in South Korea (Figure I in the Data Supplement) and were followed a median of 5.3 years (IQR, 2.9–7.2) and 2.0 years (IQR, 0.5–2.9), respectively. Among French patients, 1075 were assigned a LDL cholesterol of $100{\pm}10\mathrm{mg/dL}$ (the control group) and achieved a mean LDL cholesterol of $96\mathrm{mg/dL}$ (Figure 1A). In the experimental arm, 1073 patients were assigned a LDL cholesterol of $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ and achieved a mean LDL cholesterol $66\mathrm{mg/dL}$ (Figure 1A).  

Baseline characteristics were similar between the 2 groups (Table 1). The baseline mean LDL cholesterol level was 137 $\mathrm{mg/dL}$ in the lower target group and $138\mathrm{mg/dL}$ in the higher target group, respectively $(3.5~\mathrm{mmol/L}$ in both groups). During the trial, all patients in the ${<}70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ group and in the $100\mathrm{mg/dL}$ group received intense or moderate dose of statin and ezetimide was added to this background in $29.2\%$ , $39.0\%$ , $42.6\%$ , and $44.0\%$ at 6-month, 1-, 2-, and 3-year, respectively, as opposed to $5\%$ to $7.8\%$ in the $100{\pm}10\mathrm{mg/dL}$ group (Table I in the Data Supplement). Patients also received blood pressure lowering therapy and diabetes mellitus control to target recommendations, as well as active smokers at baseline stopped smoking (Figures II through IV in the Data Supplement).  

# Effect on LDL Cholesterol  

The average LDL cholesterol was $66~\mathrm{mg/dL}$ in the lower target group and $96\mathrm{mg/dL}$ in the higher target group. Among patients assigned in the lower target level $(<70\mathrm{mg/dL})$ , $47\%$ had an average LDL cholesterol above the target $\mathrm{(>70~mg/}$ dL), $45\%$ between 50 and $70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , and $8\%$ below $50~\mathrm{mg/}$ dL. Among patient assigned to the higher target level (90–110 $\mathrm{mg/dL}$ ), $23\%$ had an average LDL cholesterol above the target $(>110\mathrm{mg/dL}$ ), and $34\%$ below the target $(<90\mathrm{mg/dL}$ ).

# Discussion  

This analysis of the TST trial showed that targeting a LDL cholesterol $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ $\mathrm{(1.8~mmol/L)}$ ) during 5.3 years after a recent ischemic stroke or TIA with evidence of atherosclerotic disease avoided ${>}1$ in 4 subsequent major cardiovascular events, ${>}1$ in 4 recurrent cerebral infarction or urgent carotid revascularization following a TIA, and 1 in 4 recurrent cerebral infarction or hemorrhage (all strokes), as compared with patients assigned to a target LDL cholesterol of $100{\pm}10~\mathrm{mg/}$ dL $2.4\mathrm{mmol}$ per liter). This was obtained without increasing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage with a number needed to treat of 30. The lower target level of LDL cholesterol was obtained by titration of statin drugs with addition of ezetimibe in $29\%$ to $44\%$ of participants.  

These results were obtained in the French cohort of the TST trial that was followed a median of 5.3 years, whereas the Korean patients were followed a median of 2.0 years. The TST trial was originally designed to enroll patients in 61 centers in France with a short accrual period. However, the steering committee noticed a lower than expected enrollment rate and amended the protocol to enroll patients until December 31, 2018 and to extend enrollment to Korean patients starting in late 2015. As a result, Korean patients had a much shorter follow-up, and no risk reduction was observed in these patients, although we found no significant treatment-by-country interaction.5 In the context of all randomized clinical trials with statin and other lipid-lowering drug,13–15 there is no reason to think that Asian patients do not benefit from statin treatment and from a lower target LDL cholesterol. Hence, we are continuing the follow-up of the 742 Korean patients until a median follow-up of 5 years.

Of note, the 2 Kaplan-Meier curves already separated immediately the first year (Figure 1B), at variance with the main SPARCL results in which they separated after 2 years3 but in keeping with the SPARCL subanalysis of patients enrolled with atherosclerotic disease.4 The opposite results obtained in patient enrolled with proven brain ischemia on neuroimaging and in patient with transient symptoms with no ischemia on neuroimaging with a significant interaction (Figure  2) was surprising. It might be worth in the future not to enroll patients without proven ischemia on neuroimaging because it may increase noise with transient neurological symptoms with another mechanism than true ischemia.  

In patients with a recent stroke, the SPARCL trial showed that lowering LDL cholesterol with intense statin therapy reduced the risk of recurrent stroke by $16\%$ , by using a unique dosage of atorvastatin $\mathrm{80mg/day,}$ during a median of 5 years (number needed to treat 53).3 A post hoc analysis of that trial found that those patients who achieved a LDL cholesterol $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ $\mathrm{(1.8~mmol/L)}$ had a $28\%$ relative risk reduction in stroke recurrence as compared with those who achieved a LDL cholesterol of $100\mathrm{mg/dL}$ or above during the 5 years of follow-up.16 Hence, we designed the TST trial to demonstrate that the target LDL cholesterol of $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ was superior to a target LDL cholesterol of 90 to $110\mathrm{mg/dL}$ in patients with an ischemic stroke due to atherosclerotic disease.6 The positive result of the TST trial with a number needed to treat 30 sets a new target range for LDL cholesterol to achieve of ${<}70~\mathrm{mg/}$ dL during 5 years after a stroke of atherosclerotic origin. This result was obtained in the TST trial while other risk factors were also treated to optimal target (blood pressure was 135/75 $\mathrm{mmHg}$ on average throughout the trial, and active smokers being $30\%$ at baseline and $5\%$ over the course of the trial, HbA1c $<7\%$ , Figures II through IV in the Data Supplement). The CTT meta-analysis found that any further reduction in LDL cholesterol provided a further benefit, without any threshold effect.14 Yet, the 2019 ESC guidelines17 recommend a target LDL cholesterol of $<55\mathrm{mg/dL}$ based on the  

IMPROVE-IT trial results18 and the PCSK9-trial results that showed that achieving an average LDL cholesterol $<55~\mathrm{mg/\Omega}$ dL using statin and ezetimibe,18 or an LDL cholesterol of 30 to $40\mathrm{mg/dL^{19,20}}$ using statin and PCSK9 inhibitors significantly reduced the risk of major vascular events, including stroke. These studies18–20 and our trial results suggest that lowering LDL cholesterol with new, more potent lipid lowering drug in future randomized clinical trial may further address the residual risk in patients with stroke. Yet, the transfer in daily practice (and explanation to the patient) of the concept of the lower the LDL cholesterol, the better observed in cohorts of patients and its extrapolation to an individual patient is often too abstract for a given patient. Hence, stroke physicians, general practitioners, and their patients may find useful in practice to target a number, such as $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ $\mathrm{(1.8~mmol/L)}$ ) as a first objective in prevention of new stroke, as suggested by our results.  

One confirmation from this analysis is that lowering LDL cholesterol to $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ did not increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage, as reported in meta-analysis.13,14 The same observation was made in trials that lowered LDL cholesterol to $<55~\mathrm{mg}$ or to 30 to $40\mathrm{mg/dL}$ in which there was no significant increase in intracranial hemorrhage.18–20 In the SPARCL3 and $\mathrm{HPS^{21}}$ trials, there was a significant increase in intracranial hemorrhage in patients with previous stroke. The main predictors of intracranial hemorrhage in the SPARCL trial were uncontrolled hypertension, brain hemorrhage as qualifying stroke event, being on atorvastatin, age, and male sex.22 In SPARCL, there was no evidence that this increase was related to low LDL cholesterol, but there was a suggestion that small vessel disease and uncontrolled hypertension were the main counfunders.22 Alike SPARCL, IMPROVE-IT, FOURIER, and ODYSSEYOutcome trials, the TST trial suggests that achieving a very low LDL cholesterol is not associated with intracranial hemorrhage, as the absolute increase was $0.2\%$ , whereas the absolute risk reduction was $3.3\%$ . As in the SPARCL trial, in which there was a $37\%$ increase in newly diagnosed diabetes mellitus,23 in our trial, we observed a relative increase in new diabetes mellitus of $33\%$ , absolute increase of $2.0\%$ , although not reaching statistical significance. The mechanism underlying these newly diagnosed diabetes mellitus is unknown, involving a decrease sensibility in insulin in liver or muscles, and a predisposition by a higher baseline glucose level, triglyceride, weight and blood pressure.23 While these newly diagnosed diabetes mellitus needs careful clinical attention and monitoring, the vascular risk of these patients appeared not different than other patients on statin who did not developed a new diabetes mellitus.23  

In conclusions, targeting a LDL cholesterol of ${<}70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ (1.8 mmol/L) during 5.3 years avoided 1 subsequent vascular event in 4, and 1 ischemic stroke or intracranial hemorrhage in 4, without increasing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage, with a number needed to treat of 30.
