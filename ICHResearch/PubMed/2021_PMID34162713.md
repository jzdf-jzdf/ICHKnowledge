# Lessons learned from a failed clinical trial of chronic subdural hematoma treatment  



# To the Editor  

We read with interest the Editor’s column in the April 2021 issue of Journal of NeuroInterventional Surgery, titled “Embolization of the middle meningeal artery for the treatment of chronic subdural hematoma: considerations for pragmatic trial design”, which discusses the challenges in rigorously evaluating the efficacy of middle meningeal artery (MMA) embolization for chronic subdural hematomas (cSDH).1 The practical considerations raised by the authors clearly highlight the complexities of designing a clinical trial comparing interventions that can guide practice in what is an unexpectedly heterogeneous population. During our residency training, we devised and attempted to perform a randomized, controlled trial comparing standard burr hole evacuation vs medical therapy (dexamethasone) for cSDH treatment, under the guidance of the late neurosurgeon-scientist Dr. Edward Oldfield.2 The trial ultimately failed to recruit enough patients, but our experience mirrored many of the pitfalls described in the current editorial. As such, our experience might provide some insight for those contemplating clinical trials of MMA embolization in a similar population.  

First, achieving buy-in from a diverse group of neurosurgeons required multiple discussions about inclusion and exclusion criteria that ultimately restricted the population we were able to enroll. The primary concern was how to establish equipoise between an unproven treatment (medical therapy with dexamethasone) and standard of care (surgical cSDH evacuation) in cases presenting with acute symptoms. Establishing consensus regarding the maximum permissible cSDH depth and midline brain shift also limited the eligible population, particularly as many elderly patients have large cSDHs but minimal symptoms. Conversely, other patients with smaller volume cSDHs can still present with significant symptoms, such as seizures, aphasia, or focal neurological deficits. Therefore, participating neurosurgeons may be hesitant to delay definitive surgical treatment. cSDH patients also tend to have a high rate of medical comorbidities that could impair the suitability for adjuvant treatments, although limiting a study to those already deemed surgical candidates is a reasonable first step to address this issue. Despite our initial perception of a high incidence of cSDHs requiring treatment at our center, after the various exclusion factors were applied the accrual was glacial and, ultimately, too slow to perform a pragmatic trial.  

Based on prior retrospective studies and a randomized trial, corticosteroids appeared to be a promising therapeutic option for cSDH.3 4 However, the challenges of translating existing limitedquality research into definitive Level I evidence was reflected in a multicenter randomized trial, Dex-CSDH, recently published in the New England Journal of Medicine.5 By electing to compare steroids with placebo, the investigators included both surgically and conservatively managed cSDH patients in each group, likely diluting their outcomes data and resulting in a trial that is unlikely to substantially alter clinical practice.5  

We agree that perhaps the “low hanging fruit” for an endovascular cSDH trial would be the surgical adjunctive therapy strategy, in which MMA embolization would aim to reduce the reoperation rate. Of note, the baseline reoperation rate after burr hole drainage in the recent DexCSDH trial was only $7.1\%$ (and much lower, $1.7\%$ , with steroid therapy),5 so demonstrating a significant treatment effect will likely require large cohorts in each arm. Regarding outcome assessment, cSDH-specific clinical scoring systems, such as the Markwalder Grading Scale, may also be useful in supplementing the clinical, radiographic, and surgical outcomes discussed by the Editors. Other complexities that may affect outcome, or at least the incidence of complications, include approach (radial vs femoral) and level of anesthesia (monitored anesthesia care vs general anesthesia). These details may be critical to consider standardizing in the trial planning phases. What should be done about patients on anticoagulants or antiplatelet medications? Some currently enrolling trials are excluding these patients due to an increased risk of recurrence. However, these are the patients that may be the most likely to benefit from MMA embolization. Finally, patients already undergoing standard of care surgery for a condition that may be asymptomatic or with minimal symptoms may be hesitant to undergo randomization for a second, more speculative, procedure.  

Eight trials on clinicaltrials.gov  are currently recruiting patients to assess MMA embolization for cSDH, with an additional six trials registered but not yet recruiting. Of these, at least three are multicenter, prospective randomized trials of a single-liquid embolic agent in addition to standard of care surgical treatment. The power analysis for these trials is quite challenging, and failure due to Type 2 error and inadequate power (similar to the DEx-CSDH study) is a real possibility. Although there may not be a significant difference in functional outcome, secondary outcomes such as reoperation may be a more achievable endpoint. The success of these efforts will largely depend on careful consideration of the issues raised by Drs. Fiorella, Hirsch, and Arthur.
