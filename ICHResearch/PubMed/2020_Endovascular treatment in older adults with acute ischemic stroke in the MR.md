# Abstract  

# Objective  

To explore clinical outcomes in older adults with acute ischemic stroke treated with endovascular thrombectomy (EVT).  

# Methods  

We included consecutive patients (2014–2016) with an anterior circulation occlusion undergoing EVT from the Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial of Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands (MR CLEAN) Registry. We assessed the effect of age (dichotomized at ${\ge}80$ years and as continuous variable) on the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at 90 days, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), and reperfusion rate. The association between age and mRS was assessed with multivariable ordinal logistic regression, and a multiplicative interaction term was added to the model to assess modification of reperfusion by age on outcome.  

# Results  

Of the 1,526 patients, 380 $(25\%)$ were ${\ge}80$ years of age (referred to here as older adults). Older adults had a worse functional outcome than younger patients (adjusted common odds ratio [acOR] for an mRS score shift toward better outcome 0.31, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 0.24–0.39). Mortality was also higher in older adults $51\%$ vs $22\%$ , adjusted odds ratio 3.12, $95\%$ CI 2.33–4.19). There were no differences in proportion of patients with mRS scores of 4 to 5, sICH, or reperfusion rates. Successful reperfusion was more strongly associated with a shift toward good functional outcome in older adults than in younger patients (acOR 3.22, $95\%$ CI 2.04–5.10 vs 2.00, $95\%$ CI 1.56–2.57, $p_{\mathrm{interaction}}=0.026,$ ).  

# Conclusion  

Older age is associated with an increased absolute risk of poor clinical outcome, while the relative benefit of successful reperfusion seems to be higher in these patients. These results should be taken into consideration in the selection of older adults for EVT.

# Glossary  

$\mathbf{acOR}=$ adjusted common OR; AIS $=$ acute ischemic stroke; $\mathbf{CI}=$ confidence interval; $\mathbf{DSA}=$ digital subtraction angiography; eTICI $=$ extended Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction; $\mathbf{EVT}=$ endovascular treatment; HERMES $=$ Highly Effective Reperfusion Evaluated in Multiple Endovascular Stroke; $\mathbf{IOR}=$ interquartile range; MR CLEAN $=$ Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial of Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands; $\mathbf{m}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}=$ modified Rankin Scale; $\mathbf{NIHSS}=\mathbf{NIH}$ Stroke Scale; $\mathbf{OR}=$ odds ratio; $\mathbf{sICH}=$ symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage.  

The risk of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) increases sharply with advancing age. The annual incidence of AIS for patients 75 to 84 years of age is 12 to 20 per 1,000 person-years compared to 0.1 to 0.3 per 1,000 person-years in patients ${<}45$ years of age.  

Endovascular treatment (EVT) has become standard of care for patients with AIS and a large vessel occlusion of the anterior circulation.2–8 Older adults were underrepresented in trials that evaluated the efficacy and safety of EVT. Three of the 7 trials excluded patients ${>}80$ years of age,4,6,8 while in the other trials, patients with severe comorbidity or preexisting disability, who are often older patients, were excluded. As a result, patients ${>}80$ years of age constituted only $15\%$ of the trial population,9 whereas, on the basis of epidemiologic data, a proportion of $30\%$ would have been expected.10,11 A subgroup analysis of the Highly Effective Reperfusion Evaluated in Multiple Endovascular Stroke (HERMES) study showed a similar relative treatment effect of EVT in older and younger patients. In absolute terms, however, older adults had a lower chance of good functional outcome.12  

The number of older adults undergoing EVT has increased in recent years, but given the selection bias in the trials, it is uncertain whether the results from those studies apply to the population of older adults who currently receive EVT.13–16 The aims of our study were to explore clinical and safety outcomes in older adults in routine clinical practice and to assess whether the association between reperfusion and clinical outcome is modified by age.  

# Methods  

# Design of the study and patient selection  

We used data from the Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial of Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands (MR CLEAN) Registry, an ongoing, prospective, nationwide, observational study on EVT in the Netherlands.17 Enrollment in the MR CLEAN Registry started directly after the final MR CLEAN trial randomization on March 16, 2014, and all Dutch hospitals performing EVT participate in this registry. Data for consecutive patients undergoing EVT (defined as patients undergoing arterial puncture) for AIS are recorded. For the current study, we used data of patients treated until June 15, 2016, who fulfilled the following criteria: age ${\ge}18$ years, treatment in a center that participated in the MR CLEAN trial, and presence of an intracranial anterior circulation occlusion (intracranial carotid artery, middle cerebral artery $\left[\bfM1/M2\right]$ , or anterior cerebral artery [A1/A2]).  

EVT consisted of arterial catheterization with a microcatheter to the level of occlusion, followed by mechanical thrombectomy, thrombus aspiration, or a combined approach, with or without delivery of a thrombolytic agent. The exact strategy for EVT was at the discretion of the treating physician. In patients with reperfusion at the first angiography run (either spontaneously or in response to IV thrombolysis), only a diagnostic digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed. In patients in whom intracranial access could not be achieved, for example, due to tortuosity of the vascular wall, only catheterization was performed. All relevant imaging was analyzed by an imaging core laboratory; members were blinded to clinical data except for lesion side. Collaterals at baseline were scored with an established 4-point scale $(0=$ absent collaterals $0\%$ filling of the vascular territory downstream of the occlusion], $1=$ poor collaterals $\left[>0\%\right.$ and $\leq50\%$ filling], $2=$ moderate collaterals $[>50\%$ and $\textless100\%$ filling], and $3=$ excellent collaterals $100\%$ filling].18 An adverse event committee consisting of 2 vascular neurologists and 1 neuroradiologist evaluated the safety endpoints using clinical data and reports from the imaging core laboratory.  

# Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents  

The MR CLEAN Registry was approved by the ethics committee of the Erasmus University MC, Rotterdam, the Netherlands (MEC-2014-235). The institutional review board of each participating center approved the research protocol. At UMC Utrecht, approval to participate in the study was obtained from its own research board and ethics committee. They waived the necessity of written informed consent.  

# Outcomes and definitions  

The primary outcome was the functional status at 90 days measured on the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), analyzed with a shift analysis. The mRS describes patient functional outcome, with scores ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death).19 Secondary outcomes were good functional outcome (defined as an mRS score 0–2 or maintaining an mRS score 3 for patients with a prestroke mRS score 3), severe handicap (defined as mRS score 4–5), mortality at 90 days, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), successful reperfusion (defined as extended Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction [eTICI] scores of 2b–3), and other complications that occurred within 90 days after onset of stroke (new ischemic stroke, extracranial hemorrhage defined as any major extra cranial bleeding as judged by local investigator, pneumonia, and cardiac ischemia). To reach an eTICI score of 2b to 3, DSA imaging needed to include both anteroposterior and lateral views after EVT. If the lateral view was missing, eTICI 2A was the highest possible score. Intracranial hemorrhage was defined as symptomatic if patients died or deteriorated neurologically (an increase of $\geq4$ points on the NIH Stroke Scale [NIHSS] compared to NIHSS score before worsening) and the clinical deterioration was related to the hemorrhage (Heidelberg bleeding criteria).20  

# Statistical analysis  

We stratified the study population into age quartiles, comparing patients in the upper age quartile to those in the lower 3 quartiles. For patients in the upper age quartile, the term older adults is used in the remainder this article. We also analyzed age as a continuous variable. Intergroup comparisons were performed with the $\chi^{2}$ test, independent-samples $t$ test, or Mann-Whitney $U$ test as appropriate. No imputation was used for descriptive variables, and the number of missing values is reported. For regression analyses, missing variables were imputed with multivariate imputation by chained equations with 5 imputations. The cumulative probability of survival during the 3-month follow-up period was estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method. Intergroup comparison was performed with the log-rank test.  

For the primary outcome, we plotted the absolute numbers of the mRS score at 90 days for patients ${<}80$ vs ${\ge}80$ years old. We used multivariable ordinal logistic regression analysis to estimate the adjusted common odds ratio (acOR) with corresponding $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) for a shift in the direction of better outcome on the mRS. For the remaining endpoints, we used multivariable binary logistic regression analysis to estimate the odds ratio (OR) with corresponding $95\%$ CI. As adjustment variables, we selected known prognostic factors that could theoretically serve as confounders in the analyses. All adjustment variables were chosen before data analyses. For the regression analyses with mRS or mortality as a dependent variable, we adjusted for the following factors: sex, previous stroke, prestroke mRS score, baseline NIHSS score, systolic blood pressure, location of occlusion, collateral score, presence of extracranial carotid stenosis or occlusion, administration of IV alteplase, use of general anesthesia, and onset-to-groin times. For procedural endpoints (sICH and eTICI scores), we adjusted for previous stroke, use of anticoagulation, baseline NIHSS score, systolic blood pressure, presence of extracranial stenosis/occlusion, location of occlusion, collateral score, administration of IV alteplase, and onset-to-groin times. For complications, we adjusted for sex and baseline NIHSS score.  

The association between functional outcome and age as a continuous variable was assessed with multivariable ordinal logistic regression. To assess whether this association was nonlinear, we determined whether models with or without restricted cubic spline transformation fitted the data better using a likelihood ratio test. We determined whether successful reperfusion modified the association between age and functional outcome at 90 days by adding a multiplicative interaction term to the model. This was done because we have no formal control group in this cohort and we therefore used successful reperfusion as a proxy for treatment. First, we plotted the probability of good functional outcome based on the ordinal logistic regression analysis with corresponding $95\%$ CIs, and we reported common ORs with corresponding $95\%$ CIs for a shift in the direction of good functional outcome in patients ${<}80$ and ${\ge}80$ years of age. Finally, we performed subgroup analyses on patients ${<}90$ vs ${\ge}90$ years old and patients stratified by baseline NIHSS score categories (mild, moderate, and severe) and onsetto-groin time categories ( ${\cdot}3$ vs ${\ge}3$ hours).  

Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 3.4.2, R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).  

# Data availability  

Individual patient data cannot be made available under Dutch law because we did not obtain patient approval for sharing individual patient data, even in coded form. However, all syntax files and output of statistical analyses will be made available on reasonable request.  

# Results  

Of the 1,627 patients registered during the study period, we excluded data of 101 patients for the following reasons: age ${<}18$ years $(\mathfrak{n}=2)$ , posterior circulation AIS $(\mathrm{n}=79)$ , and EVT in a non–MR CLEAN trial center $\left(\mathrm{n}=20\right)$ ). Therefore, data of 1,526 patients were included in the analysis (figure 1). The median age of the study population was 71 (interquartile range [IQR] 60–79, range 18–99) years. The upper age quartile was 80 years, which included 380 patients $(24.9\%_{;}$ referred to as older adults). Fifty-two patients $\left(3.4\%\right)$ were ${\ge}90$ years old.

# Baseline characteristics  

Older adults were more often female ( $60.8\%$ vs $42.4\%$ , $p<$ 0.001), had a higher mean systolic blood pressure (156 vs $147\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg},p<0.001\rangle$ and a higher median NIHSS score (17 vs 15, $p=0.009,$ ), and more often had preexisting disability (prestroke mRS score $23\colon23.4\%$ vs $7.6\%$ , $p<0.001$ , table 1) than patients $\mathtt{\Gamma}{<}80$ years of age. Older adults also more frequently had a history of atrial fibrillation $36.7\%$ vs $17.5\%$ , $p<$ 0.001) and stroke $23.0\%$ vs $14.6\%,p<0.001)$ and more often used antihypertensive agents ( $69.0\%$ vs $46.6\%$ , $p<0.001,$ ), antiplatelet therapy ( $39.4\%$ vs $31.7\%$ , $p=0.006,$ ), and anticoagulation ( $23.9\%$ vs $15.6\%$ , $p<0.001$ , specifically vitamin K antagonists: $19.1\%$ vs $10.7\%$ , $p<0.001_{.}$ ). Median Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score was 9 in both groups. Older adults had worse collaterals (grade 0: $6.9\%$ vs $6.9\%$ , grade 1: $39.3\%$ vs $30.9\%$ , grade 2: $34.4\%$ vs $40.0\%_{;}$ , grade 3: $19.5\%$ vs $22.2\%$ , $p=0.035,$ ). The occlusion location did not differ between groups. Failure to reach the target occlusion occurred more often in older adults (catheterization only: $11.1\%$ vs $3.4\%$ , $p<0.001\$ ). The onset-to-groin time was longer in older adults (220 vs 205 minutes, $p=0.014,$ ), but onset–to–last contrast bolus time did not differ (262 vs 258 minutes, $p=0.452,$ ).  

# Outcomes  

Older adults had a worse functional outcome at 90 days than younger patients $\mathrm{~\'~ac{OR}~}0.31$ for a shift in the direction of a better outcome, $95\%~\mathrm{CI}~0.24–0.39_{.}$ , figure 2 and table 2). Twenty percent of older adults had a good functional outcome at follow-up (mRS score 0–2 or maintaining an mRS score of 3) compared to $45.6\%$ of patients ${<}80$ years old $(p<$ 0.001). Mortality at 90 days was $50.9\%$ for older adults compared to $21.8\%$ in younger patients $\binom{\dH}{\dH}<0.001$ , figure 3 and table 2). There was no difference in the proportion of patients with severe handicap (mRS score 4–5: $20.6\%$ vs $18.0\%$ , $p=0.281,$ ). The rates of sICH ( $5.5\%$ vs $5.9\%$ , $p=$ 0.769) and successful reperfusion ( $54.2\%$ vs $58.9\%$ , $p=0.108,$ ) also did not differ between groups. Lateral DSA was missing in $14\%$ of the patients (217 of 1,526). Among older adults who were functionally independent before the index event (prestroke mRS score 0–2), $24.8\%$ (64 of 258) were also functionally independent at follow-up, and mortality was $43.8\%$ . Among patients ${\ge}90$ years of age, mortality at 90 days in was $65.3\%$ (figure e-1, available from Dryad, doi.org/10.5061/ dryad.hx3ffbg98). Other outcomes among patients ${\ge}90$ years of age are provided in the online supplement (table e-1, available from Dryad). Other subgroup analyses showed that older adults with mild stroke (NIHSS score 0–5) had no significant shift on the mRS scale compared to younger patients (table e-2a, available from Dryad).  

The association between age as a continuous variable and functional outcome was nonlinear ( $\overset{\cdot}{p}$ likelihood ratio test $<0.001$ , figure 4). Increasing age was associated with a shift toward poor functional outcome with an acOR of 0.74 per 10- year increase in age $\left(95\%\mathrm{CI}0.69–0.79\right)$ , with different effects in patients ${>}68$ and $^{<68}$ years of age. In patients $^{<68}$ years of age, there was no significant association between age and good outcome (acOR per 10-year increase in age 0.99, $95\%$ CI 0.88–1.11), while in those ${>}68$ years of age, there was a strong inverse association (acOR 0.46, $95\%$ CI 0.39–0.54, table e-3, available from Dryad, doi.org/10.5061/dryad.hx3ffbg98). Results regarding outcome in analyses of age as a continuous variable were overall consistent compared to age as dichotomous variable, except for successful reperfusion; this became borderline significant (adjusted OR [aOR] 1.09, $95\%$ CI 1.00–1.18). Successful reperfusion significantly modified the association between age and functional outcome $\cdot_{p}$ for interaction $=~0.026\$ , figure 4). Successful reperfusion was more strongly associated with a shift toward good functional outcome in older adults (acOR 3.22, $95\%$ CI 2.04–5.10) than in younger patients (acOR 2.00, $95\%$ CI 1.56–2.57).  

# Discussion  

Older adults $\tilde{\cdot}280$ years of age) with AIS who received EVT as part of routine clinical practice had considerably worse outcomes than younger patients. Three months after the index event, half of the older adults had died. Still, 1 of 5 older adults had a good functional outcome at follow-up, and among older adults who were functionally independent before the stroke, this proportion was 1 of 4. In addition, the absolute risk of severe disability (mRS score 4–5) was not higher in older adults, nor did older adults have a higher risk of sICH. Of note, the relative benefit of successful reperfusion was greater in older adults compared to younger patients.  

A number of studies have previously examined outcomes of EVT in older adults.21–24 Generally, these studies also found that older adults have a lower rate of good functional outcome despite similar rates of successful reperfusion and sICH compared to younger patients. Most of these studies had a small sample size, used retrospectively collected data, or included patients who were treated with first-generation thrombectomy devices. The percentage of all patients achieving good functional outcome from the randomized controlled trials regarding EVT fluctuated between $33\%$ and $71\%$ , while the absolute percentage of older adults in our cohort achieving mRS scores of 0 to 2 is much lower $\left(19.2\%\right)$ , indicating that we should be cautious when extrapolating the results from the trials to routine clinical practice. Furthermore, rates of good functional outcome of older adults in previous studies ranged from $20\%$ to $60\%.^{15,25,26}$ A recent meta-analysis found that 1 of 4 older patients achieved good functional outcome, which is in the same range as our findings. 27 This study showed a lower mortality than our study $34\%$ vs $50\%$ ). A possible reason for this difference could be that studies with a selected population of older adults were included in the meta-analysis, while our outcomes reflect daily clinical practice. The Kaplan-Meier curve (figure 3) indicates that the difference in mortality between the 2 age groups stems predominantly from an increased mortality rate in the $\ge80$ -year age group during the first 30 days after stroke. Thereafter, mortality rates were relatively stable for both the ${\ge}80-$ and ${<}80$ -year age groups. One study found no difference in outcome for older adults, but this study excluded patients with a prestroke mRS score of >2.28

It is important to note that, despite the overall decreased absolute chance of good outcome in older adults, the risk of severe disability (mRS score 4–5) was not higher in these patients, nor did major bleeding occur more often. These data, together with the observed increased relative benefit of successful reperfusion in older adults and the fact that almost $20\%$ of older patients are independent at follow-up, should be taken into consideration in the selection of older adults for treatment with EVT. In this decision, it is important to weigh additional variables that increase or decrease the chance of good outcome such as the prestroke mRS score or frailty score.29 Results from our subgroup analyses showed that NIHSS score could also attribute to chances of good functional outcome; however, these results should be interpreted with caution because of the low number of patients in each category. A validated prediction model with sufficient data on older adults would be a helpful tool to aid physicians in decision-making. So far, only 1 prediction model for patient selection has been developed: the Multivariable Personalized Intra-arterial Treatment Clinical Decision Tool for Ischemic Stroke (MR-PREDICTS).30 This model, however, is based solely on the MR CLEAN trial data, which included a limited number of older patients.  

Despite an overall worse outcome, we found a stronger association between successful reperfusion and clinical outcome in older adults than younger patients, similar to a previous study and the HERMES meta-analysis.9,23 At first glance, these results may seem counterintuitive. The increased relative benefit is most likely explained by a difference in absolute chance of good functional outcome between old and young patients. Both age groups had a similar absolute risk difference for good functional outcome $(\approx20\%)$ , but because the overall absolute chance of good outcome is lower for older adults, the relative benefit is higher in these patients. In addition, frailty of older adults and their higher risk of complications after stroke may contribute to this observation. When no reperfusion is achieved in these patients, chances of surviving stroke are small. Younger patients in whom no reperfusion is achieved, however, will more likely survive the stroke, even in the absence of reperfusion.  

Interventionists more often had trouble reaching the target occlusion in older adults. Failure to reach the thrombus is generally caused by severe atherosclerosis or elongation of the aortic arch and cervical arteries. Because there was no difference in frequency of carotid stenosis or occlusion between the age groups, the latter reason is probably the explanation for this difference. Earlier studies indeed found that elongation of the aortic arch or tortuosity of the carotid artery is more common in older patients.31

One of the strengths of our study is the large sample size. In addition, because the data come from a nationwide, prospective registry of consecutive patients, the results likely reflect daily clinical practice regarding EVT. All data on imaging and adverse events were centrally adjudicated, and the proportion of missing data was small. Several limitations of our study warrant comment. First, only data of patients who receive EVT are recorded in the MR CLEAN Registry, and we have no information on patients with AIS and a large vessel occlusion for whom it was decided not to perform EVT. It is likely that older adults are overrepresented in this group because increased comorbidity and preexisting disability, variables that occur more often in older adults, are often weighed in the decision to perform EVT. Furthermore, we were unable to compare our results with a control group of older adults who did not receive EVT, which is why we used unsuccessful reperfusion as a proxy in our analysis. Third, we used only the mRS score to evaluate functional outcome at 90 days. The mRS is a rather crude outcome measure, and ideally, future studies that evaluate stroke outcome in older patients should use additional scales that assess disability and quality of life. Fourth, despite the fact that we adjusted our analyses for known prognostic factors, we cannot rule out the possibility of residual confounding, which may partly explain the difference between older and younger patients. For example, detailed data on etiology of stroke, lesion volume, and perfusion imaging data were not available. Although we did adjust for prestroke mRS, it is clear that this factor is evidently more often present in older adults, which is why we added an analysis in which we stratified the older adults according to prestroke mRS score. Finally, data on advanced imaging techniques such as CT perfusion and MRI were not available for our analysis. Because these techniques are increasingly used in the clinical setting to decide whether to treat patients with symptom onset between 6 and 24 hours, future studies could examine the added value of these techniques in patients not only in the late time window but also within 6 hours after stroke onset.

Our results show that in patients with AIS older age is associated with an increased absolute risk of poor clinical outcome after EVT in routine clinical practice compared to younger patients. Still, 1 of 5 older adults achieved a good functional outcome, and the risk of severe handicap was not higher in older patients. Moreover, the relative benefit of reperfusion on outcome appears to be higher in older adults, and of the older adults who were functionally independent before the index event, a quarter were functionally independent at follow-up. These data should be taken into consideration in the selection of older adults for EVT.
