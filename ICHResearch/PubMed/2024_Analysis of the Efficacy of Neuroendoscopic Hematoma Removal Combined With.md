BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: The current widely utilized clinical approach for severe intraventricular hemorrhage involves ventriculostomy with supportive drainage. The aim of our study was to evaluate the overall efficacy of neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricular lavage as a treatment approach for severe intraventricular hemorrhage.  

METHODS: A prospective randomized controlled study was conducted, selecting a total of 98 patients with severe intraventricular hemorrhage at our hospital from February 2021 to November 2022. The patients were randomly distributed into 2 groups using a randomized number table method: the neuroendoscopic group (undergoing neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricular lavage) and the control group (undergoing intraventricular trepanation and drainage), with 49 patients in each group.  

RESULTS: The neuroendoscopic group had significantly higher intraoperative blood loss than that of the control group $(P=.037)$ , while the drainage tube indwelling time and hospital stay in the neuroendoscopic group were significantly shorter $(P<.001)$ . At 6 hours $(P=.021$ ), 1 day $(P=.002)$ , 3 days $(P<.001)$ and 7 days $(P=.007)$ following surgery, the neuroendoscopic group exhibited evidently higher hematoma clearance rates compared with the control group. At 1 day and 3 days after surgery, the cerebrospinal fluid drainage volume in the neuroendoscopic group was significantly higher than that in the control group $(P<.001)$ , whereas at 7 days after surgery, it was significantly lower in the neuroendoscopic group compared with the control group $(P<.001)$ . Moreover, significantly lower incidence of intracranial infection $(P=.045)$ and increased intracranial pressure $(P=.008)$ was observed in the neuroendoscopic group compared with the control group.  

CONCLUSION: Neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricle lavage emerged as an effective treatment strategy for severe intraventricular hemorrhage, yielding significant therapeutic benefits. Therefore, this approach holds promise for broader clinical application and promotion.

ntraventricular hemorrhage is a prevalent condition in neurosurgery, accounting for $20\%$ to $60\%$ of all cases of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage. This condition is   
characterized by its abrupt onset and rapid progression.1 Severe   
intraventricular hemorrhage encompasses instances where hem  
orrhage occupies the entire ventricular space or extends into the   
subarachnoid region, resulting in an unfavorable prognosis and  

high fatality rates.2 Therefore, the imperative for rescuing individuals afflicted by severe intraventricular hemorrhage lies in the prompt and effective removal of the hematoma, elimination of its compressive impact, and mitigation of intracranial pressure to prevent the formation of cerebral herniation. Presently, the predominant clinical intervention involves ventriculostomy coupled with adjunctive drainage. $^3\mathrm{{In}}$ certain cases, institutions may introduce tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) or other fibrinolytic agents to facilitate hematoma breakdown, particularly when significant vascular malformation with a high risk of rerupture is ruled out.4 In addition, the deployment of irrigating catheter systems such as the IRRAflow-IRRAS (IRRAS) has demonstrated improvements in the efficacy of ventriculostomy alone.5 However, the surgical approaches entail protracted procedures, entail significant trauma, and carry an elevated risk of postoperative complications. Fortunately, the utilization of neuroendoscopy-assisted hematoma removal offers the dual advantages of reduced surgical trauma and an improved field of view.6 Ventricular lavage serves to systematically expunge the bleeding focus at an approximate physiological rate. Concurrently, this process serves to dilute the blood and mitigate the deleterious substances released during hematoma decomposition, thus reducing secondary brain tissue damag e.7,8 Here, this prospective study endeavored to unravel the effect of neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricular lavage as a treatment approach for severe intraventricular hemorrhage. To evaluate the potential benefits of this combined therapeutic approach in fostering long-term neurological function recovery and enhancing patient prognosis.  

# METHODS  

# General Data  

This study was designed as a prospective randomized controlled trial. A total of 98 patients diagnosed with severe intraventricular hemorrhage were recruited from our hospital from February 2021 to November 2022. The patients were randomly assigned to either the neuroendoscopic group or the control group using a randomized number table method, with 49 cases in each group. This study was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital, and the informed consent forms were obtained from all patients.

# Exclusion Criteria  

Patient with a history of cerebrovascular diseases such as ischemic or hemorrhagic infarction, prior brain surgery, severe liver and kidney dysfunction, evident abnormal coagulation function or other blood disorders, advanced cerebral herniation, decerebrate tonic state, respiratory and circulatory failure, contraindications to anesthesia and surgery, malignant tumors, pregnant and lactating women, mental illness, or those unable to cooperate for the study were excluded.  

# Treatment Method  

Patients in the control group underwent intraventricular drilling and drainage. After successful administration of general anesthesia, a frontal drilling point $2.5\mathrm{cm}$ beside the midline and $1\mathrm{cm}$ before the coronal seam) was selected, and anelectric (Shanghai Xinsheng Photoelectric Technology) drill penetrated the dura mater to insert anexternal drainage tube into the ventricle (Guangzhou Zhiyuan Medical Technology). A postoperative cranial CT (SIEMENS AG) scan was conducted 6 hours after surgery to assess further bleeding, and if no complications arose, residual hematoma drainage through lumbar cisterna drainage could be performed according to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage.  

Patients in the neuroendoscopic group underwent neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricular lavage. After successful general anesthesia, a transfrontal or triangular cortical-ventricular approach was selected based on hematoma location and size. A scalp arc incision of approximately $5\mathrm{cm}$ was made, followed by a skull window of around $3~\mathrm{cm}$ in diameter. The dural membrane was incised, the cortex was electro burned, and a transparent working channel with a core was inserted. Subsequent removal of the inner core allowed for visualization of bloody CSF and hematoma overflow. Neuroendoscopy at angles of $0^{\circ}$ and $30^{\circ}$ along with its monitoring system (Storz GMBH) was employed to remove intracerebral hematoma. By adjusting the angle and depth of the working channel, hematoma removal was performed elsewhere. After hematoma clearance, ventricular lavage was performed using $37^{\circ}C$ lavage fluid $\mathrm{500mL}$ of normal saline from Qidu Pharmaceutical and $40000$ units of gentamicin from Sigma-Aldrich, Inc), with a total lavage volume of about $500~\mathrm{mL}$ for 20 to 30 minutes. In cases of secondary intraventricular hemorrhage, adjustments to the channel angle and depth were made to remove basal or thalamic hemorrhages laterally. Hematoma removal was followed by ventricular lavage repeated for approximately 25 minutes using lavage fluid. For patient with bleeding in the fourth ventricle, a median incision was used to expose the occipital bone. A bone window of roughly $3~\mathrm{cm}$ in diameter was created, and the cortex was burned in an avascular area on the brain’s surface. A working channel with a core was placed into the fourth ventricle for hematoma removal through neuroendoscopy. Subsequent ventricular lavage was repeated for approximately 25 minutes using lavage fluid. Postoperative management for this group was consistent with that of the control group.

# Observational Indicators  

Preoperative general information of patients including gender, age, smoking and drinking habits, body mass index, bleeding type and location, presence of diabetes, coronary heart disease, preoperative GCS score, and time from onset to operation were documented.  

# Operative Details  

Information regarding operation time, intraoperative blood loss, duration of drainage tube indwelling (time from immediate surgery conclusion to removal of ventricular drainage tube), and length of hospital stay were collected.  

# Hematoma Clearance Rate  

The hematoma clearance rates at 6 hours, 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days postsurgery were recorded in the study. The hematoma clearance rate was calculated using the formula: [Number of patients with (preoperative hematoma volume  postoperative hematoma volume)/preoperative hematoma volume $275\%$ ]/total cases $\times100\%$ . The volume of intracranial hematoma was determined using Toda formula based on preoperative CT results.  

# CSF Drainage Volume  

The CSF drainage volume on days 1, 3, and 7 after surgery were recorded in the study.

# Neurological Function Assessment  

At 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days after surgery, neurological function was evaluated using the GCS.9 The GCS assesses consciousness based on eye opening response, body movement, and language function, with a total score was 15. A higher score indicates better consciousness levels.  

# Prognosis  

After a 6-month follow-up using the modified Rankin Scale,10 the prognosis of both groups was evaluated. This scale assigns scores as follows: 0: No symptoms; 1: No significant disability despite symptoms, capable of performing all usual duties and activities; 2: Slight disability, unable to perform previous activities entirely but can manage personal affairs independently; 3: Moderate disability, needing some assistance but able to walk without aid; 4: Moderately severe disability, unable to walk unaided and requires assistance for bodily needs; 5: Severe disability, bedridden incontinent, requiring constant nursing care and attention; 6: Death. For analysis purposes, scores of 0 to 4 were categorized as good prognosis, while scores of 5 to 6 were considered poor prognosis.  

Postoperative adverse events, including hydrocephalus, intracranial infection, increased intracranial pressure, and secondary hemorrhage, were also recorded.  

# Statistical Analysis  

All the data collected in this study were analyzed using SPSS 21.0 software (IBM). Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ , while non-normally distributed measurement data were expressed as median (IQR). Comparisons were conducted using the  

Student $t\cdot$ test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney test for non-normal data. Categorical data were expressed as n $(\%)$ and assessed using $\chi^{2}$ analysis or the Fisher exact test for differences between the 2 groups. A significant level of $P<.05$ was considered statistically significant.

# Comparison of Hematoma Clearance Rates  

Postsurgery hematoma clearance rates were evidently higher in the neuroendoscopic group at 6 hours $(P=.021)$ , 1 day $\left(P=.002\right)$ , 3 days $(P<.001)$ , and 7 days $\left(P=.007\right)$ compared with the control group (Table 3, Figure 3).  

# Comparison of CSF Drainage Volume  

At 1 day and 3 days after surgery, the CSF drainage volume in the neuroendoscopic group was significantly higher than that in the control group $(P<.001)$ . Conversely, at 7 days after surgery, the CSF drainage volume in the neuroendoscopic group was significantly lower than that in the control group $(P<.001)$ (Table 4, Figure 4).  

# Comparison of GCS Scores  

GCS scores at 1 day $(P=.001)$ , 3 days, and 7 days postsurgery $(P<.001)$ were significantly higher in the neuroendoscopic group compared with the control group (Table 5, Figure 5).  

# Comparison of Modified Rankin Scale  

After a 6-month period after surgery, the neuroendoscopic group exhibited significantly improved modified Rankin Scale  

scores in comparison with the control group $\left(P=.004\right)$ (Table 6, Figure 6).  

# Comparison of Adverse Events  

The neuroendoscopic group showed evidently lower incidences of intracranial infection $(P<.01)$ and increased intracranial pressure $(P=.008)$ compared with the control group. However, there were no significant differences in the incidences of hydrocephalus $(P=.05)$ and secondary hemorrhage $(P=.239)$ between the 2 groups (Table 7, Figure 7).  

# DISCUSSION  

# Summary of Results  

This study underscored that neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricular lavage held the potential to shorten the duration of drainage tube indwelling and hospital stay for severe intraventricular hemorrhage patients. The results of our study showed a significantly higher hematoma clearance rate in the neuroendoscopic group compared with the control group at 6 hours, 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days after surgery, which underscored the efficacy of neuroendoscopic hematoma clearance combined with ventricular lavage in heightening the hematoma clearance rate of patients with severe intraventricular hemorrhage. On the other hand, neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricular lavage could precisely identify hematomas and clearly distinguish them from surrounding normal structures.

# Interpretation and Context  

Intraventricular hemorrhage is a condition that predominantly affects the deeper regions of the brain, which often disrupts CSF circulation, resulting in escalated intracranial pressure and compression of the brainstem.1 Severe intraventricular hemorrhage patients commonly experience pronounced clinical manifestations such as altered consciousness, limb dysfunction, myotonia, and disruptions in water and electrolyte balance.11  

In the management of severe intraventricular hemorrhage, historical approaches have predominantly used external drainage through drilling. This method boasts advantages such as rapid puncture and ease of operation. However, complications such as drainage tube blockages due to factors such as blood coagulation and side holes can arise.12 Ventriculostomy, characterized by its minimal trauma and enhanced safety, necessitates aseptic practices throughout it execution to ensure the sterility of the shunt device and prevent multiple incisions. Nevertheless, improper puncture techniques can lead to complications such as intraventricular hemorrhage1 3 In this context, neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricle lavage emerges as a new method for the treatment of ventricle hemorrhage. It brings the benefits of reduced trauma and quicker postoperative recovery. The concurrent utilization of ventricle lavage facilitates the prompt removal of residual intracerebral hematomas, thereby significantly enhancing treatment efficiency.12 Research by Sun et $\mathrm{'al^{14}}$ indicated that patients with severe intraventricular hemorrhage treated with neuroendoscopy experienced superior therapeutic outcomes. Song et $\mathrm{al}^{159}s.$ . study showed that compared with ventricular drilling and drainage surgery, incorporating neuroendoscopy during surgery could shorten procedural duration, reduce intraoperative bleeding, improve hematoma clearance rates, and have fewer adverse reactions. Although the results of the study indicated a higher intraoperative blood loss in the neuroendoscopic group compared with the control group, the absolute disparity was relatively minor. This discrepancy was attributed to potential bias stemming from the small sample size of the study.

In addition, the intraoperative expansion of bone foramen and the utilization of an anterior angle approach to the lateral ventricle could help enlarge the surgical space and visual field, thus improving the clearance rate of hematoma.16 This technique could rapidly eliminate ventricle and intracranial hematomas during the operation, effectively clearing intraventricular blood and its degradation products. By consistently maintaining an adequate urokinase concentration, this approach accelerated clot dissolution rate and prompted elimination.17 Our study indicated a substantial increase in CSF drainage in the neuroendoscopic group at 1 day and 3 days after surgery, suggesting the successful early postoperative removal of CSF drainage in patients with severe intraventricular hemorrhage. Furthermore, the CSF drainage volume in the neuroendoscopic group was notably lower than that in the control group at 7 days after surgery. This decrease could be due to the neuroendoscopic group’s improved early postoperative drainage, time clearance of bleeding, and restoration of the dynamic balance between CSF secretion and discharge, ultimately leading to decreased drainage volume at the 7 the postoperative day.  

In addition, the results of this study depicted that the GCS score of the neuroendoscopic group was higher than that of the control group at 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days after surgery. This highlighted the efficacy of neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricular lavage in effectively improving the postoperative neurological function of patients with severe intraventricular hemorrhage. This effect may be related to the more comprehensive elimination of bleeding through neuroendoscopy in the early stage. Liu et $\mathrm{al^{18}}$ confirmed that intracranial hematomas released toxins, potentially leading to brain edema and a decline in neurological function. Timely hematoma removal could substantially reduce the rate of secondary brain injury. Moreover, Zhou et al19 asserted that neuroendoscopy effectively improved the postoperative GCS score and modified Rankin Scale in patients with severe cerebral hemorrhage, thereby elevating patients’ neurological function and minimizing the occurrence of postoperative adverse reactions. The reason may be attributed to the illuminating capabilities of neuroendoscopy, allowing surgeons to operate directly within their field of vision. The flexibility to adjust angles and directions enhances exploratory capabilities and thorough irrigation of the ventricle system, consequently promoting CSF recirculation, and reducing nerve function injuries.

The study conducted by Zhu et $\mathrm{al}^{17}$ further supported these findings, asserting that neuroendoscopy effectively improved the prognosis of severe intraventricular hemorrhage patients and their functional capacity in daily living. Based on current study, the prognosis level of the study group was higher than that of the control group at 1, 3, and 7 days after surgery, underscoring that neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricular lavage effectively improved the daily living function and prognosis of patients with severe intraventricular hemorrhage.  

The study supported by Du et al20 pointed out the swift removal of ventricular hematoma and the reduction of associated complications through the application of neuroendoscopy in severe intraventricular hemorrhage patients. Atsumi et $a|^{21}$ highlighted the high precision of neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricle lavage. This technique enabled accurate targeting of the hematoma’s location while distinguishing it from surrounding normal tissue, reduced surgical trauma, avoided the emergence of new hemorrhagic spots, and thus improved postoperative safety. Neuroendoscopic hematoma removal combined with ventricle lavage demonstrated effectiveness in flushing our bleeding clots, ameliorating CSF obstruction, and reducing the incidence of intracranial infection.13 Our study indicated a notably lower incidence of intracranial infection and increased intracranial pressure in the neuroendoscopic group compared with the control group, suggesting that the combined procedure could effectively mitigate the occurrence of postoperative adverse events in patients with severe intraventricular hemorrhage.  

# Limitations  

However, this study had certain limitation that warranted acknowledgement. (1) For patients with intraventricular hematomas located deep within the brain, particularly those without ventricular expansion on entering the ventricle, performing neuroendoscopic surgery proved challenging because of elevated intraoperative brain tissue pressure and limited operating space associated with transcorticoventricle neuroendoscopic approach, which could potentially lead to surgical failure. (2) In cases where patients exhibited obvious cerebral ventricular involvement, the straightforward removal of hemygovirus was found to be less effective. It was essential to address posterior median fourth ventricular hematoma concurrently to enhance the patient’s survival prospects by early restoration of CSF circulation. (3)The sample size in the study was relatively small because of constraints in available clinical cases. (4)This article did not explore or compare alternative treatments for intraventricular hemorrhage, such as ventriculostomy tPA or tNK irrigation, or the use of irrigating ventriculostomy systems. These issues will be addressed in subsequent follow-up studies.  

# CONCLUSION  

The combination of neuroendoscopic hematoma removal and ventricular lavage demonstrated several benefits in the treatment of severe intraventricular hemorrhage. It led to shortened drainage tube indwelling time and reduced hospital stays, while also contributing to increased hematoma clearance rates and CSF drainage volumes. Moreover, this approach improved nerve function recovery and patient prognosis while reducing the incidence of adverse events. Therefore, it is a promising technique for clinical application. Despite the evident efficacy of surgical interventions, it is important to recognize that the surgery remains a traumatic treatment method. # COMMENTS  

T his study is one in which the authors compare neuroendoscopic intraventricular hematoma evacuation and lavage to traditional ventriculostomy drainage. The results are encouraging in that neuroendoscopic hematoma removal and lavage improved both immediate and delayed Glasgow Coma Scale scores as well as modified Rankin Scale scores at 6 months. Neuroendoscopic treatment also reduced hospital stays, length of cerebrospinal fluid diversion, infection rates, and intracranial pressures. Although the authors focus their comparisons on traditional ventriculostomy drainage, they mention several additional methods and techniques that are utilized more and more commonly as technology has continued to advance. One of these is the additional of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and/or other fibrinolytic agents to aid in breakdown and clearance of the intraventricular hematoma. Another is the utilization of irrigating ventriculostomy systems, which themselves can irrigate multiple solutions, such as normal saline, lactated Ringer solution, and/or saline infused with tPA. Perhaps this article will stimulate additional research because comparisons of neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuations and lavage to irrigating ventriculostomy systems might be important in determining the most optimal course of treatment for patients suffering from such potentially devastating conditions, especially given that the latter does not immediately remove the mass effect from the hematoma itself.
