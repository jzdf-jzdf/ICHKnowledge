# Abstract  

Aims: Statins had been used as a cornerstone in the primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease. Widespread attention had been given to the risk of bleeding, especially intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) in patients receiving statins therapy. This study aimed to determine whether statins treatment was associated with the risk of bleeding and ICH in randomized controlled trials (RCTs).  

Materials and methods: Electronic databases were searched for studies up to September 8, 2022. Articles from RCTs were included in the meta-analysis if they reported the bleeding events associated with the treatment of statins or placebo/nonstatin treatment. The risk ratios (RR) of total bleeding and ICH were pooled from the number of patients with each outcome in the statins and control groups from the included studies.  

Results: Twenty-nine studies comprising 145,929 individuals (2437 incident bleeding cases) were included in the meta-analysis. After a median follow-up duration of 3.65 years, statins treatment was not associated with the risk of all bleeding $(\mathsf{RR}=1.03\$ , $95\%\mathsf{Cl}0.93\substack{-1.15}$ ). Furthermore, in 26 studies comprising 144,177 participants, after a median follow-up duration of 3.95 years, statins treatment was not associated with the risk of ICH $\mathit{\Omega}^{\prime}\mathrm{RR}=1.05\$ , $95\%$ CI 0.84 1.31). Although in the subgroup analysis with patients with prior stroke, statins treatment showed an increased risk of ICH ( $\mathsf{RR}=1.47$ , $95\%$ CI 1.07 2.01), sensitivity analysis showed that the result was unstable, which may be mainly driven by the SPARCL study.  

Conclusions: Statins therapy is not associated with the risk of all bleeding and ICH. Although a mildly increased risk of ICH in patients with prior stroke is observed, which may be caused by chance finding and warrant further documentation.

# 1 INTRODUCTION  

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has been great health and economic burden worldwide. In 2019, about 18.6 million deaths were attributed to CVD globally.1 Statins, known as 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors, can reduce cholesterol synthesis by blocking the rate-limiting step of cholesterol biosynthesis,2,3 and have been used as a cornerstone in the primary and secondary prevention of CVD.4–7  

Although numerous studies have shown that the benefits of statins therapy outweigh the disadvantages, eight widespread attentions had been given to the risk of bleeding in patients receiving statins therapy. A subanalysis of the SPARCL (Stroke Prevention by Aggressive Reduction in Cholesterol Levels) trial demonstrated that, compared with placebo, atorvastatin treatment was associated with a higher risk of hemorrhagic stroke.9 However, this association was not found in other large randomized controlled trials. A recently published metaanalysis reported that statins could increase the risk of hemorrhagic stroke, while such an association was not observed in another potent cholesterol lowing drug-proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9is).10 Interestingly, another meta-analysis of observational studies reported that statins treatment could reduce the risk of all-cause mortality, as well as the risk of bleeding in patients with atrial fibrillation.11 Our recently published study also showed that statins could reduce the risk of recurrent venous thromboembolism events (VTE) and bleeding in patients with a history of VTE.12 However, the observational characteristics on their own cannot rule out the possibilities of underlying confounders and establish a causation relationship.  

Considering these inconsistent results, we performed this metaanalysis of randomized controlled trials to explore whether there was an association between stains treatment and the risk of bleeding, especially intracranial hemorrhage (ICH).  

# 2 METHODS  

# 2.1 Data sources, search strategy, and selection criteria  

This meta-analysis was not registered previously. Following the recommendations of Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement,13 we searched studies in PubMed, Embase, The Cochrane Library with the following terms for statins (“Hydroxymethylglutaryl CoA Reductase Inhibitors,” “pravastatin,” “fluvastatin,” “lovastatin,” “simvastatin,” “rosuvastatin,” “atorvastatin,” or “pitavastatin”) and the type of study (“randomised controlled trial,” “controlled clinical trial”). The last search was performed on September 8, 2022. The detailed search strategy for PubMed is presented in Online Supplementary Table 1.  

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials, which explored the effect of statins treatment compared with placebo or nonstatin treatment; (2) The sample size is $\geq150$ individuals and with a follow-up duration $\geq$ 3 months; (3) Bleeding events had been reported. The exclusion criteria were: (1) studies that compared different doses or kinds of statins, rather than statins treatment and placebo or nonstatin treatment; (2) studies explored the effects of stains compared to other cholesterollowering drugs; (3) duplicated articles were derived from the same original study. If more than one article reported the same bleeding event were derived from the same trial, only the one with the complete data was selected for analysis. However, if one article reported a bleeding event and another reported other type of bleeding events, both would be included for analysis.  

# 2.2 Data extraction, quality assessment, and statistical analysis  

The quality of studies was assessed by Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool,14 which comprised 6 fields and was graded as “highrisk,” “low-risk” and “unclear-risk,” respectively. Two authors (XL and HZ) independently searched for studies and extracted data according to the predefined criteria. Study characteristics were recorded in a predefined chart.  

The primary outcome was the relative risk of all bleeding events associated with statins treatment compared with placebo or nonstatin treatment. The secondary outcome was the relative risk of ICH events. The risk ratios (RR) of total bleeding and ICH were pooled from the number of patients with each outcome in the statins and control groups from the included studies. Trials with zero outcome events in both treatment groups were excluded from the analysis. The Mantel–Haenszel approach was used to calculate the pooled RRs. Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed by the I2 statistics, of which a value $>50\%$ was substantial heterogeneity. Considering the underlying clinical and methodological heterogeneity, we used the random-effects models as the primary analysis methods, even when no statistical heterogeneity was observed. We evaluated the publication bias by inspecting the funnel plots and further testing with Egger’s test.  

Subgroup analyses of the outcomes were conducted on different types of trials design (primary or secondary prevention of CVD/stroke), participants’ mean ages $\left(<60\right.$ years vs. $\ge60$ years), statins intensities (low, medium, and high intensities), follow-up duration $^{\prime}<2$ years vs. ${\ge}2$ years), and ethnicity (Asians vs. non-Asians). We performed metaregression according to age, the proportion of women, and follow-up duration. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by omitting one study at a time to evaluate the impact of each study on the estimated risk. We also performed sensitivity analyses by excluding the studies extracted from previous meta-analyses, as the data could not be directly found in the RCTs’ original publications.  

All the analyses were performed using Review Manager software (version 5.3, the Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) and Stata 15.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). All $p$ values are two-tailed, and the statistical significance was set at 0.05.

# 3 RESULTS  

# 3.1 Study characteristics of individual studies  

Our initial search identified 6579 potentially relevant records. After screening the titles and abstracts, 63 articles qualified for a full review and 24 articles (from 22 RCTs) were included for analysis.9,16–38 Furthermore, we further detected seven other $\mathsf{RCTs}^{39-45}$ from a previously published meta-analysis.15 Therefore, 31 reports from 29 RCTs were finally included in our meta-analysis (Online Supplementary Figure 1).  

Characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1. Of the 29 RCTs, 8 reported the effect of statins in the primary prevention of CVD/stroke and 21 aimed to explore the role of statins in the secondary prevention of CVD/stroke. There were 145,929 participants (average age 62.08 years) with 2437 bleeding events in a median follow-up duration of 3.65 years. Only two studies were conducted in  

Asia, and all others were carried out in Europe, North America, South America, and Oceania. Quality assessments of the included studies were presented in Online Supplementary Figure 2.  

# 3.2 Statins and risk of all bleeding  

Pooled results from the 29 included studies showed that, compared with placebo or nonstatin treatment, statins treatment was not associated with the risk of bleeding events $\mathrm{\mathit{RR}}=1.03$ , $95\%~{\sfCI}~0.93$ to 1.15, Figure 1). No significant heterogeneity was found in the included studies $\mathit{\Omega}^{\prime}|^{2}=10\%$ , $p=0.32$ ). No publication bias was observed by the visual funnel plot (Online Supplementary Figure 3) and Egger’s test $(p=0.753)$ . We also excluded the data extracted from the previous meta-analysis (but not clearly reported in their originally published articles) and found that the results were consistent $\mathrm{\mathit{RR}}=1.03$ , $95\%$ CI 0.94 1.12, Online Supplementary Figure 4).

# 3.3 Statins and risk of hemorrhagic stroke  

Information on the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke was available in 26 trials comprising 144,177 randomized patients. No significant heterogeneity was found in the included studies $\mathrm{\Delta}P=30\%$ , $p=0.07\$ ). Compared with placebo or nonstatin treatment, statins treatment was not associated with the risk of intracerebral hemorrhages $\mathsf{RR}=1.05$ , $95\%$ $\mathsf{Cl}0.84\mathrm{-}1.31$ , Figure 2). Furthermore, the result was not significantly changed when the data extracted from the previous meta-analysis were excluded $\mathrm{\mathit{RR}}=1.10$ , $95\%$ CI 0.91−1.34, Online Supplementary Figure 5).  

# 3.4 Subgroup analyses, meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses  

For the risk of all bleeding, there was no significant heterogeneity among subgroups defined according to to study type, participants’ average age, ethnicity, follow-up duration, statins intensity, type of bleeding events and intervention methods in the control group (Table 2). Meta-regression analysis also demonstrated that the risk of all bleeding was not changed by follow-up duration, the proportion of females, and participants’ mean age (all $p>0.05$ , Online Supplementary Figure 6). Sensitivity analysis confirmed that there was no association between statins treatment and bleeding events when changing random-effects models to fixed-effects models or removing a study once a time and recalculating the pooled RR (Online Supplementary Figure 7).  

We also found a neutral effect of statins treatment on the risk of ICH based on subgroup analyses according to study type, participants’ average age, ethnicity, follow-up duration, statins intensity, and intervention methods in the control group (Table 3). In a subgroup analysis of patients with previous stroke (secondary prevention of stroke), statins treatment was associated with an increased risk of ICH $\mathrm{\mathit{RR}}=1.47$ , $95\%$ CI 1.07 2.01) (Table 3, Online Supplementary Figure 8). However, sensitivity analysis showed that the results were not stable. When the SPARCL or HPS trial was excluded, the association between statins treatment and risk of ICH became insignificant (Online Supplementary Figure 9).

# 4 DISCUSSION  

In this comprehensive meta-analysis of 29 studies comprising 145,929 participants, two major findings were observed. First, statins therapy was not associated with the risk of all bleeding and ICH. Second, statins therapy may increase the risk of ICH in patients with prior stroke; however, such association was mainly driven by the SPARCL study and warrant further documentation.  

With the widespread use of statins, their pleiotropic effects have been wildly discussed, including anti-inflammation, antiplatelet, anticoagulant, and fibrinolytic effects.3,46 Undoubtedly, such off-target effects caused extensive attention for the risk of bleeding, especially when wildly combined treatment with anticoagulation or antiplatelet treatment. It is interesting that some recent observational studies showed conflicting results.47,48 Two independent meta-analyses of observational studies with 43,329 and 60,523 participants respectively reported that in patients with atrial fibrillation and VTE, treatment with statins did not, while decreased the risk of bleeding.11,12 Although potential confounders cannot be excluded due to the observational characteristics of the included studies in these meta-analyses, these results supported that statins treatment is safe regarding the risk of bleeding. In the current meta-analysis, we pooled data from large sample size RCTs to overcome the confounding bias in previous studies, and our results further support the notion that statins treatment is not associated with the risk of all bleeding or ICH.  

Compared with bleeding in other sites, the risk of ICH had gotten much more cautious under the use of statins. Early epidemiologic studies had reported an association between low serum cholesterol levels and the increased risk of ICH.49–51 However, such association may be confounded by potential risk factors, for example, excessive alcohol drinking and malnutrition.52 The SPARCL trial, a landmark study of statins in patients with previous stroke or transient ischemic attack, showed that statins therapy might increase the incidence of ICH.9 Previous meta-analyses on the risk of ICH associated with statins treatment showed inconsistent results.15,53 Hackam and colleagues included 23 RCTs and 19 observational studies for analysis and showed that statins were neither associated with an increased risk of ICH in RCTs nor in observational studies.54 In contrast, recent meta-analyses showed that statins treatment increased the risk of ICH, especially in those with a previous history of stroke and who received high-dose statins.10,55 These inconsistent results may attribute to the difference in the inclusion of studies and methodology for pool analysis. In this comprehensive meta-analysis, we only included large sample size RCTs with a follow-up duration of more than three months, which could decrease the possibility of random error. Our results showed that the risk of ICH was not increased in patients who received statins treatment. The results were unstable when focused on patients with a previous history of stroke, although a mildly increased risk of ICH was observed. In sensitivity analysis, after the exclusion of the SPARCL trial, the association between statins treatment and ICH became neutral.

Mechanistically, although several hypotheses had been proposed to explain the increased risk of ICH observed in the SPARCL trial, it was still great controversial. First, it was suspected that cholesterollowering might impair blood vessel integrity in the brain, thus resulting in ICH. However, the cholesterol in the brain is insulated from changes in circulating cholesterol, and animals experiments also indicated that $95\%$ of the cholesterol content in the brain could be accounted for by de novo synthesis.56 Furthermore, the PCSK9is, a potent lipidlowering drug, which reduces LDL-C more significantly than statins, was not associated with the risk of ICH.10 Actually, post hoc analysis from the SPARCL trial showed that there were no relationships between the risk of ICH and baseline or recent LDL-C levels in atorvastatin treated patients, and $86\%$ of the increased risk of ICH can be attributed to the male sex, previous hemorrhagic stroke, and advancing age.57 Second, potential anticoagulation and antiplatelet effects of statins were also proposed to explain the increased risk of ICH in the SPARCL trial. However, no statistical interaction between statins treatment and the use of antithrombotic drugs was observed in the SPARCL trial. Furthermore, the THRombolysis and STatins (THRaST) study also showed that the use of statins after thrombolysis did not increase the risk of hemorrhagic transformation, with a reduced risk of neurologic deterioration.58 Other real-world data also found that statins use after recanalization therapy in ischemic stroke did not increase while reducing the risk of hemorrhagic transformation.59 Recently, a retrospective study found that a longer duration of statins use is associated with a lower risk of ICH.60 Taken these results with data on all bleeding risks in our studies, the potential antithrombotic effect of statins cannot explain the excess number of ICH observed in the SPARCL trial. Therefore, the observed risk of ICH associated with statins treatment in the SPARCL trial and subsequent meta-analyses might result from a chance finding.  

The neutral association between statins treatment and the risk of bleeding observed in our study may have important clinical implications. Although the risk of bleeding was not considered as a statins side-effect in the current guideline,61 and intensive therapy to lower lipid serum levels was recommended in patients with coronary heart disease and stroke, the worrying of bleeding risk (especially ICH) would make the clinical physicians and researchers consider discontinued or decreasing the dose of statins in patients with recent stroke. Research has shown that early discontinuation of statins was associated with increased death from CVD and risk of myocardial infarction.8 Further large RCTs are still needed to investigate the effect of statins therapy in patients with previous stroke. In patients with AF and acute coronary syndrome, concomitant treatment with multiple antithrombotic drugs may possess a high risk of bleeding, and these treatments should not be considered as an indication of lowing the intensity of statins treatment, as shown in our results, which was not associated with the risk of bleeding.  

The current study was strengthened by the large sample of the included studies. Furthermore, we only included data from RCTs for analysis, which would mitigate the effects of underlying confounders in previous meta-analyses.11,12 However, some limitations should be noted in our study. First, in most of the included studies, the definition of bleeding was not predefined. The risk of major bleeding events was not available. Second, except for ICH, limited studies were available to analyze bleeding in other sites, including gastrointestinal bleeding, which is a significant risk of bleeding in patients with CVD, due to the broad use of antithrombotic drugs. Further collaborative studies are needed to document the concomitant treatment of statins and antithrombotic drugs and the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding. Third, many drugs can affect the absorption and metabolism of statins. However, we did not have access to individual patients’ data to explore the drug interactions and the risk of bleeding. Finally, we did not search studies from Chinese databases because the quality of these studies might not so good.  

In conclusion, statins therapy is not associated with the risk of bleeding. However, a mildly increased risk of ICH in patients with prior stroke is observed, which may be caused by a chance error and warrant further exploration.
