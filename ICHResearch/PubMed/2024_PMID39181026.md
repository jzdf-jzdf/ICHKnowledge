Introduction: Acute subdural hemorrhage (ASDH) from traumatic brain injury is a life-threatening situation, often requiring surgical intervention. This meta-analysis is done to update the literature regarding the choice of procedure for the treatment of ASDH.  

Methods: PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane were searched from the year 2000 up to September 2023. Randomized controlled trials and observational studies were included. The odds ratio with $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) mean difference and standardized mean difference were calculated for dichotomous and continuous outcomes, respectively.  

Results: A total of 14 studies comprising 4686 patients were included in the analysis. Pooled Glasgow Outcome Scale/Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale scores were compared based on their means, with the craniotomy (CO) group having better mean scores than decompressive craniectomy (DC) (standardized mean difference 0.37, $95\%$ CI $-0.68$ to 0.06, $P=0.02$ ). The risk for poor outcomes was statistically greater in the DC group compared to the CO group (1.32, $95\%$ CI 1.05-1.66, P value ${\it\Omega}=0.02$ ). There were fewer residual subdural hematoma cases in the DC group as compared to CO (odds ratio 0.40, $95\%$ CI 0.22-0.73, P value $<0.005$ ).  

Conclusions: Our meta-analysis showed that the ASDH patients had better functional outcomes when treated with CO as compared to DC. However, there were fewer odds of residual subdural hematoma with DC.

# Introduction  

Acute subdural hemorrhage (ASDH) is a serious complication of traumatic brain injury (TBI). Approximately $10\%{-}20\%$ of patients admitted due to TBI are diagnosed with ASDH, with a higher incidence in patients presenting with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) less than 8.1 TBI has the highest global burden among all causes of ASDH, with a reported 27.16 million cases in 2019 alone.2 ASDH is responsible for substantial mortality, approaching anywhere from $50\%$ to $90\%$ . Additionally, patients of ASDH show poor brain function on recovery.  

Surgery is indicated in ASDH when there are mass effect and neurological deficits present. Additionally, if there is a rapid progression of neurological deficit despite initial consciousness being normal, surgery must be performed.4 Surgical treatment for ASDH includes cranioplastic craniotomy, decompressive craniectomy (DC), craniostomy, and/ or trephination.1 The goal of each technique is to relieve the patient of the increased intracranial pressure (ICP) due to the mass effect and to prevent consequent functional and structural neurological deficits, caused by the hematoma. In craniotomy (CO), the bone flap is removed and returned as soon as the hematoma is evacuated, whereas in DC, the bone flap is removed, without immediate replacement. Instead, a reconstructive surgery is planned with similar bone pieces or synthetic implants.  

Consensus regarding which of CO or DC is associated with better outcomes has not been achieved. A previously published meta-analysis in 2017 indicated that both CO and DC have their merits with regard to morphological and functional outcomes. DC allows for better management of ICP, as the removal of the bone flap allows for the swollen cerebral tissue to expand. However, it comes with the added risks of a second operation, whereby the removed skull flap needs to be repositioned.5 Recently a randomized controlled trial (RCT) published in the New England Journal of Medicine used the Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOSE) and EuroQoL Group 5-Dimension 5-Level questionnaire score to evaluate functional neurological outcomes and quality of life with disability outcomes, respectively. The comprehensive assessment revealed no significant difference between DC and CO. The trial showed a greater incidence of surgical site infectionerelated complications in the DC group, while patients in the CO group required a second DC to resolve brain swelling. While the sample size requirement for power of trial was met, there were still many individuals who dropped out of the study, and for whom valuable outcome data were not available.6  

Currently, the decision between the two modalities is made by the surgeon, keeping in view prior experience, patient characteristics, and associated risks. Due to a lack of adequate guidelines regarding the management of ASDH, this meta-analysis merges the latest study data with previous findings. Our study hypothesizes that DC yields better postoperative outcomes in the management of ASDH. This comprehensive study aims to guide future choice of surgery for ASDH.  

# Methods  

# Search strategy  

We performed this systematic review and meta-analysis according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.7 A comprehensive search was conducted across electronic databases including PubMed, Elsevier’s Scopus, and Cochrane’s CENTRAL for literature available from the year 2000 up to September 2023. The keywords or Medical Subject Headings terms used included “Craniotomy” AND “Craniectomy” OR “Decompressive craniectomy” AND “Acute subdural hematoma” OR “Acute subdural hemorrhage.” A detailed search string is provided in Supplementary File Table ST1. The references generated by the articles that came forth were also reviewed to identify relevant studies.  

# Screening of studies  

The studies were imported into a Zotero library and duplicates were removed. Initial screening on the basis of title and abstract was performed by three authors (M. R., H. F., and A. H.). The resulting studies were then screened using full texts of the manuscripts and any discrepancies in the screening process were resolved by two senior authors (M. H. K. and S. F. A.).  

Articles that met the following inclusion criteria were selected: (1) patients above the age of 18 y with ASDH; (2) headto-head comparison between DC and CO; and (3) comparison of Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS)/GOSE scores, mortality, reoperation, and residual subdural hematoma (SDH) as outcomes between patients undergoing DC and CO. Reoperation is defined as subsequent operation after the initial procedure, performed for repeat drainage or any complication resulting from the original surgery. Residual SDH is the persistence of hematoma within the subdural space after the initial hematoma was cleared by operation. This was a studyreported outcome, and the definition used in the studies was considered. Studies that reported on any of the mentioned outcomes were included. RCTs and observational studies (with or without propensity matching) were included. Studies were required to report the cause of ASDH, and TBI as well as non-TBIerelated ASDH patients were included.  

Those studies that did not compare craniectomy and CO directly, or those comparing DC or CO with another surgical modality, were excluded. Review articles, case series, case reports, editorials, correspondences, and single-arm studies were excluded. Additionally, preprints, animal-based studies, and studies published in languages other than English were excluded.  

# Data extraction  

Data relating to type of study, sample size, age, gender, presenting GCS score, degree of pupil reactivity, ICP elevation at presentation, and size of hematoma were extracted from the tables, figures, and texts present in the articles by three independent investigators. Any disparity that arose was resolved by consulting with M. F., who rechecked the extracted data.  

Outcomes that were studied were divided into primary and secondary outcomes. Primary outcomes were mean GOS or GOSE scores, poor outcomes (taken as the sum of death, vegetative state, and severe disability), and reoperation. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, mortality at the $6\textrm{mo}$ and $12\mathrm{mo}$ , and persistence of SDH despite operation.  

# Statistical analysis  

Statistical analysiswas conductedonReviewManager (RevMan, version 5.4; The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) for Windows. The random-effect model was used with the inversevariance method.Dichotomous outcomeswere compared using odds ratios (ORs) and $95\%$ confidence interval (CI). Continuous outcomes were compared by mean difference (MD) and standard deviation (SD), while standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to compare scores assessed using more than one scale. All the P values were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at P value $<0.05$ .  

Sensitivity analyses were carried out as a secondary analysis based on outcomes that had very high heterogeneity (assessed using the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ statistic) and for those studies that enrolled patients having ASDH only at the time of initial presentation. Cochrane’s guidelines8 were followed in performing sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method for outcomes having heterogeneity more than $50\%$ , excluding studies that gave the greatest reduction in $\operatorname{I}^{2}$ statistics. This allowed for the comparison of outcomes with and without the outlying studies, thus enabling a more robust analysis. Subgroup analysis was run based on the cause of ASDH (TBI or non-TBI) and the data were additionally split based on the type of study: RCT, propensity-matched study (PSM), and non-PSM observational studies. This split based on study type was done to evaluate outcomes when the choice of surgery made by the surgeon did not impact the enrollment of participants into study groups. In RCTs, the patients were randomly allocated to receive either DC or CO. In PSM observational studies, patients with similar baseline characteristics and presenting conditions were compared. This was done as previous studies have shown CO to be preferred over DC in patients with poor initial presentation. Randomization and PSM allow for minimizing the discrepancy in the presenting characteristics of patients assigned to the intervention and control groups. In cases where there are few RCTs and the sample size in said RCTs is small, the inclusion of observational studies in a meta-analysis may improve the precision of outcomes, resulting in more relevant results.9

# Quality assessment  

Bias assessment was undertaken using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for nonrandomized (observational) studies and Risk of Bias 2.0 (ROB2.0) by Cochrane for RCTs.10,11 Quality of evidence assessment was conducted according to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system, whereby the judgment of evidence was made as high, moderate, low, or very low according to the GRADE Working Group.12  

# Publication bias  

For publication bias, the Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index was employed to quantify the asymmetry of effect sizes from Doi plots. This was performed on STATA v17.0 (STATACorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) for Windows. The LFK index less than $\pm1$ was taken as having no asymmetry, while LFK index more than $\pm1$ but within $\pm2$ was taken as minor asymmetry, and more than $\pm2$ represented major asymmetry.  

This manuscript was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews under ProsperoID CRD42023485487.  

# Results  

The initial database search yielded a total of 1206 articles, of which 802 were assessed for eligibility-based titles and abstracts after deduplication. The full texts of 424 articles were assessed for eligibility using the inclusion criteria, of which eight studies6,13-19 were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, in addition to the six studies20-25 included in the previous meta-analysis.5 There were two RCTs and 12 observational studies. Of these 12, four observational studies presented PSM data. Figure 1 shows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses flowchart.

# Poor outcomes  

Eight studies reported data on poor outcomes. DC group was found to be significantly associated with greater odds of poor outcome (OR 1.32, $95\%$ CI 1.05-1.66, P value $=0.02$ , $\mathtt{I}^{2}=0\%$ ). GRADE assessment showed a high quality of evidence for this outcome.  

Split of the study data based on study type did not reveal a statistically significant difference in poor outcomes between DC and CO for any of the study types, as shown in Figure 3.  

# Reoperation  

The need for reoperation was reported by nine studies in our analysis. There was no statistically significant difference in the odds of reoperation between the two groups (OR 1.50, $95\%$ CI 0.87-2.58, $P>0.05$ , $\mathtt{I}^{2}=52\%$ ). Quality assessment using GRADE exhibited high quality of evidence.  

Based on study type, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of reoperation among the non-PSM studies (five studies) shown in Figure 4.  

# Secondary outcomes  

# Mortality outcomes  

A total of seven studies included in the analysis mentioned in-hospital mortality and eight studies reported the data on mortality after discharge. The risk of in-hospital mortality was not significantly associated with either DC or CO group (OR 1.46, $95\%$ CI 0.87-2.43, P value $>0.05$ , $\mathtt{I}^{2}=91\%$ ). GRADE assessment revealed a moderate quality of evidence.

Mortality at 6-mo follow-up was not significantly different between the two interventions (OR 1.30, $95\%$ CI 0.90-1.88, P value $>0.05$ , $\mathtt{I}^{2}=41\%$ ). Split of the data based on study type revealed a statistically significant difference in the odds of mortality at 6-mo follow-up in the non-PSM group (OR 1.71, $95\%$ CI 1.05-2.79, P value $<0.05$ , $\mathtt{I}^{2}=0\%$ , three studies), as shown in Figure 6. At the $12\mathrm{-mo}$ follow-up, there was no significant difference in the odds of mortality between the two interventions. However, the result should be interpreted with caution as it was reported by only two studies as shown in Figure 7. GRADE evaluation showed very poor quality of evidence, due to the small sample size.  

# Residual SDH  

Residual SDH was assessed by three studies only. However, it was included as it was an important outcome and had an important bearing on the patient’s recovery. There was a statistically significant benefit in the population that underwent DC compared to CO, with an OR of 0.40 $(95\%\mathrm{CI}0.22–0.73$ P value $<0.005$ , $\mathtt{I}^{2}=0\%$ ), given in Figure 8. The quality of evidence for this outcome was found to be poor, as assessed by GRADE. Subgroup analysis for this was not possible owing to the few studies.  

# Sensitivity analysis  

Sensitivity analysis was performed to exclude studies that reported other forms of intracranial hemorrhage (in addition to ASDH). Two studies, in addition to ASDH, also reported non-ASDH, namely extradural hemorrhage,15,18 subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage,15,18 and intraparenchymal hemorrhage.15  

After the exclusion of these studies, we received new pooled effect sizes, given in Figure 9A-D.  

Table 2 compares the effect sizes before and after the sensitivity analysis.  

When studies with only ASDH patients were considered, DC had worse outcomes compared to CO, indicated by the higher OR and greater difference in SMD for GOSE scores.  

Sensitivity analysis based on heterogeneity revealed three outcomes having $\operatorname{I}^{2}$ more than $50\%$ . To reduce it to the threshold of $50\%$ , we excluded studies in Figure 10A-C. The difference in effect estimates and $95\%$ CI is given in Table 3.  

When heterogeneity is reduced below the threshold value by excluding certain studies responsible for the heterogeneity, DC still has worse outcomes compared to CO; however, there are fewer odds for worse outcomes (lower ORs) and less difference in functional outcomes (less negative SMD for GOSE).  

# Risk of bias  

Both the RCTs exhibited low risk of bias when assessed according to the five domains mentioned in Cochrane ROB2.0.  

Of the 12 observational studies, there were nine studies of good quality as measured by the NOS criteria, while three were of poor quality. Details of bias assessment are provided in the Supplementary File Table ST3 and Table ST4. Details of the quality of evidence using GRADE are given in Supplementary File Table ST5.  

# Publication bias  

Most outcomes had a low publication bias when evaluated using the LFK index and Doi plots. However, two of the outcomes, namely Mortality at $12\mathrm{mo}$ and Residual SDH, showed considerable asymmetry (Z-index 2.40 and 4.27, respectively). Details of Doi plots and LFK indices are given in Supplementary Figures SF1-SF7.  

# Discussion  

Our meta-analysis of 14 studies compares the outcomes of DC and CO for the treatment of ASDH, to evaluate the current state of evidence for surgical management of ASDH. This meta-analysis is the largest study assessing pooled outcomes for both DC and CO in the case of ASDH. The results from the analysis exhibit a statistically significant improvement in pooled GOS/GOSE scores among the CO patients. While there was a significant benefit seen among the DC group with regards to residual SDH, this outcome was assessed only by three studies, which may limit its generalizability. The included studies largely showed a low risk of bias across primary outcomes included in our meta-analysis. Nine of the 12 observational studies were rated as good quality according to NOS criteria. The observational studies exhibiting poor quality of evidence had inadequate assessment of outcomes and poor comparability between the DC and the CO groups. One of the strengths of our study is the comparison of outcomes as they have been published in literature according to study types (RCTs, PSM studies, and non-PSM studies). Split data analysis revealed that the odds of reoperation were significantly higher in the DC group in RCTs and PSM studies, indicating that when patient characteristics are not a confounding factor, DC patients have greater odds for reoperation. In-hospital mortality was found to be significantly different in non-PSM studies, with the DC group having higher odds for in-hospital deaths. This observation can be attributed to the greater likelihood of patients with worse presenting conditions being treated with DC. However, the limited study data means more research is required to validate these results.  

ASDH is a serious condition that often requires surgical management.26 The goal of surgery is to reduce the pressure on the brain and prevent further injury. However, there is no consensus on the best surgical approach, and CO and DC are both employed frequently, among other surgical techniques such as burr-hole craniotomy, hinge craniotomy, and cranioplasty craniotomy. The outcomes of ASDH are influenced by a number of factors including age, time from injury to treatment, brainstem injury assessed by lack of pupillary reactivity, preoperative GCS, duration of increased ICP, and computed tomography features including midline shift, basal cistern compression, size of hematoma, and systemic abnormalities such as hypoxia and hypotension.27-29 Our study revealed that there was a significantly greater number of individuals with severe head injuries $({\mathsf{GCS}}\leq8)$ in the DC group as compared to the CO group.14,17,18,20,23 Thus, there is an inherent bias toward opting for DC rather than CO as the procedure of choice by the surgeons for the patients having poor neurologic status on presentation. Baseline characteristics consistently showed a greater incidence of midline shift and bilateral mydriasis in the patients of DC group as compared to the CO group indicating that TBI was more severe and extensive in patients undergoing DC. While this indicates that surgeons have greater confidence in managing potential cerebral swelling and ICP after surgery using DC compared to CO, this remained a major limitation for us, as we did not have the individual patient data to adjust for the disparity between patients undergoing DC versus CO.  

Outcomes in literature for CO and DC for ASDH have been inconclusive. Two database studies, performed using the National Trauma Data Bank and Japan Trauma Data Bank, allowed for a large cohort to be selected and PSM was performed, with no significant difference observed in the matched group of patients on the basis of mortality. However, the study performed using Japan Trauma Data Bank showed a significantly longer duration of hospital stay in the DC group.14,16 A study conducted on the elderly being treated for ASDH with DC and CO exhibited a worse set of outcomes, indicating that the complication rates tend to increase with advancing age.30  

Although the studies included in the review compared similar population groups, that is, patients of TBI with ASDH undergoing evacuation surgery, there were several limitations in the included studies. One major limitation was the retrospective design of 12 of the 14 included studies, which introduces bias related to selection and confounding factors. Although a few studies attempted to minimize the bias associated with observational studies using propensity matching, most studies did not have the same degree of comparability between the two groups undergoing DC and CO. Ahmed et al.14 and Kwon et al.21 highlighted the inability to account for specific clinical details and small patient populations, respectively, which limits the generalizability of conclusions. Furthermore, van Essen et al.17 discussed the issue of generalizability because of the variations in interventions and the specific practices of tertiary referral centers. Another critical limitation, as mentioned by Shibahashi et al.,16 is the lack of long-term outcome data, which is crucial for assessing the impact of surgical interventions. Thus, these limitations highlight the need for cautious interpretation of the findings and further research with larger, more controlled studies to validate these results.  

According to ClinicalTrials.gov, there are no ongoing clinical trials comparing DC with CO for the management of ASDH. Recently completed studies that have compared these two procedures include the RESCUE-ASDH trial,6 which was published in 2023 and found no significant difference in functional outcomes at $12\mathrm{mo}$ between patients with ASDH who underwent DC or CO. Similarly, quality-oflife measures (assessed using the EuroQoL Group 5- Dimension 5-Level utility index score) and adverse events were also comparable between the two groups. It was noted that patients undergoing CO in the said trial had to subsequently undergo a DC for the resolution of brain swelling and edema. The CENTER $\mathrm{TBI}^{17}$ observational study, which was also published in 2023, found that the proportion of patients who underwent DC for ASDH varied widely across centers in Europe and Israel. The study made the recommendation of restricting the use of DC to only those patients with severe injuries, in whom replacement of the bone flap is deemed unsuitable.

# Limitations  

A major limitation of our meta-analysis remained the lack of a substantial number of RCTs comparing the two interventions under study. Moreover, among the observational studies, only four of the 12 studies had adjusted for the discrepancy between presenting patient characteristics using propensity scoreematched analysis. The patients undergoing DC had consistently worse poor presenting GCS scores compared to those who underwent CO. When study data split based on study type were analyzed, there was some evidence to suggest greater odds of reoperation (in  

RCTs and PSM studies) and in-hospital mortality (non-PSM studies) in the DC group compared to the CO group; however, these results were not backed by substantial study data. Additionally, major publication bias was observed in the outcomes of residual SDH and 12-mo mortality rates, which should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of studies evaluating these outcomes. Three studies, including one RCT, had reported on other types of hemorrhage (such as extradural and intraventricular hemorrhage) in addition to ASDH. These studies were removed in the sensitivity analysis. As a result, only one RCT reported outcomes of solely ASDH patients. Finally, we excluded the unpublished studies and those in a language other than English, which could lead to potential publication bias. Thus, there is still a need for well-conducted RCTs with large sample sizes and PSMs comparing similar subsets of patients.  

# Conclusions  

The choice of DC or CO for the treatment of ASDH depends on the presentation of the patient and the severity of the injury. The severity of the ASDH is an important factor to consider when choosing between DC and CO. ASDH of a grave nature with poor patient presentation are more likely to require DC to reduce ICP. While the results of this metaanalysis suggest that CO is superior to DC for patients presenting with ASDH in certain domains such as GOS/ GOSE scores, the results are not conclusive in all domains. Consequently, a greater number of quality researches are needed to confirm these findings.
