# ARTICLE Feasibility of intranasal human milk as stem cell therapy in preterm infants with intraventricular hemorrhage  

Rebecca Hoban $\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\omega}}^{1,2\boxtimes},$ Alessia Gallipoli1, Marisa Signorile3, Poonam Mander4, Andree Gauthier-Fisher4, Clifford Librach4,5,6, Diane Wilson1 and Sharon Unger 7  

$\circledcirc$ The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature America, Inc. 2024  

OBJECTIVE: Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) is a common cause of preterm brain injury. Fresh parent’s own milk (POM) contains pluripotent stem cells (SCs) that produce neuronal cells in-vitro. The permeable neonatal blood brain barrier potentially allows SC delivery. We performed the first prospective trial (clinicaltrials.gov NCT04225286) of feasibility of intranasal POM (IPOM) in preterm infants with IVH and described SC content of POM samples.  

STUDY DESIGN: 37 Infants (mean gestation $27.7\pm2.6$ weeks, birthweight $1030\pm3209$ ) with IVH $(35.1\%$ grade IV) were recruited from two tertiary Toronto NICUs. IPOM was given ideally twice daily until 28 days of age. Tolerance and adverse reactions were collected and 162 administering providers surveyed.  

RESULTS: There were no major adverse reactions. Provider surveys suggested acceptability, although potential provider and subject stress requires further study. Milk cell analysis suggests wide variability between parents. CONCLUSIONS: This phase 1 study demonstrated IPOM was tolerated and feasible in preterm infants.  

Journal of Perinatology (2024) 44:1652–1657; https://doi.org/10.1038/s41372-024-01982-8  

# INTRODUCTION  

Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), a leading cause of brain injury in preterm infants, can result in morbidities and increased risk of death or neurodevelopmental impairment due to injury of existing as well as progenitor cells at critical stages of development [1]. There is no therapy for IVH or resultant injury, only management for posthemorrhagic ventricular dilatation (PHVD). Stem cell (SC) therapies have been proposed to treat brain injury to repopulate damaged areas or exert indirect effects to reduce injury and enhance repair [2–8]. Animal models show neuroprotection and improved outcomes with autologous or donor SCs via many routes, including intranasal. In mouse IVH models, SCs attenuated injury and PHVD [2, 7].  

Phase I infant studies for hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, another common neonatal brain injury, have utilized intravenous umbilical cord mesenchymal SCs, requiring specialized pre-planned processing of a single available sample [4, 9]. An innovative, easily collected, continuously produced SC source is human milk (HM). Fresh HM contains pluripotent SCs that share markers with both mesenchymal and embryonic stem cells and can form neuronal cells in vitro [10–12]. In mice, milk SCs populate pup brain tissue and can differentiate into neuronal cells [13]. However, unlike embryonic stem cells, HM SCs do not form tumors in mouse teratoma assays (sub-cutaneous SC injections into immunodeficient mice) likely due to offsprings’ tolerance to maternal cells resulting from the microchimerism associated with pregnancy and breastfeeding [12, 14]. The nares, highly vascular, close to the brain via the olfactory bulb, and lacking caustic gastric enzymes, could be an ideal route for HM SC therapy in preterm infants, who are typically tube fed previously frozen HM that lacks viable SCs [13, 15]. There is evidence that SCs are able to cross the blood-brain barrier; in animal models milk SCs have been found in the brains and organs of suckling mice pups [13]. Animal models of spinal cord injury have demonstrated functional improvements without side effects with direct intrathecal injection of washed HM SCs; circulating SCs may also indirectly facilitate paracrine processes that modify brain injury [15–18]. In addition, fresh HM, which has a unique safety profile and immunotolerance exemplified by the thousands to millions of HM SCs ingested daily by breastfeeding infants (and inadvertently nasally aspirated with reflux), has growth factors and antiinflammatory cytokines that washed, concentrated umbilical and mesenchymal SCs lack [12, 19]. Therefore, HM as SC treatment may have additive benefits [19].  

A small single center, non-protocolized series, the sole report of intranasal HM, trended to lower short-term IVH morbidities; no long-term outcomes were collected [15]. We planned a Phase I trial aimed to determine if fresh parents own milk (POM) can be feasibly delivered intranasally as SC therapy to preterm infants with IVH. POM samples were analyzed for cellular content.  

# MATERIALS/SUBJECTS AND METHODS Methods  

Recruitment took place at two tertiary referral NICUs (The Hospital for Sick Children and Mount Sinai Hospital) in urban Toronto,  

Canada that serve a highly diverse population between July 2020 and September 2021 (clinicaltrials.gov NCT04225286; Canadian New Frontiers grant ID NFRFE-2018-01610.) This study was approved by Clinical Trials Ontario provincial ethics review board; (project ID 1911), was closely monitored by a Data Safety and Monitoring Board comprised of subspecialty pediatricians and a HM scientist who met initially after every 5 patients. Written consent was obtained for infants by their parent(s). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Standard lactation care by board certified lactation consultants and hospital grade double electric pumps to use in-NICU were provided to families regardless of study enrollment; it was ensured that families also had double electric pumps for home use.  

Subjects. Infants ${<}33$ weeks gestation at birth with IVH diagnosed in the first 10 days of age with a lactating parent were eligible. Of note, initially only infants with grade 3–4 IVH (based on Papile definitions) were eligible, but due to an unusually low number of eligible infants in the first months of recruitment, the study protocol was modified to include all grades of IVH given that grade 1–2 IVH, like grade 3–4, is associated with increased odds of neurodevelopmental impairment [1, 20]. Exclusion criteria included: known lethal diagnosis and/or disorder associated with neurodevelopmental delays, critical illness with plans to redirect care, choanal atresia or anomalies that would not allow for intranasal treatment, a surgical condition (ie esophageal atresia) for which the medical team felt the study was contraindicated, already enrolled in an intervention trial targeting neurodevelopment, parent with lactation contraindications and/or who declined lactation initiation, and a lactating parent who was unable or unwilling to provide fresh POM (unable/unwilling to pump at study site or have fresh POM delivered by designee at least once/day for 3 days within $_{3\mathsf{h}}$ of pumping AND located $>30~\mathsf{km}$ from study sites [therefore ineligible for courier services]). After ensuring that the IVH had been disclosed to the family and obtaining clearance from the medical team that redirection of care was not going to occur, parents were approached.

Intranasal human milk intervention: This was a non-randomized, non-blinded prospective intervention study. For enrolled patients, the first dose of intranasal POM (IPOM) had to be given on or before day 10 after birth. After ensuring clinical stability and recording pre-intervention vital signs, type of respiratory support and oxygen requirement, at the time of scheduled infant care by nursing, an initial dose of $0.2\mathrm{ml}$ fresh (never refrigerated, pumped within $_{3\mathsf{h}}$ ) POM was administered into one nostril by a study physician or trained respiratory therapist using a standard plastic oral syringe inserted just into the nasal vestibule. If there was concern about baseline nasal secretions, infants were gently nasally suctioned prior; after dosing, infants were not suctioned for 1 h. If infants were on non-invasive positive pressure, the CPAP mask or nasal prong was removed for as brief a time as possible. If tolerated, the dose was increased to $0.4\mathrm{mL}$ given before and after nursing care, ideally twice daily for a goal of 1.6 mL/day. Administration was planned to continue until 28 days of age and was done by the bedside nurse, respiratory therapist, or physician after training either in person or via an online video; some parents were also trained at their request to perform the intervention with support by clinical staff. Peer supports were utilized to facilitate sharing of prior study experiences with new clinical members.  

The dosing used in the study protocol was justified based on the established range of SC concentrations in HM ( $10,000–13\times10^{6}$ cells/mL) [7, 9]. At high concentrations, 1 mL POM/day would meet optimal dosing in animal model studies, and would meet animal model effectiveness thresholds $(5\times10^{5}$ cells) even with a 10 fold lower cell concentration [7]. Although HM of preterm infants’ parents has higher SC concentrations some would likely fall below effective ranges, therefore we aimed to dose at 1.6 mL/day, the maximum daily dose in the sole description of IPOM in infants [8, 15]. With these calculations, most babies should receive a “therapeutic dose” in a single day, such that 3 days of courier services ensured an ability to receive at least this threshold amount. The intervention continued until day 28 of age to maximize potential therapy (beyond this threshold dose) closest to the time of injury.  

Pumping support was available throughout the intervention while transportation of POM by courier was used to allow for fresh milk to be administered within $_{3\mathsf{h}}$ of pumping when the pumping parent was not able to be in the NICU during the first 3 days of the intervention. Analysis of stem cell concentrations in POM: We sought to describe SC concentrations in POM. As our center had not worked with HM before from a SC standpoint, we first consented volunteer lactating parents in the NICU to test and optimize HM cell analysis techniques assessing for specific cell populations via markers for $\mathtt{CD45+}$ (hemoatopoietic SCs); $\mathsf{CD44+}$ and/or $\mathsf{CD90+}$ (putative mesenchymal SCs); $\mathsf{CD44}+\mathsf{/CD146}+$ , $\mathsf{CD90+1}$ ${\mathsf{CD}}146+,$ and $\mathsf{CD}145+$ (putative pericytes); $\mathsf{EPCam+}$ (putative epithelial cells), Tra- $^{1-81+}$ and/or $\mathsf{SSEA4+}$ (putative pluripotent SCs) based on previously published HM analyses [3, 11, 21]. Analysis was performed by the Librach lab (CReATe research lab) in Toronto, Canada. After initial testing with non-study parents, POM from study subjects was utilized, but this portion of the study was significantly affected by the Covid-19 pandemic with very limited access to lab personnel and the lab itself. The SC analysis was therefore more limited in its inclusion of study patients than planned, as clinical study recruitment continued and completed during the time of very limited lab availability.  

For POM analysis, based on techniques from Keller 2018, a fresh sample from a whole milk expression was brought to the lab within $_{3\mathsf{h}}$ of pumping, diluted 1:1 with Dulbecco’s phosphatebuffered saline (DPBS), and centrifuged for 20 min at 1900rpm [11]. The pellet was then resuspended gently with PBS at 1200 rpm for 3 min, centrifuged again and resuspended in $\mathsf{1}{-}2\mathsf{mL}$ buffer before filtration using a 70 micron strainer to isolate single cells. A viable cell count analysis was done at this step using an automated cytometer for 19 out of 21 samples (Eve, automatic cell counter, NanoEntek; this technique was added after the first two samples were obtained) and trypan blue exclusion.  

Cells $(n=50,000)$ ) were prepared in $200\upmu\mathrm{L}$ of $3\%$ Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS)-PBS solution per reaction. Fluorophore-conjugated antibodies, isotype controls and/or viability (Cell Tracker Blue/ Green, ThermoFisher) or nuclear (DRAQ5, Abcam) dyes were added, mixed by pipetting, and incubated for 20 min at ${\dot{4}}^{\circ}{\mathsf{C}}.$ Flow cytometry was performed using a MACSQuant Analyzer 10 multicolor digital flow cytometry (Miltenyi Biotech). FlowJoTM software was used for flow data analysis, using unstained cell controls, single antibody/dye conditions, and isotype controls, and gating for live cell events in at least 10,000 captured events. See supplementary table for cell analysis kit information.  

We had also intended to study cerebrospinal fluid from study infants who had lumbar punctures or reservoir taps for clinical indications to test for potential POM SCs by looking for maternal DNA (to be obtained from buccal swabs), but again due to Covid19 restrictions, this portion of the study was unable to be completed.

Measures. Infant characteristics (gestational age, birth weight, worst grade of IVH, unilateral or bilateral IVH) were recorded from the electronic medical record. Records of tolerance and any adverse reactions (desaturation/need for increased oxygen, need for increased respiratory support settings and/or bagging/positive pressure ventilation, need for acute suctioning to remove IPOM dose due to the aforementioned reactions) were prospectively collected for each administration with study-specific bedside charting. Administering providers (nurses, physicians, and respiratory therapists) were surveyed via an electronic survey sent via email in the days after they participated in milk administration and/or took care of a patient in the study to determine acceptability and feasibility. Some providers had multiple patients in the study and therefore answered the survey more than once.  

# Statistical analysis  

Infant characteristics and survey data were summarized by n (percent) for categorical data. Continuous data were tested for normality and reported as mean $+/-$ standard deviation. POM SC testing for each cell marker concentration was reported as median (interquartile range) as well as minimum and maximum given known wide ranges of concentrations.  

# RESULTS Intranasal parent milk intervention  

Of 108 eligible infants, 37 preterm infants were enrolled (see study enrollment Fig. 1). Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Three infants died during the study period from unrelated causes common in the very preterm population (necrotizing enterocolitis or sepsis). Fourteen infants had administration stop prior to 28 days of age. This included the 3 infants who died during this period, 3 infants who were felt by the medical and/or study team and/or the parents to be too unstable for IPOM due to surgical or other serious clinical conditions, and 8 infants who were transferred to another facility prior to day 28 of age (community level II NICUs).  

Study infants received a median of 17 [IQR 9, 20] days of IPOM; 36 $(97\%)$ received at least 3 days (our minimum target), 31 $(84\%)$ received at least 7 days, and $62\%$ received at least 14 days of IPOM. Twice daily dosing of 2 aliquots each (4 aliquots/day) was performed in 31 subjects, 5 additional subjects received once daily dosing of 2 aliquots (due to short daily parent visitation that allowed only one pumping session), and 1 subject received a combination of once and twice daily depending on parent visitation. The aliquot volume was not modified with once or twice daily dosing as it was felt that $0.4\mathrm{mL}$ seemed to be a maximum feasible dose. Therefore, when infants only received once daily dosing, they typically received $0.8\mathsf{mL}$ (vs the goal of $1.6\mathsf{mL}$ ) a day. Lack of parent visitation that day, which was exacerbated by the challenges and hospital visitor restrictions during the Covid-19 pandemic during which the study took place, was a common limiting factor for not receiving an IPOM dose. In some of the smallest patients (i.e., $500~\mathrm{g}$ infants), the $0.4\mathrm{mL}$ volume of IPOM per aliquot appeared to be too much volume for their nasal passage, as milk was noted in the mouth immediately after the IPOM administration, swallowing motions were noted, and/or milk flowed back out of the nare. Doses were personalized if these events were noted, with four patients requiring altered dosing, typically of $0.2\mathsf{mL}$ per aliquot, resulting in a lower daily volume.

Close clinical monitoring of IPOM administrations did not reveal any major safety reactions related to the study intervention, although 32 total reactions (total reactions overall, not subjects) were reported (shown in Table 2) during the study that were possibly or probably related to the IPOM intervention. Thirty of these reactions were deemed “minor” (defined a priori as requiring minimal to no treatment) and two of these reactions (increases in respiratory settings from baseline) were deemed “moderate” (defined a priori as resulting in a low level of concern requiring therapeutic measures.) Of note in the 2 moderate reactions, in one instance, the infant was found later that day to have pneumoperitoneum, so the increased ventilator settings were likely due to this unrelated (and at the time unrecognized) pathology, and in the other instance of increased respiratory settings, the infant had just been extubated to non-invasive ventilation earlier that day.

To assess staff acceptance and feasibility, a total of 162 provider surveys were collected from 34 patients. A summary of survey results is shown in Table 3. Results suggested that providers felt the administration of the IPOM was feasible in the planned protocol. Free text responses suggested that the intervention was overall able to be part of a clinical care routine without major disruption to infant, family, or nursing routines. There was some suggestion of perceived stress to the provider or infant in a minority of responses $(23-28\%$ ) The support from the study team was highlighted as a positive factor in provider ease. Of note, the study’s recruitment period during the Covid-19 pandemic corresponded to periods of nursing and staffing shortages as well as study staff bedside presence restrictions; these challenges did not appear to have major effects on intervention feasibility from a bedside provider survey standpoint.  

# Analysis of stem cell concentrations in POM  

Stem cell analysis of POM utilized samples from six volunteer “test subjects” who were not in the study (with $^{5/6}$ providing samples earlier than day 28 post partum) in order to optimize techniques, as well as 15 study subjects. Due to Covid-19 lab availability restrictions, 8 of the 15 study patients had their POM tested after the active study intervention had completed. POM analysis occurred between days 6 and 77 of age. Cell counts and viability testing was performed on 19 processed samples using trypan blue. Median cell viability was $68.0\%$ [IQR 51.5; 76.5] with a range of $29.0\substack{-83.0\%}$ . There was a significant negative correlation between viability and day of age (Pearson r $-0.60$ , $p<0.01$ .) In these 19 samples, median live cell concentration was $0.23\times10^{6}$ [IQR $0.07\times10^{\dot{6}}$ $0.30\times10^{6}]$ with a large range of $0.01\times10^{6}$ to $0.65\times10^{6}$ per mL. The samples in the highest $25\%$ of live cell concentrations were all collected in the first 28 days postpartum. Cell populations as percentiles of total live cells are shown in Table 4.  

# DISCUSSION  

The results of this pilot study suggest that administration of IPOM as potential SC therapy is tolerated in this patient population, with no major procedure-related safety reactions recorded in this phase I trial. Events such as apnea, bradycardia, and desaturation are common in the preterm population, and IPOM administration was done during nursing care (such as diaper changes), when events may also occur. Both objectively by lack of need for major intervention, as well as subjectively in provider surveys, the IPOM was felt to be tolerated by these vulnerable infants, nearly all of whom were on positive pressure respiratory support. Not all babies received the planned twice daily doses at full volume, so our tolerance data could be an underestimate, although $97\%$ received the “minimum target” of 3 days of therapy.

Surveys of a diverse group of administering providers supports that this intervention can be feasibly incorporated into NICU care in a way that is acceptable to providers and families involved. Further study may consider provider comments about perceived stress for providers and infants during the intervention as areas to expand provider training and support for administration, which would be more available in post-pandemic time periods. Our study was also feasible from a recruitment standpoint, despite launching our study at the height of the Covid-19 pandemic lockdowns in 2020. Of the 104 approached patients, $36\%$ enrolled, which we found acceptable for a phase I intervention study in a vulnerable population during a very challenging time for families with pandemic restrictions. Of patients with grade III or IV IVH, who are at risk of worse outcome and for which there are not good treatment options for complications like post-hemorrhagic ventricular dilatation, 13/19 $(68\%)$ enrolled [1]. Presumably due to the poor prognosis and lack of available therapeutic options, these families would be more likely to participate in an experimental therapy than those with infants with, for example, a grade 1 IVH, for which baseline outcomes would be more favorable.

Stem cell testing for our study revealed somewhat lower cell viability than a previous study by Keller of $76-100\%$ [11]. Our median live cell concentration was within the range of previous reports but with a wide range, with samples below the $25\mathrm{th}\%$ falling below reported ranges [3, 11]. Although POM was brought to the SC lab as soon as possible after pumping, our cell testing was done in a “real world” pragmatic way, using POM from clinical pumping sessions performed in the NICU with a sample then brought to the off-site lab. This time lag between expression and testing therefore likely underestimated cell viability and live cell concentration. The significant negative correlation between day of age and cell viability reinforces the use of POM as close to the time of birth (and injury) as possible [7]. Our cell population analysis showed wide ranges of cell type by sample, but results were consistent with published ranges [3, 11, 12, 21]. On average, putative pluripotent SCs and mesenchymal SCs accounted for about half of live cells in our samples, exemplifying the rich milieu that is HM and reinforcing the premise of our study to use POM as SC therapy. Using previous animal models, our goal daily dose of $1.6\mathrm{ml}$ would have resulted in a lower than ideal single SC dose if our live cell concentrations are indeed accurate. The goal was minimum $0.5\times10^{6},$ , and if we utilize a median concentration in our samples of $0.23\times10^{6}$ with half of these cells being SCs, we would have “underdosed” on a one time basis. However, animal models typically give a single dose, and our patients on average received 17 days of therapy, so over time would have more than met threshold for animal study effectiveness. POM SC concentration also likely varies by sample, but we were not able to longitudinally test within-parent differences due to covid-related lab limitationsthis is a question for future studies.  

Limitations to this pilot study include the small sample size, which was not powered to study clinical outcomes, and was limited to two NICUs. This study took place entirely during the Covid-19 pandemic, which led to delays in study recruitment and contributed to technical difficulties around study initiation due to limitation in study staff presence in the NICU. The pandemic also affected visitor policies, which led to some difficulties around obtaining fresh POM as needed in the study design to ensure SC viability for the intervention as well as the SC analysis, resulting in fewer samples to analyze as well as delayed samples that were collected after the infant’s intervention period had concluded.  

Additionally, we were not able to test discarded spinal fluid for POM SCs due to Covid-19 lab restrictions as well as lack of available samples. Study strengths included recruitment at urban referral NICUs in a very diverse city in two NICUs with shared protocols for management of post-IVH complications. Additional strengths include meticulous data collection of adverse reaction data with close DSMB monitoring and the addition of provider survey data to facilitate future study planning.  

The feasibility data from this study, along with ongoing analysis of outcomes to 18 months, will inform planning for potential larger multicentre trials, which would further explore safety and feasibility outside these two institutions. Future study planning should include MRIs if possible (which were not done routinely in our population) to assess potential injury reduction in addition to the routine neurodevelopmental follow-up. An unanswered question is to explore the dose and duration of the IPOM intervention, as even a natural biologic like HM may have a dose in which a risk-benefit threshold is reached when it is used “off-label” intranasally. In animal models, SC therapy is typically given soon after the injury in a single dose. Our real-world human study was very different, in that there were multi-day gaps in between actual occurrence of IVH, diagnosis of IVH, study consent (once it was certain that families were not going to redirect care for severe IVH), and then study initiation. We therefore continued through day 28 of age, but given the logistical challenges of providing fresh IPOM and dosing efficacy thresholds in theory being reached in much shorter time, shorter durations should be explored, as well as priority to quick recruitment after IVH diagnosis to reduce the window between injury and therapy. Additional etiologies of brain injury, specifically hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy in the term population, could also be amenable to such IPOM therapy, and have a nearly instant clinical diagnosis at birth, although different logistical challenges would present such as lack of POM in the first days post partum.  

From a dose perspective, although we utilized this study as a way to utilize a “ready to use” source of SCs that didn’t require expensive and time-intensive processing, it is possible that the use of a basic centrifuge, for example, to concentrate the cell pellet would allow much smaller volumes to be given intranasally, making the study more appealing for parents and providers for the smallest and/or most unstable patients or to “concentrate” dose for parents with low POM SC concentrations. However, in addition to SCs, there are many bioactives present in HM that have anti-inflammatory, immunomodulating, and other effects that could in theory also play a large role in healing brain injury; these elements may be reduced if whole milk is not utilized [19]. Finally, additional animal models to assess IPOM uptake in the brain and/ or necropsy evidence of reductions in injury would be beneficial for additional proof of concept.  

There is a need to explore novel treatment modalities for neonatal brain injury. Promising animal evidence regarding the ability of SCs found in POM to treat brain injury and improve outcomes have made this a promising area for further study. This study demonstrates that POM can be feasibly administered to preterm infants with IVH via an intranasal route in the normal clinical workflow and without severe adverse reactions related to the procedure. Future study is needed to better understand how or if IPOM can impact short and long term outcomes for this vulnerable population.
