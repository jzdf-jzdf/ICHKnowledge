# Admission CT radiomic signatures outperform hematoma volume in predicting baseline clinical severity and functional outcome in the ATACH-2 trial intracerebral hemorrhage population

# Abstract  

Background: Radiomics provides a framework for automated extraction of high-dimensional feature sets from medical images. We aimed to determine radiomics signature correlates of admission clinical severity and medium-term outcome from intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) lesions on baseline head CTs.  

Methods: We used the ATACH-2 (Antihypertensive-Treatment-of-Acute-Cerebral-HemorrhageII) trial dataset. Patients included in this analysis $\mathrm{\check{n}}\mathrm{=}895$ ) were randomly allocated to discovery $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n}=448$ ) and independent validation $\scriptstyle\mathrm{{n=}}447$ ) cohorts. We extracted 1130 radiomics features from hematoma lesions on baseline non-contrast head CTs and generated radiomics signatures associated with admission Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and 3-month modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores. Spearman’s correlation between radiomics signatures and corresponding target variables was compared with hematoma volume.  

Results: In the discovery cohort, radiomics signatures – compared to ICH volume – had significantly stronger association with admission GCS (0.47 vs 0.44, $\mathtt{p=}0.008.$ ), admission NIHSS (0.69 vs 0.57, $\mathtt{p}{<}0.001$ ), and 3-month mRS scores (0.44 vs 0.32, $\mathrm{p}{<}0.001$ ). Similarly, in independent validation, radiomics signatures – compared to ICH volume – had significantly stronger association with admission GCS (0.43 vs 0.41, $\mathrm{p}{=}0.02$ ), NIHSS (0.64 vs 0.56, $\mathrm{\Deltap{<}0.001)}$ , and 3-month mRS scores (0.43 vs 0.33, $\mathrm{p}{<}0.001$ ). In multiple regression analysis adjusted for known predictors of ICH outcome, the radiomics signature was an independent predictor of 3-month mRS in both cohorts.  

Conclusions: Limited by the enrollment criteria of the ATACH-2 trial, we showed that radiomics features quantifying hematoma texture, density and shape on baseline CT can provide imaging correlates for clinical presentation and medium-term outcome.  

# Keywords  

Radiomics; intracerebral hemorrhage; hematoma; volume; outcome  

# Introduction  

Among the admission imaging characteristics of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), the hematoma volume is the strongest predictor of 30-day mortality rates and clinical outcome.1-3 The hematoma volume is reflective of the size of primary parenchymal injury, and correlates with the severity of neurological deficit at presentation as determined by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS).4 Recent studies, however, showed that other imaging features of hematomas on non-contrast CT are predictive of mortality and outcome,1 including the black hole sign,5 the swirl sign,6 and sedimentation levels.7 Thus, a comprehensive analysis of hematoma density and texture features on baseline head CT may contribute to improved prediction of ICH outcome, and identification of CT neuroimaging correlates of cerebral parenchymal injury.  

During the last decade, dramatic advancements in high-throughput computing and availability of medical mega-data led to the emergence of the “-omics” concepts. The “-omics” refers to collective quantification, characterization and exploitation of pools of biologic information – e.g. genomics, proteomics, or metabolomics. In “radiomics”, preset algorithms utilize information encoded in millions of voxels from tomographic or volumetric imaging data to derive a comprehensive quantitative representation of tissue shape, intensity, and texture.8,9 Compared to the subjective and qualitative assessment by radiologists, radiomics offers the distinctive advantage of objective automatic extraction of imaging features, providing insight into intra-lesion tissue heterogeneity.10 In this study, we aimed to investigate the correlation of hematoma radiomics features and feature combinations (“radiomics signatures”) with clinical presentation and outcome in patients with supratentorial ICH. Additionally, we compared the predictive value of radiomics with visual prognostic biomarkers of ICH on non-contrast head CT.  

The ATACH-2 (Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage II) trial, from 2011 to 2015, evaluated the effects of targeted intensive systolic blood pressure reduction in patients with spontaneous supratentorial ICH versus standard blood pressure lowering; however, no significant treatment benefit with regards to mortality or disability was found.11 Using the detailed baseline and follow-up clinical phenotypes of the ATACH-2 dataset, we derived hematoma radiomic signature correlates of admission neurological deficit (assessed by GCS and NIHSS) as well as medium-term outcome (modified Ranking Scale, mRS at 3 months). In separate model derivation and independent validation cohorts, we compared the correlation between radiomics signatures and target variables with the correlation between hematoma volume and target variables.  

# Materials and Methods  

# Data acquisition and allocation  

We used the anonymized clinical data and corresponding baseline CT imaging datasets from the ATACH-2 trial in this study. Briefly, ATACH-2 was a multicenter randomized trial that enrolled 1000 patients with primary supratentorial ICH, within 4.5 hours of onset, who had a baseline hematoma volume $<60~\mathrm{mL}$ and admission elevated systolic blood pressure $\mathrm{>180~mm~Hg}$ ). Participants were randomized to intensive treatment versus standard treatment.11 Our study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki; institutional review boards in participating centers approved the study.11 From the ATACH-2 dataset, we excluded patients with (1) CT artifacts affecting the ICH, (2) corrupted CT imaging data, (3) unavailable baseline CT scans, or (4) missing GCS, NIHSS, or mRS score (fig. 1). Then, subjects were randomly allocated to a model discovery and an independent validation cohort (fig. 1).  

# Segmentation of hematomas  

All available baseline non-contrast head CT scans were retrieved and reviewed in 3D-Slicer version 4.10.1 software.12 The hematomas were located and contours were manually segmented on axial CT slices using the “Paint” and “Erase” tools provided in the “Segment Editor” module (i.e. “slice by slice” manual segmentation). Segmentations covered the intracerebral hematoma, but not the perilesional edema or other sites of ICH manifestation. Finally, segmentations were reviewed and adjusted by an expert neuroradiologist (SP) with more than 9 years of experience. The resulting 3-dimensional hematoma masks as well as corresponding CT images were saved for subsequent pre-processing and radiomics feature extraction steps – fig. 2 illustrates the radiomics and analysis pipeline.  

# Image pre-processing and radiomics feature extraction  

The CT pre-processing pipeline consisted of voxel dimension resampling to an isotropic spacing of $1\mathrm{x}1\mathrm{x}1\mathrm{mm}$ , “re-segmentation” of ICH masks to remove voxels outside the 1-200 Hounsfield unit range, and filtering to generate eleven derivative images per original (eight “coif-1” wavelet decompositions, three Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filters; fig. 2).13,14 We extracted $\mathrm{n}{=}14$ “shape”, $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{n}}=18$ “first-order” and $\mathtt{n}{=}75$ “texture-matrix” features from original and derivative images, resulting in $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n=1}130$ radiomics features per hematoma.13,14 A complete list of features and a detailed description of our pre-processing and radiomics pipeline are provided in supplemental table 1 and the supplemental methods, respectively.  

# Radiomics signature generation  

“Radiomics signatures” were generated by linearly combining sets of robust radiomics features exhibiting strong association with admission GCS, admission NIHSS, and 3-month mRS scores, while minimizing feature multicollinearity (fig. 2). The signatures were devised in the discovery cohort, and validated in the independent validation cohort (fig. 1). We calculated inter-/intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) to exclude features with low robustness to inter-/intra-rater segmentation variability (1002/1130 features retained), and generated a feature correlation matrix to exclude collinear features (424/1002 features retained). Finally, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-regularized ordinal logistic regression (OLR) models were fitted to create three separate radiomics signatures associated with the target scores (i.e. GCS, NIHSS and mRS). A detailed methodological description is included in the supplemental methods.  

# Visual markers of ICH on non-contrast CT  

Three independent readers identified $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{n}}=8$ prognostic visual markers of ICH on baseline non-contrast head CT scans of $\scriptstyle\mathtt{n=400}$ patients, who in equal parts were randomly sampled from the discovery and independent validation cohorts. Markers were identified as binary variables (i.e. present or absent) using diagnostic criteria adopted from Morotti et al., including density markers (“Blend sign”, “Hypodensity”, “Swirl sign”, “Black hole sign”, “Fluid level”) and shape markers (“Island sign”, “Satellite sign”, “Irregular shape”). The majority vote across the three reads was determined and used for all analyses. ICH markers were visually detected in the original CT scans. Then, we utilized the discovery cohort subsample to generate three separate visual ICH marker signatures associated with the target scores (i.e. GCS, NIHSS and mRS) by fitting LASSO-regularized OLR models as described for radiomics signatures above. The signatures were validated in the independent validation cohort subsample.  

# Statistical analysis  

All analyses were performed in R version 3.6.0.15 We calculated Spearman’s rho to evaluate the association of GCS, NIHSS or mRS scores with corresponding radiomics signature scores and visual ICH marker signature scores. Similarly, Spearman’s rho was used to evaluate the correlation with ICH volume. We adopted R.R. Wilcox' percentile bootstrap method to compare dependent correlations.16 All correlation analysis was based on authentic target scores (and not scores with pooled levels created for OLR model fitting, see supplemental methods).  

Multiple OLR analysis adjusted for known predictors of ICH outcome17-19 was performed to further investigate the association of the mRS radiomics signature with mRS score at 3-months (R “MASS” package “polr” function)20 The regression coefficients and corresponding standard errors, t-values and p-values were analyzed. A Wilcoxon rank sum test measured the association between the mRS radiomics signature and hematoma expansion.  

# Results  

# Patients’ characteristics  

In total, 895 ATACH-2 trial participants were included in this analysis; of whom, 448 were allocated to the discovery cohort, and 447 to the independent validation cohort (fig. 1). Approximately $60\%$ were male; and the mean (SD) age was 60 (13) years at presentation. Table 1 summarizes demographics and imaging characteristics of discovery and validation cohorts.  

# Radiomics signature for baseline GCS  

The GCS radiomics signature consisted of seven features, including three shape and four texture-matrix features extracted from wavelet derivative images (supplemental table 2.1). Two shape features, namely “Maximum 2D Diameter Slice” and “Least Axis Length” were weighted the strongest. The “Busyness” feature (an “ngtdm” texture matrix feature) received the third strongest weight. “Maximum 2D Diameter Slice” represents the largest ICH diameter in the axial plane; “Least Axis Length” is the smallest axis length of an ellipsoid fully enclosing the ICH; “Busyness” quantifies the magnitude of gray level intensity changes from a voxel to its neighbors, with high feature values indicating rapid intensity changes.13 The radiomics signature was significantly associated with GCS score in both the discovery cohort $(\mathrm{rho}=0.47\$ , $\mathrm{p}<0.001,$ ) and the independent validation cohort (r $10=0.43$ , p < 0.001). ICH volume was significantly associated with GCS score in both cohorts as well $(\mathbf{p}<0.001)$ ). The correlation between the radiomics signature and GCS score was stronger than the correlation between ICH volume and GCS score in both discovery $\mathbf{\tilde{p}}=0.008)$ and validation cohorts $\mathbf{\bar{p}}=0.02$ , table 2).  

# Radiomics signature for baseline NIHSS  

The NIHSS radiomics signature consisted of 23 features, including six first-order, three shape and 14 texture-matrix features (supplemental table 2.2). The “Maximum 2D Diameter Row” shape feature had the strongest impact on radiomics signature scores, followed by the “Gray Level Non Uniformity” feature (a “glszm” texture matrix feature extracted from a LoG-filtered image). “Maximum 2D Diameter Row” quantifies the largest ICH diameter in the sagittal plane; “Gray Level Non Uniformity” describes the variability of gray level intensities in the image, with higher values indicating greater heterogeneity.13 The radiomics signature exhibited strong correlation with NIHSS score in the discovery $(\mathrm{rho}=0.69\$ , p $<0.001\$ ) and validation $(\mathrm{rho}=0.64\$ , $\mathrm{p}<0.001;$ ) cohorts, which was significantly stronger than the association of NIHSS score with ICH volume in both the discovery and validation cohorts (both $\mathrm{p}<0.001$ , table 2).  

# Radiomics signature for medium-term mRS  

The mRS radiomics signature consisted of nine features, including two shape, and seven texture-matrix features (supplemental table 2.3). The “Least Axis Length” shape feature and the “Run Variance” texture feature (a “glrlm” texture matrix feature extracted from a LoGfiltered image) had the highest impact on signature scores. The “glrlm” matrix quantifies so called “gray level runs”, which reflect the length (in number of voxels) of consecutive voxels with identical gray levels in a specific direction in the image; “Run Variance” measures the variance in runs for the run lengths. We found significant association between the radiomics signature and mRS score in the discovery ( $\cdot\mathrm{ho}=0.44$ , $\mathrm{p}<0.001)$ and validation ( $\mathrm{rho}=0.43$ , $\mathbf{p}<0.001,$ ) cohorts. The radiomics signature had significantly stronger association with mRS score than ICH volume in both the discovery and validation cohort (both $\mathrm{p}<0.001$ , table 2).  

Notably, in multiple OLR analysis adjusted for admission GCS and NIHSS scores, age and the presence of intraventricular hemorrhage, the mRS radiomics signature was independently predictive of medium-term mRS score in both the discovery $\mathrm{t}=5.21$ , $\mathsf{p}<$ 0.001) and the validation $\mathrm{\Deltat}=4.36$ , $\mathrm{p}<0.001$ ) cohort (table 3). The radiomics signature retained statistical significance when the model was additionally adjusted for ICH volume and hematoma expansion (supplemental table 3); with the caveat that our signatures are proposed as substitutes for baseline ICH volume, and that hematoma expansion is a followup marker.  

The mRS radiomics signature was not significantly associated with hematoma expansion in the discovery and independent validation cohorts $\mathrm{\tilde{p}=0.11}$ and $\mathrm{p}=0.24$ , respectively; Wilcoxon rank sum test). However, a weak trend was observed, suggesting patients with hematoma expansion had higher median (IQR) mRS signature scores than those without hematoma expansion: 0.019 (−0.32-0.42) versus $-0.096$ (−0.40-0.30) in the discovery cohort, and 0.051 (−0.40-0.31) versus $-0.098$ (−0.38-0.30) in the independent validation cohort.  

Notably, several features were shared among different radiomics signatures, with $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n}=4$ , $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n}=2$ and $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n}=1$ features present in the intersections mRS∩NIHSS, GCS∩NIHSS and GCS∩mRS, respectively (supplemental table 4). In addition, all signatures included features from both the “shape” and “texture-matrix” families, while “first-order” features were only included in the NIHSS signature (supplemental table 2). Figure 3 depicts examples of two patients with similar hematoma volume but different radiomics signature scores reflective of their divergent clinical presentation and outcome.  

# Visual ICH marker signatures  

Supplemental table 5 summarizes the frequency of patients with different markers on their baseline non-contrast CTs. The GCS, NIHSS and mRS signatures all consisted of the  

same two visual markers, namely “Irregular shape” and “Hypodensity” (supplemental table 6). The OLR coefficients, however, varied across different signatures, with the “Irregular shape” marker weighted the strongest in all signatures (supplemental table 6). Of note, the “Irregular shape” marker was identified in $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n=}82$ and $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{n}}=68$ , and the “Hypodensity” marker in $\mathrm{n}{=}147$ and $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n=147}$ hematomas in the discovery and independent validation subsamples, respectively (supplemental table 5).  

The visual ICH marker signatures were significantly associated with the GCS, NIHSS and mRS score in the discovery and the independent validation subsamples (all p < 0.001), and provided similar performance compared to ICH volume alone in predicting admission severity and outcome (supplemental table 7). In contrast, the radiomics signatures demonstrated significantly stronger correlation with target scores than ICH volume in all but one comparison, where the correlation was only numerically stronger (GCS score in the validation cohort subsample, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}=0.08$ , supplemental table 7).  

# Discussion  

In this study, we devised and validated hematoma radiomics signature correlates of admission GCS and NIHSS as well as 3-month mRS scores. Our results demonstrate that hematoma radiomics features provide clinical correlates beyond the ICH volume. Using rigorous statistical methodology, we combined radiomics variables with high stability in intra- and inter-rater assessment and low collinearity in our signatures, which were devised in a discovery cohort, and tested in a fully independent validation dataset. Notably, a radiomics signature was an independent predictor of medium-term mRS score in multiple regression analysis adjusted for known predictors of ICH outcome. The proposed ICH radiomics signatures may represent the neuroimaging correlates of hemorrhagic cerebral parenchymal injury on head CT. Our results suggest that hematoma radiomics may aid risk-assessment, treatment triage, and prognostication in ICH patients.  

Radiomics analysis has been widely applied in oncologic imaging for molecular subtyping, survival prognostication, and prediction of treatment response.8,21-23 Recent studies suggested that hematoma radiomics features can predict the likelihood of hematoma expansion.24-27 In this study, we showed that while hematoma volume and radiomic shape features had strong association with severity of baseline clinical presentation and medium-term outcome, radiomic heterogeneity features provided added predictive value to radiomics signatures (supplemental table 2, table 2). Thus, we concluded that the texture of hemorrhagic lesions on baseline CT provides clinical and prognostic correlates beyond the hematoma volume.  

Non-contrast head CT remains the preferred baseline imaging modality for ICH patients, given its wide availability, low cost, and fast acquisition. Previous studies applied several visual markers of ICH on admission non-contrast CT to predict impending hematoma expansion – including hypodensities, heterogeneous density, swirl sign, blend sign, irregular shape, fluid level and black hole sign.1,28 Some of these descriptive radiological signs are also predictors of clinical outcome and mortality in ICH patients.5-7,29,30 By the same token, radiomics can provide comprehensive, objective and user-independent volumetric  

shape- and texture-related, and CT-densitometric predictors for ICH outcome. We compared radiomics signatures and signatures consisting of visual ICH markers for prediction of 3-month outcome and clinical severity at admission. While the radiomics and visual marker signatures as well as the ICH volume were significantly associated with GCS, NIHSS and mRS scores, radiomics analysis leveraged the highest predictive ability in independent validation (table 2, supplemental table 7). Visual ICH marker signatures, on the other hand, had similar performance compared to ICH volume alone in predicting admission severity and outcome (supplemental table 7). Thus, radiomics analysis, in comparison with visual ICH markers, enabled more comprehensive characterization of hematoma density, shape and texture features, greater predictive ability with regards to the analyzed clinical scores, and superior reproducibility, given that radiomics feature extraction is fully automated.  

The severity of ICH at the time of admission is commonly assessed by the GCS score. Admission GCS score generally dictates whether immediate aggressive interventions, such as decompressive craniectomy or hematoma evacuation, are needed, or withdrawal of care should be considered.31 While the correlation of ICH volume and GCS score is well established,31 irregular hematoma shape32 or heterogeneity (“swirl sign”)33 are also associated with low admission GCS score. Thus, imaging markers reflecting admission GCS score may supplement clinical findings when sedation, intubation, language barriers, or preexisting comorbidities interfere with the clinical exam and confound GCS score determination. We demonstrated that the combination of radiomic shape and texture features of hematomas provides predictive value beyond the hematoma volume in correlation analysis with admission GCS score.  

While admission NIHSS scoring has primarily been used in patients presenting with acute ischemic stroke, it has proven prognostic utility regarding mortality and disability in patients with spontaneous ICH as well.18,34 The hemorrhage volume was also found to significantly correlate with the admission NIHSS score.35 Additionally, analysis of baseline CT scans and clinical data in the Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage Trial (INTERACT2) showed significant association of irregular hematoma shape and heterogeneous density with higher baseline NIHSS scores.30 Our hematoma radiomics signature’s composition and performance (supplemental table 2.2, table 2) substantiates the relevance of hematoma texture heterogeneity with regards to admission NIHSS score.  

Finally, we showed that radiomics signature correlates of clinical outcome had significantly stronger $(\mathbf{p}{<}0.001)$ ) association with medium-term mRS score compared to hematoma volume in both the discovery and validation cohorts (rho of 0.44 vs 0.32, and rho of 0.43 vs 0.33, respectively, table 2). Multiple regression analysis (table 3, supplemental table 3) confirmed that the radiomics signature was a predictor of 3-month mRS score independent from admission clinical severity (NIHSS and GCS scores), baseline CT variables (ICH volume and presence of intraventricular hemorrhage), patient age and hematoma expansion, all of which are known predictors of functional outcome.17-19 These findings highlight the clinical relevance of hematoma radiomics features with regards to medium-term functional outcome, and indicate that baseline CT radiomics may be a means to improve the current models for ICH outcome prognostication. Given its independence from admission variables in predicting 3-month mRS score (table 3), the radiomics signature may reveal early  

neuroimaging characteristics of cerebral injury processes which predominantly manifest after admission. One readily measurable effect of such processes is hematoma expansion. While we could not identify a significant association $(\mathsf{p}{>}0.05)$ , a trend was observed, suggesting patients with hematoma expansion had higher median mRS signature scores. Thus, the radiomics signature may in part serve as a marker of impending hematoma expansion. Another marker of secondary brain injury after ICH which received considerable attention in recent years is perihematomal edema (PHE).36 However, literature suggests ICH volume may confound the relationship between PHE and functional outcome, and additionally, multiple studies failed to demonstrate an association of relative PHE with outcome.36 Notably, 24- and 72-hour PHE growth may be stronger predictors of poor functional outcome compared to baseline PHE.37,38 However, the lack of consensus on the definition of PHE expansion is a limiting factor: for example, while some groups used the rate of PHE volume growth between baseline and follow-up scans,39 Parry-Jones et al.40 proposed the edema extension distance as a marker of PHE growth. Future studies with more extensive follow-up CT data may investigate the potential correlation of hematoma radiomics with PHE growth.  

Interestingly, the three radiomics signatures demonstrated a certain similarity in that several features were included in more than one signature (supplemental table 4), suggesting these features may reflect common pathophysiological processes resulting in worse presentation as well as poorer 3-month mRS.  

The strengths of our study lie in the large sample size, use of multicentric cohorts, prospective clinical assessment of subjects, and independent validation of radiomics signatures. This analysis is, however, inherently limited to certain population characteristics of the study cohort, including ICH location (spontaneous supratentorial ICH), hematoma size $\cdot{\<}60\mathrm{cm}^{3},$ ), and admission systolic blood pressure $>180\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ . Notably, while the median ICH volume in the discovery and independent validation cohort was 8.4 and 9.1 $\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ , respectively, prior studies reported higher median volumes of up to ${>}20~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ .41 Besides the enrollment criterion $\mathrm{ICH}<60\mathrm{mL}$ ), the relatively small hematomas in our study may be due to hypertension-driven pathogenesis in the majority of patients, which is associated with lesser ICH volumes.42 In addition, the fatality rate at 90 days in the ATACH-2 trial was conspicuously low at ${\sim}7\%$ .11 Thus, further studies are needed to investigate radiomics signatures in more typical ICH populations. Only 29/895 $(3.2\%)$ and 29/895 $(3.2\%)$ patients presented at admission with GCS scores $^{<9}$ and NIHSS scores $>25$ , respectively (table 1), and thus, these score levels were pooled prior to OLR model fitting (see supplemental methods), which may limit the generalizability of signatures. While our analysis was primarily focused on hematoma imaging characteristics in comparison with ICH volume, further studies are needed to investigate the value of ICH radiomics in improving prognostication beyond currently available models. Finally, future studies may apply (radiomic) analysis of additional ICH manifestations and characteristics on CT to improve signatures, such as intraventricular hemorrhage, perihematomal edema and hematoma location.  

# Conclusions  

In the ATACH-2 trial cohort, automatically extracted hematoma radiomics features characterizing the density, shape, and heterogeneity of hemorrhagic lesions on baseline non-contrast CT can provide imaging correlates of patients’ clinical presentation and outcome beyond hematoma volume. These results could lead to a paradigm shift where imaging biomarkers of cerebral parenchymal injury in addition to the size of hemorrhagic lesions may potentially improve current models for prognostication, risk-stratification, and treatment triage of ICH patients.
