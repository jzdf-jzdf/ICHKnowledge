# Surgical Management of Intracerebral Hemorrhage: New Light on the Horizon?  

Floris H.B.M. Schreuder , MD, PhD; Thomas Gaberel , MD, PhD; Charlotte Cordonnier , MD, PhD  

ABSTRACT: After 30 years of disappointment, 2 randomized controlled trials investigating the effect of neurosurgical treatment on functional outcome in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage were published in 2024. The ENRICH trial (Early Minimally Invasive Removal of Intracerebral Hemorrhage) studied the efficacy of early minimally invasive hematoma removal in patients with lobar or anterior basal ganglia intracerebral hemorrhage, whereas the SWITCH trial investigated the effect of decompressive craniectomy without hematoma removal in severe deep intracerebral hemorrhage. In this critique article, we will discuss the main findings of these trials, their implications and future perspectives.  

Key Words: basal ganglia ◼ cerebral hemorrhage ◼ decompressive craniectomy ◼ hematoma  

# IN 2024, INTRACEREBRAL HEMORRHAGE REMAINS THE DEADLIEST TYPE OF STROKE  

Of all stroke subtypes, intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) has the highest case fatality with up to $40\%$ of patients dying within 30 days.1 While in ischemic stroke, revascularization therapies have resulted in significant improvement of functional outcome, optimal treatment for ICH remains uncertain. In 2024, 2 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been published that may alter our appreciation of neurosurgical treatment in patients with supratentorial ICH.2,3  

# THEORETICALLY, SURGERY IS A GOOD IDEA  

Prevention of mass effect and limitation of secondary brain injury due to the abrupt entry of blood (breakdown products) in the brain parenchyma could be achieved by hematoma evacuation. However, surgical hematoma removal is an old but disappointing concept. Since the 1960s,4 various RCTs failed to demonstrate significant effects on functional outcome.5–7 Current guidelines refrain from advocating surgical hematoma removal in most patients with supratentorial ICH.8–10  

# IN 2024, 2 SURGICAL RCTs BROUGHT FOOD FOR THOUGHTS  

The ENRICH trial (Early Minimally Invasive Removal of Intracerebral Hemorrhage) investigated the efficacy of early ${\tt\left(<}24{\tt\right)}$ hours), minimally invasive hematoma removal using a transsulcal, parafascicular approach versus standard medical management in patients with lobar or anterior basal ganglia ICH.2 Adults ${<}80$ years of age with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 5 to 14 and an ICH volume of 30 to $80~\mathsf{mL}$ were included. The trial had an adaptive design to allow for selection on hemorrhage location. Between December 2016 and August 2022, ENRICH recruited 300 participants (median age, 63 years; $50\%$ female) from 37 US centers. After enrollment of 175 participants, the prespecified futility criterium was met for deep ICH, and subsequently, only patients with lobar ICH were recruited. Subsequently, 92 participants $(31\%)$ had a deep ICH. Median ICH volume was $54\mathrm{mL}$ in the surgically treated patients compared with $55~\mathsf{mL}$ in the control group. Median time from onset to surgery was 16.8 hours. Decompressive craniectomy was performed more often in control participants compared with surgically treated patients $20\%$ versus $3.3\%$ ). The primary outcome, defined as the mean score on the utility-weighted modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 180 days, was 0.458 in the surgical arm versus 0.374 in the control group (difference of 0.084 $[95\%$ CI, 0.005–0.163]; posterior probability of superiority of 0.981, exceeding the 0.975 prespecified threshold). This observed difference was mainly the result of the effect in patients with lobar ICH (0.127 $[95\%$ CI, 0.035–0.219]), whereas in patients with deep ICH, the effect was neutral (−0.013 $[95\%$ CI, −0.147 to 0.116]).  

The SWITCH trial compared decompressive craniectomy without hematoma removal (performed ${<}72$ hours of symptom onset) versus medical management in patients with severe deep ICH (volume, $30\mathrm{-}100~\mathrm{mL}$ ; Glasgow Coma Scale score, 8–13; National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, 10–30). It aimed to randomize 300 participants.3 Recruitment was difficult: it took 9 years to include 197 participants (median age, 61 years; $32\%$ female) across 42 European centers from 9 countries. In 2023, the trial was stopped due to lack of funding after allocating 96 patients (median hematoma volume, $55~\mathsf{mL}$ ) to surgery and 101 (median hematoma volume, $59mL$ ) to standard care. Median time from onset to surgery was 26 hours. Primary outcome (mRS score of $_{5-6}$ at 180 days) was present in $44\%$ of participants assigned to decompressive craniotomy versus $58\%$ in the standard care group (adjusted risk ratio, 0.77 $[95\%$ CI, $0.59\substack{-1.01}]$ ; $_{\mathsf{P=0.057}}$ ). Surgery resulted in more patients having an mRS score of 4 at 6 months, though not at the expense of an increased number of patients with an mRS score of 5. Secondary outcomes including mortality, mRS score 4 to 6, or quality of life at 180 days did not differ between groups, nor did serious adverse events.  

# ENCOURAGING NEW DATA BUT NOTENOUGH TO RADICALLY CHANGE OURPRACTICE  

Why were previous RCTs, such as STICH (Surgery Trial in Intracerebral Hemorrhage),5 STICH-II,6 and MISTIE-III (Minimally Invasive Surgery With Thrombolysis in Intracerebral Hemorrhage Evacuation III),7 not able to show an effect on functional outcome, in contrast to ENRICH? Was this discrepancy caused by differences in patient selection, surgical techniques, timing of surgery, or other factors?  

First, in ENRICH, 11 603 patients were screened to include 300 participants. While selecting participants who might benefit the most from surgery seems reasonable, it also limits generalizability of the results. This is inherent to all RCTs performed in this field to date, for example, in MISTIE-III ${\approx}20$ 000 patients were screened to include 506 participants. In upper-middle-income countries, the weight of ICH relies mainly on elderly people with severe comorbidities that drastically differs from the ENRICH cohort (mean age, 64 years). Besides demographic characteristics, ICH characteristics also differ: in ENRICH, $31\%$ of ICHs were in deep locations, while these locations represent ${\approx}50\%$ in population-based registries.11 The selection based on a minimum hematoma volume of $30\mathsf{mL}$ might also have an impact, while previous data suggested that surgery might be beneficial for a volume as low as 20 mL.12 However, because one of the aim is to limit secondary brain injury, restricting surgery to high ICH volume may exclude many patients from a potentially effective treatment. What about the impact of the surgical technique? The preferred technique to gain access to the hematoma in STICH and STICH-II was craniotomy and hematoma evacuation, using standard microsurgical techniques, including a microscope. Less invasive approaches may reduce iatrogenic brain damage, thus improving the odds of good functional outcome. Stereotactic hematoma aspiration used in MISTIE-III, however, did not result in a significant improved outcome. Regarding endoscopic evacuation, only 3 small trials, of moderate study quality, are available.13–15 Combining these trials, endoscopic surgery was associated with improved functional outcome (relative risk, 1.93 $[95\%$ CI, 1.12–3.33]; 2 trials; 156 participants) and decreased risk of death (relative risk, 0.63 $[95\%$ CI, 0.44–0.90]; 3 trials; 166 participants).16 This supports the technique used in ENRICH, but was this indeed the key to a successful outcome? ENRICH associated BrainPath minimal access port, aiming at maintaining subcortical tract integrity, and the Myriad device, which is supposed to increase the visibility in the hematoma cavity and limit parenchymal lesion in the perihematomal area. The Brainpath device has a large diameter $(15.8\mathrm{mm})$ ), which may have caused it to be less effective in deeply located hematomas. However, with just 92 participants with deep ICH included in ENRICH, we need to interpret these results with caution. Possibly, deeply located hematomas may also benefit from other surgical techniques such as endoscopy-guided approaches together with careful mapping and navigation. This strategy would enhance the philosophy of ENRICH, with a transparenchymal approach significantly reduced as the diameter of endoscope is only few millimeters, and an excellent visibility in the hematoma cavity, thus minimizing iatrogenic brain damage. A general minimally invasive mindset is probably more important than the specific device used.

Surgery within 8 hours might be the most effective.12 The rationale for early surgery is that it limits the development of secondary brain injury. However, 2 RCTs performed within the early time frame found conflicting results. Craniotomy performed within 12 hours did not improve Barthel index at 6 months in a small trial of 36 participants.17 In 108 patients with lobar and putaminal ICH, craniotomy within 8 hours improved functional outcome at 1 year (Glasgow Outcome Scale score 4–5, $33\%$ versus $9\%$ ; $P{=}0.002$ ).18 A meta-regression analysis of 12 studies demonstrated that surgery appeared to be more effective when performed sooner after symptom onset $(\beta=-0.0063$ ; $P{=}0.04$ ).19 This is supported by a recent retrospective cohort study of 90 patients who underwent endoscopy-guided surgery, in which every hour the surgery was performed earlier was associated with a $5\%$ increased odds on good functional outcome.20 The risk of rebleeding in early surgery is often discussed based on a small craniotomy cohort from 2001,21 but recent studies using endoscopyguided techniques performed within 8 hours did not confirm that.22,23  

For large deep ICH, SWITCH hypothesized that decompressive craniectomy would have an effect on severe functional dependency state (mRS score, 5) and death, as it has been demonstrated in large hemispheric infarcts.24 Decompressive craniectomy is an invasive surgery on the cranial vault, even if the effect on the parenchyma is minimal. It is a simple technique not requiring specific surgical devices or skills, as opposed to the transsulcal or endoscopic approaches. SWITCH provides weak evidence for a decrease of death and severe functional dependence. We should keep in mind that the trial was underpowered due to its premature termination. As no other trials investigating decompressive craniectomy for ICH are underway, it is unlikely that the results of SWITCH will be replicated. Hence, it will remain uncertain whether decompressive craniectomy should be considered in patients with severe deep ICH.  

# TOWARD A MULTIDISCIPLINARY MANAGEMENT TO IMPROVE THE ICH CARE PATHWAY AND ICH RESEARCH  

Recent improvement in ICH care, illustrated by the bundled care approach25,26 and a call for code $\mathsf{ICH},\mathsf{^{27}}$ is encouraging. ENRICH and SWITCH highlight that neurosurgical treatments in patients with supratentorial ICH deserve reconsideration. It is, however, too early to adapt guidelines, as many questions remain unanswered. Six RCTs are ongoing (Table). They differ on several aspects, for example, required hematoma volume, clinical condition, surgical techniques and timing of surgery, and are eagerly awaited.
