# Tranexamic acid and traumatic brain injuries  

Katarina Sanford, PA-C; Sarah Garcia, PharmD  

# ABSTRACT  

The CRASH-3 trial examined the use of tranexamic acid (TXA) in patients with intracranial bleeding secondary to traumatic brain injury. The trial demonstrated that TXA, an antifibrinolytic medication, reduces mortality in patients with mild to moderate head injuries. However, because of the trial’s multiple limitations, TXA cannot yet be called the gold standard.  

Keywords: tranexamic acid, intracranial bleeding, traumatic brain injury, antifibrinolytic, trauma, head injury  

Mhe Clinical Randomization of an Antifbrinolytic in Significant Head Injury (CRASH-3) trial may have found a treatment for traumatic brain injury (TBI).1 According to the World Health Organization, 10 million people worldwide are hospitalized or killed every year as a result of a TBI.2 For patients in North America under age 45 years, TBI is a leading cause of death.3 Patients with TBI can develop an intracranial hemorrhage, resulting in brain herniation or death.4  

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is an antifbrinolytic medication developed in the 1950s to address mortality from postpartum hemorrhage.3 The drug is used to reduce bleeding during tooth extraction, postpartum hemorrhage, and heavy menstrual cycles.3 TXA is commonly used in surgery to reduce bleeding and mortality in patients with traumatic extracranial bleeding.3  

The CRASH-3 trial examined TXA and its efficacy of reducing mortality and disability in patients with TBI, and found mortality reduction in a subgroup of patients. This international trial involved 12,737 patients with TBI from 29 countries and 175 hospitals; the trial was randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled trial.5 To be included in the study, participants had to be within 8 hours of the injury, while the latter part of the trial only accepted participants if they were within 3 hours of the injury.5 The participants had to demonstrate intracranial bleeding on CT scan or have a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 12 or less in order to be a candidate for the trial.5 The trial initially included patients with a GCS of 3 and/or patients with bilateral fixed pupils; however, during the trial, the protocol was changed, and patients with this criterion were excluded from the trial.5 Follow-up for patients occurred at death, 28 days status-post injury, or at hospital discharge.5  

The mortality reduction was found in a subgroup of patients who had suffered a mild to moderate TBI (GCS score of 9 to 15).5 Data demonstrated that these patients had more benefit from TXA (risk ratio [RR] 0.78; $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] $0.64\mathrm{-}0.95$ ; $P=.005$ ) than those who had suffered a severe head injury (RR 0.99 [CI 0.91– 1.07]; $P=.73$ ).5 This shows that TXA may be beneficial in treatment of mild to moderate head injuries, but is not beneficial in the treatment of severe head injuries (GCS score of 3 to 8). However, these results have multiple limitations. The confidence interval for the mild-moderate head injury subgroup is very wide (0.64-0.95), which makes it less precise. Plus, the all-cause mortality was not reported for the mild-moderate head injury subgroup. Subgroup analysis typically is not used for clinical application, but rather for hypothesis generation. This shows that the subgroup analysis is not as reliable as the primary outcome. This should be cautiously used for treatment recommendations, if not used at all, unless further research is performed.  

The CRASH-3 trial released that “[TXA] could prevent deaths from TBI by as much as $20\%$ depending on the severity of the injury,” although the results must be cautiously examined.5 Protocols were changed throughout the trial, including time frame of administration of TXA, as well as change in inclusion criterion. The changes made in protocol during the trial allow the researchers to sift through the data and find a statistically significant result. Secondly, the primary outcome of the trial was not clearly defined. According to the CRASH-3 trial, the primary outcome was head injuryrelated death in hospital within 28 days of injury, but the primary outcome stated on clinicaltrials.gov was all-cause mortality.1,5  

# WHAT CLINICIANS NEED TO KNOW  

CRASH-3 provided additional research needed after the CRASH-2 trial, which demonstrated that TXA reduced mortality secondary to bleeding; however, the results of CRASH-2 were not statistically significant.6  

CRASH-2 was a response to a 2012 study known as the Military Application of Tranexamic Acid in Trauma Emergency Resuscitation (MATTERs) trial, which was one of the first to examine TXA in a military and traumatic setting.7 The MATTERs trial concluded that TXA was effective in the treatment of extracranial traumatic hemorrhages; however, it did not look specifically at intracranial bleeding.7 CRASH-2 evaluated the effcacy of TXA in trauma patients with bleeding.8 The nested CRASH-2 trial was a randomized, placebo-controlled trial that specifically examined the effects of TXA in TBI patients.6  

# SAFETY ISSUES  

Because TXA is an antifibrinolytic, CRASH-3 evaluated patient risk for vascular occlusive events. The study determined that the placebo and TXA groups had similar risk for vascular occlusive events (RR 0.98 [CI 0.74-1.28]).5 However, no systematic screening was performed to rule out vascular occlusive events, which leaves open the question of whether vascular occlusive events contributed to the all-cause mortality.  

The non-head injury-related deaths in patients who were given TXA had a relative risk of 1.31 $95\%$ CI 0.93-1.85), showing that TXA administration increased deaths. However, this result is statistically insignificant and raises the question of whether TXA contributed to the all-cause mortality.5  

A meta-analysis performed in 2017 found that patients who were given TXA during cardiac surgery were at a more than fourfold increased risk for postoperative seizures.9 The CRASH-3 trial evaluated seizure risk and found very similar risk between the two groups (RR 1.09 $[95\%$ $\operatorname{CI}0.90\substack{-1.33}]$ ).5 The CRASH-2 trial, which involved 20,211 patients, also examined seizure risk and reported no increase in seizure activity secondary to administration of TXA.8  

# CONCLUSION  

The results of CRASH-3 will not change treatment of isolated head injuries just yet. The mortality was unchanged (RR 0.96; $95\%$ $\left[\mathrm{CI}0.89{-}1.04\right]$ ), the disability rate was unchanged, and the primary outcome was negative. The reduction in mild to moderate head injury-related death was minimally significant and the trial did not report the all-cause mortality for the subgroup. Further research is needed before TXA can be called the new treatment for head injuries. JAAPA
