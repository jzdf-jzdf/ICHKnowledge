# Umbilical Cord Milking Versus Delayed Cord Clamping in Infants 28 to 32 Weeks: A Randomized Trial

# abstract  

OBJECTIVES: To determine whether rate of severe intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) or death among preterm infants receiving placental transfusion with UCM is noninferior to delayed cord clamping (DCC).  

METHODS: Noninferiority randomized controlled trial comparing UCM versus DCC in preterm infants born 28 to 32 weeks recruited between June 2017 through September 2022 from 19 university and private medical centers in 4 countries. The primary outcome was Grade III/IV IVH or death evaluated at a $1\%$ noninferiority margin.  

RESULTS: Among 1019 infants (UCM $n=511$ and DCC $n=508.$ ), all completed the trial from birth through initial hospitalization (mean gestational age 31 weeks, $44\%$ female). For the primary outcome, 7 of 511 $\left(1.4\%\right)$ infants randomized to UCM developed severe IVH or died compared to 7 of 508 $\left(1.4\%\right)$ infants randomized to DCC (rate difference $0.01\%$ , $95\%$ confidence interval: $(-1.4\%$ to $1.4\%$ ), $P=.99{}.$ ).  

CONCLUSIONS: In this randomized controlled trial of UCM versus DCC among preterm infants born between 28 and 32 weeks’ gestation, there was no difference in the rates of severe IVH or death. UCM may be a safe alternative to DCC in premature infants born at 28 to 32 weeks who require resuscitation.  

WHAT’S KNOWN ON THIS SUBJECT: Umbilical cord milking is not safe in preterm infants 23 to 27 weeks because of an increase in severe intraventricular hemorrhage at birth.  

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS: In preterm infants 28 to 32 weeks’ gestation, there was no differences in severe IVH or death comparing umbilical cord milking (UCM) versus delayed cord clamping (DCC). UCM appears to be a safe and effective alternative to accomplish placental transfusion in clinical circumstances that might preclude DCC.

Caring for preterm infants begins with a decision of when to clamp and cut the umbilical cord. Waiting to clamp and cut the cord after a designated period of time is delayed cord clamping (DCC). For full-term infants, the recommendations vary from 30 to 180 seconds after birth.1,2 Because preterm infants may require ventilatory assistance and maintenance of thermal regulation, the optimal timing is challenging. There are options to provide respiratory support before cord clamping,3 but these methods are not widely available.  

Some centers use umbilical cord milking (UCM) exclusively based on reduction in morbidities such as intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) and death associated with UCM.4,5 A recent survey of obstetricians and perinatologists in the United States reported $39\%$ provide UCM in healthy (term and preterm) infants and in $25\%$ of infants who need resuscitation.6 There is an urgent need for high-quality evidence to compare UCM to DCC in those infants who require resuscitation. Three recent trials from India and the United States demonstrated it is feasible to study UCM in term and preterm infants with cardiorespiratory depression at birth.7–9  

In UCM, the unclamped umbilical cord is grasped and blood is pushed toward the infant 3 to 4 times before clamping. This infuses blood into the preterm infant and can be done in 20 seconds, similar to the time it takes for early cord clamping (generally defined as $<30$ seconds).10 In the PREMOD pilot study (premature newborns delivered by cesarean delivery), UCM was associated with a larger placental transfusion and increased systemic blood flow compared to DCC.11  

In the PREMOD2 trial, umbilical cord milking was associated with an increased risk of severe IVH in the extremely preterm infant strata born 23 to 27 weeks’ gestational age (GA). This association was not seen in infants born 28 to 32 weeks’ GA and we continued to evaluate UCM in this group after approval by the data safety monitoring board (DSMB) and institutional review boards in the more mature group of infants born 28 to 32 weeks’ gestation. Consistent with the original trial design, we sought to determine whether there is noninferiority of UCM compared to DCC for the composite outcome of severe IVH and/or death. If UCM is noninferior to DCC it could provide effective placental transfusion in infants who require immediate resuscitation. US and international organizations (International Liason Committe on Resuscitation, World Health Organization, Society of Obstetricians and Gynecologists of Canada, European Association of Perinatal Medicine,13 American Academy of Pediatrics14) recommend randomized trials to study this specific issue.  

# METHODS  

The PREMOD2 trial was designed as a noninferiority trial hypothesizing that UCM is noninferior to DCC with respect to the primary composite outcome of severe IVH (Grade III or IV) or death.15,16 The study received individual institutional review board approval at each of the participating hospitals. The study was paused after enrollment of 474 infants because of a safety signal in neonates born ${<}28$ weeks, specifically a possible increase in severe IVH associated with UCM. There were no safety signals in more mature GAs so the trial restarted with enrollment restricted to infants born 28 to 32 weeks. There was no modification of the procedures (DCC for 60 seconds, and UCM 4 times) nor any of the predesignated outcomes. All instances of IVH (grades 1–4) were monitored by the study investigators and DSMB in the trial’s restart phase. The primary outcome was shifted to 2-year neurodevelopmental outcomes, which are ongoing and will be reported on completion. However, the DSMB, along with the funding agency of this cooperative agreement, the NICHD, requested that the original composite outcome of severe IVH and/or death following delivery be reported in a timely fashion as an important safety endpoint in the 28 to 32 weeks’ GA infants.  

The PREMOD2 study’s overall sample size of 1200 (600 per group) was determined to provide $90\%$ power after attrition, to demonstrate noninferiority of UCM compared to DCC for the composite outcome of severe IVH and/or death, with a $1\%$ noninferiority margin.16 This sample size provided ${>}85\%$ power for the 2-year neurodevelopmental outcome to detect noninferiority of UCM compared to DCC in standardized neurocognitive outcomes (Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development 3 or 4), with a prespecified 2.5-point noninferiority margin.  

This analysis focuses on the older (28–32 week) group only and includes 1019 infants in the strata born 28 weeks 0 of 7 days to 32 weeks 6 of 7 days GA, enrolled and randomized during the prepause era of the trial $(n=292)$ and all infants enrolled and randomized after the study restart $(n=727)$ . Analysis of the postrestart cohort alone was performed as a sensitivity analysis. The overall sample size and noninferiority margin for this analysis were not modified from the original study design.  

Maternal, neonatal, and delivery characteristics were compared between the UCM and DCC groups. The National Institutes of Health standard classifications of race were used for demographic purposes.17 Data were obtained directly from the parent or guardian or from the medical record. Classification of race and ethnicity was designated by the parent. Race and ethnicity is understood to be a social construct and was collected to evaluate for any racial or ethnic biases between treatment groups. Summary statistics included means and standard deviations for continuous measures and frequencies and percentages for categorical measures.  

All the original prespecified study outcomes were evaluated for infants born 28 to 32 weeks’ GA.16 Unadjusted treatment group rates were calculated and presented with risk differences ( $95\%$ confidence intervals) for all primary, secondary, and exploratory outcomes. Risk differences were calculated within prespecified mode of delivery subgroups. Consistency of associations between treatment group assignment and outcomes were assessed by modes of delivery evaluating interaction terms in multivariable regression models. Sensitivity analyses included generalized estimating equations to estimate treatment effects accounting for correlation within sites and multiple gestations. $\upalpha$ levels for all hypothesis tests of main effects were set at 0.05. All hypothesis tests were 2-sided. SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) was used for all analyses. No adjustments are made for multiple comparisons.  

# RESULTS  

From 5998 screened pregnancies a total of 1019 infants born at $28~0$ of 7 to 32 6 of 7 weeks’ GA were enrolled from June 2017 to September 2022, with 511 and 508 randomized to UCM and DCC, respectively (Fig 1). No missing data were identified for the primary outcomes.  

The correct treatment was performed on 487 of 511 $(95\%)$ of UCM assigned infants and 471 of 508 $(93\%)$ of DCC assigned infants. Average gestational age at enrollment was 31 weeks, $44\%$ were girls, and there were no notable imbalances in maternal, infant, or delivery characteristics between study groups (Table 1).  

The mean time to umbilical cord clamping was higher in the DCC group versus the UCM group (58.4 vs 24.5 seconds, $P<.001\$ ). A greater number of newborns were breathing before the cord was clamped in those randomized to DCC $86\%$ vs $75\%$ , $P<.0001\$ .  

For the primary outcome, 7 of 511 $(1.4\%)$ infants randomized to UCM developed severe IVH or died compared to 7 of 508 $\left(1.4\%\right)$ infants randomized to DCC (rate difference $0.01\%$ , $95\%$ confidence interval [CI]: $(-1.4\%$ to $1.4\%$ ), $P=.99$ , Table 2). There were no significant differences in severe IVH, death, any grade IVH, hemoglobin at 4 hours of life, and all exploratory outcomes (Table 2).  

Secondary outcomes are shown in Table 2 and Fig 2. For secondary outcomes, data were missing in 244 for hemoglobin and 250 for hematocrit at 4 hours. There were no statistically significant differences between groups shown in Table 2. The test of interaction between mode of delivery and treatment group was significant for severe IVH but none of the other subgroups. Exploratory neonatal morbidities are shown in Supplemental Table 3.  

In the sensitivity analysis analyzing only the restart cohort born at 28 to 32 weeks’ GA, the composite primary outcome occurred in 4 of 367 $(1\%)$ and in 3 of 360 $(1\%)$ of infants randomized to UCM and DCC, respectively (rate difference $0\%95\%$ CI: $[-1\%$ to $2\%]$ ). There were no differences in the rates of morbidity between groups between the prestoppage and restart cohorts.  

# DISCUSSION  

In this randomized clinical trial of UCM versus DCC in preterm infants born 28 to 32 weeks’ gestation, there was no difference in the rate of a composite outcome of severe IVH or death, although we are unable to demonstrate noninferiority. Nevertheless, these findings are reassuring in comparison with our earlier study that suggested an increased risk of severe IVH in extremely preterm infants (born 23–27 weeks’ GA).16  

Unlike extremely preterm infants, there are biologically plausible explanations for why 28- to 32-week infants would be at lower risk for UCM-related IVH compared to 23 to 27 week infants. First, 28- to 32-week infants have a less vascularized germinal matrix, which reduces the risk of bleeding.18 Second, 28- to 32-week infants have adequate cerebral autoregulation compared to 23- to 27-week infants. Beausoleil et al analyzed cerebral autoregulation using near infrared spectroscopy and demonstrated that cerebral perfusion fluctuates above baseline ${>}50\%$ of the time depending on systemic blood pressure in 23 to 27 week infants compared to $20\%$ of the time in 28- to 32- week infants.19,20 This is important if UCM causes fluctuations in cerebral blood flow. A study in anesthetized 128- day-old preterm lambs (equivalent to a 26-week gestation human) demonstrated that repeated cord milking caused substantial fluctuations in carotid artery flow.21  

Third, 28- to 32-week infants are more likely to initiate breathing before cord clamping which may reduce fluctuations in carotid blood flow during UCM. Although there were fewer infants randomized to UCM who were breathing before cord clamping compared to DCC, this may be because of the shorter time required to complete UCM.  

Our study provides additional supportive evidence for UCM in premature infants born 28 to 32 weeks who might not be able to receive DCC. This evidence is consistent with ILCOR recommendations that UCM is a reasonable alternative to delayed cord clamping.23 Although there are centers providing resuscitation and DCC with an intact cord, trials to date have not shown a benefit with this approach and it is still being evaluated in ongoing randomized controlled trials.3 Logistical concerns and proper sterile equipment have prevented resuscitation and DCC with an intact cord from being widely adopted.24 Until alternative approaches are adequately tested and implemented into delivery room care, UCM may be a reasonable alternative in nonvigorous preterm infants born 28 to 32 weeks’ gestation.

The study was appropriately paused by the DSMB because of the increased rate of severe IVH in those infants born ${<}28$ weeks GA in the UCM group.16 We restarted the trial limiting the enrollment and randomization to infants born 28 to 32 weeks’ GA, a group with an expected lower rate of the primary outcome (severe IVH and/or death) in both the UCM and DCC groups. It is notable there were no significant differences in the rates of other important morbidities in the 28- to 32-week GA infants (see Supplemental Table 3) comparing both the initial enrollment and restart periods.  

# Limitations  

Our data were limited by not all infants having hemoglobin available in the time window (4 hours of life) because of variations of local practice for laboratory draws. Several studies have demonstrated decreased placental transfusion with DCC compared to early cord clamping (ECC) or UCM during cesarean delivery.25,26 Because milking provides an active rather than passive method of transferring blood into the newborn, our data suggests it may be beneficial in cesarean delivery births.  

Although the event rates for the primary outcome are both low and the same for the 2 groups, the $95\%$ CI includes the $1\%$ noninferiority margin and thus we cannot formally demonstrate noninferiority of UCM. However, the conclusion would differ with a less conservative noninferiority margin (eg, $2\%$ ). Further, when the recruitment protocol shifted to exclude infants born ${<}28$ weeks’ GA who have a higher risk of severe IVH and/or death, the target sample size was not increased to account for the resulting lower event rate. This lower event rate of our primary outcome in 28- to 32-week GA infants increases the risk of a Type 2 error (a real difference between the comparator groups but our sample size was insufficient to detect such). A sufficiently powered study would require approximately 3000 infants born between 28 to 32 weeks GA to test noninferiority with a $1\%$ margin for the outcome of severe IVH and/or death in UCM compared to DCC.

# CONCLUSIONS  

In the largest randomized clinical trial to date comparing UCM to DCC among preterm infants born at 28 to 32 weeks’ GA, there were no differences in the composite rate outcome of severe intraventricular hemorrhage or death. Hemoglobin and hematocrit values were equivalent, and no other secondary outcomes were noted. UCM may be a safe alternative to DCC for premature infants who require resuscitation.
