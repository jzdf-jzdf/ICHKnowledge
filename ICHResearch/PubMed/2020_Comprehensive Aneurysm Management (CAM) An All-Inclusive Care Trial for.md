# Comprehensive Aneurysm Management (CAM): An All-Inclusive Care Trial for Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysms

- BACKGROUND: In the absence of randomized evidence, the optimal management of patients with unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIA) remains uncertain.  

- METHODS: Comprehensive Aneurysm Management (CAM) is an all-inclusive care trial combined with a registry. Any patient with a UIA (no history of intracranial hemorrhage within the previous 30 days) can be recruited, and treatment allocation will follow an algorithm combining clinical judgment and randomization. Patients eligible for at least 2 management options will be randomly allocated 1:1 to conservative or curative treatment. Minimization will be used to balance risk factors, using aneurysm size $(\ge7\mathrm{mm}),$ location (anterior or posterior circulation), and age $\mathtt{<}60$ years.  

- RESULTS: The CAM primary outcome is survival without neurologic dependency (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score $\leftrightsquigarrow$ at 10 years. Secondary outcome measures include the incidence of subarachnoid hemorrhage during follow-up and related morbidity and mortality; morbidity and mortality related to endovascular treatment or surgical treatment of the  

UIA at 1 year; overall morbidity and mortality at 1, 5, and 10 years; when relevant, duration of hospitalization; and, when relevant, discharge to a location other than home. The primary hypothesis for patients randomly allocated to at least 2 options, 1 of which is conservative management, is that active UIA treatment will reduce the 10-year combined neurologic morbidity and mortality (mRS score $>21$ from $24\%$ to $16\%$ . At least 961 patients recruited from at least 20 centers over 4 years will be needed for the randomized portion of the study.  

- CONCLUSIONS: Patients with unruptured intracranial aneurysms can be comprehensively managed within the context of an all-inclusive care trial.

Most aneurysms remain asymptomatic unless they rupture, an event that occurs infrequently (annual incidence of $5\mathrm{-}\mathrm{\mathrm{I}0}/$ 100,000). The annual risk of bleeding from incidentally discovered aneurysms is debated, but most series and metaanalyses have reported a low rate between $0.\mathrm{{I}\%}$ and $2\%$ .4-9 Although subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is associated with a high morbidity and mortality $(45\%-75\%)$ , preventive treatments also carry risk. It would seem essential to preemptively treat those patients deemed to be at risk for rupture to prevent the morbidity and mortality associated with SAH. On the other hand, many patients diagnosed with UIAs may never suffer SAH, and it is imperative to avoid iatrogenic injuries to patients “destined to coexist peacefully with their unruptured lesions.”10  

The time-honored approach to treating UIAs is with microsurgical clipping, which involves excluding the aneurysm from the circulation by placing a metallic clip across the aneurysm neck. Surgical clipping is widely considered a safe and durable means to treat UIAs, but in recent years it is being increasingly replaced by endovascular treatment (EVT).11  

An international randomized controlled trial (RCT) on ruptured aneurysms (ISAT) and the Barrow Randomized Aneurysm Trial (BRAT) have shown that EVT is associated with improved outcomes of patients treated after SAH compared with surgical clipping, but this finding cannot be extrapolated to unruptured lesions.12,13  

The efficacy of endovascular treatment in the preventing aneurysm rupture remains unknown.14-16 Some observational studies and registries have suggested that immediate treatment-related risks for UIA are lower with EVT than with surgical clipping,17 but EVT may be less effective than surgery due to long-term angiographic recurrences.18  

Meta-analyses of the safety and efficacy of surgical and endovascular management of UIAs have been conducted. The overall quality of the literature on the subject is poor, with no justification for preventive treatments for most patients. Surgical clipping was associated with a risk of unfavorable outcome of $6.7\%$ $99\%$ confidence interval [CI], $4{\cdot}9^{0}/{0}{-}9{\cdot}0\%)$ and a risk of death of $\mathbf{I}.7\%$ $\left(99\%$ CI, $0.9^{0}/0{-}3.0^{0}/0\AA)$ .19,20 Endovascular treatment was associated with a risk of unfavorable outcome of $4.8\%$ $\left(99^{\mathrm{o}}/0\right)$ CI, $3{\cdot}9\%$ to $6.0\%$ ) and a risk of death of $2.0\%$ $(99\%\mathrm{CI}$ , $\mathrm{I}.5\%{-2.6\%})$ .21,22 Neither surgery nor EVT has proven superior to conservative management,23 yet both endovascular and surgical treatments are performed in large numbers of patients daily without evidence that they are doing more good than harm and without evidence that one approach is superior to the other. There is currently no scientific evidence to support treatment of UIAs, and there are no well-accepted guidelines.24 Several prospective registries comparing patients selected for medical management with patients selected for surgical management constitute the most significant studies on UIAs.25 Unfortunately, these efforts suffer from systematic biases, a consequence of the current “best clinical judgment” approach to managing these patients. Patients recruited in these studies were selected in such a fashion that those considered at low treatment risk and at high risk for rupture were offered treatment; only those patients at low risk for rupture, or with comorbid conditions making treatment unadvisable, were included in the “natural history” group.25-29  

The uncertainty regarding the management of UIAs has not progressed in the last 30 years. Time alone and observational cohort studies of treated or untreated patients cannot provide a valid answer to the fundamental question regarding whether or not they should be treated. The fundamental ethical basis for the present study is that physicians should offer a risky preventive treatment only when it has been shown to be beneficial. Before such time, UIA treatment should be offered as an RCT, giving the patient a $50\%$ chance of receiving a treatment aimed at preventing SAH and an equal $50\%$ chance of avoiding the potential complications of treatment.30 In the presence of uncertainty, a care trial is the best way to address the question of whether patients with UIAs are best managed medically until definite indications arise or with endovascular or surgical treatment.  

This study addresses the problem of multiple management alternatives by offering an all-inclusive framework trial that combines clinical judgment and randomized allocation, as inspired by a successfully recruiting, similarly all-inclusive study on brain arteriovenous malformations.31  

# OBJECTIVES  

The general objective is to use CAM to provide a care research framework for managing all patients with UIAs.30 For patients for whom conservative management is contemplated, CAM may validate previous observational studies that claim a very low risk of rupture. The registry portion of CAM could also serve as a source of patients for secondary trials to assess the value of imaging follow-up studies or various pharmaceutical agents to prevent ruptures (e.g., aspirin, statins). For patients for whom preventive treatment is contemplated, CAM will provide a context that can optimize outcomes in the presence of uncertainty, by giving each patient a $50\%$ chance of being treated and a $50\%$ chance of being spared a potentially useless or harmful treatment. Similarly, when surgical clipping is contemplated, CAM will provide a $50\%$ chance of being offered the less invasive EVT instead.  

# METHODS  

This study has been registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT04155606).  

# Study Questions  

Specific study questions include the following: (1) can a care trial context assist clinicians and patients in managing the UIA dilemma?; (2) when conservative management is contemplated, is the risk of rupture as low as unvalidated observational studies currently suggest?; (3) do patients with UIAs considered for curative treatments have better long-term clinical outcomes with active treatment or conservative management?; and (4) is surgical clipping a more effective management than EVT?  

# Design  

CAM combines large simple clinical care trials whenever patients are judged eligible for more than 1 management option, or otherwise a registry of each management option. All patients with 1 or more UIAs will be eligible for participation in either a registry or 1 or more of the embedded trials. All patients will be treated medically for hypertension if necessary and will receive counseling for behavioral risk factors (e.g., smoking, excessive drinking) when indicated. A noninvasive (magnetic resonance angiography or computed tomography [CT] angiography) or catheter angiography and a baseline CT scan or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain is required to enter the study. These studies should demonstrate the unequivocal presence of a subarachnoid saccular aneurysm, not recently ruptured $(>30$ days since the last SAH). If they accept, all subjects will be registered, and when eligible, included in a specific randomized trial using the algorithm below. Conventional randomization will be performed whenever and wherever possible. A double-consent prerandomization design is possible wherever the method is accepted by the local Institutional Review Board.32  

The first principle of an outcome-based practice is to question the merits of as-yet validated interventions. The most fundamental question—whether or not to treat intracranial aneurysms—cannot be answered without specifying which treatment we are talking about. The algorithmic process combining clinical judgment and randomized allocation will proceed by asking the following questions for the treating physicians to answer on the electronic platform (Figure 1):  

Q1: Is the patient being considered for treatment? Possible answers: yes or no.  

If the clinician selects no, the patient will be included in the observational registry. If yes, the following question will be:  

Q2: What treatment option is being considered for this patient?  

Clinicians will use clinical judgment to select 1 of 3 options: endovascular, surgical, or t either treatment is reasonable.  

Then comes the most important question:  

Q3: Is conservative management a reasonable alternative given the lack of evidence that treatments are beneficial?  

Potential answers are yes or no. If no, they will be included in the trial group comparing EVT and surgery (if the answer to Q2 is “either treatment is reasonable”) or in 1 of the 2 treatment registries (surgical or endovascular). If yes, they will be included in the appropriate randomized subtrial (according to the answer to Q2) that compares EVT to conservative management (TEAM-2; sub-Trial on Endovascular Aneurysm Management-2), surgical management to conservative management (TSAM; or sub-Trial on Surgical Aneurysm Management), or they will be included in the TOES arm (sub-Trial on Observational, Endovascular or Surgical management), in which if they are allocated treatment will undergo a second randomization to determine the specific treatment modality.  

# Conservative and Active Management  

Some patients will be judged not suitable for active treatment, and they can be included in the conservative management registry; they could eventually be deemed eligible to participate in secondary studies on imaging follow-up or pharmacologic management of  

UIAs. Some patients will be judged suitable for treatment with a surgical, an endovascular, or either approach. Those patients who are randomly allocated to conservative management will have active treatment deferred for at least 10 years or until definite indications are thought to have arisen (i.e., progressive symptoms, mass effect, hemorrhage, or signs/symptoms suggesting “impending” rupture). Such events will be managed appropriately and reported as “adverse events” to the Data Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC). The final outcome, including whether the patient was eventually treated, will be considered within a conservative management strategy and included in the primary analysis. For patient's allocated treatment, surgical clipping or endovascular treatment will be performed within 12 weeks of randomization, according to standards of practice and under general anesthesia. Imaging (CT or MRI) will be performed before discharge to detect asymptomatic complications.  

# Allocation  

Clinical judgment along with the aforementioned algorithm (Figure 1) will be used to determine the primary treatment method planned. Patients will be randomly allocated 1:1 to the selected treatment or to conservative management (i.e., deferral of treatment for 10 years). Patients deemed most suitable for surgery will be stratified into the TSAM; those judged most suitable for endovascular treatment, into the TEAM-2; and those judged suitable for either surgical/endovascular, into TOES, followed by a second randomization in the Collaborative UnRuptured Endovascular Versus Surgery (CURES) trial when active treatment is allocated. Randomization will be concealed, centrally adjudicated, and stratified according to treatment eligibility (endovascular, surgical, or both). Minimization will be used to balance risk factors, using size $\mathrm{\rVert\ge7~mm\}$ , location (anterior or posterior circulation), and age $<60$ years. The whole randomization process will be done using a web-based platform that is available 24 hours. Patients eligible for only 1 option will be registered and followed using the same case report forms.  

# Study Hypotheses  

The primary study hypothesis for patients allocated to at least 2 options, 1 of which is conservative management I, that the 10-year combined neurologic morbidity and mortality $(\mathrm{mRS}>2)$ will be reduced from $24\%$ to $\tau6\%$ ( $\upbeta=80\%$ ; $\alpha=0.048$ ) for a sample size of $9^{6\textupI}$ patients $\mathrm{\Delta}836$ plus $\mathbf{I}5^{\mathbf{0}}/\mathbf{0}$ losses to follow-up and cross-overs) with active treatment. To demonstrate the superiority of treatment, a follow-up period of 10 years is required for SAH-related events to occur. Over this 10-year follow-up period, $\tau2\%$ of the population is expected to become deceased.33 For treated patients, using the interim results from the CURES trial, we estimate that active treatment carries a $4\%$ risk of death or dependency at 1 year.33 In the medical management group, we assume a $20\%$ rate of SAH at 10 years, with SAH resulting in a ${\sim}60\%$ morbidity and mortality rate.  

Individual treatments will eventually require separate validation. We estimate that recruitment of at least 1000 patients for each subtrial (TEAM and TSAM) will be necessary to answer the clinical dilemma. The similar rates of death and dependency at 1 year observed in CURES $(4\%$ for both EVT and clipping) suggest that enrolling a sufficient number of patients to show a difference in long-term clinical outcomes between clipping and coiling is unlikely. However, it is essential that the more invasive surgical clipping be offered within a transparent care trial framework.

# Outcomes  

The primary outcome of CAM is survival without neurologic dependency (mRS score ${<}3{\mathrm{)}}$ ) at 10 years. Secondary outcome measures are needed to monitor patient outcomes for the duration of the trial: (1) incidence of SAH during follow-up and related morbidity and mortality; (2) morbidity and mortality related to EVT or surgical treatment of the UIA at 1 year; (3) overall mortality at 1, 5, and 10 years; (4) overall morbidity (mRS score ${\ge}3.$ ) at 1, 5, and 10 years; (5) duration of hospitalization; and (6) discharge to location other than home.  

The most important factor in the choice of outcome measure is that it be hard (easily ascertainable, reliable and not debatable; e.g., death or dependency), simple (to minimally disrupt routine clinical care and contain costs), and clinically meaningful to both doctors and patients. Death and dependency are the outcomes that the patients and clinicians most want to avoid, are difficult to debate and simple to measure. We have not included imaging as a study endpoint, as many clinicians do not repeatedly image aneurysms they have decided not to treat, to avoid generating anxiety.  

# End Point Measures  

The mRS score will be determined by using a standardized questionnaire administered by care personnel blinded to treatment allocation.34 An intracranial hemorrhage will be judged from cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI), from a positive lumbar puncture in the setting of an acute headache, or following sudden death without other suspicious cause. Length of hospitalization and discharge to a location other than home will be recorded by the attending physician on discharge. For multiple hospitalizations, days will be summed.  

# Eligibility Criteria  

CAM is a pragmatic care trial,30 and any patient with a subarachnoid UIA can be included in the randomized or registry portion of the study. Inclusion criteria are patients age ${\ge}\mathrm{I}8$ years, with at least 1 documented, intracranial subarachnoid aneurysm (cavernous aneurysms are excluded). Exclusion criteria include patients with any intracranial hemorrhage, including SAH, within the previous 30 days; patients with arteriovenous malformationeassociated aneurysms; and patients unable to provide informed consent.  

All the investigations proposed in CAM constitute part of routine, standard care in the treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms. Patients who undergo treatment will be seen in clinic at approximately $6\mathrm{-}\mathbf{\tau}_{12}$ weeks after treatment as part of routine followup care. All patients will be followed with subsequent routine clinic visits at 1 year and at 5 and 10 years thereafter. At these intervals, contact information will be verified, mRS scores determined, and inquiries made about possible aneurysm rupture, treatment, or retreatment. All treated patients will undergo catheter angiography or noninvasive vascular imaging (CT angiography or magnetic resonance angiography) at $\mathbf{\sigma}_{12}\pm\mathbf{\sigma}_{2}$ months posttreatment to identify recurrence of a major saccular aneurysm. We also expect treated patients to have follow-up vascular imaging studies at 5 and 10 years.  

# Number and Source of Patients  

Testing the primary hypothesis will require at least 961 patients (836 plus $\mathtt{I}5^{\mathrm{{o}}}/\mathrm{o}$ losses to follow-up and cross-overs). This number does not include patients enrolled in the registries. We will aim to involve as many centers as possible worldwide that perform both clipping and coiling of aneurysms, and we aim to recruit all patients with a UIA presenting for neurovascular care. The study will continue to recruit patients until the questions have been definitively answered. As the study proceeds and data accrue, the study questions and hypotheses will be modified and refined in accordance with the guidance provided to the Steering Committee by the DSMC. The first interim analysis will be performed when 500 patients have completed the first year of follow-up.  

The importance of minimizing losses to follow-up will be stressed from the outset. Patients lost to follow-up will be attributed the last observed clinical outcome on analyses of secondary outcomes (last observation carried forward imputation). In all centers, collaborators will provide a means to rapidly see patients when concerns arise and to provide reassurance when appropriate. Until evidence shows that 1 management paradigm is best, dissuasion of crossover is ethical, especially given the risk of aneurysm treatments and the high proportion of patients with a benign natural history. Nonetheless, it is anticipated that some of the patients allocated to conservative management will eventually want and receive treatment for their unruptured aneurysm. These patients will be followed in their assigned group for intention-totreat analyses, but they will also be analyzed in an as-treated fashion and identified for secondary analyses.  

# Planned Statistical Analysis  

The $4$ stratified trial arms (TEAM-II, TSAM, TOES, and CURES) and the registries will be analyzed separately. Combined exploratory analyses will also be performed, looking for differences in group characteristics and in treatment outcomes between RCTs and registries in an attempt to better understand the scope and limits of the trial results. As with any trials of surgery, early risks may be followed by later benefit; therefore, the hazard ratio will be unfavorable during the recruitment years, while interventions are being performed. Initially, the DSMC will ensure that treatmentrelated complications and hemorrhagic events are within confidence intervals compatible with a safe and meaningful trial. To describe how and when hemorrhagic events occur, analyses will include Kaplan-Meyer life table methods to assess the 1-, 5-, and 10-year survival and survival without hemorrhage among all those patients allocated to immediate treatment (including the few who did not undergo it), and all those allocated deferral of any intervention (including the few who will eventually be treated). Perioperative morbidity and mortality will be excluded from these analyses, for they assume a constant hazard ratio over time. The “survival” functions will be compared graphically and using a logrank statistic. The main statistical tests will involve comparisons between the probabilities of mortality and morbidity/mortality from hemorrhage, excluding perioperative complications, and from hemorrhage or complications of treatment, as well as comparisons of the 1-, 5-, and 10-year probabilities of combined disease/treatment-related mortality/morbidity in the absence of other causes of death or disability. Descriptive statistics will be analyzed for demographic variables and potential risk factors to compare the groups at baseline. Mean, standard deviation, and range will be presented for quantitative variables, such as size of aneurysms, and frequency tables will be presented for categorical variables, such as the number of patients with a previous history of  

SAH or multiple aneurysms. Statistics will be broken down by center and by treatment arm. Comparability of the groups will be assessed through independent analysis of variance for quantitative data or ManteleHaentzel and $\chi^{2}$ tests for categorical data. Assuming comparability of groups across centers, the primary outcome—disease or treatment-related combined morbidity/ mortality (for both intent-to-treat and per-protocol populations)— will be compared between groups through a Fisher's exact test for independent proportions at 1, 5, and 10 years. Similar analyses will be done for disease or treatment-related mortality and combined mortality-morbidity. Secondary outcomes and overall morbidity will be compared between groups through independent t tests (quantitative variables) or $\upchi$ statistics (categorical data). The analyses of neurologic data at follow-up will control for baseline data using logistic regression, analysis of variance, or Cox regression multivariate models. All tests will be interpreted with adjustment for multiplicity to have the 0.05 level of confidence at 10 years only. Finally, a logistic regression will be used to find baseline variables capable of predicting hemorrhages or complications in both groups. The planned method is stepwise forward on a likelihood ratio with a probability of 0.05 to enter a predictor (PIN in) and a probability of 0.10 to exclude a predictor (PIN out). Possible predictors include the size of the aneurysm $\ge_{7}$ mm vs. ${<}7\mathrm{mm}$ ), location (posterior vs. anterior circulation aneurysms), patient age ${\cdot}<60$ years vs. ${\geq}60$ years), status of the aneurysm (previously treated vs. never treated) and (previous history of SAH vs. unruptured only), as well as other baseline characteristics. All analyses of the primary endpoint will be performed taking into account the multiplicity of tests and any interim analyses. Secondary analyses will be performed using an $\mathfrak{a}$ value of $5\%$ .  

# Frequency of Analyses  

Initial assessment of the rate of hemorrhagic events in the conservative management group, the morbidity of active treatment, and the frequency of cross-over will be performed after $5^{00}$ patients have reached 1 year and 5 years to ensure that events are within confidence intervals compatible with a meaningful trial (as judged by the DSMC). Interim analyses on efficacy in the prevention of hemorrhagic events will be conducted after 500 patients have completed 1 year of follow-up, to ensure that patients are not unnecessarily exposed to the intervention or to the risk of hemorrhage. Safety data will be reviewed periodically by the DSMC, which will meet on a yearly basis. This committee will provide periodic reports and decide to conduct comparative analysis whenever they deem necessary. Using Peto's method, the primary outcomes will be assessed when 500 patients have completed 1 year of follow-up $a=0.001\$ , when 500 patients have completed 5 years of follow-up ( $a=0.001$ ), and when all patients have completed 10 years of follow-up $(a=.048)$ .  

# Subgroup Analyses  

It is possible that treatment morbidity, or the incidence of hemorrhage, will be affected by some characteristics of the patient or aneurysm, such as age, size, aneurysm location (anterior or posterior), previous treatment, or previous history of SAH, all predefined at the time of recruitment. These factors will be analyzed, displayed using a forest plot, and analyzed by introducing an interaction term in the logistic model.  

# Trial Management  

The Steering Committee, consisting of a neurosurgeon, an interventionist, a statistician, and a research coordinator, will be responsible for trial promotion, communication with centers, and monitoring trial progress. The DSMC will safeguard trial participants and trial integrity and ensure that all procedures respect ethical norms. Based on unblinded data, the DSMC may recommend that the Steering Committee modify or stop the trial. Because the initial treatment risks may eventually be balanced by frequent aneurysmal ruptures in the medically managed group, the DSMC should be prepared to halt the trial for either excessive ruptures in the untreated group or excessive treatment-related morbidity in the treated groups. In general, the DSMC will recommend that treatment of UIAs be abandoned if the lower bound of the confidence interval around the initial complication rate is higher than the projected rate of morbidity and mortality due to subarachnoid hemorrhage over 10 years. Initially, when few patients are enrolled in the trial, stopping the trial would require high surgical or endovascular complication rates, and it is unlikely that the DSMC would be in a position to recommend trial discontinuation, given that both treatment options are standard treatments in current use.  

# Ethics Approval  

CAM has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHU Montreal (January 9, 2020) and has been submitted to Institutional Review Board at other centers. Prospective participants will be made fully aware of the study purposes, the procedure, and the risks of each intervention. When signing the study consent form, they will be informed that participation is voluntary and that they can request to be withdrawn from the study at any time. Patient enrollment in the trial will be in compliance with the principles enunciated in the Declaration of Helsinki.  

# DISCUSSION  

UIAs are being increasingly discovered, and an ethical, rational means to help physicians properly manage affected patients is needed. Two recently elaborated scoring systems have proposed expedient answers to the clinical dilemma, but neither offers a satisfactory solution. The PHASES (Population, Hypertension, Age, Size, Earlier subarachnoid hemorrhage, Site of aneurysm) score is based on the reanalysis of past case series reporting the risk of rupture of aneurysms in patients selected for conservative management,9 but it could not be validated because all patients of the initial studies were used to construct the model, and the scientific veracity of the system has been called into question.35 The UIATS (Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysm Treatment Score) is a decision aid that attributes points for factors thought to increase rupture risk versus points for factors associated with treatment risks. This aid was conceived after multiple Delphi consensus sessions of widely diverging experts' opinions, but has been criticized as not corresponding to real-world practice.37,38  

Expert opinion cannot replace evidence nor justify risky preventive treatments of incidental imaging findings. In the absence of a research framework, clinicians believe they have little choice but to rely on clinical judgment or homemade algorithms, taking into account the patient's age, the aneurysm's size and location, and the patient's attitude about having an untreated aneurysm, as well as a self-assessment of treatment skills and limitations. This decision making process is unreliable, even among clinicians of the same specialty working in the same institution.39  

There are no randomized trials comparing active and conservative management for UIAs. There is one ongoing randomized trial comparing clipping and coiling for UIAs that recently provided some interim results, but it is too early to draw any conclusions.40,41 The premature termination of a previous trial that addressed coiling versus conservative management has left clinicians and patients in a scientific void, with no recourse to ever know how to best manage these lesions.14-16 A trial directly comparing medical management, surgical clipping, and endovascular coiling at a 1:1:1 randomization ratio has been called for. We have previously discussed why such an approach would be misguided, if only because very few patients would be considered eligible for all 3 management options.42  

CAM is designed to be an all-inclusive care trial, but clinicians and patients cannot be coerced to participate. Clinical judgment and freedom to refuse treatments or withdraw from participation remain essential components of ethical care and research. There will be variability in decision making, which hopefully will come at the level of which subtrial is chosen (TEAM-2, TSAM, or TOES), rather than which management approach is chosen for which patient. The registry portions of CAM undoubtedly will be affected by many of the same biases (including selection) that permeate all nonrandomized studies of unruptured aneurysms.25 Because all care scenarios will be acceptable within the CAM framework, few reasons will remain to refuse participation, and it will be possible to identify sources of bias by comparing patients included in the subtrials and registries. Nonetheless, valid comparisons between patient outcomes will only come from randomized allocation. Thus, participating physicians will frequently be reminded that for almost all patients, the best care is within the randomized portions of the study rather than the registries.43  

CAM has been designed as a care trial, combining clinical care and research in the best medical interest of patients. CAM is a simple, all-inclusive, pragmatic randomized trial with a meaningful long-term clinical outcome; it includes no extra risk or cost of visits beyond what is required in routine care.30 Integrating RCTs within care may require reconsideration of many received views.44 Although its general objective is ambitious, we see no other way to finally practice outcome-based medical care for patients with UIA. Although providing optimal care taking into account the uncertainties is immediately possible within the context of such a trial, the participation of many centers, clinicians, and patients will be necessary to finally answer the difficult question regarding the best management of patients with UIA.  

# CONCLUSION  

CAM is designed to offer a care research context to manage UIA patients in the presence of uncertainty.
