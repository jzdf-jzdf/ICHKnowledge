The relationship between 30- and 90-day modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores in intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) patients was evaluated. This post hoc cohort analysis of the ATACH-2 trial included patients with acute ICH who were alive at 30 days and who had mRS scores reported at 30 and 90 days. The mRS score was then converted to a utility (EuroQol-5 Dimension-3 Level [EQ-5D-3L])–weighted mRS score. After adjustment of 30-day mRS score for key covariates using multivariable ordinal regression, the relationship between 30-day and observed 90-day functional outcome was assessed via absolute difference in the utility-weighted version. Of the 1000 trial sub­ jects, 898 met inclusion criteria. This low-moderate severity ICH cohort had a median baseline GCS score of 15 and median hematoma volume of $9.7~\mathrm{mL}$ . Observed 30-day mRS had the largest association with observed 90- day values $(\chi^{2}=302.9$ , $p<0.0001.$ . Patients generally either maintained the same mRS scores between 30 and 90 days $48\%)$ or experienced a 1-point $(32\%)$ or 2-point $(10\%)$ improvement by 90 days. The mean $\pm$ standard deviation (SD) EQ-5D-3L at 90 days was $0.67\pm0.26$ . Following adjustment, the mean absolute difference be­ tween predicted and observed utility-weighted 90-day mRS scores was $0.006\pm0.13$ points and less than the estimated minimal clinically important difference of 0.13 points. The difference in average utility-weighted mRS scores at 30 and 90 days was not clinically relevant, suggesting 30-day score may be a reasonable proxy for 90- day values in patients with ICH when 90-day values are not available.  

# 1. Introduction  

Understanding long-term functional outcome in stroke survivors is important for rehabilitation strategies and assessing the effectiveness of acute stroke interventions and management [1,2]. Ninety days after ischemic stroke has become a standard time point for primary functional outcome measurement in clinical trials [3]. Functional recovery after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) evolves over weeks and months, making earlier assessments liable to misestimate final functional outcome status [1,4]. This may be particularly relevant among acute intracerebral hemorrhage patients who typically have a longer recovery time than those suffering from ischemic strokes [1]. However, the true impact of an index stroke event may be more difficult to assess at later time points as competing causes of morbidity and mortality may occur over longer follow-up times.  

Functional outcome status at 30 days has been shown to reliably estimate 90-day status following an acute ischemic stroke, with covariate-adjusted, 30-day, ordinal modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores found to be strongly associated with 90-day scores [5]. However, there is a paucity of data attempting to better understand the agreement be­ tween early (30-day) and later (90-day) assessments of functional outcome following an acute intracerebral hemorrhage. Furthermore, ordinal mRS values may not reflect differences in perceived quality of life that are important to patients. Consequently, experts have recommended the use of utility-weighted mRS in stroke trials [6,7].  

In this analysis of Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage-2 (ATACH-2) trial [8,9] data, we sought to understand the relationship between 30- and 90-day mRS scores and identify indepen­ dent predictors of 90-day ordinal mRS scores after acute intracerebral hemorrhage.  

# 2. Methods  

# 2.1. Study design and patient population  

This was a post hoc cohort analysis of the multicenter, randomized, controlled ATACH-2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT01176565). Full details of the ATACH-2 study design and results have been pub­ lished previously [8,9]. Inclusion criteria for ATACH-2 was individuals 18 years of age or older, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 5 or higher on arrival to the emergency department, an intraparenchymal hema­ toma volume of $<60~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ , and an admission systolic blood pressure (SBP) between 180 and $240~\mathrm{mmHg}$ . Exclusion criteria included those with an ICH considered to be traumatic, any history of bleeding, anti­ coagulant use within the prior 5 days, or a low platelet count $(<50,000/$ $\mathrm{mm}^{3\setminus}$ ). Trial participants were then randomized to either an intensive blood pressure reduction strategy (goal SBP of $110–139\mathrm{mmHg})$ or a standard blood pressure reduction strategy (goal SBP of 140–179 $\mathrm{mmHg})$ initiated within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ of intracerebral hemorrhage symptom onset and continued for $24\mathrm{~h~}$ thereafter. The primary outcome in ATACH-2 was an mRS score of 4 to 6 at 90 days post randomization. Of note, no significant difference in this outcome was seen between groups in the main trial $(\mathtt{p}=0.72)$ ).  

Patients from ATACH-2 were included in this analysis if they were alive at 30 days post randomization and had an mRS score reported at both 30- and 90-days post randomization. The primary objective of this analysis was to assess the relationship between 30- and 90-day mRS scores.  

# 2.2. Outcomes and assessments  

In the ATACH-2 trial [8,9], mRS score (an ordinal hierarchical scale of disability or death) was assessed after discharge by a blinded inves­ tigator via telephone at 30 days and during an in-person clinical eval­ uation at 90 days. Assessments were conducted by qualified site investigators who did not participate in the randomization or in-hospital clinical treatment of the patient.  

# 2.3. Statistical analysis  

Descriptive statistics were reported as proportions for categorical or ordinal data, and as mean $\pm$ standard deviations (SDs) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous data.  

Missing baseline data were assumed to be missing at random. Mul­ tiple imputations with predictive-mean matching using all predictor variables and outcomes as independent variables were performed. Effect estimates and test statistics were pooled using Rubin’s rules.  

A multivariable ordinal regression model was constructed using prespecified covariates [10,11], including observed 30-day mRS, with a dependent variable of observed 90-day post randomization mRS score. Covariates entered in the regression model included observed 30-day mRS score (ordinal value), age (years), sex (female vs. male [refer­ ence]), race (White vs. other [reference]), SBP at randomization $\mathrm{(mmHg)}$ , serum creatinine at randomization (mg/dL), baseline GCS score, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after randomization, history of diabetes, cigarette smoking (current, former, unknown vs. never [reference]), use of mechanical ventilation during the hospitalization, previous stroke or transient ischemic attack, history of heart failure, history of atrial fibrillation, history of ischemic heart disease (previous ischemic heart disease, angina pectoris, coronary artery bypass graft, or percutaneous coronary intervention), history of peripheral vascular disease, history of hypercholesterolemia, treatment group (standard vs. intensive [reference]), country of enrolling site (Japan, China, South Korea, Taiwan, Germany vs. United States [refer­ ence]), time (minutes) from symptom onset to randomization, hema­ toma volume (measured manually on computed tomography with the largest area of hemorrhage identified and volume calculated using the ABC/2 equation and reported as $\mathrm{cm}^{3}.$ , hematoma location (thalamus, lobar vs. basal ganglia [reference]), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH; present vs. absent [reference]), total hospital length of stay (LOS; days), and discharge destination (home, still in hospital vs. not home [refer­ ence]). The impact of covariates on the odds of having a higher/worse observed 90-day ordinal mRS score was reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs). Wald statistics were used to construct corresponding $95~\%$ con­ fidence intervals (CIs) for ORs, with a higher Wald statistic suggesting a stronger association between a covariate and observed 90-day mRS. The coefficient of determination $(\mathtt{R}^{2})$ , which depicts the proportion of vari­ ation in the dependent variable (90-day ordinal mRS) that is predicted by covariates within the regression model, was reported. Sensitivity analysis was also conducted to estimate the performance of a more parsimonious model. Following multiple imputation, variable selection was performed using a stacked adaptive elastic net method within the ‘miselect’ package (version 0.9.1)[12]. Variables remaining were then included in the multivariable ordinal regression model as before.  

Following multivariable ordinal regression, utility-weighted mRS scores were calculated. This was done to both simplify the interpretation of changes, or shifts, in ordinal mRS scores from 30 to 90 days, and to account for patient preference (perceived quality of life) for a given mRS health state [6,7,13]. To do so, mean EuroQol-5 Dimension-3 Level (EQ5D-3L) health utility scores calculated using the US scoring algorithm [14] were determined for each mRS score category using surveys administered as part of the ATACH-2 trial at 90 days. The EQ-5D-3L has two components, the EQ-5D descriptive system and the EQ visual analogue scale. It involves 5 dimensions including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression each of which has 3 levels (no problems, some problems, and extreme prob­ lems). EQ-5D-3L was not administered in the ATACH-2 trial at 30 days. Patients’ predicted and observed 90-day ordinal mRS scores were assigned the corresponding mean EQ-5D-3L score, and absolute differ­ ences between covariate-adjusted, 90-day utility–weighted mRS scores (henceforth referred to as predicted 90-day utility–weighted mRS scores) and observed 90-day health utility–weighted mRS scores were calculated on a linear scale. A distribution-based approach, using 0.5 of the SD of the total population’s EQ-5D-3L scores, was utilized to esti­ mate the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) in utilityweighted mRS for the studied population [15].  

Multivariable ordinal regression and subsequent adjusted mean ab­ solute difference in utility-weighted mRS between predicted and observed 90-day values were calculated using the IBM® SPSS $^\mathrm{\textregistered}$ Statistics (version 27.0) software.  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Patients  

Of the 1000 patients enrolled in ATACH-2, 898 $(90\%)$ patients met the inclusion criteria for this analysis (Fig. 1) and their characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median (IQR) age was 61 (52–70) years, $62\%$ of patients were male, and $25~\%$ were White. At baseline, the majority $(81\%)$ of patients had hypertension, $15\%$ had a prior stroke or transient ischemic attack, $25~\%$ had hyperlipidemia, $18~\%$ had type 2 diabetes, and $3\%$ had atrial fibrillation. The median (IQR) NIHSS score at baseline was 10 (5–14), and the median (IQR) GCS score at baseline was 15 (13–15). The median (IQR) hematoma volume was 9.7 $(4.5{-}18.9)\mathrm{mL}$ , with the most common hematoma location being the basal ganglia $(56\%)$ , followed by the thalamus $(33\%)$ and lobar $(11\%)$ .

The distribution of 30- by 90-day ordinal mRS scores prior to adjustment are provided in Fig. 2. While many patients $(48\%)$ had the same mRS score at both 30 and 90 days, those who did not have the same score generally experienced improvement $(43\%)$ , typically having a lower mRS score by 1 to 2 points at 90 days compared with their 30-day score. The remaining $8\%$ of patients had higher mRS scores at 90 days compared to 30 days, generally by 1 point.  

# 3.2. Multivariable ordinal regression model for predicting 90-day mRS score  

The multivariable ordinal regression model had an $\mathrm{R}^{2}=0.74$ . The variables which most predicted 90-day ordinal mRS score were observed 30-day mRS score $(\chi^{2}=302.9$ ; $p<0.0001)$ , followed by the NIHSS score at $24\ln{(\chi^{2}=44.0;p<0.0001)}$ , age $(\chi^{2}=27.9;p<0.0001)$ , country of enrollment $(\chi^{2}=22.5$ ; $p=0.0004)$ , and total hospital LOS $(\chi^{2}=16.3;p$ $=0.001$ ; Fig. 3). When looking at the mean predicted 90-day mRS score with the model by country of enrollment, lower predicted scores were seen in Taiwan, China, and Japan compared with the United States, South Korea, and Germany (eFigure 1); However, most mean scores were in the 3–4 range regardless of country of enrollment. Upon sensi­ tivity analysis, penalized regression using elastic net maintained 30-day mRS score, age, and NIHSS score in the model. Performance was slightly reduced compared to the full model, with an $\mathrm{R}^{2}=0.71$ .  

# 3.3. EQ-5D-3L scores by mRS score category and utility-weighted mRS scores  

The mean $\pm$ SD EQ-5D-3L for the ATACH-2 population at 90 days was $0.67\pm0.26$ . The mean EQ-5D-3L values at each observed ordinal mRS score at 90 days are presented in Table 2. The largest difference in utility score between patients at each mRS level occurred in patients with mRS scores of 4 versus 5 (0.29 points) and 3 versus 4 (0.21 points).  

After substituting predicted and observed 90-day mRS scores with their corresponding mean EQ-5D-3L score, the mean $\pm\mathrm{\Omega}S\mathrm{D}$ absolute difference in health utility-weighted mRS score between the predicted and observed 90-day time points was $0.006\pm\:0.13$ . This value was below the calculated MCID of $(0.26^{*}0.5)=0.13$ .  

# 4. Discussion  

In this post hoc analysis of data from the ATACH-2 trial [8,9], almost all patients either maintained their functional status from 30 to 90 days or experienced slight improvements. The factor most associated with ordinal 90-day mRS score after acute intracerebral hemorrhage was observed 30-day mRS score, followed by NIHSS score, age, country of enrollment, and length of hospital stay. The mRS score is a clinician-reported outcome that may not have optimal interobserver variability, and this may have had an impact on our analysis. Three site-comparison interobserver variability studies designed to simulate multicenter stroke trials (e.g., ATACH-2) have re­ ported unweighted $\kappa$ statistic values as low as 0.25 [18,19]. In a study performed by Quinn and colleagues [20], when assessors were asked to assign mRS categories to patients deemed to have an mRS score of 3 (i.e., a score of 3 was designated the “correct answer”), they were nearly evenly split between assigning scores of 2 and 3. This data suggests that patients with mid-range scores are more likely to contribute to inter­ observer variability. Our study found a parallel trend of a lesser likeli­ hood of exact agreement between 30- and 90-day observed mRS in patients with 30-day mRS scores of 2 or 3, indicating either that patients moved between these levels more frequently compared with the more extreme levels and/or that it was more difficult for the clinician raters to differentiate between these levels compared with the more extreme levels (i.e., mRS 0 vs. 1 or 5 vs. 6). Importantly, EQ-5D-3L data from ATACH-2 suggests that the difference in health utility values for patients with an mRS of 2 versus 3 is relatively small (difference $=0.13$ points) compared with other single mRS category steps (4 vs $5=0.29$ -point difference). This may, in part, explain the small difference in utilityweighted mRS scores seen between time points in our analysis. Impor­ tantly, despite the interobserver variability and different trajectories seen between the different mRS categories, the ATACH-2 data suggest that mRS is generally maintained or improved between 30 and 90 days, and that 90-day mRS can be reliably predicted by 30-day mRS.  

There are several limitations of our analysis to consider. The post hoc nature of this study limited our analysis of candidate predictors to baseline variables available from the ATACH-2 trial [7,8]. For example, anticoagulation was used as an exclusion criterion in ATACH-2, so the association between 30- and 90-day mRS scores in patients who were anticoagulated at the time of intracerebral hemorrhage could not be addressed. Furthermore, post-stroke recovery may be impacted by a variety of factors. Prior studies suggest that admission to a rehabilitation ward directly from the hospital is associated with improved post-stroke functional state; married marital status and greater family care capacity are associated with increased efficacy of post-stroke physiotherapy [21]; and pre-stroke unemployment is associated with greater post-stroke cognitive impairment [22]. Unfortunately, there were insufficient data in ATACH-2 to enable adjustment of these covariates in our regression models [8,9]. However, given the high $\mathtt{R}^{2}$ value observed for the present study’s multivariable ordinal regression model, it is unlikely that the above-mentioned covariates, even taken together, could be as predictive of observed 90-day mRS as the 30-day mRS value. We also could not adjust for in-hospital events and factors. Whether the model applies to that timeframe, as well as beyond 90-days requires further study [23]. Lastly, the results of the present study should not be generalized to all patients with hemorrhagic stroke, as only patients with intracerebral hemorrhage were included in the ATACH-2 trial [8,9]. It is also unclear if similar results would be seen in an RCT population if the intervention itself had resulted in greater changes to 90-day mRS values compared to 30-day mRS.  

# 5. Conclusion  

In conclusion, 30-day mRS score was a strong predictor of 90-day mRS score in a low-moderate severity acute ICH cohort. Patients generally either maintained the same mRS score between 30 and 90 days or experienced improvement of 1 to $2\mathrm{mRS}$ points by 90 days. The small absolute mean difference in utility-weighted mRS between predicted and observed 90-day values suggests utility-weighted 30-day mRS may be a reasonable proxy for 90-day values among patients with acute intracerebral hemorrhage.
