# Effect of Baseline Antihypertensive Treatments on Stroke Severity and Outcomes in the BP TARGET Trial  

Benjamin Maïer , MD, MSc; Benjamin Gory , MD, PhD; Bertrand Lapergue , MD, PhD; Igor Sibon , MD, PhD; Sebastien Richard , MD, PhD; Maeva Kyheng, BST; Julien Labreuche , BST; Jean-Philippe Desilles , MD, PhD; Raphael Blanc , MD; Michel Piotin , MD, PhD; Mikael Mazighi , MD, PhD\*; Jean-Michel Halimi , MD, PhD\*; for the BP TARGET Investigators†  

BACKGROUND: Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients with a history of hypertension experience worse outcomes, which may be explained by a deleterious impact of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) overactivation. We sought to investigate whether prestroke antihypertensive treatments (AHT) influenced baseline stroke severity and neurological outcomes, in patients with AIS successfully treated by endovascular therapy.  

METHODS: We performed a post hoc analysis of the BP TARGET trial (Blood Pressure Target in Acute Stroke to Reduce Hemorrhage After Endovascular Therapy) and included hypertensive patients with available data regarding AHT at admission, categorized as RAS inhibitors (ACE [angiotensin-converting enzyme] inhibitors, ARBs [angiotensin 2 receptor blockers], and $\upbeta$ -blockers) and nonRAS inhibitors (calcium channel blockers and diuretics). Associations of each AHT with National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at baseline were investigated in linear mixed model adjusted for the number of treatments and center. Associations of each AHT with 24-hour NIHSS change, intracranial hemorrhage were performed using linear mixed model adjusted for baseline NIHSS, the number of treatments, center, age, and sex and adjusted for age, sex, diabetes, and current smoking for favorable outcome. All analyses were performed on cases-available data regarding the low number of missing data.  

RESULTS: Overall, 203 patients with at least one AHT were included. Patients under non-RAS inhibitor treatments had a higher NIHSS score at baseline (adjusted mean difference $\scriptstyle=3.28$ $[95\%$ CI, 1.33–5.22]; $P=0.001$ ). Conversely, patients under RAS inhibitor treatments had a lower baseline NIHSS score (adjusted mean differenc $\scriptstyle=-2.81$ $[95\%$ CI, $-5.37$ to $-0.25]$ ; $P\mathrm{=}0.031$ ). Intracranial hemorrhage occurrence was significantly more frequent in patients under non-RAS inhibitor treatments (adjusted odds ratio of 2.48 $[95\%$ CI, 1.12–5.47]; $P_{=0.025}^{}$ ). Conversely, the use of RAS inhibitor treatments before AIS was not associated with higher odds of radiographic intracranial hemorrhage. Patients with non-RAS inhibitor treatments had less improvement of NIHSS at 24 hours compared with patients without (adjusted mean difference, 2.83 $[95\%$ CI, −0.16 to 5.81]; $P_{=0.063}^{\phantom{\dagger}}$ ). Baseline RAS inhibitor or noninhibitor treatments were not associated with favorable outcome.  

CONCLUSIONS: We showed an opposite effect of baseline AHT, based on their effect on the RAS. Patients treated with RAS inhibitor agents before AIS exhibited less severe AIS compared with patients under non-RAS inhibitor treatments, developed less intracranial hemorrhage at 24 hours and had a trend toward better NIHSS score at 24 hours.

ypertension is the first modifiable risk factor, affecting nearly $50\%$ of the US population,1 and is strongly, independently, and linearly associated with the risk of stroke.2,3 Hypertension is also extremely frequent within the first hours of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and has been described by several seminal works to be strongly associated with intracranial hemorrhage $\mathrm{(|CH)^{4,5}}$ or worse outcomes.2,6 Interestingly, patients with AIS with a known history of hypertension experience worse outcomes.3 Some hypotheses have been raised to explain this result, such as the rightward shift of the autoregulation curve due to the chronic consequence of hypertension, putting these patients at risk of infarct growth in case of blood pressure (BP) variability. However, some recent studies also point towards the inflammatory consequence of long-term hypertension on the brain parenchyma, especially after AIS.3,7 Several preclinical studies have indeed highlighted the deleterious impact of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) overactivation, especially after AIS, mainly mediated by the angiotensin receptor AT1, leading to an upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines and worse outcomes.8–10 However, blocking the RAS before and after AIS have shown promising results in the preclinical setting, potentially through an activation of the angiotensin receptors AT2,11–14 which are overexpressed in cerebral ischemia.15 The relevance of this intriguing duality of the RAS in AIS  

has been evaluated in the clinical setting but with conflicting results. Most observational studies assessed the effect of prestroke use of ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors on baseline severity and short-term mortality after AIS.16–20 Conversely, a nationwide population-based cohort study (100 043 patients) evaluated the impact of prestroke use of $\upbeta$ -blockers (BBs) or calcium channel blockers (CCB) with nonusers and did not find an association with 30-day mortality.21 However, these studies did not specifically compare the effect of prestroke RAS inhibitor treatments such as ACE inhibitor, ARBs (angiotensin 2 receptor blockers), or BB versus treatments with either no effect on RAS or activating RAS (non-RAS inhibitor treatments), such as diuretics and CCB. In addition, these studies did not assess conventional end points in the acute phase (ie, ICH, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score at 24 hours) and concerned heterogeneous AIS cohorts, with different stroke causes (small vessel disease and large vessel occlusions [LVOs]) with or without reperfusion therapies, limiting the overall relevance of the results.22–24  

In this context, we sought to investigate whether prestroke antihypertensive treatments (AHT) categorized as RAS inhibitors or non-RAS inhibitors influenced baseline stroke severity and neurological outcomes, in a homogeneous cohort of patients with AIS due to an anterior LVO and successfully treated by endovascular therapy (EVT).  

# METHODS  

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author, upon reasonable request.  

# Study Population  

We performed a post hoc analysis of the BP TARGET trial (Blood Pressure Target in Acute Stroke to Reduce Hemorrhage After EVT).25 Briefly, BP TARGET was a randomized, multicenter, open-label, blind-assessed clinical trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of an intensive BP lowering strategy (systolic BP target between 100 and $129\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg})$ versus a standard systolic BP target $(130{-}185\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg})$ after successful EVT in patients with AIS consecutive to an anterior LVO.25 Eligible individuals were adults $(\geq18$ years old) with an AIS consecutive to an anterior circulation LVO (intracranial internal carotid artery and proximal middle cerebral artery occlusions) with successful reperfusion by EVT (defined as modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction score 2b or 3). Key exclusion criteria included hemorrhagic complications during EVT, spontaneous systolic BP decrease to $<130\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ after reperfusion, severe, or fatal comorbidities that would have rendered the procedure unlikely to benefit the individual or a preexisting stroke disability defined by a modified Rankin Scale score ${>}2$ and known pregnancy.25  

The study protocol and consent forms were approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud Méditerranée IV (N°ID-RCB: 2016-AO1884-47, March 14, 2017).  

To specifically address the effect of RAS inhibitor versus non-RAS inhibitor treatments on baseline severity and neurological outcomes, we included patients with a known history of hypertension before randomization, with available data about AHTs before AIS. As the present study is observational, the adherence to the STROBE criteria was enforced.26  

# Data Collection  

Baseline characteristics and clinical information of each patient were retrieved from the BP TARGET database and included age, sex, number of AHTs, history of diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking status, history of previous stroke, baseline antithrombotic medications, baseline systolic BP, diastolic BP, and NIHSS, treatment with intravenous thrombolysis and the final modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction score (2b or 3).  

# AHTs Before AIS  

Baseline AHTs included CCB, BB, thiazide diuretics, ACE inhibitor, and ARB. We categorized these treatments as RAS inhibitors (ACE inhibitor, ARB, and BB) and non-RAS inhibitors (CCB and diuretics), as previously published.24,27  

# Baseline Severity, Short-, and Long-Term Outcomes  

We first assessed the effect of baseline AHTs on AIS severity. AIS severity was evaluated with baseline NIHSS and the ischemic core volume on the first neuroimaging performed before EVT. Admission diffusion magnetic resonance imaging were reprocessed with the RAPID software to define the ischemic core. We further evaluated the effect of baseline AHTs on neurological outcomes. To specifically capture the effect of the baseline AHTs, which are usually stopped or modified between the acute and long-term management of AIS, we first defined short-term end points and chose 24-hour NIHSS change and the rate of radiographic ICHs. ICHs were assessed on brain computed tomography within 24 to 36 hours after reperfusion. Although parenchymal hemorrhage types 1 and 2 are commonly associated with unfavorable outcomes at 3 months, hemorrhagic infarction types 1 and 2 were also considered in the BP TARGET trial, as this study evaluated all injuries suggestive of reperfusion lesions.25 As several studies have highlighted the association of mild, asymptomatic (hemorrhagic infarction types 1 and 2) ICH after successful EVT with worse outcomes, all subgroups of ICH were thus considered.28–31 Computed tomography scans were forwarded to the core image analysis center, and senior neuroradiologists, who were unaware of the treatment assignments, clinical findings, and time of image acquisition, centrally determined the presence of ICH.  

Long-term outcomes consisted of favorable functional outcomes, defined as a modified Rankin Scale score between 0 and 2 at 3 months, assessed by neurologists or research nurses blinded to study arm, in telephone contact and in-person clinical evaluation.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean (SD), or median (interquartile range) for non-normal distribution.  

Normality of distributions was assessed graphically and by using the Shapiro-Wilk test.  

Associations of NIHSS score and volume at baseline with AHTs were investigated in linear mixed model adjusted for the number of treatment (1 or more) and center (including as a random effect). Associations between baseline volume and AHTs were tested using the Mann-Whitney $U$ test, as the normality of residuals for the volume was not established despite log-transformation.  

Associations of each AHT with ICH and favorable outcome were firstly investigated using generalized linear mixed models (with binomial distribution and logit link function) adjusted for the number of treatment (1 or more) as an assessment of the severity of chronic hypertension, and the center (including as random effect). We further assessed the associations after additional adjustment on age, sex, and NIHSS score at baseline for ICH, and on age, sex, diabetes, and current smoking for favorable outcome. Odds ratio (OR) per each AHT were derived from generalized linear mixed models as effect sizes.  

Associations of each AHT with 24-hour NIHSS change were performed using linear mixed model adjusted for baseline NIHSS, the number of treatment (1 or more), and center (including as random effect). Additional adjustments were done on age and sex. Adjusted mean differences were derived from linear mixed model as effects sizes for AHTs. All analyses were performed on cases-available data regarding the low number of missing data.  

Statistical testing was done at the 2-tailed α level of 0.05. Data were analyzed using the SAS software package, release 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).  

# RESULTS  

# Population and Participating Centers  

A total of 324 patients were randomized in the BP TARGET trial and 203 patients with at least one AHT were included in the present study. Among them, $66\%$ $\left(\mathsf{n}{=}134\right.$ ) had more than one treatment, 106 patients $(52.2\%)$ ) had at least one BB, 126 $(62.1\%)$ had at least one ARB or ACE inhibitor, 68 $(33.5\%)$ at least one CCB, and 79 $(38.9\%)$ patients had at least one thiazide diuretic (Figure S1).  

Main characteristics of patients, treatment and outcomes of the study sample are described in Table  1, overall and according to each AHT. Overall, mean age was 78.6 $(\pm11.6)$ years, and 86 $(42.4\%)$ patients were men. At admission, median NIHSS score was 17 (interquartile range, 13–20) and 102 patients $(50.2\%)$ received IV tPA (intravenous tissue-type plasminogen activator). The mean 24-hour NIHSS score change from baseline was -4.0 $(\pm8.5)$ and 86 patients $(43.7\%)$ had a radiographic ICH at 24 hours. Favorable outcome (modified Rankin Scale score 0–2) at 3 months concerned 76 $(43.7\%)$ patients. Patients treated with CCB and diuretics before AIS had a mean age of $81.1\pm10.6$ and $81.1\pm9.1$ , respectively. In contrast, patients under BB and ACE inhibitor or ARB had a mean age of $76.6\pm10.1$ and $76.9{\pm}12.1$ , respectively. As for sex, BB and diuretics seemed to be more prescribed in women $(61.3\%$ and $64.6\%$ , respectively), while CCB and ACE inhibitor or ARB seemed to be well balanced between the 2 sexes (Table  1). Of note, patients under CCB were less treated with thrombolysis.

# Baseline AHTs and 24-Hour NIHSS Change  

Patients under CCB had higher odds of neurological deterioration at 24 hours, compared with patients without (adjusted mean difference of 3.37 $[95\%$ CI, 0.72–6.02]; $\widetilde{\calP{=}}0.0$ 13; Table 4). We also found opposite, but nonsignificant associations between RAS inhibitor and nonRAS inhibitor treatments with 24-hour NIHSS. Patients with non-RAS inhibitor treatments had less improvement of NIHSS at 24 hours compared with patients without (adjusted mean difference, 2.83 $[95\%$ CI, $-0.16$ to 5.81]; $_{\mathsf{P=0.063}}$ ). Patients with RAS inhibitor treatments had better improvement of NIHSS at 24 hours compared with patients without (adjusted mean difference, $-2.21$ $[95\%$ CI, $-6.16$ to 1.74]; $P{=}0.27$ ). Results were unchanged for patients under ACE inhibitor or ARB versus CCB or diuretics using the same multivariate model.  

# Baseline AHTs and Favorable Outcome  

As shown in Table 5, patients treated with at least one ARB or ACE inhibitor had increased odds of favorable outcome at 3 months compared with patients without (OR, 2.09 $[95\%$ CI, 1.06–4.13]; $\mathsf{P{=}0.034,}$ ), whereas patients treated with at least one CCB had lower odds of favorable outcome (OR, 0.40 $[95\%$ CI, $0.20\substack{-0.81}]$ ; $\widetilde{P{=}0.011}$ ). However, these associations did not remain significant after adjustments (Table  5), except for patients treated with at least one CCB (OR, 0.42 $[95\%$ CI, 0.18–0.98]). Baseline RAS inhibitor or non-RAS inhibitor treatments were not associated with favorable outcome. Results were unchanged for patients under ACE inhibitor or ARB versus CCB or diuretics using the same multivariate model.  

# DISCUSSION  

In this post hoc analysis of the BP TARGET trial, we showed that baseline AIS severity and early AIS outcomes differed according to baseline AHTs. Patients treated with RAS inhibitor agents before AIS exhibited less severe AIS and less ICH at 24 hours. Second, patients under RAS inhibitor treatments had a trend toward better NIHSS score at 24 hours. Finally, there was no association between baseline AHT and 3-month functional outcome.  

Hypertension is highly prevalent worldwide and is linearly associated with the risk of stroke. Many pharmacological agents are currently available for the treatment of hypertension, and it is, therefore, crucial to evaluate their effects on baseline AIS severity and short- or longterm outcomes. ACE inhibitor and ARB inhibit the RAS through inhibition of angiotensin 2 synthesis and antagonism of angiotensin 2 in the angiotensin AT1 receptor, respectively. As BB effectively suppress renin secretion through the blockade of the $\beta-1$ adrenoreceptor in the juxta-glomerular apparatus,27 we categorized ACE inhibitor, ARB, and BB as RAS inhibitors. However, as several preclinical and clinical studies have shown either no effect or an increase in plasma renin activity,32 CCBs were classified as non-RAS inhibitors. Diuretics increase renin secretion due to reduced sodium delivery to the macula densa and was, therefore, also classified as a non-RAS inhibitor.27  

Patients treated with RAS inhibitor agents before AIS exhibited less ICH at 24 hours. Previous studies rarely considered ICH as a major outcome. Recently, Gilliot et $a|^{23}$ found that on-going treatment with ACE inhibitor or ARBs did not influence hemorrhagic transformation occurrence in a cohort of patients solely treated with intravenous thrombolysis. Of note, in the BP TARGET trial, hemorrhagic transformations consisted of all ICH after EVT, given their deleterious impact on long-term outcomes. Indeed, recent data have shed light on the deleterious effect of asymptomatic hemorrhagic transformations (ie, hemorrhagic infarction types 1 and 2) and the rational for considering all intraparenchymal hemorrhages as a major end point has recently been underlined.31 Interestingly, this result could also be explained by the deleterious effect of angiotensin II. Angiotensin II disrupts the blood-brain barrier,33 which is a major known predictive factor of hemorrhagic transformation. In addition, the overaction of the RAS has been described to increase levels of MMP-9 (matrix metalloproteinase-9) while decreasing levels of TIMP-1 (tissue inhibitor of matrix metalloproteinase-1).34 Increased levels of MMP-9 are associated with ICH. Therefore, baseline AHTs could influence the imbalance between MMP-9 and TIMP-1 levels, especially in patients treated with non-RAS inhibitor agents. An alternative hypothesis could be related to the platelet inhibitory function of CCB. Several studies have indeed shown this inhibitory effect in each of the 3 major CCB, leading to increased risks of gastrointestinal bleedings.35,36 These effects on platelet inhibition could also explain the higher odds of ICH in patients treated with CCB. These hypotheses are plausible given the early assessment of ICH (24 hours), and the possible remaining effect of these treatments within this timeframe.

To move forward from this work, the crucial question at hand is to discern by which specific mechanisms prestroke AHTs may have an impact on short-term outcomes. Different avenues could be explored, such as an assessment of the early expression of thromboinflammation biomarkers or a deep immune profiling according to baseline AHTs, to definitively provide an answer about the effect of RAS inhibitor agents on inflammation. From a public health point of view, these issues are crucial given the tremendous burden of hypertension worldwide. Should these hypotheses be confirmed, further studies could target patients considered at high risk of stroke to optimize their AHTs, in favor of RAS inhibitor agents.  

This study has several strengths. Previous studies were mainly retrospective, concerned heterogenous patients (with and without LVO), solely treated with intravenous thrombolysis, and focused their analysis on short-term mortality. In the present study, we provide for the first-time baseline, short- and 3-month outcomes in a homogeneous cohort of patients with AIS due to an LVO and treated with EVT. Second, this report comes from a randomized, controlled, multicenter trial, with a central assessment of ICH, ensuring a homogeneous evaluation of hemorrhagic transformations. This point is important, as several biases limited previous studies on the subject. Several limitations must be acknowledged. The main limitation is the observational design, potentially leading to confounding biases in the analysis. Second, our study sample was moderate and larger studies or individual patient data meta-analysis will be required to fully understand the effect of baseline AHT on short- and longterm outcomes. In addition, due to combinations in the antihypertensive regimen before AIS, direct comparisons between each AHT could not be performed (Table  1). Notably, as age and sex seemed to differ according to the antihypertensive regimen (older patients under CCB or diuretics, more women under BB or diuretics), these parameters were included in the multivariate analyses. Third, since patients were included in an emergency setting in the BP TARGET trial, the duration of hypertension, or the level and quality of BP control before AIS under the different antihypertensive regimens could not be determined and considered in the analyses. This point is of importance as long-term and uncontrolled hypertension may be associated with short-term outcomes after AIS, independently of prestroke antihypertensive regimen. Finally, as this study assessed patients with LVO who were successfully treated with EVT, the present results do not apply to all patients with AIS.

# CONCLUSIONS  

In this post hoc analysis of the BP TARGET trial, we found an opposite effect of baseline AHTs on baseline, short- and 3-month outcomes, with patients priorly treated with non-RAS inhibitor agents exhibiting more severe AIS, more ICH and a trend toward less neurological improvements after EVT compared with patients treated with RAS inhibitor agents.
