Stroke genetics research has benefited in the last decade from rapid advances in genotyping technology and large international collaborations, leading to exponential progress in the identification of polymorphisms that influence cerebrovascular risk. The field of pharmacogenomics is currently well positioned to inform crucial dilemmas in stroke management, such as the decision to resume anticoagulation for patients with atrial fibrillation following intracerebral hemorrhage. In parallel with the clinical trial ASPIRE (Anticoagulation for Stroke Prevention and Recovery after Intracerebral Hemorrhage), genetic analyses are anticipated to yield valuable insight into patient risk stratification. Here, we explore how ASPIRE presents an opportunity to align clinical trial design with modern population genetics, bringing us closer to precision medicine strategies for the management of stroke.  

Rapidly increasing discoveries in the field of population genetics promise to transform the ways we approach disease risk stratification and drug design. Driven by high-throughput genotyping technologies, innovative analytical methods and enlarging collaborative research networks, numerous genetic discoveries in the last decade have accelerated the advent of precision medicine based on genetic information, in which clinical approaches are tailored to an individual’s genetic characteristics. This strategy is particularly well suited to clinical scenarios with complex risk-benefit ratios, in which both the intervention and natural history of disease carry significant risks. The decision to anticoagulate patients with atrial fibrillation after an intracerebral hemorrhage is one such scenario, as the risk of cardioembolic events, which can be mitigated by anticoagulant therapy, must be weighed against the risk of recurrent hemorrhage.  

Before the first large genome-wide association study was published in 2007 [1], population genetics focused on candidate-gene studies of rare mutations that cause mendelian disorders. Since then, hypothesis-free interrogation of the human genome has identified thousands of single nucleotide polymorphisms associated with risk of numerous complex diseases. We are now aware of 87,013 unique polymorphism-to-trait associations that in turn deepen our understanding of pathophysiology and allow identification of novel therapeutic targets [2]. Much of the contemporary progress in stroke genetics is owed to the creation of the International Stroke Genetics Consortium, a collaborative network of over 100 investigators from around the world who have come together to share ideas and data, in order to achieve the necessary sample sizes for proper statistical evaluation of millions of genetic variants. To date, 36 stroke risk loci have been identified [3,4]. The growing numbers of known disease susceptibility loci further allow the creation of polygenic risk scores, which can heighten our ability to stratify risk and determine which individuals will benefit most from targeted interventions.  

In pharmacogenomics, research aimed at understanding how genetic variation modifies medication efficacy has received increasing attention. For example, secondary analyses of cardiovascular trials have shown that genetic risk scores can be used to identify individuals with higher coronary risk and higher clinical benefit from statin therapy [5] and PCSK9 inhibitors [6,7]. Pharmacogenomic substudies of the Clopidogrel in High-Risk Patients with Acute Nondisabling Cerebrovascular Events (CHANCE) trial have identified two genes, CPY2C19 and ABCB1, that modify the beneficial effects of combining clopidogrel with aspirin for secondary stroke prevention [8,9]. There is enormous potential for genetic studies to further illuminate similar stroke risk modifiers and individual responses to therapeutic interventions, eventually leading to the direct clinical application of such genetic information.  

One of the critical dilemmas in stroke management today is the decision to prescribe oral anticoagulation following spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). Patients with atrial fibrillation comprise an estimated $20\%$ of ICH survivors and remain at substantial risk for cardioembolic events, including ischemic stroke, following ICH [10]. At the same time, oral anticoagulation is associated with increased ICH risk, with $10{-}15\%$ of ICH cases occurring in the setting of anticoagulation aimed at preventing cardioembolic stroke [11]. Patients and clinicians are therefore faced with balancing the risk of recurrent bleeding against protection from thromboembolic events. Although anticoagulation in patients with atrial fibrillation and a $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -VASc score ${\geq}2$ is a class IA recommendation from the American Heart Association [12], resumption of oral anticoagulation is infrequently pursued following spontaneous ICH [13]. Current guidelines acknowledge that the optimal timing for resumption of anticoagulation is uncertain, as is the usefulness of the newer direct anticoagulant drugs such as dabigatran, rivaroxaban or apixaban [14], which have improved safety profiles but whose studies excluded ICH patients.  

The ASPIRE trial is a randomized, double-blinded, Phase III clinical trial sponsored by the National Institutes of Health designed to address the uncertainties surrounding resumption of oral anticoagulation after ICH. It will test the efficacy and safety of apixaban, compared with aspirin, for reducing recurrent stroke or death in patients with recent ICH and high-risk nonvalvular atrial fibrillation (NCT03907046). Over 3.5 years, ASPIRE will aim to enroll 700 participants with diagnosed atrial fibrillation and radiographically documented ICH occurring in 14–120 days prior to randomization. Exclusion criteria include lobar ICH, indications for anticoagulation other than atrial fibrillation, indications for antiplatelet use and chronic kidney disease. All patients will have at least 1 year of follow-up and additional imaging and blood will be collected and banked where possible for use in ancillary studies and secondary analyses.  

The ASPIRE trial constitutes a unique opportunity to deploy a genetics-based precision medicine approach to improve the selection of patients that will benefit from anticoagulation. Genetic variation plays an important role in all aspects of this clinical question, including risk and severity of atrial fibrillation, risk and severity of ICH and individual metabolism of aspirin and apixaban. All of these genetic determinants could be combined in a polygenic risk score in synergy with point estimates for degree and magnitude of risk and degree and magnitude of benefit. Such an approach would help us astutely identify patients with the highest risk of cardioembolic recurrence, lower risk of ICH recurrence and maximal response to the evaluated medications. This precision medicine approach could be further enhanced by neuroimaging information related to the severity of cerebral small vessel disease, as these imaging makers strongly associate with the risk of recurrent ICH. Furthermore, the utilization of artificial intelligence and machine learning tools to discerningly select and weigh components of such an approach could further contribute to the production of a tool with real impact in everyday clinical practice.  

Enfolded in the execution of the ASPIRE trial is an opportunity to design and test a genetics-based precision medicine strategy to identify patients whose genetic risk profiles indicate the most potential to derive clinical benefit from anticoagulant resumption. Information from genetic risk stratification techniques can be directly linked to clinical outcomes observed in the trial. Thus, the combination of clinical and genetic research may powerfully shape individualized strategies for the prevention of stroke in patients with atrial fibrillation and recent ICH.
