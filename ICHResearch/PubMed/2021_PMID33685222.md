# Intracerebral Hemorrhage Incidence, Mortality, and Association With Oral Anticoagulation Use  

A Population Study

BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a devastating form of stroke associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Recent epidemiological data on incidence, mortality, and association with oral anticoagulation are needed.  

METHODS: Retrospective cohort study of adult patients $(\geq18$ years) with ICH in the entire population of Ontario, Canada (April 1, 2009–March 30, 2019). We captured outcome data using linked health administrative databases. The primary outcome was mortality during hospitalization, as well as at 1 year following ICH.  

RESULTS: We included 20 738 patients with ICH. Mean (SD) age was 71.3 (15.1) years, and $52.6\%$ of patients were male. Overall incidence of ICH throughout the study period was $19.1/100000$ person-years and did not markedly change over the study period. In-hospital and 1-year mortality were high $32.4\%$ and $45.4\%,$ , respectively). Mortality at 2 years was $49.5\%$ . Only $14.5\%$ of patients were discharged home independently. Over the study period, both in-hospital and 1-year mortality reduced by $10.4\%$ $(37.5\%$ to $27.1\%,$ $P{<}0.001$ ) and $7.6\%$ $(50.0\%$ to $42.4\%,$ , $P<0.001$ ), respectively. Use of oral anticoagulation was associated with both in-hospital mortality (adjusted odds ratio 1.37 $[95\%$ CI, 1.26–1.49]) and 1-year mortality (hazard ratio, 1.18 $[95\%$ CI, 1.12–1.25]) following ICH.  

CONCLUSIONS: Both short- and long-term mortality have decreased in the past decade. Most survivors from ICH are likely to be discharged to long-term care. Oral anticoagulation is associated with both short- and long-term mortality following ICH. These findings highlight the devastating nature of ICH, but also identify significant improvement in outcomes over time.

# Data Sources and Setting  

We conducted a cohort study using health administrative databases from the entire population of Ontario, Canada (population of 13 million). In Ontario’s single-payer healthcare system, all medically necessary health care services, physician, hospital, and demographic information for residents are recorded in these databases (Tables I and II in the Data Supplement). These data are held and linked at ICES, an independent, nonprofit custodian of health data.18 ICES is funded by an annual grant from the Ontario Ministry of Health and Long-Term Care.  



# Patients—Overall Cohort  

We included adult patients $(\geq18$ years of age) with a diagnosis of ICH in the province of Ontario between April 1, 2009, and March 31, 2019. We used the Discharge Abstract Database within ICES to identify patients with spontaneous, nontraumatic ICH using a diagnostic algorithm based on the Most Responsible Diagnosis coded in the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10-CA) codes (Table III in the Data Supplement). This algorithm was specifically used to avoid misclassification of patients with hemorrhagic transformation from ischemic stroke, for example. We used the recommended ICD-10 codes for diagnosis of ICH, previously published by the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association.19 The identification algorithm used is validated, with a positive predictive value of $92\%$ (CI, $88\%{-}95\%;$ ) and high agreement as measured by the Kappa statistic (0.89).  

For each subject, we identified relevant comorbidities using hospitalization and physician billing before the date of admission. Previous diagnoses of atrial fibrillation/flutter, ischemic stroke, ICH, major hemorrhage, and other comorbidities were identified using previously described methods (Table III in the Data Supplement).20 Where applicable, we used validated algorithms to ascertain comorbidities. All other conditions were based on the presence of any one inpatient hospital diagnostic code or ${}^{\ge2}$ outpatient physician billing codes within a 2-year period, using relevant ICD, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) and ICD-10 codes.  



# OAC Use  

All Ontario citizens have access to universal public healthcare with drug coverage for individuals over the age of 65 years. This database contains highly accurate records of all outpatient prescriptions dispensed to patients ${\ttz}65$ years of age, with an error rate of $<1\%$ .21 We identified OAC prescriptions using methods described previously (Table I in the Data Supplement).22 In our analysis of OAC use, we included patients ${\ttze}66$ years of age, and with hospitalization for ICH between April 1, 2009 and March 31, 2019, to allow one full year from government-funded prescription coverage and complete medical records. Those who had an OAC prescription overlap with the index admission were classified as an OAC user. We also allowed for a $50\%$ grace period ( $1.5\times$ the days supplied) after the end of the prescription to be more inclusive of OAC classification. OAC includes warfarin and the novel direct OAC drugs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, and dabigatran). We also identified the use of previously prescribed antiplatelet agents at admission (ticagrelor, clopidogrel, aggrenox). Acetylsalicylic acid was not included as it is available as an over-the-counter medication without a prescription.  



# Outcomes  

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes were mortality at 3 days, 7 days, 30 days, 1 year, 2 years, and 5 years following ICH. Length of stay for the index ICH hospitalization was reported from the date of hospital admission to the date of discharge or in-hospital death. Discharge disposition was determined using a hierarchical approach (Table IV in the Data Supplement).23,24 Other interventions potentially performed during the index admission, including invasive mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, decompressive craniectomy, and external ventricular drain insertion were determined using the relevant procedural codes (Table II in the Data Supplement) in the Discharge Abstract Database or physician billings from the Ontario Health Insurance Plan.  

Since data on functional outcomes were not available, we evaluated discharge destination among survivors after hospitalization. This includes discharge to a home setting, discharge to a home setting with government-funded home care assistance, discharge to a rehabilitation facility (including long-term hospital facilities), or discharge to a long-term care facility (ie, nursing home or retirement home). We also evaluated home-time following hospital discharge, defined as number of nights in a private residence, and which is known to correlate with common functional scales (such as the modified Rankin Scale).25,26  

We calculated ICH incidence per 100 000 person-years using all Ontario residents alive and with a valid health card number on July 1 of each respective year as the denominator. We compared the rates of males versus females and across age groups using the incidence rate ratio. $95\%$ Wald CIs were computed for comparison. We assumed that virtually all patients with ICH are admitted to hospital, knowing that small numbers of patients may have suffered ICH and died at home or were never admitted to hospital. Furthermore, we counted ICH occurrence, including patients who had previously suffered ICH. Thus, our operational definition of ICH incidence probably very slightly undercounts ICH events and represents both new ICH and potentially recurrent ICH. We additionally evaluated ICH incidence following exclusion of patients with previous ICH.  



# Statistical Analyses  

We conducted all statistical analyses using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC). We present data as mean values, with SD, or medians, with interquartile range, where appropriate. The Student $t$ test (parametric values), Mann-Whitney test (nonparametric values), and $\chi^{2}$ (for categorical values) were performed to determine between-group differences. We used Kaplan-Meier survival curves to describe 1-year survival. To evaluate for statistical significance in trends, we applied the Cochran-Armitage test for linear trend in proportions. To identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality, we performed multivariable logistic regression. Similarly, to determine factors associated with 1-year mortality, we utilized a Cox proportional hazards regression model. In performing model creation, we followed the Prognosis Research Strategy guidelines.27 These guidelines recommend a clinically hypothesis-driven approach for a priori selection of all model variables, as opposed to bivariate association testing methods. Because preadmission medication use was only available for patients ${\ttze}66$ years, we generated 2 sets of models: one for the entire cohort (not including OAC/oral antiplatelet use) and one only for patients ${\ge}66$ years (including OAC/oral antiplatelet use).  

To evaluate the association between OAC and in-hospital mortality, we followed existing recommendations and utilized logistic regression modeling with confounding covariates determined a priori (age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, dementia, chronic kidney disease, previous ICH, previous ischemic stroke, and oral antiplatelet use).28,29 To account for possible unmeasured confounders, we computed the $E$ value, as described previously.30 A large $E$ value implies considerable unmeasured confounding would be needed to explain away the effect estimate. A $P$ value of 0.05 was taken to represent statistical significance.

# DISCUSSION  

Using comprehensive data over an 11-year period from the entire population of Ontario, Canada, we found that incidence of ICH was higher among males, increased with age, and has remained constant over a decade. Mortality was substantial, with an in-hospital mortality rate of $32.4\%$ , a 30-day mortality rate of $34.7\%$ , and a 1-year mortality rate of $45.4\%$ . Both in-hospital and 1-year mortality have substantially declined over the study period. About $20.9\%$ of ICH occurs in the context of OAC use, and OAC use was associated with increased mortality and morbidity.  

Previous work has shown that incidence of ICH varies geographically, with slightly lower rates in Europe than East Asia.3 Overall, our incidence rates were markedly lower than the pooled incidence rates from studies before $2010^{3}$ and may be related to better control of risk factors. As demonstrated previously,3 the incidence of ICH increased with advancing age. This is likely due to a higher prevalence of ICH risk factors in the elderly, including hypertension and amyloid angiopathy, as well as higher prevalence of OAC use.2,31 This growth in the use of OAC may have contributed to the changes in ICH epidemiology that we have shown.17 We also found that incidence of ICH was higher among males than females. This has been reported in other countries, such as Japan and the United States, although the reason for this sex difference is unclear.3

Mortality was markedly high in our cohort, with $32.4\%$ of patients dying in-hospital, and $17.5\%$ dying within 3 days. The large proportion of mortality occurs early (within the first 30–40 days) and then subsides over time. We found that long-term mortality was very high, with $45.4\%$ of patients dying within 1 year of ICH and $49.5\%$ dying within 2 years.  

Although confirming that advanced age, increased comorbidity burden, and preexisting long-term care residency were associated with both short- and longterm mortality, we additionally found that rural residency and lower-income quintiles were also associated with mortality. Similar geographic and socioeconomic trends have been seen previously in ischemic stroke populations32 and may reflect reduced access to primary care for risk-factor management or stroke care for acute management.  

Only 1 in 7 $(14.5\%)$ of our cohort were discharged to an independent setting. Over a third of the cohort were discharged to either a long-term rehabilitation hospital or a long-term care facility (such as a nursing home or retirement home). This loss of independence is further indicated by our measure of home-time.25 In the first year following discharge, patients spent a mean of only 240.5 days in a private residence.  

Previous work before 2010 demonstrated that shortterm mortality had largely remained constant over time.3 Our recent data show that both short- and long-term mortality from ICH have significantly decreased in the most recent decade. In-hospital mortality has declined in absolute magnitude by $10.4\%$ and 1-year mortality by $7.6\%$ . Among survivors to hospital discharge, little change was seen in 1-year mortality, with an absolute decrease of only $0.8\%$ . This suggests that factors during the acute hospitalization may be related to the subsequent reduction in mortality. Although there have been few new therapies for ICH in the past decade,2 stroke care has more recently evolved to be provided in dedicated hospitals and stroke-care units,11 and these units appear to have potential efficacy in the care of patients with ischemic stroke.33,34 Evidence suggests that this benefit is just as notable in ICH populations as ischemic stroke patients.35 This reduction in in-hospital mortality may also be related to avoidance of early withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy, which has previously been noted to be the factor most often associated with mortality after ICH.36 In our cohort, mortality at day 3 following hospitalization had an absolute decrease of $7.0\%$ , which could be consistent with reduction in early withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy.

In the cohort with available prescription data, $20.9\%$ of ICH cases were associated with OAC use. Even when controlling for relevant confounders, OAC use was associated with mortality following ICH, as shown previously.37 This is likely secondary to increased hematoma volumes and hematoma growth, both of which have been associated with OAC use,2 even with the use of reversal agents.15 With the use of OAC growing,16 this is a risk that healthcare providers must continue to consider. Current randomized data suggest that the incidence of intracranial bleeding is lower with direct OAC drugs, as compared to Warfarin,38 and our data demonstrating the devastating nature of OAC-associated ICH should be considered when prescribing.  

The principal strength of our study is that it arises from a complete population. However, we identified patients through the use of ICD codes at the time of admission, which can result in misclassification, although we used the recommended codes provided by the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association.19 The diagnosis at patient admission may not capture all patients with ICH, especially in cases of delay to imaging. The high-level nature of administrative data means that we were unable to evaluate radiographic variables that are prognostic of outcome from ICH, including hematoma size, volume, intraventricular extension, and hematoma expansion.2 We inferred functional outcome from other variables, although home-time is a commonly used surrogate, and corresponds well with traditional functional scales.25,26 Our analysis of OAC-associated outcomes were limited to patients ${\ttz}66$ years of age, as only this population has universal drug coverage in Ontario. However, the large majority of OAC-associated hemorrhage does occur in this age group.2 We were unable to obtain data on acetylsalicylic acid use because it is predominantly obtained without a prescription. Finally, we could not determine the indication for OAC, which has implications in OAC prescribing.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

We found that incidence of ICH has remained constant over time and is higher among male patients and those with advanced age. Both short- and long-term mortality have declined significantly over the past decade. OAC use was a significant predictor of mortality in ICH, highlighting the important impact of appropriate OAC prescribing. Taken together, this study provides recent and novel data on the population-level burden of ICH and highlights trends demonstrating improved mortality over time.
