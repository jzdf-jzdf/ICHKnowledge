# ABSTRACT  

Objective: The purpose of this study was to compare the effect of single burr hole (SBH) versus double burr hole (DBH) drainage on the recurrence rate of chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH).   
Method: Forty-four patients undergoing burr hole craniostomy (BHC) between July 2022 and December 2022 were enrolled in a randomized clinical trial (RCT) comparing SBH to DBH surgeries. The primary endpoint of this study was the recurrence rate of CSDH. Radiological characteristics such as midline shift, thickness, volume, density, type of hematoma, brain atrophy and so on were secondary endpoints.   
Results: Forty-four patients participated in this study. Twenty-two hematomas underwent SBH craniostomy, and the other 22 had DBH craniostomy. The mean age in the SBH and DBH groups were $68.59\pm7.94$ and $69.54\pm{}$ 10.58, respectively. In each group, the proportion of males $\mathrm{\DeltaSBH}{=}16$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{DBH}=15}$ ) was higher than that of females (SBH $\scriptstyle=6$ ; DBH $\scriptstyle=7$ ). The mean surgery time in the SBH group was significantly less than in the DBH group $\left(\mathtt{p}=\right.$ 0.001). However, the two groups had no statistically significant difference in the CSDH recurrence rate $\boldsymbol{\left(\mathtt{p}\right)}=$ 0.312).   
Conclusion: Our findings showed that SBH craniostomy is equally effective as DBH craniostomy at draining he­ matomas and does not increase the recurrence rate. Contrarily, the SBH craniostomy had a shorter surgical duration than the DBH craniostomy. As a result, we recommend SBH surgeries for all patients, with emphasis on the elderly and those with severe comorbidities, as well as in situations with few surgical facilities and a high patient admission rate.  



# 1. Introduction  

Chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) is a common neurological condition caused by blood breakdown products and accumulation be­ tween the dura and the arachnoid membrane [1,10]. Despite being treatable, this disease has a $28\%$ recurrence rate and a $25\%$ neurological prognosis rate [8,46]. CSDH was previously assumed to be caused by recurrent and progressive bleeding after rupture of cortical veins caused by minor trauma [48]. However, it has recently been linked to a complicated pathway that includes angiogenesis, inflammation, recur­ rent microbleeds, and localized coagulation disorders in the subdural area. Nonetheless, developing of this condition does not necessarily require a history of trauma [17,39].  

There is strong evidence that subdural bleeding produced by local angiogenesis, inflammation, coagulation disorders, and active fibrino­ lysis causes hematoma growth [39]. There is a $6.7\%$ risk of CSDH after new bleeding [21]. Therefore, for better overall quality of life of affected patients, appropriate care of the condition and its recurrence is essential. Surgery is often indicated when a symptomatic hematoma or a hema­ toma with a midline shift of more than $5\mathrm{mm}$ or a hematoma thickness of more than one centimeter is encountered [44]. The management of CSDH currently employs four hematoma drainage techniques: burr hole  

craniostomy (BHC); twist drill craniostomy (TDC); endoscopic drainage;   
and craniotomy. However, TDC or BHC are the most popular [5,27].  

One of the major concerns regarding these surgical techniques is that in addition to the fact that TDC has been associated with higher recur­ rence and recommended in high-risk patients, not a candidate for sur­ gery [44], the data about BHC and other surgical interventions remains inconclusive [44]. Although BHC is regularly performed worldwide, there are ongoing debates regarding the number and location of burr holes, irrigation, and drain placement [11]. Notably, the discussion about the number of burr holes is essential because of its association with operation times, complications and even recurrence after surgery [4,41,51]. Here, we describe our experience with introducing a less complicated surgical method to treat CSDH and reduce the recurrence rate in affected patients at a tertiary hospital in Iran. We aimed to evaluate the difference in the effect of using a single burr hole (SBH) or double burr hole (DBH) drainage on the recurrence rate of CSDH.  



# 2. Method  



# 2.1. Patient recruitment  

All patients over 18 years of age with a definite diagnosis of primary CSDH according to brain computer tomography (CT) scan- midline shift> 5 mm, with no history of acute head trauma and with no hyperdense homogenous hematoma on CT scan who referred to our institution between the period July 2022 and December 2022 were eligible for inclusion in this study. However, patients with prior history of CSDH, CSDH limited to one brain lobe, subdural empyema, localized subdural hematoma before and after surgery, history of a ven­ triculoperitoneal shunt, conservatively managed hematoma, and a confounding condition (e.g., pregnancy/breastfeeding) or a disease that could impact postoperative recovery (e.g., coagulation disorders, recent myocardial infarction, severe alcohol or drug addiction, autoimmune diseases, immunodeficiency/ history of untreated hematological cancer, etc.) were excluded. Also, patients who underwent craniotomy before the hematoma drainage, repeat surgery in the first $48\mathrm{~h~}$ due to technical problems of the first surgery, those who died during the 6-month followup period due to non-brain related causes, and those likely to be lost in follow-up were excluded. Fifty-six patients were assessed for eligibility before enrollment (Fig. 1).  

Twelve patients were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, refusal to participate, or other reasons; therefore, 44 patients were enrolled. After informed consent was obtained, patients were prospec­ tively randomized into two groups by computerized random number generation conducted by the research coordinator: SBH group or DBH group. A total of 22 patients were allocated to the SBH group and un­ derwent SBH craniostomy. The remaining 22 patients were allocated to the DBH group and received DBH craniostomy. The group randomiza­ tion was performed based on random blocks of 4, 6 and 8 and was done centrally by the person responsible for randomization with the patient’s knowledge. Given the requirement of the operating surgeon, it was impossible to perform the study with blinded patients and care pro­ viders. However, those who collected data and assessed outcomes were blinded from the patients’ study arm.  



# 2.2. Surgical parameters  

Considering that both groups of patients were candidates for surgery, the patients of both groups were placed in the supine position after anesthesia. The head was tilted to the opposite side of the hematoma. The incision was made medial to the superior temporal line and aligned with the mid-papillary line, and was $3\mathrm{cm}$ long. In the SBH group, only one incision was performed anterior to the coronal suture (Fig. 2). Alternatively, two incisions were performed in the DBH group, with the first incision made anterior to the coronal suture and the second incision posterior to the coronal suture with a distance of $5{-}7\mathrm{mm}$ (Fig. 3). Following the burr hole procedure, the dura was opened. After ensuring complete cauterization of bleeding vessels, irrigation was performed with $\geq3$ liters of warm normal saline (note: when needed, the irrigation was continued until complete hematoma drainage was ensured and only normal saline was observed). Subsequently, a drain was placed in the burr hole in the sub-periosteal area. The drain was kept in place for $^{48\mathrm{~h~}}$ after the surgery. Postoperatively, all patients were kept supine and only allowed head or any movements after $24\mathrm{~h~}$ . Furthermore, all patients received anticoagulant prophylaxis with $40~\mathrm{mg}$ of enoxaparin daily $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after surgery. Additionally, a non-contrast brain CT scan was performed for all patients $^{48\mathrm{~h~}}$ after surgery. Follow-up visits were conducted in the neurosurgery clinic one and 6 months postoperatively.

# 3. Compliance with ethical standards  

Informed consent was obtained from each participant in this study. The review and analysis of patient information were approved by the Ethical Committee of Shariati Hospital-Tehran University of Medical Sciences (ethics code: IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1401.279). This ran­ domized controlled trial was registered with the Iranian Registry of  

Clinical Trial (https://www.irct.ir/trial/66394), and its registration no.   
is IRCT20221016056192N1.  



# 4. Outcome Measurements  

Patient demographic data (age, gender), comorbidities (smoking, alcohol use, diabetes, Hypertension) and history of anticoagulant and antiplatelet use were assessed and recorded at admission. The primary endpoint of this study was recurrence, which was defined as the recur­ rence of clinical symptoms and radiological signs of CSDH within 6 months after the surgery, and requires re-surgery for CSDH. The sec­ ondary endpoint included changes in radiological characteristics such as midline shift (measured at the level of the intraventricular foramen), hematoma thickness (measured in millimeters) and hematoma volume (measured in cubic centimeters). Non-contrast brain CT scans of all patients performed $48\mathrm{~h~}$ after surgery were also recorded. Based on the opinion of an experienced radiologist, the appearance of hematomas was divided into four groups; laminar, layered, homogenous, and trabecular. The hematoma density was also categorized as low, Iso, and mixed. Brain atrophy was classified as non, mild, moderate and severe, and the remaining air was grouped into mild, moderate and severe. Patients’ Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was also recorded. Furthermore, the duration of hospitalization after surgery, the duration of surgery and the infection of the surgical site were compared between the two groups.

# 5. Statistical analyses  

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 22, IBM Corp. New York, USA). The data on the difference in recurrence in the two groups (i.e., SBH and DBH) were collected and analyzed. Also, all the variables with possible distortion of the outcome, including gender, age, hematoma density, anticoagulants/anti-platelet use, diabetes and midline shift among the two groups, were examined separately and then analyzed. Descriptive statistics of the SBH and DBH groups were used to compare all relevant patient characteristics. Continuous data with a normal distribution were statistically tested for group differences using the Independent t-test. Data without normal distribution were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square tests or the Fisher exact test. A p-value $<0.05$ was consid­ ered statistically significant.  



# 6. Results  

Forty-four patients participated in this study. Twenty-two hema­ tomas were drained by the SBH craniostomy, and the other 22 via the DBH craniostomy. The two groups were similar with respect to sex, age, trauma history, anesthesia, and comorbidities (Table 1). Baseline char­ acteristics did not significantly differ between the two groups. Comor­ bidities such as headache, confusion, hypertension, diabetes, memory loss, etc., were equally distributed between the two groups. In each group, the proportion of males was higher than that of females, although there was no significant sex difference between the two groups. The mean age in the SBH and DBH groups was $68.59\pm7.94$ and 69.54 $\pm10.58\$ , respectively. There was no significant difference in the age distribution of the two groups (Table 1).

Regarding the history of trauma, 10 $(45.45\%)$ patients with hema­ toma in the SBH group had a history of head trauma more than two months ago, and 7 patient’s history of trauma was within 2 months of referral. Contrarily, most of the hematoma $(59.09\%)$ treated with DBH craniostomy has a history of trauma below 2 months. There was no correlation between trauma and the side of hematoma $\left(\mathtt{p}=0.8\right)$ , Chisquare test) and preoperative GCS level $(\mathtt{p}=0.32$ , Chi-square test). In the SBH group, 12 $(54.55\%)$ and 10 $45.45\%\$ ) hematomas were located on the right and the left side, respectively. Similarly, in the DBH group, more hematoma was observed on the right $(59.10\%)$ ) compared with the left side $(40.90\%)$ . The distribution of brain atrophy in the two groups before surgery was similar $(\mathbf{p}=1.000)$ . In addition, preoperative GCS was not different between the two groups $(\mathtt{p}=1.000)$ .  

All patients in both groups underwent BHC by the same experienced surgical team, and all patients received their allocated intervention. The mean admission days were $3.77\pm0.87$ and $3.82\pm0.79$ in the SBH and DBH groups, respectively. Coagulative derangement among patients with coagulative disorders was corrected before enrolling in the study. Anticoagulant/antiplatelet medications were discontinued hours before surgery. In the SBH group, 18 $(81.82\%)$ ) patients were operated on under general anesthesia, and 4 $(18.18\%)$ patients under sedation. In the DBH group, 19 $(86.36\%)$ ) patients were operated on under general anesthesia and 3 $(13.64\%)$ patients under sedation (Table 1). There was no dif­ ference in the width of hematoma in the SBH $15.50\pm6.46)$ and DBH $(15.32\pm5.46)$ groups $(\mathbf{p}=0.920)$ .  

The majority of the hematoma width was $20\mathrm{mm}$ or less (SBH; 18 $(81.82\%)$ and DBH; $19(86.36\%)$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.680)$ . However, the mean sur­ gery time was $42.27\pm11.20$ in the SBH group and $54.54\pm11.12$ in the DBH group. Compared to the DBH group, the surgery time was signifi­ cantly less in the SBH group $(\mathtt{p}<0.001)$ . At the end of six months follow-up, one patient in each group had recurrence, with recurrence occurring early in the patient in the SBH group and late in the DBH group. However, there was no statistically significant difference be­ tween the two groups regarding recurrence $(\mathbf{p}=0.312)$ . The relevant details of primary and secondary outcomes are shown in Table 2. In terms of secondary outcomes, the volume $(\mathtt{p}=0.686)$ , density $(\mathbf{p}=0.639)$ and type of hematoma $(\mathbf{p}=0.756).$ did not vary signifi­ cantly between the two groups. Also, the length of hospitalization from surgery to discharge was not significantly different in the two groups. Again, the two groups had no significant difference in postoperative pneumocephalus $(\mathbf{p}=0.472)$ . Both groups had no deaths or post­ operative infections during the study period.  



# 7. Discussion  

The prevalence of CSDH has increased over time due to increased life expectancy and the sensitivity of neuroimaging techniques [31-33,37]. Iran has had a steady rise in the prevalence of CSDH over the past 15 years [2,23], and as a developing country, this trend is worrisome. Although numerous studies revealed that the risk of recurrence was comparable between SBH and DBH craniostomy [4,41,51,6], there are still disagreements over the optimal techniques to use, particularly in cases when the patient is elderly and has numerous comorbidities. We wanted to compare the recurrence rate of CSDH between the SBH and DBH craniostomies in this prospective randomized clinical trial study. We believe the findings of the present study can help surgeons decide which strategy to choose, particularly in developing countries.

According to our results, male patients had a two times higher CSDH incidence than female patients (male/female ratio ${\scriptstyle=}2.4.$ ). This higher incidence can be because male patients were more exposed to trauma than female patients [38]. However, in older people, both sexes are equally at risk of falling, minor head traumas and brain atrophy [38]. Another factor that may have contributed to a higher incidence of CSDH in males was the increased rate of comorbidities in males in our study. In both groups, DM, HTN, and cognition disorders were found in only male patients. In contrast, a larger percentage of symptoms like headaches, hemiparesis and gait disturbance was found in female patients. Again, alcohol consumption and smoking were reported in only male patients. In a recent meta-analysis by Zhu et al., male sex, DM, bilateral hema­ toma, anti-thrombotic drugs, and no postoperative drainage usage were associated with recurrence [55]. However, HTN, alcohol consumption, cardiac disease and epilepsy were not related to this outcome [55]. In a systematic review, Shlobin et al. also observed that anticoagulation or antiplatelet agent use was not associated with poorer outcomes in older adults [40].  

In elderly patients with coagulation disorder, it is recommended to correct the coagulation disorder before treatment [42]. The European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) and the British Society of Hematol­ ogy propose a 24-hour delay from the most recent dose for patients using new oral anticoagulants. However, a 48-hour wait before surgery is advised in cases of renal impairment or a history of using direct thrombin inhibitors [16,25,49]. Although four patients had a history of anticoagulant use in this study, these guidelines were observed in the two patients with anticoagulant use. The other two patients required emergency surgery; therefore, prothrombin complex concentration (PCC) was prescribed, and surgery was undertaken [16]. For patients with a history of antiplatelet use, platelet was administered before emergency surgery in life-threatening conditions [25]. Conversely, in non-life-threatening cases, we waited for seven to ten days until platelet function returned before surgery. Although trauma is not necessarily triggering for CSDH onset [17,39], $77.3\%$ of the patients in the present study had a history of trauma. However, there was no correlation be­ tween trauma and the side of hematoma $(\mathtt{p}=0.8)$ and preoperative GCS level $(\mathtt{p}=0.32)$ . Also, we observed bilateral hematoma in older patients with an atrophic brain. In addition, two studies observed that although infrequent, recurrence was more common in bilateral hematomas [15, 18].  

Until 1970, craniotomy was considered the preferred method for the treatment of CSDH. However, its usage has been limited to cases with extensive hematomas recurrence with thick membranes or calcification [25,28]. In recent years, endoscope-assisted surgery for CSDH man­ agement has been associated with significantly decreased recurrence risk among patients with CSDH compared to BHC [13,53]. However, BHC is performed in most setting since it is similar to TDC in terms of outcomes [22,27] and is applicable in most developing countries where surgical equipment is often limited. The data on the use of BHC and recurrence rate are many and inconsistent [24,30,36]. In this study, the overall recurrence rate was $4.54\%$ . Two patients in our study had recurrence at the end of the 6 months follow-up period. One patient in the SBH group (occurred in the first month) and the other in the DBH group (occurred between the first month and sixth month). This recur­ rence rate was not statistically significantly different between the two groups $(\mathtt{p}=0.312)$ . Our finding was similar to other studies [24,30,36, 41]. Besides, in one study [14], DBH was associated with a lower recurrence rate than the SBH procedure [30,34,36,47]. However, in all studies [29,30,34,36,47,51], there were no statistically significant dif­ ferences between the two procedures, even though one study [30] had a relatively long follow-up period.  

Furthermore, the SBH procedure has been associated with a higher risk of infections [41,47]. In this present study, however, no post­ operative infection was observed in the two groups. Concordant to Sale Danjuma’s study [36], the time of surgery in our study was substantially reduced in the SBH group than in the DBH group $(\mathbf{p}=0.001\$ ). Hypo­ thetically, this means older patients with CSDH, particularly those with comorbidities, may undergo SDH rather than DBH. However, the final decision for CSDH treatment should be at the discretion of the clinician. Postoperatively, a sub-periosteal drain (SPD) was inserted in all patients in this study. In terms of CSDH recurrence, Zhang et al. observed no differences between SPD and subdural drain (SDD) insertion $(\mathbf{p}=0.502)$ [54]. However, an RCT study by Soleman et al. [43] showed that SPD insertion was associated with lower recurrence rates, fewer surgical infections, and lower drain misplacement rates.  

Also, a recent meta-analysis showed recurrent CSDH was lower in the SPD group in subgroup analysis at 3 months $(\mathtt{p}=0.04)$ and 6 months $(\mathbf{p}=0.01)$ follow-up [35]. All patients underwent SPD in our study because we wanted to prevent complications such as iatrogenic injury to the bridging veins between the cortical surface and hematoma mem­ brane [9] and parenchymal injuries [19]. Such a complication would have confounded the study outcome. In terms of elderly patients, Greuter et al. observed that patients $\geq80$ years of age and treated with SDD showed higher recurrence rates than those treated with SPD, without a significant difference $(\mathtt{p}=0.46)$ . They also found significantly higher drain misplacement rates for patients $\geq80$ years and treated with SDD compared with SPD $(\mathtt{p}=0.01\$ ) [12]. However, there were no sig­ nificant differences between the two drains in terms of incidence of mortality, postoperative seizures, surgical infection, pneumocephalus, and GCS score 4–5 [19,52].  

Shen et al. retrospectively evaluated several secondary predictors in 461 patients. In a multivariate analysis, they found that severe brain atrophy, severe postoperative pneumocephalus, and volume of drainage $\geq100\mathrm{ml}$ were independent risk factors for CSDH recurrence [38]. Also, Zhu et al., in a meta-analysis study, showed that higher hematoma volume, severe brain atrophy, and midline shift $\geq10\mathrm{mm}$ hematoma width $\geq20\mathrm{mm}$ were associated with CSDH recurrence [55]. We found no difference in width, volume, density and type of hematoma between the two groups in this study. Postoperative midline shift was more prominent in the SBH group than the DBH group. We also observed that older patients had higher hematoma width, which can be explained by the higher prevalence of brain atrophy among this age group. Also, the increased inflammatory mediators in older patients [26] could influence hematoma growth among these patients. Some authors [45,50] docu­ mented that the higher ratio of inflammatory mediators to anti-inflammatory mediators and the uncoordinated response of the innate immune system in older patients results in increased capillary permeability and, consequently, an increase in the size of the hematoma. Similar to one study [20], the duration of hospitalization in this study did not differ between the two groups. Furthermore, we did not docu­ ment any deaths in our study. However, studies have shown higher mortality among older patients [12,40] and those in whom the drain was not inserted [37]. Nevertheless, Wan et al. [51], in a meta-analysis, did not observe increased mortality with DBH compared with SBH in affected patients.  



# 8. Limitation  

The limitations of this study included the lack of sufficient follow-up and participation in the study by the patients at any point of the study. To reduce this, the researchers kept in constant contact with the patients and fully disclosed to them all the advantages and disadvantages of taking part in this study. Another drawback of this study was our inability to blind the patients and the surgeon because of how the number of burr holes was counted both during and after the operation. Furthermore, selection bias may also be a concern due to the study’s location in a referral center. Additionally, the study’s power may be lowered by the small number of cases available for participation. Therefore, future studies with higher sample sizes are recommended. We also believe that stronger investigations can be carried out if facilities and coordination are made available to researchers especially from developing countries with insufficient CSDH data.  



# 9. Conclusion  

The objective of CSDH management is the introduction of less invasive and more effective procedures. Although recent studies have documented a significant reduction in recurrence rate with endoscopeassisted surgeries, the most frequent CSDH surgery performed by neu­ rosurgeons worldwide is BHC. Our findings show that SBH cranios­ tomies are equally effective as DBH craniostomies at draining hematomas and do not increase the recurrence rate. On the other hand, the single burr hole craniostomy had a shorter surgical duration than the double burr hole craniotomy. Consequently, we recommend single burr hole surgeries for all patients, with emphasis on the elderly and those with severe comorbidities, as well as in situations with few surgical fa­ cilities and a high patient admission rate.
