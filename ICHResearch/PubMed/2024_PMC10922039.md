# Association of Fibrinolysis Phenotype with Patient Outcomes following Traumatic Brain Injury  

Samantha Durbin, MD1, Alexandra Brito, MD1,2, Alicia Johnson, MPH1, Bryan Cotton, MD3, Susan Rowell, MD, MBA4, Martin Schreiber, MD1  

1Oregon Health & Science University, Portland, OR  

2The Queen’s Medical Center, Honolulu, HI  

3University of Texas Health Houston, Houston, TX  

4The University of Chicago Medicine & Biological Sciences, Chicago, IL  

# Abstract  

Background: Impaired coagulation is associated with elevated risk of mortality in trauma patients. Prior studies have demonstrated increased mortality in patients with hyperfibrinolysis (HF) and fibrinolysis shutdown (SD). Additionally, prior studies have demonstrated no effect of tranexamic acid (TXA) on fibrinolysis phenotypes. We examined the association of admission fibrinolysis phenotype with traumatic brain injury (TBI) patient outcomes.  

Methods: Data were extracted from a placebo-controlled multicenter clinical trial. Patients ≥15 years old with TBI (Glasgow Coma Scale 3–12) and systolic blood pressure $\small{\mathrm{{}\times90~mmHg}}$ were randomized in the out-of-hospital setting to receive placebo bolus/placebo infusion (Placebo), 1 gram (g) TXA bolus/1g TXA infusion (Bolus Maintenance [BM]); or $2\mathrm{g}$ TXA bolus/placebo infusion (Bolus Only [BO]). Fibrinolysis phenotypes on admission were determined by clot lysis at 30 minutes (LY30): SD $\leq0.8\%$ , physiologic $0.9{-}2.9\%$ , HF $23\%$ . Logistic regression was used to control for age, sex, penetrating injury, ISS, maximum head AIS, and TXA treatment group.  

Results: 747 patients met inclusion criteria. Fibrinolysis shutdown was the most common phenotype in all treatment groups and was associated with increased age, ISS and presence of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). Inpatient mortality was $15.2\%$ for SD and HF, and $10.6\%$ for physiologic $(\mathtt{p}=0.49)$ ). No differences in mortality, disability rating scale at 6 months, acute kidney injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, or multi-organ failure were noted between fibrinolysis phenotypes.  

Conclusion: SD is the most common phenotype expressed in moderate to severe TBI. In TBI, there is no association between fibrinolysis phenotype and mortality or other major complications.  

Level of Evidence— III  

Study Type: diagnostic test/criteria  

# Keywords  

Thromboelastography; fibrinolysis; conventional coagulation assays; tranexamic acid; traumatic brain injury  

# Background:  

One in three trauma patients will present with impaired coagulation.1 Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is associated with a unique coagulopathic state, the underlying physiology of which is only partially understood.2–5 Cerebral endothelial cells contain high contents of tissue factor as well as microvesicles that contain multiple procoagulant components.2,6–7 Following TBI, tissue factor and microvesicles are released and activate the coagulation cascade via the extrinsic pathway. This activation may happen within the circulating blood rather than remain localized to the vessel wall, resulting in systemic dissemination of clots as well as exposure of active sites for fibrinolysis.7 This can lead to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), microvascular thrombosis, and consumptive coagulopathy.2  

One way to characterize traumatic coagulopathies is through fibrinolysis phenotypes. Fibrinolysis is the process by which clot is broken down which may be a normal part of hemostasis or a result of disordered coagulation.1,8 The fibrinolysis pathway is a key part of the coagulation cascade and helps balance post-injury bleeding and clot formation.9 Most patients with severe traumatic injuries have abnormalities of fibrinolysis within 12 hours of their injury. 2 There are 3 associated fibrinolysis phenotypes: physiologic with normal clot lysis, shutdown (SD) with acute impairment of clot lysis, and hyperfibrinolysis (HF) with excessive clot lysis. SD causes microvascular thrombosis, which has been implicated in the pathogenesis of organ failure and venous thrombotic events whereas HF results in destabilization of clots, bleeding and potentially DIC. 1 Prior studies have demonstrated increased mortality in HF and in SD in trauma patients.1  

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is a lysine analogue that binds to plasminogen, preventing its activation into plasmin.9 This inhibits plasminogen from binding to fibrin via kringle domains to lysate on fibrin reducing fibrin breakdown.9 Prior studies have demonstrated no impact of TXA on fibrinolysis as measured by the LY30 component of thrombelastography (TEG) in TBI patients, but the relationship between fibrinolysis phenotype and outcomes in TBI patients has not been examined. 9 Moore et al have previously shown that fibrinolysis phenotype is associated with mortality in a mixed severely injured trauma patient population with SD and HF having a higher mortality than physiologic fibrinolysis.1 The goal of our study was to investigate the association of admission fibrinolysis phenotype with outcomes after TBI. We hypothesized that impairment of fibrinolysis would be associated with worse outcomes in trauma patients with TBI.  

# Methods:  

Data were extracted from a previously published NIH funded, randomized placebocontrolled, multicenter clinical trial.10 Patients with moderate to severe TBI (blunt or penetrating) were randomized in the out-of-hospital setting to receive placebo or TXA under a Food and Drug Administration Exemption from Informed Consent (21 CFR $\S50.24\$ ) trial. Data were collected from May 2015 through March 2017. This trial used the infrastructure of the Resuscitation Outcomes Consortium, a North American clinical trials network which investigates out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and severe traumatic injury. This trial occurred in 12 regions and included 20 trauma centers and 39 emergency medical service (EMS) agencies across the United States and Canada. All site ethics review boards and the Human Research Protection Office of the US Department of Defense reviewed and approved the local community consultation process, public disclosure plans, and conduct of the study. The full CONSORT checklist and diagram are available in Supplemental 1 and 2.  

Inclusion criteria were as listed: 1) patients 15 years or older, 2) penetrating or blunt TBI with initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 3–12, 3) systolic blood pressure ${\geq}90\mathrm{mmHg}$ , and 4) at least one reactive pupil on initial EMS assessment. Exclusion criteria included: GCS of 3 without a reactive pupil, unknown time of injury, greater than 2 hours from injury to initial randomization, prehospital cardiopulmonary resuscitation, seizures, myocardial infarction, stroke, known or suspected dialysis, known or suspected prisoners, burns $>$ $20\%$ of total body surface area, drowning or hanging, and patients who opted out during community consultation. Two hours was chosen as an exclusion time to avoid harm from late administration of TXA. There were no exclusion criteria for multiple injuries or low ISS, but hypotensive patients were excluded differentiating this study from prior studies. Patients were randomized to receive placebo bolus/placebo infusion (Placebo), 1 gram (g) TXA bolus/1g TXA infusion (Bolus Maintenance [BM]); or $2\mathrm{g}$ TXA bolus/placebo infusion (Bolus Only [BO]) in the prehospital setting. Whole blood samples were obtained on ED arrival and citrated kaolin-activated thromboelastography (TEG) tests were performed using the TEG 5000 instrument (Haemonetics, Boston, MA) within 30 minutes of obtaining samples.  

Patients were grouped based on LY30 levels as follows: fibrinolysis shutdown $\mathrm{\calLY}30<=$ $0.8\%$ ), physiologic $\mathrm{LY}30>0.8\%$ and $<3\%$ ), and hyperfibrinolysis $(\mathrm{LY}30>=3\%$ ). Patients without LY30 values on admission were excluded. In the analysis, patients were grouped by fibrinolysis phenotype, controlling for the TXA treatment group from the initial randomized trial. For the primary outcome of discharge mortality, a logistic regression was used to examine the effect of fibrinolysis group, controlling for age, sex, penetrating injury, injury severity score (ISS), head abbreviated injury scale (AIS), intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) and TXA treatment group. For the secondary outcome of 6-month disability rating scale (DRS) in patients who were alive at discharge, a Kruskal Wallis test was used to examine group differences. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used as appropriate to  

examine differences among the groups in acute kidney injury (AKI), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and death from multiorgan failure (MOF).  

# Results:  

Table 1 displays the group characteristics. Of the 747 patients with LY30 measurements, 162 $(21.7\%)$ were in the physiologic group, 488 $(65.3\%)$ were in the fibrinolysis shutdown group, and 97 $(13.0\%)$ were in the hyperfibrinolysis group. The groups differed in age (p $<0.001;$ ), with the fibrinolysis shutdown patients being older. They also differed in ISS $(\mathbf{p}=0.002)$ , with the fibrinolysis shutdown group having higher mean ISS than the other groups. Lastly, the groups differed in presence of ICH $\mathbf{\rho}_{\mathrm{p}}<0.001;$ ), with the fibrinolysis shutdown patients having the highest incidence of ICH. Eleven patients included were on anticoagulants prior to admission. Of these patients, 10 presented in shutdown and 1 was physiologic. There was no association between TXA treatment group and admission fibrinolysis phenotype.  

We found no difference among groups in the primary outcome of discharge mortality $\left({\mathfrak{p}}=\right.$ 0.49) in the logistic regression model. Of the 747 total patients, 716 had discharge mortality status available. 102 died prior to discharge: 16 $10.6\%)$ in the physiologic group, 72 $(15.2\%)$ in the fibrinolysis shutdown group, and 14 $15.2\%)$ in the hyperfibrinolysis group (Figure 1). The area under the curve is 0.90 ( $95\%$ CI: 0.86–0.93). The Youden Index is 0.66 at a probability cutoff of 0.269, and at this cut point the sensitivity is 0.78 and specificity is 0.88 (Table 2).  

There was no difference among groups in median DRS scores at 6 months in patients who survived to discharge (physiologic $=0$ , $\mathrm{SD}=0$ , $\mathrm{HF}=1$ , $\mathrm{p}=0.50,$ . There was also no difference among groups in the proportions of patients with AKI $(\mathtt{p}=0.40)$ , ARDS $\left(\mathtt{p}=\right.$ 0.23), or death from multiple organ failure $(\mathtt{p}=0.51\$ ).  

# Discussion:  

In mixed trauma populations including patients who present in shock, SD has been shown to be the most common fibrinolysis phenotype on admission TEG and is associated with increased mortality.1,11–12 Further, within a mixed trauma population SD has been shown to be significantly more common in patients with TBI,13 which is consistent with the known hypercoagulable effects of tissue factor and microvesicle release into the circulation at the site of blood brain barrier disruption in TBI patients.2,6–7 In line with these findings, our study showed that the most common fibrinolysis phenotype in an isolated TBI population was SD. Also, similar to previous studies on mixed trauma populations,1,11 we found that the average age in the SD group was higher than the HF and physiologic groups. Other measures of fibrinolysis abnormalities such as fibrinogen and D-dimer levels in TBI patients have been previously shown to be more abnormal and associated with worse prognosis in elderly patients.14 This study markedly differs from those done in mixed trauma populations due to the lack of association between fibrinolysis phenotype and mortality or other major complications in TBI patients, even when controlling for ICH. The combination of tissue trauma and hemorrhagic shock produces unique fibrinolysis phenotypes that are predictive of outcomes. The current study differs from prior studies in that patients with hemorrhagic shock were excluded. This was done intentionally as an effort to focus more on outcomes related to TBI as opposed to multiple trauma and hemorrhagic shock. This represents a relevant change from prior published studies.  

Although it is difficult to separate TBI from other types of tissue trauma in research as TBI is often seen as part of a polytrauma presentation, the mechanisms of coagulopathy seem to be different and even more complex when they coexist.3 In a study by Meizoso et al (2017) in a mixed trauma population, transient SD was more common after TBI but persistent SD was more common with penetrating injury.12 Even within TBI, the effects of modifying fibrinolysis can differ based on the type of injury. Our previous study showed that TXA only provided a mortality benefit in patients with confirmed intracranial hemorrhage,10 and it has been shown that TXA can decrease the expansion of intracranial hemorrhage.15 However, animal studies have shown that TXA can actually increase the size of cerebral contusion lesions likely due to increased microvascular thrombus formation.16 This difference of coagulopathic phenotype between TBI with and without hemorrhage could possibly account for some of the conflicting data that have come from large studies such as CRASH-3 that looked at all types of TBI together.  

With the potential for rapid evaluation of coagulopathy using viscoelastic testing such as TEG, there was hope that identification of disordered fibrinolysis based on LY30 could guide management of trauma patients. In a mixed trauma population, SD present on admission LY30 has been shown to correlate with persistent SD and was an independent predictor of mortality.12 However, the relationship between fibrinolytic phenotype on TEG and clinical and biochemical findings is inconsistent in both mixed trauma17–19 and isolated TBI patients20–21 with several studies showing little to no difference in LY30 values in TBI patients even when other TEG measurements are abnormal22 or when TXA is given in the prehospital setting, prior to obtaining an admission TEG.9–10 Additionally, in our prior study there was no difference in fibrinolysis phenotype regardless of TXA treatment group.11 It is important to note that prior retrospective studies have demonstrated, in polytrauma patients, administration of TXA is associated with fibrinolysis shutdown and more deranged markers of metabolic derangement, as well as increased mortality in physiologic fibrinolysis.12,23 In this randomized clinical trial, our findings did not reflect this. Levels of the endogenous inhibitor of fibrinolysis PAI-1 are also not well correlated with TEG; in a swine model of tissue injury compared to hemorrhagic shock, PAI-1 levels were significantly higher than sham animals in hemorrhagic shock and similar to sham in tissue injury, but the hemorrhagic shock animals showed a trend toward hyperfibrinolysis whereas tissue injury animals had significantly suppressed fibrinolysis.24 D-dimers seem to be more sensitive to the effects of exogenous TXA administration9 and have also been shown, in multiple studies, to more consistently predict poor outcomes in TBI patients.2, 21,25–26 Although characterizing fibrinolysis phenotype by TEG in trauma patients has utility in predicting outcomes in mixed trauma populations,1,12 we did not see a similar correlation with outcomes in a nonhemorrhagic shock TBI population. Additionally, given the lack of effect of TXA on these phenotypes, the potential utility for guiding interventions is questionable. Future studies will plan to explore the relationship between D-dimer, PAI-1 and fibrinolysis phenotype in this study population.  

There are several limitations to this study. Similar to other studies on TXA, our findings are within a sub-analysis of a larger study and as such there are inherent power limitations as retrospective power calculations are not appropriate. This can lead to missing subtle differences in outcomes between groups due to type II error. Also, due to multiple dosing groups of TXA, separate examination of the subgroups of patients is limited by the small sample sizes. Additionally, this analysis of TEG results was limited to the single time point of patient presentation to the ED. It is possible that changes of fibrinolysis phenotype or persistence of a specific phenotype may be predictive of outcomes. That analysis is beyond the scope of this study and is currently ongoing. Although the intent of excluding patients with SBP $<90$ was intentional to reduce the confounding influence of shock, this is an imperfect measure and did not completely exclude this confounder. As previously stated, 11 patients were on anticoagulation prior to admission with 10 of these patients presenting in SD. Of note, studies have shown that the oral anticoagulants have poor correlation with TEG results so it is difficult to say whether any effect of these medications would be significant in the context of these laboratory results.17, 27–28 Additionally, due to the relatively low number of females in the trauma population and the complex relationship between sex and coagulation this study is underpowered to look at these effects in females.  

In conclusion, despite data supporting a significant relationship between fibrinolysis phenotype and outcomes in a mixed severely injured trauma population our study did not show any significant association between fibrinolysis phenotype and outcomes in TBI patients regardless of TXA administration. These results question the utility of TEG to guide TXA administration in TBI patients and further studies are necessary to clarify this. Further analyses of how other markers of coagulopathy are related to fibrinolysis phenotype are needed.
