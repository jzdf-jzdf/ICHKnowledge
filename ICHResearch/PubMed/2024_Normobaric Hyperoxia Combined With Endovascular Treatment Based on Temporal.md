# Normobaric Hyperoxia Combined With Endovascular Treatment Based on Temporal Gradient: A Dose-Escalation Study  

Weili Li , MD\*; Sifei Wang , MD\*; Lan Liu, PhD; Jiahao Chen , MD; Jing Lan, MD; Jiayue Ding , MD; Zhiying Chen, MD; Shuhua Yuan , MD; Zhifeng Qi , PhD; Ming Wei , MD; Xunming Ji , MD, PhD  

BACKGROUND: Normobaric hyperoxia (NBO) has neuroprotective effects in acute ischemic stroke. Thus, we aimed to identify the optimal NBO treatment duration combined with endovascular treatment.  

METHODS: This is a single-center, randomized controlled, open-label, blinded-end point dose-escalation clinical trial. Patients with acute ischemic stroke who had an indication for endovascular treatment at Tianjin Huanhu Hospital were randomly assigned to 4 groups (1:1 ratio) based on NBO therapy duration: (1) control group (1 L/min oxygen for 4 hours); (2) NBO-2h group (10 L/min for 2 hours); (3) NBO-4h group (10 L/min for 4 hours); and (4) NBO-6h group (10 L/min for 6 hours). The primary outcome was cerebral infarction volume at 72 hours after randomization using an intention-to-treat analysis model. The primary safety outcome was the 90-day mortality rate.  

RESULTS: Between June 2022 and September 2023, 100 patients were randomly assigned to the following groups: control group $(\mathsf{n}{=}25\$ ), NBO-2h group $(\mathsf{n}{=}25\$ ), NBO-4h group $\tt(n=25$ ), and NBO-6h group $(\mathsf{n}{=}25\$ ). The 72-hour cerebral infarct volumes were $39.4\pm34.3\mathrm{mL}$ $30.6{\pm}30.1mL_{1}$ $19.7\pm15.4\mathsf{mL},$ and $22.6\pm22.4mL$ respectively $\mathrm{\Delta}P=0.013_{\cdot}$ ). The NBO-4h and NBO-6h groups both showed statistically significant differences (adjusted $P$ values: 0.011 and 0.027, respectively) compared with the control group. Compared with the control group, both the NBO-4h and NBO-6h groups showed significant differences $(P{<}0.05)$ in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores at 24 hours, 72 hours, and 7 days, as well as in the change of the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores from baseline to 24 hours. Additionally, there were no significant differences among the 4 groups in terms of 90-day mortality rate, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, early neurological deterioration, or severe adverse events.  

CONCLUSIONS: The effectiveness of NBO therapy was associated with oxygen administration duration. Among patients with acute ischemic stroke who underwent endovascular treatment, NBO therapy for 4 and 6 hours was found to be more effective. Larger-scale multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings.

Currently, the most effective treatment for AIS is to recanalize the occluded blood vessels.3 With technological advancements, the success rate of vascular recanalization has surpassed $80\%$ . However, despite these improvements, the rate of favorable outcomes for patients remains below $50\%^{4,5}$ This disparity can be attributed to several factors, including the limited salvageable ischemic penumbra during vascular recanalization and the occurrence of reperfusion injury following the reopening of the vessel.6–8  

Considering these challenges, we proposed a multistage, sequential treatment approach that builds upon vascular recanalization. This approach aims to optimize protection of the ischemic penumbra before recanalization and to mitigate reperfusion injury after the vessel is reopened.9,10  

Presently, the optimal therapeutic approach for AIS involves a combination of vascular recanalization and neuroprotective treatment.11–13 Normobaric hyperoxia (NBO) is considered a promising neuroprotective therapy due to its rapid onset, minimal adverse effects, ease of administration, cost-effectiveness, and wide applicability. Basic and clinical research conducted by our team has demonstrated the significant neuroprotective benefits of NBO.14–16 Specifically, its ability to enhance oxygen levels in ischemic regions, mitigate blood-brain barrier damage, enhance neurological function scores, and reduce cerebral infarct volumes leads to improved clinical outcomes. However, no consensus is currently available in clinical studies on the optimal duration of NBO treatment. NBO treatment has been administered for 8 hours in patients who have not undergone revascularization therapy,17 and in patients before revascularization.18 NBO treatment has also been administered for 6 hours after revascularization.19 To the best of our knowledge, the optimal duration of NBO treatment remains unreported.  

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the optimal duration of NBO treatment by employing sequential time gradients. This research strategy sought to achieve a balance between minimizing the potential side effects of NBO treatment and maximizing its therapeutic advantages. The aim was to generate empirical evidence that can inform and promote the future clinical application of NBO therapy.  

# METHODS  

The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This article was prepared following the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) 2010 guidelines (see the Supplemental Material for the CONSORT checklist).20  

# Study Design  

This single-center, randomized controlled dose-escalation study with a blinded end point was designed to evaluate the impact of varying durations of NBO therapy on the efficacy and safety of treatment for AIS. The trial was conducted at Tianjin Huanhu Hospital Stroke Center in China.  

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huanhu Hospital, Tianjin Medical University (approval No. 2022-076) and is registered on Clinicaltrials.gov with the Unique Protocol ID: TD-NBO and Identifier: NCT05404373. The trial protocol can be found in the Supplemental Material.  

An independent steering committee, consisting of independent researchers, physicians, and statisticians, oversaw the design and implementation of the research. The committee members were responsible for ensuring the meticulous execution of the study, data collection, and subsequent statistical analysis. All patients or their legal representatives provided informed consent before participating in the study.  

# Patient Selection  

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patient aged $\geq18$ years, symptoms and signs consistent with acute anterior circulation ischemic stroke, a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of ${\ge}6,$ and an Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography score of ${\ge}6$ . Patients met the indications for endovascular treatment (EVT). Detailed indications for EVT can be found in the Supplemental Material.  

The following exclusion criteria were considered: rapid neurological improvement; an NIHSS score $\mathtt{<}10$ or evidence of spontaneous vessel recanalization; active bleeding or a known bleeding tendency; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pulmonary inflammation, pleural effusion, acute respiratory distress syndrome, or irregular respiration; patients with unstable vital signs; and patients unable to complete a 90-day follow-up. Additional details are listed in the Supplemental Material.  

# Randomization and Blinding Method  

Patients were allocated randomly in a 1:1 ratio to 1 of 4 groups: control group, NBO-2h group, NBO-4h group, and NBO-6h group, using a completely randomized design facilitated by the Interactive Web Response System. The randomization code is generated by a specially trained physician who logs into the randomization system. This physician then informs the study practitioner whether the patient is assigned to the NBO or low-flow oxygen group. This study used an open-label design with no blinding during the administration of oxygen treatment. However, a blinded approach was rigorously implemented for the assessment of outcomes. Specifically, blinding measures were implemented for the imaging assessors, clinical outcome assessors, and follow-up personnel. The statistician responsible for data analysis was also blinded. Throughout the experiment, strict protocols were in place to prevent nonblinded personnel from participating in clinical assessments or engaging in communication with the blinded assessors.  

# NBO Protocol  

Upon randomization, initiating oxygen therapy promptly is crucial, with a strict time limit of no later than 30 minutes postrandomization. NBO treatment was administered through inhalation of $100\%$ oxygen at a flow rate of 10 L/min, using a storage oxygen mask. According to group assignment, patients underwent NBO therapy for either 2, 4, or 6 hours. Conversely, patients in the control group were treated with oxygen at a rate of 1 L/min through a mask for a duration of 4 hours. To ensure comprehensive monitoring, blood gas analysis was conducted for all patients before the completion of their respective oxygen therapy sessions.  

Additionally, local anesthesia was preferred for patients when feasible, but in cases where patients became agitated or uncooperative, it was necessary to switch to general anesthesia. In such instances, the method of oxygen delivery was modified from mask oxygen delivery to intubation for oxygen administration. In this scenario, for a flow rate of $10\mathrm{{L}/\mathrm{{min}}}$ , the method was adjusted to deliver an inspiratory oxygen fraction $({\sfFiO}_{2})$ of 1.0. Similarly, for a flow rate of 1 L/min, the ${\sfFiO}_{2}$ was adjusted to 0.3 if intubation was performed. Please refer to the Supplemental Material for additional details.  

# Endovascular Treatment  

All patients should receive EVT to facilitate recanalization of the occluded vessel. EVT included mechanical thrombectomy, thrombus aspiration, or a combination of both, with stent implantation if necessary. Additional details can be found in the Supplemental Material.  

# Study Outcomes  

The primary outcome was the cerebral infarction volume at 72 hours after randomization, evaluated by magnetic resonance diffusion-weighted imaging. The core infarct was defined as the region of apparent diffusion coefficient values $<620\times10^{-6}$ $\mathsf{mm}^{2}/\mathsf{s}.^{21}$ We measured infarct volumes using eStroke software (https://www.brainomix.com/stroke/).15,21  

The following secondary efficacy outcomes were recorded: modified Rankin Scale score at 90 days; the proportion of patients achieving modified Rankin Scale scores of 0 to 2 at 90 days; $\mathsf{Pao}_{_{2}}$ following oxygen therapy; the NIHSS scores at various time intervals (24 hours, 72 hours, and 7 days); changes in the NIHSS score from baseline to 24 hours; and successful vessel recanalization, defined as grade 2b, 2c, or 3 on the extended Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction scale (0 to 3, with higher grades indicating increased reperfusion).22  

The modified Rankin Scale scores were obtained through structured telephone follow-ups conducted by personnel who had undergone specialized training and were unaware of the treatment group assignments. Conversely, NIHSS scores were evaluated onsite by assessors with a certification for NIHSS score assessment.  

The following safety outcomes were evaluated: the 90-day mortality rate, the 90-day stroke-related mortality rate, the incidence of any intracranial hemorrhage, the incidence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage,23 early neurological deterioration,24 severe adverse events, the incidence of surgical complications, and pneumonia.  

Severe adverse events were characterized as events resulting in a deterioration of the patient’s condition, prolonged length of hospital stay, life-threatening situations, or death, which specifically included early neurological deterioration, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, 90-day mortality, re-occlusion of a recanalized blood vessel, an increase in the infarct volume, recurrent stroke, severe pulmonary infection, and deep venous thrombosis of the lower limbs.  

# Statistical Analysis  

This study adopted an intention-to-treat analysis approach. Continuous variables were presented as mean (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), while categorical data were presented as counts and percentages. Descriptive statistical analysis was used for data on baseline characteristics.  

For continuous variables with a normal distribution, a 1-way ANOVA was used. If continuous variables did not follow a normal distribution, a nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis $H$ test was used. In the event of observed differences between the groups, pairwise comparisons were conducted. To mitigate the risk of false positive rates (type I errors) due to multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni method was used to obtain the corrected $P$ values. For comparisons involving proportions across multiple groups, the Pearson $\chi^{2}$ test or Fisher exact test was used as appropriate for the analysis. We imputed the missing data by multiple imputation methods for the primary end point event (infarct volume). A $P{<}0.05$ was considered statistically significant.  

The study was analyzed in compliance with standard Good Clinical Practice regulation database, and the statistical analysis plan was locked before unblinding. Analyses were performed using SPSS software for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).  

# Sample Size Calculation  

The sample size was determined based on the literature and preexperimental results.14 Under the assumption that the dose response to the infarct volume is monotone but not necessarily equal among NBO groups, we used William test25 to calculate the minimum effective dose. We considered 4 groups: control group, NBO for 2 hours, 4 hours, and 6 hours. The expected difference in infarct volume was $15mL$ between the NBO group and the control group with a within-group SE of $20~\mathsf{mL}$ With a type I error $\mathtt{{a}=0.05}$ and a power $1-\beta{=}0.8$ , the required sample size was 25 per group with a total of 100 patients.  

# RESULTS  

# Baseline Characteristics  

From June 2022 to September 2023, 100 patients were randomly allocated into 4 groups, with 25 patients in each group for the ITT analysis (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics included age, sex, medical history, admission NIHSS score, Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography score, baseline oxygen tension levels, infarct volume, location of occluded vessels, TOAST (Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) classification, method of anesthesia, and time intervals from onset to randomization and from oxygen inhalation to recanalization. No significant differences were observed in patient baseline clinical characteristics among the 4 groups $_{\cdot}P{>}0.05$ ; Table 1).  

# Safety Outcomes  

Two deaths $(8\%)$ occurred in the NBO-2h group, with 1 death $(4\%)$ occurring in each of the remaining groups. However, the difference in mortality rates was not significant. Two deaths (1 in the control group and 1 in the NBO-2h group) were attributed to strokerelated causes. The incidence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was $4\%$ in the control group and the NBO-6h group (1 case in each group) and $8\%$ in the NBO-2h and NBO-4h groups (2 cases in each group). However, these differences were not significant. The incidence of any intracranial hemorrhage was as follows: $20\%$ in the control group, $28\%$ in the NBO-2h group, $28\%$ in the NBO-4h group, and $24\%$ in the NBO-6h group. However, these differences were not significant. Notably, no cases of oxygen-related adverse symptoms were detected in any of the groups. Early neurological deterioration occurred in all the groups with 2 cases observed in the NBO-2h group and 1 case observed in each of the remaining groups. Severe adverse events occurred in 6 cases in both the control group $(24\%)$ and the NBO-6h $(24\%)$ group, with 5 cases $(20\%)$ reported in the NBO- $\cdot2\mathsf{h}$ group and 4 $(16\%)$ in the NBO-4h group. Notably, no significant differences were observed among the 4 groups about the incidence of surgical complications and pneumonia (Table 2).  

# Efficacy Outcomes  

# Primary Efficacy Outcomes  

Infarct volume at 72 hours, measured by magnetic resonance diffusion-weighted imaging, was as follows: $39.4{\pm}34.3~\mathsf{mL}$ in the control group, $30.6{\pm}30.1$ mL in the NBO-2h group, $19.7{\pm}15.4\ mL$ in the NBO-4h group, and $22.6{\pm}22.4\mathrm{mL}$ in the NBO-6h group. The differences in infarct volume among the groups were significant $\mathrm{\\Delta}P=0.013$ ; Figure 2; Table 3). Pairwise comparisons showed significant differences in the 72-hour infarct volume of the NBO-4h and NBO-6h groups when compared with the control group $_{(P=0.006}$ and $P{=}0.005$ , respectively), with adjusted $P$ values of 0.011 and 0.027, respectively. However, no significant differences were observed between the NBO-4h and NBO6h groups $\scriptstyle(P=0.702)$ ). Similarly, no statistical difference was observed in the comparison between the NBO-2h group and the control group $'P{=}0.271$ ); see pairwise comparisons in Table S1.  

Furthermore, it was observed that the NBO-4h group (8.6 [1.4–17.5] mL) and the NBO-6h group (8.6 [1.4–17.5] mL) had the smallest infarct growth, whereas the control group exhibited the largest infarct growth (12.2 [1.8–32.8] mL), although there was no statistically significant difference observed among the 4 groups ( $_{\mathrm{\Delta}P>0.05}$ ; Table 3).  

# Secondary Efficacy Outcomes  

The 90-day modified Rankin Scale scores (median [IQR]) were 3 (2–4) in the control group, 3 (1–3.5) in the NBO-2h group, 3 (1–3.5) in the NBO-4h group, and 3 $(1-3.5)$ in the NBO-6h group, with no significant differences observed (Table 3).

# DISCUSSION  

In the present study, a specialized storage oxygen mask was used, which allows the delivery of higher concentrations of oxygen compared with regular masks. Various time gradients (2h, 4h, and 6h) were established, and a randomized controlled trial was conducted to investigate the most effective treatment duration of NBO. This study revealed a significant reduction in infarct volume in both the NBO-4h and NBO-6h groups compared with that in the control group. Importantly, there was no significant difference in infarct volume between the NBO-4h group and the NBO-6h group. Additionally, no significant differences were found at baseline among the groups, which enhances the comparability of the groups and supports the robustness of the results.  

Previous clinical studies on the investigation of NBO therapy applied different treatment regimens.17,26,27  

Singhal et al17 applied NBO therapy consistently for 8 hours at a rate of $45\mathsf{L}/\mathsf{min}$ with a simple face mask and reported a beneficial neuroprotective effect for 1 week, although treatment was found to be ineffective at 90 days. Padma et $a|^{27}$ found NBO therapy for 12 hours at $10\mathsf{L/min}$ with a simple face mask to be ineffective; Mazdeh et $a|^{28}$ found that NBO therapy via a Venturi mask can improve long-term outcomes in patients who have either an ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke with an oxygen saturation set at $50\%$ and continuous oxygen inhalation for 12 hours. Similarly, Cheng et al19 administered NBO via a Venturi mask $(\mathsf{FiO}_{2},50\%;$ flow, $15\mathsf{L}/\mathsf{min})$ for 6 hours after vessel recanalization, concluding that high-flow NBO therapy after endovascular recanalization was safe and effective in improving functional outcomes, decreasing mortality, and reducing infarct volumes in patients with anterior circulation stroke within 6 hours from stroke onset. Furthermore, the PROOF study, an ongoing study by Poli et al,18 investigated the application of NBO before vessel recanalization, with results pending disclosure. Our team conducted the OPENS-1 study (Normobaric Hyperoxia Combined With Reperfusion for Acute Ischemic Stroke), in which NBO therapy was administered in multiple sequential stages—namely, before, during, and after vessel recanalization—using a continuous storage oxygen mask at 10 L/min for 4 hours, demonstrating both the effectiveness and safety of treatment.15 Notably, these studies vary in terms of study populations, ethnicities, and the types of oxygen masks used, so comparisons of results need to be approached with caution.

Henninger et al29 established a model of permanent focal cerebral ischemia to investigate the treatment effect of NBO at different durations of 3 and 6 hours, which showed that NBO therapy for 6 resulted in a significantly better neuroprotection effect, with a $44\%$ reduction in cerebral infarct volume compared with the normoxia group. This effect was reduced by $10\%$ with 3 hours of NBO therapy indicating that increased durations of NBO therapy offer improved neuroprotective effects.

Our study found that both 4 and 6 hours of NBO therapy were more effective in reducing infarct volume and improving neurological function compared with low-flow oxygen therapy or 2 hours of NBO therapy. However, there were no significant differences in effectiveness between the 4- and 6-hour NBO groups. Safety outcomes did not significantly differ among the 2-, 4-, and 6-hour NBO treatment groups. Overall, our results suggest that NBO therapy for 4 to 6 hours is feasible and beneficial for reducing infarct volume and improving neurological outcomes. Considering both effectiveness and safety, a 4-hour duration of oxygen therapy may offer a balanced approach in terms of patient acceptance, cost-effectiveness, and maximizing benefits while ensuring patient tolerance and compliance.  

This study has several strengths. First, it used a randomized controlled design to investigate the effectiveness and safety of different durations of NBO treatment for AIS, enhancing result objectivity and generating robust evidence. Second, the study aimed to identify the optimal duration of NBO treatment specifically in the context of recanalizing occluded blood vessels, setting it apart from previous research17,26,27 that primarily focused on stroke patients without vessel recanalization. Additionally, while previous studies29,30 often relied on animal experiments to study NBO temporal gradients, this study directly examined these gradients in stroke patients, providing results more directly applicable to clinical practice.  

# Limitations  

This study has several limitations: it was a singlecenter exploratory study, potentially introducing statistical biases. The investigation focused on NBO delivery within the 2- to 6-hour time frame, suggesting future studies should explore longer durations like 8 to 10 hours cautiously due to possible increased risks of NBOassociated adverse effects. Additionally, the primary end point was the assessment of 72-hour infarct volume, prompting future research to consider more reliable end points such as changes in penumbra volume and functional outcomes pre- and post-NBO therapy.  

# Conclusions  

In conclusion, the efficacy of NBO varied with treatment duration. Continuous NBO treatment for 4 hours and 6 hours before and after reperfusion was found to be more effective than 2-hour treatment or no oxygen therapy. Larger-scale multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings. These findings provide insights into the clinical application of NBO in managing stroke patients.
