# Cilostazol Versus Aspirin for Secondary Stroke Prevention: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis  

Michelle P. Lin, MD, MPH,\* James F. Meschia, MD,\* Neethu Gopal, MBBS,\* Kevin M. Barrett, MD,\* Owen A. Ross, PhD,† Nilifer Ertekin-Taner, MD, PhD,\*+ and Thomas G. Brott, MD\*  

Objectives: Cilostazol has promise as an alternative to aspirin for secondary stroke prevention given its vasodilatory and anti-inflammatory properties in addition to platelet aggregation inhibition. We aimed to conduct a systematic review and metaanalysis to estimate the efficacy and safety of cilostazol compared to aspirin for stroke prevention in patients with previous stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA). Materials and methods: We searched PubMed and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from 1996 to 2019. Randomized clinical trials that compared cilostazol to aspirin and reported the endpoints of ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage and any bleeding were included. A random-effects estimate was computed based on the Mantel-Haenszel method. The pooled risk estimates with $95\%$ confidence intervals were compared between cilostazol and aspirin. Results: The search identified 5 randomized clinical trials comparing cilostazol vs. aspirin for secondary stroke prevention that collectively enrolled 7240 patients, all from Asian countries (3615 received cilostazol and 3625 received aspirin). Pooled results from the random-effects model showed that cilostazol was associated with significantly lower risk of recurrent ischemic stroke (RR 0.68; $95\%$ CI, 0.54 to 0.87), intracranial hemorrhage (RR 0.42; $95\%$ CI, 0.27 to 0.65) and any bleeding (RR 0.71; $95\%$ CI, 0.55 to 0.91). Conclusions: This meta-analysis suggests that cilostazol is more effective than aspirin in preventing recurrent ischemic stroke with lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage and other bleeding. Since all trials to date are from Asian countries, confirmatory trials of cilostazol for secondary stroke prevention in other populations are needed.

# Introduction  

Long-term risk of recurrent ischemic stroke remains high in contemporary cohorts ranging from $12.9\%$ to $22.3\%$ at 5- years after stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA).1,2 In these studies aspirin lowered the risk of ischemic stroke by  

$17\%$ compared to placebo, but the risk of hemorrhagic stroke was increased by $39\%$ . More recently, the PlateletOriented Inhibition in New TIA and Minor Ischemic Stroke (POINT) trial showed that in patients with minor stroke or ${\mathrm{TIA}},$ aspirin combined with clopidogrel resulted in lower risk of ischemic stroke but was associated with an increased risk of major hemorrhage compared to aspirin alone. The optimal antiplatelet regimen remains unclear, particularly for those with high burden or small-vessel disease and intracranial atherosclerosis.5  

Given the limitation of aspirin due to its side effects especially in the elderly6,7 and the pharmacogenetic variability in response to clopidogrel,8 alternative antiplatelet therapy through the selective phosphodiesterase 3 inhibitor (PDE3), cilostazol, has garnered considerable interest, particularly in Asian countries. Cilostazol has been FDAapproved for treating peripheral artery disease in the United States since 1999, with no labeling specific to prevention of stroke. Cilostazol has been shown to prevent recurrent ischemic stroke without increasing serious bleeding risk compared to aspirin.9,10 Cilostazol could have promise in patients with ischemic stroke and increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage, such as those with cerebral small vessel disease like deep and lobar cerebral microbleeds or white-matter disease of presumed vascular origin.  

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the effectiveness and safety of cilostazol compared to aspirin for stroke prevention in patients with previous stroke or TIA. We hypothesize that cilostazol is more effective in preventing ischemic stroke with lower bleeding risk.  

# Methods  

# Search methods and study selection  

The study was performed in accordance with the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement.11 We systematically searched the PubMed from January 1996 to August 2, 2019 using pre-specified key terms: “stroke”, “cilostazol,” “aspirin”, and limited the search to “humans”, “full-text”, and “randomized-controlled trials”. Inclusion criteria were 1) randomized clinical trial study design, 2) subjects aged ${\geq}18$ years with ischemic stroke, 3) comparisons of cilostazol and aspirin, 4) comparisons of dual antiplatelet therapy with cilostazol and antiplatelet monotherapy other than cilostazol, 5) reporting of the number of subjects with history of ischemic stroke who underwent randomization, 6) reporting the number of events and hazard ratio of prespecified outcomes including recurrent ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, major or any bleeding. We excluded from analysis studies with any of the following characteristics: Nonrandomized trials, results not reported in English, no pre-specified outcomes, pilot studies, methodology papers, or duplicate studies. # Data abstraction and quality assessment  

The outcomes analyzed were recurrent ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, or any bleeding reported in hazard ratios comparing cilostazol and aspirin. We also abstracted data on study and patient characteristics, including countries of recruitment, sample size, duration of follow-up, age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and stroke severity in National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the studies $(\mathrm{ML},\mathrm{NG})$ , and discrepancies were resolved by joint discussion. The Jadad score was used to assess study quality because all included studies were randomized clinical trials (RCTs).12 This 5-point scoring system evaluates the randomization process, blinding, and the description of withdrawals and dropouts (0 worst, 5 best). Bias for each randomized trial, including sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective outcome reporting and other issues, were further using the Cochrane risk of bias tool. Two authors independently reviewed for bias. Any discrepancy was resolved by joint discussion.  

# Statistical analysis  

Data were analyzed according to the intention-to-treat. Pooled, weighted means of patient characteristics including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, and stroke severity were calculated. The primary efficacy end-point was the risk for recurrent ischemic stroke, cilostazol compared to aspirin. The primary safety end-points were the risks of intracranial hemorrhage and any bleeding. Risk ratios with $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) was used as a measure of the association between cilostazol versus aspirin and risk of recurrent ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage and any bleeding.  

We computed a random-effect estimate based on the Mantel-Haenszel method when two or more studies provided sufficient data for a given outcome. Study-level estimates were considered heterogeneous if either the chisquare statistic was significant at the $P<0.10$ level or the $I^{2}$ statistic was greater than $50\%$ . Forest plots were constructed. Publication bias was assessed by visual examination of funnel plots where y-axis represents study precision and the $\mathbf{x}$ -axis shows the effect size of the study outcome. In the absence of publication bias, the studies with high precision would be near the mean, and the studies with low precision would be spread evenly on both sides of the average, creating a funnel shaped distribution. Deviation from the funnel shape is interpreted as publication bias. We also performed a sensitivity analysis to evaluate whether excluding the Cilostazol Stroke Prevention Study for Antiplatelet Combination (CSPS.com), the only dual antiplatelet comparison study in this metaanalysis, may exert a disproportionate influence on the direction of treatment effect, and compared the change in random-effect estimates in a summary table. The Review Manager Software (RevMan 5.3; Cochrane Collaboration) was used for this meta-analysis. The review is registered (Review ID 42871907241935027).  

# Results  

# Study characteristics  

We screened 174 abstracts 137 of which were excluded for not being a randomized controlled trial, and not in the English language. After review of the remaining 37 full articles, 14 were excluded for not having ischemic stroke as the indication for cilostazol, 12 were excluded for not reporting pre-specified outcomes, 4 were excluded for no reporting of data (protocol only), and 2 excluded for duplicate reporting of preliminary data. The included randomized controlled studies were high quality with mean Jadad score of 4.21 (range 3 to 5). Fig. 1 delineates the flow diagram of study selection.  

Our final analysis included 5 randomized-controlled trials with 7,240 subjects with ischemic stroke; 3,615 received cilostazol and 3,625 received aspirin. Baseline study and patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Of the 5 studies, the mean duration of follow-up was 1.82 years (range 0.25 to 2.42 years). Two trails enrolled patients from Japan $\scriptstyle(\mathtt{n}{=}4,551\$ ), 1 from China $({\mathrm{n}}{=}719).$ ), 1 from Korea $\scriptstyle(\mathtt{n}=458)$ ), and 1 from Korea, Hong Kong and the Philippines $(\mathrm{n}{=}1,512)$ . Four trials compared cilostazol to aspirin as monotherapy $(\mathrm{n}{=}5,361)$ ), 1 trial (CSPS.com) compared dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin or clopidogrel with monotherapy of aspirin or clopidogrel $(\mathrm{n}{=}1{,}879)$ ). Mean age was 65.1 years, $31.1\%$ were female sex, $79.3\%$ had hypertension, $31.2\%$ had diabetes, and mean NIHSS was 2.3 points. There were no significant differences in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics among the groups.

# Recurrent ischemic stroke  

Pooling the results from the random-effects models showed that cilostazol compared with aspirin was associated with significantly lower risk of recurrent ischemic stroke (5 studies; $4.81\%$ vs $6.92\%$ ; RR 0.68; $95\%$ CI 0.54 to 0.87) (Fig. 2A). Absolute risk reduction was $2.11\%$ with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 47. No significant heterogeneity among studies was detected $(\mathrm{P}{=}0.25,$ $I^{2}26\%;$ ). There was no evidence of publication bias on funnel plot analysis (Supplemental Figure 1A).  

# Intracranial hemorrhage  

Pooling the results from the random-effects models showed that cilostazol compared with aspirin was associated with significantly lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage (5 studies; $0.74\%$ vs $1.85\%$ ; RR 0.42; $95\%$ CI 0.27 to 0.65) (Fig. 2B). Absolute risk reduction was $1.11\%$ with a NNT of 91. No significant heterogeneity among studies was detected $(\mathrm{P}{=}0.65,$ $I^{2}_{\mathrm{~}}0\%$ ). There was no evidence of publication bias on funnel plot analysis (Supplemental Figure 1B).

# Any bleeding  

Pooling the results from the random-effects models showed that cilostazol compared with aspirin was associated with significantly lower risk of any bleeding event (5 studies; $7.39\%$ vs $10.59\%$ ; RR 0.71; $95\%$ CI 0.55 to 0.91) (Fig. 2C). Absolute risk reduction was $3.21\%$ with a NNT of 31. No significant heterogeneity among studies was detected $(\mathrm{P}{=}0.10,I^{2}48\%)$ . Publication bias is not apparent on funnel plot analysis (Supplemental Figure 1C).  

# Sensitivity analysis  

Given the varied study design comparing cilostazol dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin or clopidogrel monotherapy, sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding CSPS.com to observe the change in the outcomes and heterogeneity measures when random-effect model included only the 4 monotherapy studies. When CSPS.com study was excluded, the effect size of cilostazol vs aspirin in lowering risk of recurrent ischemic stroke was attenuated, but the association remained significant (4 studies; RR 0.78; $95\%$ ${\mathrm{CI~}}0.63$ to 0.97). On the other hand, there was a stronger association with cilostazol treatment and a lower risk of hemorrhage (4 studies; RR 0.36; $95\%$ CI 0.21 to 0.61) and a lower risk of any bleeding (4 studies; RR 0.65; $95\%$ CI 0.56 to 0.76), compared to aspirin. # Discussion  

The current meta-analysis of 5 randomized trials with more than 7000 patients with ischemic stroke from Asian countries demonstrated that patients who received cilostazol had a $30\%$ reduction in recurrent ischemic stroke, $59\%$ reduction in intracranial hemorrhage, and $29\%$ lower risk in any bleeding compared to patients who received aspirin. The quality of evidence for efficacy and safety outcomes was good with Jadad score above 4. It is uncertain, however, whether these findings are generalizable to other race/ethnicity groups as there have not been robust randomized trials on cilostazol for secondary stroke prevention outside of Asian countries.  

The use of antiplatelet agents is recommended to reduce the long-term risk of noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or TIA. However, the guideline by Kernan et al5 and other guidelines1315 have not been specific on how to best individualize the choice of antiplatelet agents on the basis of risk factors, tolerance, brain imaging findings and bleeding profiles. Aspirin is a commonly used antiplatelet agent for primary and secondary stroke prevention, but its benefit must be weighed against its bleeding risks, particularly in the aging population. For primary prevention, the Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly (ASPREE) trial randomized individuals aged 70-years or above to enteric coated aspirin $100\mathrm{mg}$ or placebo. The investigators reported that aspirin failed to prevent cardiovascular events but resulted in significantly increased risk of major bleeding (HR 1.38; $95\%$ CI 1.18 to 1.62) and death (HR 1.14; $95\%$ CI 1.01 to 1.29).6, 7 Similarly, in secondary ischemic stroke prevention, aspirin lowered the risk of ischemic stroke by $17\%$ but the benefit was off-set by a $39\%$ increase in hemorrhagic stroke.3 Recently, the POINT trial showed that combined aspirin and clopidogrel resulted in lower risk of ischemic stroke (HR 0.72; $95\%$ $_{\mathrm{CI}0.56}$ to 0.92; $\mathtt{p=}0.01$ ) but with increased risk of major hemorrhage (HR 2.32; $95\%$ CI 1.10 to 4.87; $\mathtt{p}{=}0.02$ ) compared to aspirin alone in patients with minor stroke or TIA.4 The Cardiovascular Outcomes for People Using Anticoagulation Strategies (COMPASS) trial that enrolled subjects with stable atherosclerotic vascular disease to receive low dose rivaroxaban plus aspirin compared to aspirin alone similarly showed lower risk of ischemic stroke (HR 0.52; $95\%$ CI 0.38 to 0.68; $\mathtt{p}{<}0.01$ ) but with increased risk of major bleeding (HR 2.15; $95\%$ CI 1.60 to 2.89; $\mathrm{p}{<}0.001\$ ). Clearly, the optimal antiplatelet regimen, particularly in individuals at high-risk for cerebral hemorrhage such as those with high burden of cerebral small-vessel disease such as cerebral microbleeds (CMB) and white matter disease (WMD) remains unclear and needs further investigation in well-designed clinical trials. LACI-2 (ISRCTN 14911850) is an ongoing randomized trial assessing cilostazol after lacunar ischemic stroke in the United Kingdom for stroke, cognitive and MRI brain outcomes for cerebral small vessel disease markers; parallel trials in North America are needed.  

The current study showed that cilostazol has an overall good safety profile and appears to be safer than aspirin in reducing intracranial hemorrhage complications. The pleiotropic mechanisms by which cilostazol reduces ischemic stroke and intracranial hemorrhage have been the subject of animal studies1618 but it remains unclear if the same anti-inflammatory, cerebral vasodilatory and neuroprotective effects occur in humans. Translational research is needed to validate these mechanistic properties in human studies, and to elucidate whether cilostazol may indeed modify the progression of cerebral small vessel disease on cognitive and imaging outcome measures.  

The Prevention of Cardiovascular Events in Ischemic Stroke Patients With High Risk of Cerebral Hemorrhage (PICASO) exemplifies a human clinical trial that had selected patients, who may mechanistically benefit from the pleiotropic properties of cilostazol, using neuroimaging. It enrolled patients at high risk of cerebral hemorrhage, of whom more than two thirds of the cohort had advanced cerebral small vessel disease (multiple CMB or moderatehigh burden of WMD). In this very high ICH risk cohort, they found significant reduction in risk of incident stroke (HR 0.67; $95\%$ ${\mathrm{CI}}0.46$ to 0.96; $\mathtt{p=}0.0273,$ ). Further, the risk of ICH was significantly lower in subgroups with multiple CMB (HR 0.008; $95\%$ CI 0.01 to 0.61; $\mathtt{p=}0.01\$ ), and moderate WMH (HR 0.56; $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}0.29\$ to 0.92; $\mathrm{p}{=}0.03$ ).20 Confirmatory randomized trials in assessing safety and efficacy of cilostazol in patients at high risk of ICH and from additional ethnic/racial populations are needed.  

Two recent meta-analyses examined the effect of cilostazol in risk prevention.21,22 Both studies concluded that cilostazol is more effective in preventing ischemic stroke than other antiplatelet agents. One looked primarily at cilostazol as combination (cilostazol plus aspirin or clopidogrel) or monotherapy treatment compared to conventional single antiplatelet therapy for secondary stroke prevention.21 This previous study, however, did not include the CSPS.com, a recently published large cohort comparing cilostazol combination vs monotherapy. Our meta-analysis included CSPS. com study, which contributed 1,879 patients, or constituted $23.7\%$ of our meta-analysis model for recurrent ischemic stroke. We further performed sensitivity analysis with and without CSPS.com study and found that the effect size on the risk reduction of ischemic stroke was increased with the inclusion of CSPS.com (supplemental Table). The other meta-analysis had multiple subgroup analyses looking at various outcomes including recurrent ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, composite of major adverse cardiovascular events, death, cognition, radiological markers of SVD and adverse symptoms.22 The study importantly found that cilostazol is more effective in reducing recurrent ischemic stroke when given as a long-term prevention regimen for 6 or more months as compared to short-term treatment. Our meta-analysis further adds to the literature by reporting on risk of bleeding as neither of these studies reported the risk of any bleeding. We uniquely reported a significant risk reduction of $35\%$ in any bleeding event when cilostazol was used as monotherapy, and $23\%$ lower risk of any bleeding when used as monotherapy or combination treatment.  

The large effect size observed in our meta-analysis, particularly regarding intracranial hemorrhage, is worth emphasizing. This is remarkable when compared to other secondary stroke prevention studies comparing antiplatelet agents. For instance, in the CAPRIE study, the absolute risk reduction of stroke comparing clopidogrel with aspirin was $0.56\%$ , did not achieve statistical significance, and had a relatively high NNT of 179 people.23 In comparison, in the current metaanalysis study, the NNT for ischemic stroke and intracranial hemorrhage were 47 and 91, respectively. Furthermore, our study included only randomized clinical trials such that bias is minimized. All studies scored 3 or above on Jadad scale, suggesting high quality of RCT design and implementation. Lowest scoring study was CSPS.com of 3 due to open-label design. Given researchers and participants were aware of the assigned antiplatelet regimen during the trial, this could introduce bias to detection of events with potential bias favoring cilostazol.  

Our study has several limitations. First, meta-analyses may be biased when the literature search fails to identify all relevant RCTs. To reduce such bias, two reviewers performed independent literature review and bibliographies of each study were screened. Second, the trials analyzed enrolled solely Asian populations and therefore the findings should be interpreted with caution when applied to nonAsian populations. The absorption, metabolism and excretion of cilostazol may be modified by race/ethnicity and environment such as dietary consumption.24 For instance, common polymorphisms in the CYP2C19 gene for clopidogrel metabolism varies by race/ethnicity, noted in approximately $30\%$ of Caucasians, $40\%$ of blacks, and more than $50\%$ of East Asian.25 The pharmacogenetic of cilostazol is less well described but it has been observed that genetic polymorphisms in CYP3A and CYP2C19 genes influence cilostazol pharmacokinetics.26 This is therefore possible that race/ethnicity may influence the effect of cilostazol in lowering ischemic stroke, ICH and bleeding in non-Asian populations but more studies are needed to examine how genetics and environment may affect the metabolism of cilostazol. Furthermore, the potential effect of aspirin resistance on outcome was not assessed in the current study. It is possible that aspirin resistance secondary to poor medication adherence, genetic variability, poor absorption, and high body mass index may have contributed to greater ischemic stroke risk reduction associated to cilostazol when compared to aspirin. A limitation of meta-analysis is that aspirin resistance assay was not performed or reported in the selected studies, so it is possible that aspirin resistance may skew the finding toward the null. Third, the current meta-analysis did not perform subgroup analysis to evaluate the effect of time to randomization after incident stroke and duration of taking study drug on efficacy and safety outcomes. Finally, our study was a study-level rather than patient-level metaanalysis, and thus we were able to analyze only univariate associations without adjusting for patient-specific risk or protective factors for more precise estimates of cilostazol effect.  

# Conclusions  

This meta-analysis suggests cilostazol is more effective than aspirin in the prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke with lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage and bleeding. Confirmatory randomized trials of cilostazol for secondary stroke prevention need to be performed in additional populations, particularly in those with cerebral small vessel disease at higher risk of intracranial hemorrhage and different ethnic/racial groups to determine the generalizability of our findings.
