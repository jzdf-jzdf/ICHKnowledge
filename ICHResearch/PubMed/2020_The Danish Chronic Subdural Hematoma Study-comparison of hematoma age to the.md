# The Danish Chronic Subdural Hematoma Study—comparison of hematoma age to the radiological appearance at time of diagnosis

# Abstract  

Introduction Chronic subdural hematomas (CSDH) show different radiological characteristics on CT scans at the time of diagnosis. The reason for this is largely unknown. We hypothesize that the imaging characteristics reflect a time-linked pathophysiological evolution. We therefore conducted a retrospective study to examine a possible relation between the hematoma age and the radiological subtype of a CSDH.  

Methods Demographic data on patients with CSDH were retrieved from a Danish national cohort from 2010 to 2012. CT scans obtained on admission to a neurosurgical department were categorized as homogenous, separated, mixed, or membranous hematoma subtypes. The time from a known date of head injury to time of diagnostic CT was defined as hematoma age. The hematoma age was correlated to radiological hematoma subtype at the time of diagnosis by analysis of variance testing.  

Results In total, 543 patients were analyzed for hematoma age and classified in the following hematoma subtypes: 231 homogenous, 44 separated, 119 mixed, and 149 membranous. Patients with homogenous, separated, mixed, and membranous hematoma subtypes had a median interval of 37, 36, 40, and 60 days from head injury to diagnostic CT. We found that membranous hematoma is significantly older than other subtypes. Comparison between the other radiological subtypes showed no statistical hematoma age difference. The distribution of radiological subtypes in 590 patients without a known head injury was similar to that of patients with a known head injury. Additionally, we found that hematoma age was significantly younger for patients on antiplatelet medication. Conclusion In this large national cohort, patients with membranous CSDH had a significantly longer interval between head injury and diagnosis compared to other radiological subtypes. This indicates that the radiological appearance of CSDH evolves over time, causing an alteration from different early radiological subtypes to a radiological subtype with membranes.

# Introduction  

The exact pathogenesis of chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) remains unclear, though it is evident that head injury and inflammatory processes are important contributors to the evolvement [4, 6]. In the everyday neurosurgical practice, CSDH is most commonly diagnosed at a late stage, but as some patients have early computed tomography (CT) scans before the diagnostic scan, we observe examples of CSDH following both hygroma, acute subdural hematoma (ASDH) and normal CT just after a head injury (Fig. 1). If one or more primary structural alterations launch the initial event leading to CSDH is therefore not entirely clear, but a recent study supports the notion that ASDH formation is not the only primary event [8].

Once a hematoma is formed, the prevailing pathophysiological theory is that an inflammatory response is initiated by damage to the dural border cells, causing immigration of inflammatory cells and fibroblasts to the subdural space. A healing process is commenced leading to proliferation of border cells and the resulting formation of an inner and outer hematoma membrane. Within the hematoma membranes, fragile sinusoidal vessels with dysfunctioning endothelial cell junctions are developed, and repeated multifocal bleedings from these vessels cause enlargement of the CSDH [4, 7].  

CT remains the preferred radiological tool for CSDH diagnosis. CT verified CSDH may present a variety of imaging characteristics and it is well-known that the radiological subtype of the same CSDH may change over time [11]. Moreover, different subtypes seem to represent different inflammatory chemical compositions of CSDH [5].  

As inflammatory reaction typically evolves over time, we hypothesize that the different CT subtypes represent different stages of CSDH in a sequential time-linked pathogenesis.  

# Methods  

This study was performed by the Danish Chronic Subdural Hematoma Study (DACSUHS). DACSUHS is a national steering group with participants from all neurosurgical departments in Denmark. DACSUHS coordinates CSDH management and research in Denmark [12].  

This study is a sub-study of a retrospective multicenter cohort study including all Danish neurosurgical departments: Rigshospitalet University Hospital, Odense University Hospital, Aarhus University Hospital, and Aalborg University Hospital.  

Clinical and demographic data on all Danish patients admitted to a neurosurgical department with CSDH were retrospectively recorded for a 3-year period (2010–2012). Patient data included age, sex, use of antithrombotic (AT) medication, history of head injury, comorbidity, previous neurosurgery, radiological hematoma subtype, hematoma volume, midline shift on CT, and time from head injury to CT defined as hematoma age. AT was subclassified into the following groups: anticoagulant treatment (AC), antiplatelet treatment (AP), and combined anticoagulant and antiplatelet treatment $(\mathrm{AC}+\mathrm{AP})$ .  

This analysis of hematoma age included the subset of the cohort with a recorded date of a head injury prior to the diagnosis. Patients with bilateral hematomas were excluded, if the two hematomas were of different subtype. Also, patients were excluded if more than one head injury were described, or if the head injury was more than a year from the diagnosis. Hematoma age of CSDH from patients without a known head injury date could not be established and the CT appearance of the CSDH from these patients could only be assessed for radiological subtype. The distribution of radiological subtypes was calculated for this group of patients for reference.  

# Radiological hematoma subtypes  

Classification of CSDH subtypes was based on the initial CT and depended on the architecture of each hematoma. The classification was done by one observer to avoid interobserver variability. The CSDH’s were classified into four subtypes: homogenous, separated (also known as layered), mixed and membranous. The homogeneous type was defined as a hematoma that displayed homogeneous density. Homogenous hematomas could be hyper-, iso-, or hypodense. The separated type was defined as a hematoma containing two components of different densities with no membranes. The hematoma was defined as mixed when it consisted of a combination of acute and chronic bleeding with or without membranes. The membranous type was defined as inhomogeneous hematoma with a high-density membrane in the subdural space running between the inner and outer membrane. For radiological examples, see Fig. 2.

# Statistical analysis  

![](images/35261d52420c7cc5c201a5b5094b351b205140a6ae4942736516be7a7a3834f1.jpg)  
Fig. 2 Example of the radiological subtypes on CT scan: homogenous (a), membranous (b), layered (c), and mixed hematoma (d). Randomly selected from the analyzed cohort  

Hematoma age showed a non-normal distribution. The variable was log-transformed to meet assumptions of normal distribution. For comparisons of means between groups, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was applied. Residual plots were used to test for linearity and homogeneity of variance. To perform pairwise comparisons and account for multiple testing, a Tukey test was applied. Distribution of baseline variables between radiological CSDH subtypes was tested using a chi-square test for categorical variables and ANOVA test for continuous variables. Variables showing an unequal distribution $(p<0.10)$ among hematoma subtypes were subsequently used in a generalized linear model to investigate other potential predictors of hematoma age. Residual plots of the multivariate model were used to test for linearity and homogeneity of variance. All analyses were performed using the statistical software package R (http://www.r-project.org), and a $p$ value $<0.05$ was considered statistically significant.  

# Results  

# Baseline characteristics  

In the period 2010–2012, 1164 patients with CSDH were identified, and of these, 573 had a documented date of head injury. Twenty-six patients with dissimilar bilateral hematomas were excluded. Additional three patients were excluded due to head injury more than 1 year prior to the diagnostic CT scan and one patient was excluded due to missing radiological data. Finally, 543 patients were included for analysis. Baseline clinical and radiological variables are shown in Table 1. Of the 543 patients, 396 $72.8\%)$ were male. Mean age was 71.4 years for men and 74.1 years for women. AT treatment was used by 215 $(39.6\%)$ of the patients upon admission. The distribution of used AT and/or AC medication was 133 $(24.5\%)$ patients received AP, 63 $(11.6\%)$ patients received AC, and 19 $(3.5\%)$ patients received $\mathrm{AC}+\mathrm{AP}$ upon admission.

Regarding radiological subtypes, 231 patients had homogenous, 44 separated, 119 mixed, and 149 membranous hematomas (Table 2). The median hematoma age was 37, 36, and 40 days for homogenous, separated, and mixed hematomas, respectively, and 60 days for patients with membranous hematomas.  

Distribution of hematoma subtypes for patients without a known head injury is listed in Table 2 for comparison.  

# Univariate analysis  

Between the different hematoma subtypes, membranous hematomas were significantly older compared to the other three hematoma subtypes (membranous vs. homogenous: odds ratio (OR) 1.46 ( $95\%$ CI 1.20–1.79, $p<0.0001;$ , membranous vs. separated: OR 1.68 $(95\%\mathrm{CI}1.23-2.32,p<0.0001)$ , membranous vs. mixed: OR 0.67 $(95\%\mathrm{CI}0.54-0.84,p<0.0001)$ . Comparison of the other hematoma subtypes revealed no statistically significant difference (Table 3). Distribution of the use of antithrombotic agents (AT) divided by hematoma subtype was tested, and only use of AT and age were found to have unequal distribution at the significance level $p<0.10$ (Table 4). Consequently, use of AT, age, and hematoma subtype were included in a multivariate analysis.  

# Multivariate analysis  

In a multivariate generalized linear model, membranous subtype and use of AP where significantly associated to hematoma age. The model found membranous hematomas to be significantly older (OR 1.47, $95\%$ CI 1.26–1.71) compared to the homogenous subtype. The analysis is similar to the univariate ANOVA test whereas this analysis includes adjustment for confounders as AT use and age. When correlating use of AT to hematoma age, use of AC, AP, or $\mathrm{AC}+\mathrm{AP}$ was associated with decreasing hematoma age with OR 0.93 (CI 0.74), 0.83 (0.71–0.95) and 0.79 (0.55–1.08), respectively. However, only AP use was significantly associated with hematoma age $\left(p=0.0089\right)$ (Table 5).

# Discussion  

Our hypothesis that the radiological subtype of CSDH at time of diagnosis simply reflects different stages of continuous pathophysiological events could not be confirmed. However, the membranous type was associated with longer time from head injury to the diagnostic CT scan. This leaves homogeneous, mixed, and separated subtypes as earlier stages, possibly representing different pathophysiological pathways to the late stage membranous type.  

Studies on subdural hematoma age determination based on radiological appearance have shown variation in clinical practice among neuroradiologist regarding the different hematoma subtypes [14]. As such, large variation in clinical practice makes the radiological statement with respect to dating a CSDH less valuable in, e.g., legal matters in which different conclusions may be drawn [2]. Beside contributing to the general knowledge of CSDH development, this study may assist in cases of abuse or work-related injury. An association between a specific event and a CSDH may be difficult to determine as larger part of the pathogenesis remains unclear including the uncertainty about the involvement of head injuries. Already, a time-linked pathophysiology has been found between the content of subdural hematomas and time from the bleeding [13], and this study confirms such a time-linked CSDH development on a radiological level.  

Another discovery worth highlighting from this study is the association between AT treatment and hematoma age. We found that hematoma age was younger for patients with AT treatment, though only AP stood out significantly. It is wellknown that AT is a risk factor for CSDH development [16]. Hence, it is likely that patients with AT medication more rapidly develop CSDH expansion and thereby faster present with symptoms. Different pathophysiological pathways in the early stage can be explained by extrinsic (type of head injury, medication, hygroma, or ASDH as the initial change) or intrinsic (individual inflammatory response type, possible spontaneous formation, etc.) factors. The reason that the precise mechanism for CSDH formation has evaded detection for so many years may in fact be several different early pathophysiological mechanisms, and that CSDH therefore may not be one stereotypic disease process, but several different pathophysiological cascades leading to a growing subdural collection and a common clinical presentation due to mass effect. Our data support the theory of homogeneous, separated, and mixed hematomas as an early stage of CSDH development. Our results therefore also support the current concepts of CSDH pathophysiology in which fibrinolysis, inflammation, and angiogenesis are considered central mechanisms [4]. In fibrinolysis, fibrin is broken down to fibrinogen degradation products (FDPs) facilitating clot-breakdown. The levels of fibrin and FDPs are found to be higher in mixed and separated hematomas [3, 10]. Separated hematomas are the subtype found to have the highest recurrence rate, and very high levels of FDPs are correlated with the amount of recent hemorrhage suggesting that fibrinolysis encourages rebleeding [9]. Likely, separated and mixed hematomas are an early step in CSDH development where bleeding is ongoing. This would explain the high recurrence rate in separated hematomas when operation is performed at this stage, as bleeding may persist postoperatively, causing recurrence.

We found membranous hematomas to represent a late stage in CSDH development. This is in accordance with the general assumption regarding hematoma membrane formation, though most studies on CSDH membranes concerns the outer and inner membrane and not the membranes within the hematoma. It is possible that the pathophysiology is similar, though it has been suggested that membranes surrounding the hematoma are fully developed within 3 weeks [15]. In the present study, we show that the membranes within the hematoma are on average formed several weeks later, maybe due to delayed, but ongoing inflammatory and angiogenetic processes, similar to those of the surrounding membranes. Alternatively, the internal hematoma membranes are formed by a yet unknown mechanism.  

Whether a head injury is required for formation of a CSDH is still an open question. As in previous studies, roughly half the patients or relatives in our cohort could remember and date a head injury prior to diagnosis [1]. The distribution of hematomas was similar with or without a known head injury. Accepting that no clear conclusion can be drawn from this, it still suggests that the span of pathophysiological events is similar to those after a recorded head injury, even if a head injury is not recorded prior to diagnosis.  

# Strengths and limitations  

The retrospective character of this study is an obvious limitation. Also, separation of hematoma subtypes in this study is limited to homogenous, membranous, separated, and mixed. This subdivision is in line with other studies, but previous studies also suggest a laminar and gradation subtype [1, 9]. A further subdivision could be valuable to gain a more detailed perspective but would potentially weaken the conclusion drawn from this study, as the classification will be less stringent, the more classes are introduced. Also, this study does not have a longitudinal setting. As such, it remains ambiguous whether the membranous subtype is developed at a later stage in a continuous pathophysiological process, or if the membranous subtype is prone to be diagnosed at a later stage. To fully clarify this aspect, studies of patients with conservatively treated subdural hematomas and prospective radiological follow-up are required.  

It is a strength of the study that the cohort is a large complete national 3-year cohort from a country with welldeveloped IT infrastructure, ensuring that very few data have been lost.  

# Conclusion  

This study shows that the radiological membranous subtype of CSDH occurs significantly later after head injury, than three other radiological subtypes. This suggests several early pathophysiological pathways in CSDH formation. Furthermore, hematomas from patients on antiplatelet treatment were significantly younger suggesting a more rapid CSDH development in these patients.

Comments Chronic Subdural Hematomas (CSDH) are typically diagnosed by a computed tomography (CT) scan after head injury. Nevertheless, the appearance at time of diagnosis is not uniform, and while little is known about the time correlation between the radiological appearance and the head injury, it is an issue often debated in i.e. legal matters. In this study, the authors performed a large nation-wide study aimed at correlating radiological phenotype of CSDH to the time of head injury, while strengthening their analysis with a regression model.  

The authors identify membranous CSDH as having longer time from head injury to diagnosis than homogenous, separated and mixed CSDH. As such, the study provides simple but clinically relevant and novel insights in the field of CSDH.
