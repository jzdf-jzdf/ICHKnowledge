# Abstract  

Introduction: The use of alteplase (ALT) bridging to endovascular mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has become the standard approach in treating patients with large vessel occlusion (LVO) stroke. Tenecteplase (TNK) has emerged as an equivalent fibrinolytic agent in treating ischemic stroke due to its remarkable pharmacological characteristics. This study aimed to compare the use of intravenous TNK to ALT bridging to MT in patients with LVO. Methods: We included observational and randomized controlled trials of patients with LVO who received bridging TNK versus ALT before undergoing MT. Efficacy outcomes included functional independence which is indicated by a modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score of 0–2 at 90 days. Radiological outcomes included the rate of successful recanalization post-MT (Modified Treatment in Cerebral Ischemia [mTICI] score of 2b/3) and the rate of pre-MT recanalization, indicated by an mTICI of $2\mathrm{b}/3$ at the first angiographic assessment. The all-cause mortality at 90 days (mRS of 6) was considered the primary safety outcome, while the symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rate was reported as an adverse event. Results: We identified 5 comparative observational studies and 1 randomized controlled trial, totaling 4,186 patients with LVO. The crude odds ratio (OR) for post-MT recanalization in patients with LVO who received TNK was comparable to those who received ALT $\mathrm{\Delta}Q\mathsf{R}=1.14$ ; $95\%$ CI: 0.57–2.27, $P^{2}=54\%)$ . The rate of pre-MT recanalization was significantly higher in those given TNK as a bridging therapy to MT compared to those who received ALT $(\mathsf{OR}=2.66;95\%$ CI: 1.60–4.41, $I^{2}=0\%;$ $p<0.001.$ ). Functional independence at 90 days was not significantly different between patients with stroke who received TNK and those who were given ALT before MT $\mathsf{\Gamma}(\mathsf{OR}=$ 1.41; $95\%$ CI: 0.84–2.35; $P^{2}=45\%_{\cdot}^{\cdot}$ ). The 90-day mortality was similar between patients with LVO who received TNK and those who were given ALT prior to undergoing MT $\left(\mathrm{OR}=0.74;\right.$ $95\%$ CI: 0.46–1.21; $I^{2}=0\%)$ . Conclusion: Patients with LVO who received TNK as the primary fibrinolytic agent bridging to MT demonstrated higher rates of pre-MT recanalization, similar rates in post-MT recanalization and equivalent functional independence outcomes at 90 days compared to those who received ALT. The administration of TNK before MT showed comparable results in the 90-day all-cause mortality rate compared to those who received ALT. These results warrant further trials for TNK to be used as a superior fibrinolytic agent to ALT in LVO-MT candidates.

# Introduction  

Endovascular mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has emerged as a standard therapeutic approach for treating patients with large vessel occlusion (LVO) stroke, resultant from its compelling clinical outcomes [1–6]. Subsequent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared the use of direct MT, bypassing the administration of intravenous (IV) thrombolysis, with the use of IV thrombolysis as bridging therapy with MT in patients experiencing acute vessel occlusion [7–10]. Importantly, these specific RCTs solely utilized alteplase (ALT) as the primary IV thrombolytic agent.  

ALT necessitates infusion over an approximate duration of $^{1\mathrm{~h~}}$ and has demonstrated limited efficacy in achieving recanalization in LVO prior to thrombectomy, as evidenced by various trial findings [2–4]. In contrast, tenecteplase (TNK) represents a genetically engineered derivative of ALT and is characterized by heightened fibrin specificity and a prolonged half-life facilitating rapid bolus administration [11]. This unique pharmacokinetic profile allows for quicker delivery of TNK in comparison to ALT. Comprehensive meta-analyses have consistently supported the noninferiority of TNK to ALT in terms of safety, and in some instances, its superiority in achieving favorable outcomes in treating ischemic strokes [12–16]. Since the emergence of these promising results, multiple studies have been conducted to assess the efficacy and safety of administering TNK rather than ALT before undergoing MT in patients with LVO. The current review and meta-analysis aimed to examine the recently published literature and conduct a comparative evaluation of TNK and ALT as bridging strategies preceding endovascular MT in patients diagnosed with LVO.  

# Methodology  

# Search Strategy  

Following the guidelines set forth in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 framework for conducting systematic reviews, we executed a comprehensive systematic review of the use of TNK before MT in patients with LVO stroke [17]. The registration of this review can be found in the Prospero registry: CRD42023447960. A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, MEDLINE, Central, Scopus, and Web of Science databases was conducted from inception till May 2023, using diverse alterations of keywords and MeSH terms tailored to the individual characteristics of each database included. Mesh terms included “Stroke,” “Large Vessel Occlusion,” “Intravenous Thrombolysis,” “Bridging Therapy,” “Alteplase,” “Tenecteplase,” “Mechanical Thrombectomy,” “Endovascular Thrombectomy.” References cited within the included studies were manually screened to identify any relevant literature that might have been overlooked. Screening of clinical trial registries was also completed in order to ensure that every trial was included in the study.  

Two independent authors conducted a thorough search within the included databases. This review included cohort studies (both prospective and retrospective) and RCTs that reported patients with LVO who underwent bridging therapy with either TNK or ALT before undergoing MT. Case reports, case series, and conference abstracts were excluded. We incorporated studies that conducted a comparative analysis between TNK and ALT. Studies reporting findings of either agent alone or did not report the use of MT were excluded. Our review was limited to studies published in the English language.  

# Risk of Bias Assessment  

The ROBINS-1 tool assesses bias in nonrandomized studies and examines domains such as confounding, participant selection, and outcome measurement [18]. The RoB2 Cochrane tool evaluates bias in RCTs and considers randomization, intervention deviations, and outcome reporting [19]. In our review, both tools were employed to assess the risk of bias for each included study by two independent reviewers, and any inconsistencies between the two assessors were addressed through reaching a consensus following consultation with a third investigator. The results from the risk of bias testing are included in the supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000536669).  

# Data Extraction and Assessment  

The primary outcome of the study was successful recanalization after MT, which is determined by a post-MT Modified Treatment in Cerebral Ischemia (mTICI) score of 2b/3, indicating more than $50\%$ reperfusion of the ischemic territory [20]. The rate of pre-MT recanalization, defined as a mTICI score of $2\mathrm{b}/3$ at the initial angiographic assessment, thus averting the need for MT, was another primary focus in this study. Favorable functional outcome at 3 months after stroke onset was considered in this study, using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores from 0 to 2 [21]. Due to the lack of a standard definition of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), the SITS-MOST criteria were utilized for the identification of sICH [22]. If a study employed the ECASS criteria, we only pooled patients with parenchymal hematoma type 2 alongside those having subarachnoid hemorrhage avoiding the overestimation of sICH rates as the ECASS criteria is more inclusive [23]. All-cause mortality at 90 days was our primary safety outcome. If any of the eligible studies lacked data on any of the outcomes, efforts were made to contact the corresponding authors to acquire the missing data.  

Two authors, M.B. and Y.A., independently extracted the data from each study, including study design, patient eligibility criteria, patient characteristics, baseline parameters, time intervals from stroke onset to M.T., radiological outcomes, functional outcomes, safety outcomes, and adverse events. These variables were pooled for analysis. In instances where disparities arose between the two authors, a third reviewer, M.A., intervened to facilitate resolution through discussion.  

# Statistical Analysis  

For each outcome, we gathered and pooled unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) using the random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird). The existence of heterogeneity across the studies’ outcomes was assessed using Cochran Q and ${\dot{I}}^{2}$ statistics, where an $I^{2}$ value surpassing $50\%$ was suggestive of substantial heterogeneity [24]. If significant heterogeneity is observed, a sensitivity analysis is used to identify potential causes. Publication bias assessment was not conducted due to the limited number of studies included in the meta-analysis. All statistical analyses were executed using Review Manager, RevMan 5.4, software.  

# Results  

# Characteristics of Included Studies  

We initially identified a total of 1,038 records. Following evaluation of titles, abstracts, and full-text reports and subsequent removal of duplicates, five eligible studies met our inclusion criteria for the quantitative synthesis [25–29]. Due to the large number of stroke patients in the Qureshi et al.’s [30] study, we included it in the review, but it was not quantitatively analyzed as the reported outcomes did not completely meet our predefined criteria (Fig. 1). A list of the excluded studies is provided in the supplementary material (online suppl. Table 2). Patient characteristics of these studies are presented in Table 1. We analyzed data from 4,186 patients with stroke. Among them, 454 received TNK as bridging therapy prior to MT, while 3,732 received ALT. Percentage of males were similar in both groups $51\%$ in TNK and $50.8\%$ in ALT). Median baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores were slightly higher in the TNK group, ranging from 14 to 19.5 compared to the ALT group, which was from 13.5 to 17. The majority of patients in both groups had anterior circulation strokes, accounting for $65{-}98\%$ in TNK and $72.5{-}99\%$ in ALT. Posterior circulation strokes were less common, ranging from $1{-}11.4\%$ in TNK and $1{-}11\%$ in ALT.  

# Risk of Bias Results  

Following bias assessment, one study had low risk of bias [25], two had moderate risk of bias [26, 27], two exhibited a serious risk of bias [28, 30], while one had undetermined risk due to insufficient information provided [29]. The primary cause was often the absence of acknowledgment of potential confounding variables; these include baseline NIHSS scores on hospital admission and the time intervals from stroke onset to MT. In other cases, bias arose from inadequate reporting within the desired subgroup of patients receiving bridging therapy and MT for stroke. Due to a lack of consensus, inconsistency in the definition of sICH across the included studies was noted. This inconsistency resulted in significant discrepancies in the reported sICH rates, potentially introducing bias into the analysis. Additional details regarding the risk of bias are available in the supplementary material (online suppl. Table 1).  

# Radiological and Clinical Outcomes  

Study outcomes of the included studies are presented in Table 2. The crude OR for post-MT recanalization (mTICI of 2b/3) in patients with LVO who underwent bridging therapy with TNK demonstrated comparable results to those who received ALT, 85.54 and $83.33\%$ , respectively $(\mathrm{OR}=1.14\$ ; $95\%$ CI: $0.57{-}2.27$ , $I^{2}=54\%^{\cdot}$ . The rate of preMT recanalization, averting the need for MT, was, however, found to be clinically significant among the TNK group, $21.82\%$ in the TNK group, and $9.49\%$ in the ALT group $(\mathrm{OR}=2.66\$ ; $95\%$ CI: 1.60–4.41, $I^{2}=0\%$ ; $p<0.001;$ . The difference in functional independence at 90 days $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq2)$ ) between patients receiving TNK and those receiving ALT was deemed clinically insignificant $\mathrm{(OR=}$ 1.41; $95\%$ CI: 0.84–2.35; $I^{2}=45.1\%$ ) (Fig. 2a–c).  

# Safety Outcomes  

There was no difference in the odds of 90-day all-cause mortality between those who received TNK and those who were given ALT before MT $\mathrm{{'OR}=0.74}$ ; $95\%$ CI: 0.46–1.21; $\bar{I}^{2}=0\%$ ). Intracranial hemorrhage outcomes are discussed in Table 3 (Fig. 2d).  

# Sensitivity Analysis  

The inclusion of randomized and nonrandomized studies in this meta-analysis could potentially be the cause of the notable heterogeneity in the post-MT recanalization and functional independence outcomes. To address this limitation, a sensitivity analysis was conducted (Table 4, 5, online suppl. Tables 3–4). This analysis revealed that the inclusion of Hendrix et al.’s study significantly contributed to this heterogeneity. Excluding the study from the analysis not only reduced the heterogeneity, ${\dot{I}}^{2}$ value from $54\%$ to $29\%$ in post-MT recanalization and from $45\%$ to $0\%$ in functional independence outcomes, but also resulted in a statistically significant improvement in the functional independence outcome $(\mathrm{OR}=1.41\$ ; $95\%$ Cl: 0.84–2.35, $I^{2}=45\%;$ to $\mathrm{'OR}=1.79$ ; $95\%$ Cl: 1.14–2.81, $I^{2}=0\%$ ; $p<0.05)$ . Further examination identified several confounding factors. Hendrix et al. [27] failed to address the cause of the LVO stroke. Moreover, the ALT cohort had higher baseline NIHSS scores, more ICA occlusions, and a higher number of patients presenting primarily at a comprehensive stroke center, whereas the TNK group had worse

# Discussion  

Our systematic review and meta-analysis compared TNK to ALT in patients with LVO stroke undergoing MT. Our findings demonstrate that both the TNK and ALT groups exhibited similar rates of successful recanalization post-MT but the former showed a significant increase in the rate of pre-MT recanalization. Both cohorts showed similar levels of 90-day functional independence. Regarding safety outcomes, the 90-day allcause mortality rates were similar between the two agents.

When examining the baseline characteristics of the studies included, it is important to highlight the distinct features of each. Campbell et al. conducted an RCT, whereas the other studies relied on retrospective cohort designs [25–30]. Most of the included studies reported a dose of $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ for TNK, with the exception of Qurashi et al. study which lacked dose specifications [30]. The baseline NIHSS scores, a potentially confounding variable, were not accounted for in the Ostos et al. and Qureshi et al. studies [29, 30]. With the exception of Campbell et al., Teivane et al., and Hendrix et al., the time intervals from stroke onset to MT were inconsistently reported across studies [25–27]. The time from primary presentation to comprehensive stroke center was significantly higher in patients who received TNK compared to those who received ALT in Hendrix et al. [27] study. Discrepancies in the prevalence of risk factors among the studies’ cohorts possibly introduced a confounding bias. Hendrix et al. [27] reported a significant distinction in hypertension rates between the two groups, favoring the ALT group. In Teivene et al.’s [26] study, the ALT cohort exhibited more cases of congestive heart disease, whereas the TNK cohort had a higher proportion of patients with previous ischemic strokes. Complicating the bias assessment, Ostos et al. and Qureshi et al. examined bridging therapy cohorts as subgroups, leading to limited reporting of the important variables [29, 30].  

Although the mTICI outcomes varied among the studies, all five studies documented an insignificant rise in the recanalization rate among individuals who received TNK [25–29]. The rate of pre-MT recanalization demonstrated significance in favor of TNK prior to MT across all three studies [25–27]. Campbell et al. [25] noted a significantly worse median 90-day mRs score for those who received ALT. In contrast, Hendrix et. al reported a similar median mRs scores at 90 days for both the TNK and ALT cohorts [27]. In terms of functional independence outcomes, both Campbell et al. and Teivane et al. observed clinically insignificant better results for those who received TNK than those who received ALT [25, 26]. However, this trend did not hold true in Hendrix’s study, as the ALT cohort demonstrated an insignificant better functional independence outcomes compared to the TNK cohort [27]. Teivane et al. [26] noted patients who received TNK prior to MT exhibited a statistically significant improvement in functional independence outcomes upon discharge.  

The 90-day mortality rate among patients who received TNK as a bridging therapy was similar to that for patients bridged with ALT in all 4 studies [25–28]. The lack of standardized assessment methods resulted in a wide range of reported sICH rates across studies. For patients receiving TNK, rates varied from $1\%$ in Campbell’s trial to $30\%$ in Estella’s cohort, whereas for those given ALT, rates ranged from $1\%$ in Campbell’s trial to $15\%$ in Estella’s cohort [25–28]. All studies reporting ICH incidence showed equivalent ORs for patients receiving either agent [25–28, 30].  

In our research, we observed heterogeneity in outcomes among patients who received TNK and ALT prior to MT in LVO, specifically in post-MT recanalization and functional independence. It is important to acknowledge the differences in baseline characteristics of patients across the included studies. This heterogeneity in postMT recanalization outcome may arise from inconsistencies in reporting critical variables like the time from stroke onset to MT, stroke etiology, and subtle variations in occlusion sites. Additionally, the methods for assessing functional independence using the mRs score differed among the included studies, potentially contributing to this heterogeneity. Lastly, the inclusion of retrospective studies in our analysis introduces a risk of hidden biases, which could affect the overall validity of our findings.

Patients with LVO stroke have better functional outcomes when early and complete recanalization is accomplished [31]. In a meta-analysis led by Tsivgoulis et al. [32], the utilization of tPA before MT in patients with LVO stroke resulted in pre-MT recanalization in just  

1 out of every 10 cases. It is important to note that the meta-analysis exclusively included studies that utilized ALT as the primary bridging therapy agent. When comparing TNK and ALT, the AcT trial conducted by Menon et al. [33] showed similar pre-MT recanalization rates for both TNK and ALT in treating patients with LVO stroke. Although, Checkouri et al. [34] demonstrated equivalent overall early recanalization rates between the TNK and ALT groups, it is important to note that in large thrombi strokes ${>}10\mathrm{mm}$ , TNK proved to be superior to ALT. Moreover, a pooled analysis conducted by Yogendrakumar et al. [35] from the Melbourne Stroke Registry, EXTEND-IA, and EXTEND-IA TNK trials, as well as our analysis, revealed markedly increased pre-MT recanalization rates when TNK was administered before MT in patients with LVO stroke compared to ALT. Achieving recanalization through bridging therapy before MT was linked to superior functional outcomes compared to those achieving recanalization solely after MT [35]. These positive results can potentially lead to better patient outcomes due to averting endovascular procedures with their site-access and thrombectomy-related complications. Another benefit of the early recanalization associated with TNK is the decrease of financial burdens that come with using MT.

It is important to examine the economic implications and cost-effectiveness of these thrombolytic agents. A post hoc analysis of the EXTENDED-IA TNK trial by Gao et al. [36] has shown that using TNK before MT is highly likely to be cost-effective in both the short-term and the long-term in the management of acute stroke cases requiring bridging therapy before MT. A separate post hoc analysis conducted by Goa et al., evaluating the cost-effectiveness of administering TNK versus ALT in ambulance settings, has demonstrated TNK’s superior long-term cost-effectiveness [37]. Nepal et al. [38] revealed a disconcerting one-third of their stroke cohort, despite meeting the thrombolysis time window, was denied ALT due to not being able to afford it. While TNK has both enhanced thrombolytic properties and superior cost-effectiveness, ALT still maintains its position as the only FDA-approved thrombolytic agent for ischemic stroke. Nevertheless, the economic advantages with the demonstrated superiority of intravenous TNK prior to MT in patients with LVO stroke highlight a compelling argument for the switch from the conventional ALT.  

# Strengths and Limitations  

Our systematic review and meta-analysis stand out as the first to compare the use of TNK versus ALT prior to MT in LVO stroke, marking a significant strength in this area of research. However, it is important to acknowledge several limitations that may impact the interpretation of our findings. The primary limitation of our study is the inclusion of both randomized and nonrandomized studies, which introduces a degree of variability in the data, and unavoidable bias due to the inherit nature of the nonrandomized studies. Additionally, the analysis is based on a small number of papers, which may not have had enough statistical power to identify minor effect sizes. Lastly, our review is limited to English-only literature, which may exclude relevant studies published in other languages, thus potentially introducing bias to our findings.  

# Conclusion  

This meta-analysis indicates that utilizing TNK as a bridging treatment for patients with LVO stroke yields similar effectiveness and safety results compared to ALT. Moreover, we demonstrated that TNK outperforms ALT for greater pre-MT recanalization rates before MT. This outcome could offer a way for patients to completely avoid the need for MT and its associated risks. Additional research and randomized trials are warranted for further investigation in this domain.
