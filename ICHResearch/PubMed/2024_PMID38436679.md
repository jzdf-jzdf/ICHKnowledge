# Tenecteplase versus alteplase for acute ischemic stroke: a systematic review and meta‑analysis of randomized and non‑randomized studies

# Abstract  

Objective  Alteplase is the current standard of care for acute ischemic stroke. Tenecteplase is a newer fibrinolytic agent with preferable administration and lower costs; however, its comparative effectiveness to alteplase remains uncertain. We set out to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis to establish the benefits and harms of tenecteplase versus alteplase for acute ischemic stroke.  

Methods  We searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and ClinicalTrials. gov from inception to April 2023 for randomized and non-randomized studies that compared tenecteplase versus alteplase for acute ischemic stroke. Paired reviewers independently assessed risk of bias and extracted data. We performed both conventional meta-analyses and Bayesian network meta-analyses (NMA) with random-effects models and used the GRADE approach to evaluate the certainty of evidence. Our primary efficacy outcome was excellent functional outcome at 3 months, defined as a score of 0–1 on the modified Rankin Scale. Our primary safety outcomes were symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage and all-cause mortality.  

Results  Thirty-six studies were eligible for review, including 12 randomized $(n=5533$ ) and 24 non-randomized studies $(n=44,956)$ . Moderate certainty evidence showed that there was no difference between tenecteplase and alteplase in increasing the proportion of patients achieving excellent functional outcome at 3 months (odds ratio [OR], 1.10; $95\%$ CI  0.98–1.23; risk difference [RD] $2.4\%$ , $95\%$ CI − 0.5 to 5.2), while moderate certainty evidence from NMA suggested that $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase significantly improved excellent functional outcome at 3 months (OR, 1.16; $95\%$ credible interval 1.02–1.32). Moderate certainty evidence showed that, compared to alteplase, tenecteplase may make little to no difference in the prevalence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (OR, 1.12; $95\%$ CI 0.79–1.59; RD $0.3\%$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}-0.5$ to 1.4), and probably reduces all-cause mortality (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.44; $95\%$ CI 0.30–0.64; $\mathrm{RD}-4.6\%$ ; $95\%$ CI − 5.8 to − 2.9).  

Conclusions  Moderate certainty evidence suggested that there was little to no difference between tenecteplase and alteplase in increasing the proportion of patients achieving excellent functional outcome at 3 months and the risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, while compared to alteplase, tenecteplase probably reduce all-cause mortality. Administration of $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase after acute ischemic stroke is suggestive of increasing the proportion of patients that achieve excellent functional outcome at 3 months.  

Keywords  Acute ischemic stroke $^{\ast}$ Tenecteplase $^{\ast}$ Alteplase $\cdot$ Meta-analysis  

# Introduction  

There are estimated 14 million stroke cases per year worldwide, and ischemic strokes constitute $62{-}87\%$ of all strokes [1–3]. Intravenous thrombolysis by administration of alteplase $(0.9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ to a maximum of $90~\mathrm{mg}$ , $0.09\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{kg}$ as a bolus and then a $60\mathrm{min}$ infusion of the remaining $0.81\mathrm{mg/kg})$ is the standard practice for patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [4], and is strongly recommended by the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) and the European Stroke Organization (ESO) [5, 6]. Tenecteplase is a newer-generation fibrinolytic agent [5, 6] which exhibits 14-fold greater relative fibrin specificity and 80-fold higher resistance to inhibition by plasminogen activator inhibitor type 1, longer half-life, more preferable administration (i.e., a single intravenous bolus in 5–10 s vs. 1 h for alteplase) and lower costs than alteplase [7–13]. However, the AHA/ASA and ESO have made only a weak recommendation for the use of tenecteplase to treat AIS on the basis of low certainty evidence for benefits and concerns about increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) versus alteplase [5, 14–26].  

Despite this uncertainty, there is increasing use of tenecteplase in stroke centers worldwide [27–29]. Several pivotal randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and large observational studies have been recently published comparing tenecteplase with alteplase [17, 18, 25, 30–32]. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review of randomized and non-randomized studies to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of tenecteplase versus alteplase for AIS.

# Patient and public involvement  

A guideline panel prioritized outcomes and informed if measures of precision associated with pooled effect estimates were imprecise. The panel included seven general stroke experts, three neurointerventionalists, six methodologists, four patient partners who had recovered from an AIS, one caregiver, two academic pharmacists, and one emergency physician [36].  

# Data sources and searches  

We searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) to identify relevant studies from inception to April 20, 2023, without language restrictions. We combined both MeSH and freetext terms to search for potentially eligible studies, and searched ClinicalTrials.gov for additional relevant clinical trials (appendix 3). We reviewed reference lists of included trials and relevant reviews [21, 22, 37, 38] for additional eligible studies.  

# Study selection  

Paired reviewers (YM, HX) independently screened titles/ abstracts and full texts for eligibility, assessed risk of bias, and collected data from each eligible study using standardized, pilot tested forms with detailed instructions.  

Reviewers resolved discrepancies through discussion or, if necessary, by adjudication by a third reviewer (LL).  

We included RCTs and non-randomized studies (NRSs), including non-randomized controlled trials, cohort studies and case–control studies, that compared tenecteplase versus alteplase in patients with AIS. Eligible studies had to report on at least one outcome of interest: Primary effectiveness outcome was excellent functional outcome at 3 months, defined as a score of 0–1 on the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Secondary effectiveness outcomes included good functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2)$ and early neurologic improvement as measured by NIH Stroke Scale scores. Primary safety outcomes were symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) and all-cause mortality, and secondary safety outcome was intracranial hemorrhage (ICH).  

# Data extraction and quality assessment  

The same pair of reviewers collected the following information from each eligible study: general study characteristics (e.g., author name, year of publication, total number of patients), patient characteristics (e.g., sex, age, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score, time to onset of treatment, large vessel occlusion [LVO], stroke risk factors), interventions details and outcomes. For NRSs, we also collected details on study design and methods used to control confounding and adjustment factors. We collected adjusted estimates and their corresponding $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) and extracted raw event data when adjusted estimates were not reported.  

We used version 2 of the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB2) to assess the risk of bias of RCTs, and the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for NRSs [39, 40].  

# Data synthesis and analysis  

We conducted analyses for RCTs and NRSs separately and prioritized presentation of the higher certainty evidence, or evidence from RCTs when the certainty was equivalent. For analyses of RCTs, we pooled outcome data using the inverse variance method and random-effects models and reported pooled odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding $95\%$ CIs. To adjust for risk of type I errors, we applied trial sequential analysis (TSA) and calculated the required information size according to an overall type I error of 0.05 and a power of $80\%$ [41, 42]. We used an a priori specified relative risk reduction (RRR) of $15\%$ across outcomes [43, 44]. We conducted TSA using Trial Sequential Analysis program version 0.9.5.10 beta.  

We performed network meta-analyses (NMA) on our primary efficacy outcome and safety outcomes in RCTs to explore the optimal dose of tenecteplase for AIS. We performed NMAs within a Bayesian framework using Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulation [45–47]. We used vague priors and ran the estimation with a burn-in of 10,000 iterations and sampling of 50,000 iterations from 3 chains of initial values. The selection between fixed- or random-effects model was based on the deviance information criteria (DIC), with smaller values indicative of a greater fit [48, 49]. The consistency between the direct and indirect evidence was evaluated by the node-splitting analysis [50]. We reported NMA estimates as ORs and $95\%$ credible intervals.  

In the analysis of NRSs, we used the inverse variance method and random-effects models to pool adjusted and unadjusted ORs separately. We transformed adjusted RRs to adjusted ORs using the method proposed in the Cochrane Handbook [51]. We also calculated the risk difference (RD) for all outcomes.  

We assessed statistical heterogeneity across studies by visual inspection of forest plots and the $I^{2}$ statistic. We explored between study variability using six prespecified subgroup hypotheses and assessed credibility of all significant subgroup effects with ICEMAN criteria [52]: (1) dose of tenecteplase ( $0.1~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ vs. $0.2\mathrm{mg/kg}$ vs. $0.25~\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{kg}$ vs. $0.32~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ vs. $0.4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ ; larger effect in patients receiving $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ of tenecteplase); (2) NIHSS at baseline (minor vs. moderate vs. severe; larger effect in patients with severe NIHSS score); (3) proportion of patients with large vessel occlusion $\r(\ge50$ vs. $<50\%$ ; larger effect in studies including $\ge50\%$ patients with large vessel occlusion); (4) proportion of patients with bridging therapy (intravenous thrombolytic plus endovascular thrombectomy) [53] $(\geq20$ vs. $<20\%$ ; larger effect in studies including $\geq20\%$ patients receiving bridging therapy); (5) time window $(<3\mathrm{~h~}$ vs. $<4.5\mathrm{h}$ vs. $<6\mathrm{{h}}$ ; larger effect in patients treated sooner); and (6) risk of bias (larger effect in studies at high risk of bias). Publication bias was examined using funnel plots and Egger’s test for all outcomes when there were $\geq10$ studies contributing to a meta-analysis [54]. Comparisons were 2-tailed using a $P\le0.05$ threshold, except for the test of heterogeneity $(I^{2})$ which was set at $\mathrm{P}<0.10$ . All statistical analyses were performed with R version 4.1.3.  

We assessed the overall certainty of outcomes using the GRADE approach [55], in which RCTs start as high certainty evidence but may be rated down for risk of bias, imprecision, indirectness, inconsistency or publication bias. NRSs start as low certainty evidence and may be further rated down for the same 5 issues, and rated up when pooled estimates show large effects (i.e., $\mathrm{RR}>2$ of $<0.5$ ), a dose–response gradient, or if all plausible residual confounding would reduce the effect or increase the effect if none was observed.  

We rated down for imprecision if the $95\%$ CI of pooled effect estimate included both patient-important and unimportant estimates. The minimally important difference (MID) was informed by a survey of guideline panel members’ views of patient values and preferences, and thresholds for MID were $1\%$ for recovery with no or minimal disability (mRS 0–1, mRS 0–2), $0.8\%$ for mortality, and $1\%$ for sICH and ICH [56, 57]. Additionally, we included an MID of $3\%$ for early neurologic improvement by referencing published studies and consulting clinical experts [14, 20]. We also rated down significant effects for imprecision if the required information size was not met. We did not rate down the same effect estimate twice for both inconsistency and imprecision. We assessed inconsistency among studies by differences in point estimates and overlap of CIs, and the $I^{2}$ statistics. An $I^{2}$ of $0{-}40\%$ might not be important, $30{-}60\%$ may represent moderate heterogeneity, $50{\-}90\%$ may represent substantial heterogeneity and $75{-}100\%$ indicates considerable heterogeneity [58].  

# Results  

Of 1676 potentially relevant records identified, 36 studies met our inclusion criteria, including 12 RCTs [14–20, 32, 59–62] (5533 patients) and 24 NRSs [24–26, 30, 31, 60, 63–80] (44,956 patients) (eFigure 1). Among patients in the $12\mathrm{RCTs}$ , the mean or median age ranged from 60 to 74, the median baseline NIHSS ranged from 4 to 17, and the proportion with hypertension ranged from 50 to $80\%$ . Of the 24 NRSs, 12 $(50\%)$ reported adjusted analyses; the mean or median age of included patients ranged from 55 to 75, and the median baseline NIHSS ranged 2.5–17 (Table 1; eTable 1).  

# Risk of bias assessment  

Among the 12 RCTs, 7 $(58\%)$ trails [14–18, 20, 59] were at low risk of bias, one trial [60] was at high risk of bias for deviations from intended interventions and blinding, and 4 $(33\%)$ trials [19, 32, 61, 62] were at unclear risk of bias predominantly due to unclear reporting about randomization (eFigure 2).  

The overall risk of bias assessment of NRSs was moderate to serious. Twelve of 24 studies [25, 26, 31, 60, 64, 65, 69, 72, 75, 78–80] did not control for confounding factors. Among the 12 studies [24, 30, 63, 66–68, 70, 71, 73, 74, 76, 77] that reported adjusted analysis, none mentioned ascertainment of confounding variables. Only 3 studies [64, 73, 75] reported blinding of outcome assessors (eFigure 2).  

# Outcomes for tenecteplase vs alteplase  

# Excellent functional outcome (mRS 0–1) at 3 months  

Moderate certainty evidence from 10 RCTs (5090 patients) [14–20, 32, 61, 62] showed that there was no difference between tenecteplase and alteplase in increasing the proportion of stroke patients that achieve excellent functional outcome at 3 months, despite an apparent positive trend (OR, 1.10; $95\%$ CI  0.98–1.23; RD $2.4\%$ , $95\%$ CI − 0.5 to 5.2; Table 2; Figs. 1 and 3, eFigure 3). We found no evidence of publication bias (Egger’s test $\mathrm{P}{=}0.78$ ; eFigure 4). Evidence from NRSs was only of very low certainty (Fig. 3, eTable 6).

# Good functional outcome (mRS 0–2) at 3 months  

Low certainty evidence from RCTs suggested there was no difference between tenecteplase and alteplase in increasing the proportion of patients achieving 3 months good functional outcome, despite an apparent positive trend (6 studies [14–18, 32], 3585 patients; OR, 1.10; $95\%$ CI 0.96–1.26; RD $2.2\%$ , $95\%$ CI − 1.0 to 5.2; Fig. 3; Table 2, eFigure 5). Evidence from NRSs was only of very low certainty (eTable 6).

We found no evidence of credible subgroup effects for any outcome (Fig. 1; eTables 2–5).  

# Discussion  

# Main findings  

Compared with alteplase, we found moderate certainty evidence that there was no difference between tenecteplase and alteplase in increasing the proportion of AIS patients that achieve excellent functional outcome and good functional outcome at 3  months, although there were apparent positive trend. Moderate certainty evidence also suggested that, compared to alteplase, tenecteplase reduced the risk of all-cause mortality and resulted in little to no difference in the risk of sICH. Comparative effects on early neurologic improvement are uncertain, and there was no difference for ICH when compared tenecteplase with alteplase. Our findings also suggested that $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ of tenecteplase may be optimal in terms of net benefit, as low and moderate certainty evidence suggested higher doses may increase the risk of sICH compared to alteplase.  

# Comparison with other studies  

Several previous meta-analyses [22, 23, 81–88] have compared the efficacy and safety of tenecteplase with alteplase for the treatment of AIS patients. Four reviews [22, 23, 81, 82] just included RCTs, and one review [86] only considered phase III RCTs. There were two meta-analyses [84, 85] incorporating both RCTs and observational studies, of which the number of included studies was 14 and 8 respectively, while the authors directly pooled data from these two different types of studies together. Three published network meta-analyses [83, 87, 88] synthesizing RCTs showed that tenecteplase $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ was superior to alteplase in excellent functional outcome at 3 months, which was in lines with our results. Additionally, our systematic review established the MID for each outcome based on survey of guideline panel members as well as stroke experts’ consultation, and included several trials that were not considered by prior reviews, which provided moderate certainty evidence comparing tenecteplase and alteplase for excellent functional outcome.  

A dose-escalation safety study of 88 patients with AIS concluded that tenecteplase only increased risk of sICH and ICH versus alteplase at doses of $0.5\mathrm{mg/kg}$ , and did not find evidence of dose–response for functional outcome [69]. However, larger studies have found $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase vs. alteplase after AIS was more likely to result in excellent functional outcome [69, 87–90], and that doses as high as  

$0.4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase may increase chances of sICH [19, 59, 89]. Further, while a cohort study of 30,643 AIS patients treated with alteplase $(n=29,480)$ ) or tenecteplase $(n=1163)$ [24] suggested increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage for intravenous tenecteplase without thrombectomy (but not with thrombectomy), these findings were compromised by the use of administrative data that lacked reliable information on tenecteplase dose and information on important prognostic variables, such as time to treatment and baseline NIHSS score.  

We found moderate certainty evidence from NRSs that tenecteplase probably reduces all-cause mortality after AIS compared to alteplase, and low certainty evidence from RCTs for little to no difference between tenecteplase and alteplase. While additional studies are required to clarify this issue, the dose of tenecteplase used in all three studies included in the adjusted analysis of NRSs was $0.25~\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{kg}$ , whereas three of the ten RCTs that contributed data to our analysis of all-cause mortality used doses of $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase, including the NOR-TEST 2 trial [19], which was prematurely terminated due to a high incidence of sICH.  

# Strengths and limitations  

Strengths of our review include a comprehensive search for eligible trials in any language that identified a number of studies of alteplase vs. tenecteplase for AIS that have not been considered by prior reviews [21, 22, 37, 38]. Further, our study including both RCTs and NRSs, allowing us to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of tenecteplase versus alteplase for the treatment of AIS patients. NRSs did provide additional evidence for reducing all-cause mortality with more patients and narrow confidence intervals. Additionally, we conducted NMA to explore the comparative effectiveness and safety of different doses of tenecteplase vs. alteplase for both functional outcome and safety outcomes. We engaged a guideline panel of patients and clinical experts to fully contextualize our assessment of the evidence, and to establish MIDs for all outcomes. Finally, we used the GRADE approach to assess the certainty of evidence and converted all pooled relative effects to RDs to facilitate interpretation.  

Our study also had limitations. First, only half of included observational studies reported adjusted estimates, so we were unable to integrate the evidence from RCTs and NRSs using advanced statistical analysis methods, such as bias adjustment methods [91]. Second, the definitions of some clinical outcomes varied across the included studies. For example, both the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study (ECASS) II and III criteria were used to judge sICH, and the time assessing early neurologic improvement ranging from 24 to $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ . However, both the ECASS II and III criteria are recognized by the AHA/ASA guideline [5], and our primary efficacy outcome was based on the mRS scale, which is a widely accepted categorical scale for functional neurological outcome [18].  

# Implications for practice  

The current evidence suggests that $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase is associated with significantly higher probability of 3-month excellent functional outcome, while there was no statistical difference when comparing 0.1, 0.32, and $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ dose of tenecteplase with alteplase. Tenecteplase is a genetically engineered derivative of alteplase, shares a mechanism of action analogous to alteplase but has several notable advantageous disparities [92]. Animal studies showed that tenecteplase, compared with alteplase, exhibits 14-fold greater relative fibrin specificity and 80-fold higher resistance to inhibition by plasminogen activator inhibitor type 1 (PAI-1), thus increases the benefit of reperfusion and early recanalization [93, 94]. Furthermore, the single intravenous bolus administration makes it more convenient compared with the $^{1\textrm{h}}$ infusion of alteplase, particularly in the setting of prehospital and inter-hospital transportation, which may improve clinical prognosis by reducing needle time [8, 10]. Due to the superior pharmacological and economic properties, as well as the favorable evidence found in our study, tenecteplase might be a promising alternative to alteplase for AIS patients, and $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase is the potentially optimal dose choice. However, the use of other doses of tenecteplase warrants caution due to inadequate or controversial evidence regarding their efficacy and safety.  

# Conclusion  

In this meta-analysis of randomized and non-randomized trials, moderate certainty evidence suggests that, there was little to no difference between tenecteplase and alteplase in increasing the proportion of patients achieving excellent functional outcome at 3 months and the risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, while tenecteplase probably reduce all-cause mortality. Administration of $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase after acute ischemic stroke is suggestive of increasing the proportion of patients that achieve excellent functional outcome at 3 months.
