# Reversing Oral Anticoagulation in Intracerebral Hemorrhage  

Wade S. Smith, M.D., Ph.D., and J. Claude Hemphill, M.D.  

Intracerebral hemorrhage remains a challenging disorder to treat. Hematoma expansion is a recognized complication that worsens outcomes and is especially common in patients who are taking oral anticoagulant medications. With the shift in practice from vitamin K antagonists to direct-acting oral anticoagulants, especially factor Xa inhibitors such as apixaban and rivaroxaban, the need to identify the best strategy to reverse their effects has become compelling. Andexanet binds and sequesters factor Xa molecules, and in an openlabel study of this agent in patients with acute major bleeding at any site, anti–factor Xa activity was reduced and good or excellent hemostatic efficacy (defined as an increase of $\leq35\%$ in hematoma volume) was achieved in $80\%$ of the subgroup of patients who had intracranial hemorrhage.1 However, $10\%$ of patients had a thrombotic event and one third of these events were ischemic strokes. Other strategies for the management of intracerebral hemorrhage in patients receiving a factor Xa inhibitor include the use of prothrombin complex concentrate, activated charcoal, or observation after discontinuation of the factor Xa inhibitor. The lack of clinical trials comparing these approaches among patients with intracerebral hemorrhage is reflected in the range of current recommendations for its management.2  

In this issue of the Journal, Connolly and colleagues3 report the results from a large, randomized trial (ANNEXA-I [Andexanet Alfa, a Novel Antidote to the Anticoagulation Effects of FXA Inhibitors]) in which andexanet was compared with usual care in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage who had received a factor Xa inhibitor within the previous 15 hours. Of the patients randomly assigned to the usual-care group, $85.5\%$ received prothrombin complex concentrate. The primary end point was hemostatic efficacy, defined by three different measures at 12 hours after baseline: expansion of the hematoma volume by $35\%$ or less, an increase in the score on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale of less than 7 points (scores range from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicting worse neurologic deficit), and no receipt of medical or surgical rescue therapy. A significantly greater percentage of patients who received andexanet than those who received usual care met this end point $\mathrm{\it{(67.0\%}}$ vs. $53.1\%)$ . A secondary efficacy end point was the change from baseline to nadir in anti–factor Xa activity during the first 2 hours after randomization, and more patients receiving andexanet than those receiving usual care met this end point $(94.5\%$ vs. $26.9\%)$ . However, more patients who received andexanet had thrombotic events $(10.3\%$ , vs. $5.6\%$ with usual care) and this increase was driven primarily by ischemic strokes $6.5\%$ vs. $1.5\%$ ). The incidence of death and of disability at 30 days did not differ between the groups, although the trial was not powered to assess these outcomes.  

These results are important because they show that andexanet rapidly reduces factor Xa activity and decreases early hematoma expansion more than usual care. However, they were assessed in patients with small hematoma volumes and mildto-moderate neurologic deficits at baseline — enrollment criteria for the trial — although this is the population that could perhaps benefit most from avoiding hematoma expansion. Several issues remain. The “usual care” of the $15\%$ of patients who did not receive prothrombin complex concentrate in the control group is not described; if the difference in hematoma expansion is driven by those who did not receive a reversal agent, that information would be relevant to interpreting the results.  

Some patients with intracranial hemorrhage other than intracerebral hemorrhage (for example, subdural hemorrhage) were enrolled early in the trial (more in the andexanet group than in the usual care group), and these patients may have a different predisposition to hematoma enlargement than those with intracerebral hemorrhage. The timing and severity of ischemic strokes that occurred after the trial intervention are not described. It would be useful to know whether many of these patients had atrial fibrillation, because they may be particularly vulnerable to transient hypercoagulability after receiving andexanet.  

Finally, the biggest challenge in understanding how to apply the results of the ANNEXA-I trial comes from the choice of end points. The ability to limit hematoma expansion soon after intracerebral hemorrhage occurs is intuitively important, but ultimately what matters is whether patients have better long-term functional outcomes. The trial assessed efficacy at 12 hours after baseline, and although three efficacy measures were used as part of the primary end point, only hematoma expansion, not early clinical deterioration or the need for rescue therapy, differed appreciably between the trial groups. The incidence of death at 30 days and the functional outcome apparently also did not differ substantially between groups, and longerterm outcomes at 3 or more months, a standard measure in most stroke clinical trials, including those for intracerebral hemorrhage,4-6 were not reported. In a 2008 editorial in the Journal regarding a phase 3 clinical trial of recombinant factor VIIa in noncoagulopathic intracerebral hemorrhage, which showed less hematoma expansion but no difference in clinical outcome,6 the author stated, “a single treatment approach may accomplish its physiological goal but may be insufficient to produce clinical benefit.”7 We hope that this is not the case for factor Xa reversal in intracerebral hemorrhage.

# Neoadjuvant or Perioperative Approach in Lung Cancer  

Marina Chiara Garassino, M.D., and Valter Torri, M.D.  

Numerous treatment options are currently available to reduce the risk of recurrence in patients with early-stage resectable non–small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). These approaches include neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy, which have been shown to improve outcomes in patients undergoing surgery. Perioperative treatment (i.e., neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery and adjuvant therapy) may further improve clinical outcomes.  

Two previous trials of adjuvant therapy have shown the benefit of immunotherapy after surgical resection. In the IMpower010 trial,1 atezolizumab had a disease-free survival benefit after adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with resected stage II to IIIA NSCLC, with more benefit in the subgroup of patients with tumors that expressed more than $1\%$ of programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1). A similar benefit was reported in the PEARLS/ Keynote-091 trial2 of pembrolizumab involving patients with resected stage IB to IIIA NSCLC regardless of PD-L1 expression. However, neither of these trials showed an overall survival benefit.
