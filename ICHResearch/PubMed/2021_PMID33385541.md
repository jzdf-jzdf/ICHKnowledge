# ABSTRACT  

Keywords:   
Dual antiplatelet therapy

Meta-analysis, acute coronary syndrome   
Mortality, bleedings Background: Ticagrelor has been shown to offer potential outcome benefits in acute coronary syndromes and for the long-term cardiovascular prevention, reducing mortality and the recurrence of ischemic events. However, data from real-world and recent meta-analyses have suggested that the anti-ischemic benefits of ticagrelor could be lower than expected, potentially outweighed by an increased risk of bleeding complications.   
Therefore, the aim of the present meta-analysis was to evaluate the prognostic impact of ticagrelor as compared to the conventional antiplatelet agents (ASA and clopidogrel) in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD), enclosing patients with acute coronary syndromes and stable CAD.   
Methods: Literature and main scientific session abstracts were searched for studies comparing a ticagrelor-based antiplatelet regimen vs different antiplatelet agents in patients with CAD. The primary efficacy endpoint was mortality, and the primary safety endpoint was the occurrence of major bleedings. Secondary endpoints were cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH).   
Results: We included 15 randomized clinical trials, with a total population of 73,402 patients ( $55\%$ randomized to ticagrelor). At a mean follow-up of $15\pm11.3$ months, the ticagrelor based strategy did not significantly reduce mortality as compared to the traditional therapy $(\mathrm{OR}[95\%\mathrm{CI}]=0.88[0.70,1.09]$ , $p=0.24$ ; phet $<0.00001$ ), with comparable results and no interaction according to patients’ presentation (p interaction $=0.80^{}$ ).   
A similar result was achieved for cardiovascular mortality, whereas the reduction of recurrent myocardial infarction was significant $\mathrm{(OR}[95\%\mathrm{CI}]=0.84[0.78,0.90]$ , $p<0.00001$ ; phet $=0.76\$ ). As for the risk of stroke, the largest reduction was observed in stable patients, while a neutral effect was observed in the ACS subgroup (p $\mathrm{int}=0.35)$ . Major bleeding events were increased in ticagrelor-treated patients (OR $[95\%\mathrm{CI}]=1.40$ [1.06, 1.84], $p=0.02$ ; phet $<0.00001>$ , especially ICH (OR $[95\%\mathrm{CI}]=1.45[1.13,1.84]$ , $p=0.003$ ; phet $=0.93\$ ), and mainly among non-ACS patient. No interaction for any outcome endpoint was observed according to patients’ age.  

Conclusions: Based on the current meta-analysis, a ticagrelor-based strategy for the treatment of patients with coronary artery disease is associated to a significant increase in major bleeding complications and especially for intracranial hemorrhage, as compared to a strategy based on conventional antiplatelet agents, while resulting in a neutral effect on survival. However, a significant reduction of recurrent myocardial infarction was observed with ticagrelor.  



# 1. Background  

Ticagrelor currently represents the pivotal antiplatelet agent for the management of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) in patients with an acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [1], having shown a more potent and predictable effect as compared to clopidogrel, and additionally offering the opportunity of larger use in different subsets of patients, as compared to prasugrel [2–5].

In effect, ticagrelor represents the only antiplatelet drug to have demonstrated a survival benefit in its major trial, providing a $22\%$ reduction in major cardiovascular ischemic events (MACE) while reporting a non-significant $19\%$ increase in bleeding complications [6].  

In addition, the prolongation of a ticagrelor treatment, even beyond the indicated 12 months DAPT, has been associated with a further reduction in the recurrence of ischemic events in the PEGASUS-TIMI 54 trial [7], thus reinforcing the indications to ticagrelor-based antiplatelet regimens for the secondary cardiovascular prevention in CAD. However, hemorrhagic complications were significantly enhanced in this higherrisk subset of patients, despite the reduced-dose of ticagrelor.  

On the contrary, more restrained benefits have been observed in subsequent studies [8,9], enclosing patients with stable coronary artery disease or non-coronary atherothrombotic events and also in large-scale real life cohorts of patients [10–13], raising instead some potential warnings for the excess of hemorrhagic complications, in particular for certain ethnicities and with a disturbing increase in intracranial bleedings.  

The recent completion of randomized clinical trials comparing a ticagrelor based antiplatelet regimen with a conventional antiplatelet strategy, specifically dedicated to the evaluation of those particular particular patient populations tless explored in the PLATO trial [14–16], such as elderly, Asian patients or subjects undergoing coronary revas­ cularization with bypass grafting, paves the way to the present updated meta-analysis aiming at comparing ticagrelor-based regimens with the conventional antiplatelet therapy in patients with CAD presenting either with ACS or stable disease.  



# 2. Methods  



# 2.1. Eligibility and search strategy  

The literature was scanned by formal searches of electronic data­ bases (MEDLINE, Cochrane and EMBASE) for clinical studies and the scientific session abstracts, searched on the TCT (www.tctmd.com), EuroPCR (www.europcr.com), ACC (www.acc.org), AHA (www.aha. org), and ESC (www.escardio.org) websites, for oral presentations and/or expert slide presentations from January 2007 to January 2020. The following key words were used: “ticagrelor”, “randomized trial”, “mortality”; “Outcome”.  

No language restrictions were enforced. Inclusion criteria were: 1) studies with randomized allocation to different antiplatelet regimens, comprising the allocation to ticagrelor (as single or dual therapy) as experimental strategy; 2) established coronary artery disease, with or without revascularization; 3) availability of complete clinical and outcome data after discharge. Exclusion criteria were: 1) follow-up data in less than $90\%$ of patients, 2) ongoing studies or irretrievable data; 3) use of a new antiplatelet agent (ticagrelor or prasugrel) in the control arm in more than $10\%$ of the study population; 4) follow-up $\leq30$ days.  



# 2.2. Data extraction and validity assessment  

Data were independently abstracted by two investigators (MV, GDL). In case of incomplete or unclear data, the authors were contacted. Dis­ agreements were resolved by consensus. Data were managed according to the intention-to-treat principle. Data on cardiovascular mortality were collected if data on overall mortality were not available. Ethical approval was referred to the individual included trials.  



# 2.3. Outcome measures  

Primary efficacy endpoint was overall mortality. Primary safety endpoint was the rate of major bleeding complications (according to protocol definition).  

Secondary endpoints were: 1) cardiovascular mortality, 2) recurrent myocardial infarction, 3) stroke and 4) intracranial hemorrhage (ICH).  



# 2.4. Data analysis  

Statistical analysis was performed using the Review Manager 5.3 freeware package, SPSS 23.0 statistical package. Odds ratio (OR) and $95\%$ confidence intervals $(95\%\mathrm{CI})$ were used as summary statistics. The pooled odds ratio was calculated by using a fixed effect model (MantelHaensel). The Breslow-Day test was used to examine the statistical ev­ idence of heterogeneity across the studies $(p<0.1)$ . Potential publica­ tion bias was examined by constructing a “funnel plot”, in which sample size was plotted against odds ratios (for the primary endpoint). The study quality was evaluated by the same two investigators according to a score, that was expressed on ordinal scale, allocating 1 point for the presence of each of the following: 1) statement of objectives, 2) explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, 3) description of intervention, 4) objective means of follow-up, 5) description of adverse events, 6) power analysis, 7) description of statistical methods, 8) multicenter design, 9) discussion of withdrawals, and, 10) details on medical therapy.

In case of different ticagrelor-based strategies or dosing were present, data were considered as the pooling of the data of the ticagrelor-treated patients.

# 3. Results  



# 3.1. Eligible studies  

A total of 789 studies were screened for inclusion in our metaanalysis, as shown in the flow-chart for the selection process in Fig. 1. Two trials were excluded since comparing a ticagrelor-based antiplatelet regimen with another new antiplatelet drug [18,19], and three trials since using ticagrelor also in the control arm [20–22]. Two trials were excluded since providing only in-hospital follow-up [23,24] and one study providing only 30-day outcomes [25].  

Finally, 15 studies [6,7,15,16,26–36] were included in the present meta-analysis, with an overall population of 73,402 patients. Among them, 40,351 $(55\%)$ were randomized to a ticagrelor-based antiplatelet strategy, and 33,051 patients to a different antiplatelet regimen. Nine trials [6,14,15,26–32] were conducted in the setting of ACS, while 6 studies [7,16,33–36] enrolled subjects with stable CAD, especially those undergoing bypass grafting (CABG) (in 3 trials, 16,34,35).  

Antiplatelet agents used for the ticagrelor-based regimen were tica­ grelor and acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) in 9 trials [6,15,26–31], ticagrelor alone in 2 trials [16,36] while one trial allowed ticagrelor both as DAPT or single antiplatelet regimen [35]. In one study, [28] prasugrel was allowed in a small percentage of the patients $(<1\%)$ . In the control arm antiplatelet therapy comprised DAPT with ASA and clopidogrel in 8 trials, clopidogrel in 1 trial [36], ASA alone in 5 trials [7,16,33–35] and in one trial ASA $^+$ clopidogrel was administered in the majority of the study population, whereas $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor alone was indicated in pa­ tients with indication to OAC and $\mathrm{P}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}$ inhibitors different from clo­ pidogrel were allowed in a minority of the patients [28]. Ticagrelor daily dose was $180~\mathrm{mg}$ , whereas 2 trials tested a reduced $120~\mathrm{mg}$ -dose in one treatment arm [7,33].  

Study characteristics of included trials are shown in Table 1. Mean follow-up was $15\pm11.3$ months, (median 12 months) ranging from 3 months [26] to 36 months [7,33].  

Table 2 displays the characteristics of enrolled patients, with a mean age of $64.6\pm4.3$ years, $76\%$ males, and $34.7\%$ with diabetes. Among the 9 trials conducted in patients with acute coronary syndrome, $61\%$ of the patients had ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction ( $100\%$ in 3 studies, but excluded in 2 studies −26,28-).

# 6. Discussion  

The present study represents one of the most updated and compre­ hensive meta-analyses evaluating the prognostic impact of ticagrelorbased therapy, in patients with coronary artery disease, either present­ ing with an acute coronary syndrome or stable CAD.  

We demonstrated that the use of a ticagrelor-based antiplatelet regimen, either in single or dual antiplatelet therapy, significantly re­ duces the rate of myocardial infarction, while displaying a neutral effect on stroke. No significant reduction was observed in total and cardio­ vascular mortality. Moreover, a significant increase in major bleeding complications was observed with ticagrelor, and especially ICH.  

The efforts for the optimization of antiplatelet strategies and the availability of more potent antiplatelet drugs have certainly dramati­ cally changed the prognosis of patients with coronary artery disease [37], although often requiring to balance between the risk of thrombotic and bleeding complications, and especially in certain complex subsets of patients, displaying enhancing of both risks and still impaired outcomes [38,39].  

Ticagrelor has been introduced as the first reversible direct-acting P2Y12 receptor antagonist, providing a faster onset and a greater inhibiting effect on platelets, therefore offering enhanced antischemic protection and outcome benefits in patients with ACS, and especially when compared to the conventional DAPT regimen comprising ASA and

In the PLATO trial [6], ticagrelor decreased the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and total mortality in pa­ tients with ACS, either treated with PCI or medically managed, with the surprising positive outcome benefits (NNT $=53$ for the composite endpoint of MACE) being confirmed in major higher risk-subsets of patients and supported by a reassuring safety profile (NNH for bleedings $=167^{\circ}.$ ). These benefits were also explained by the existence of additional non-platelet mediated pleiotropic effects of ticagrelor, pointing in particular at adenosine-mediated effects on endothelial function, myocardial perfusion and inflammation [40,41].  

Several trials and meta-analysis, restricted to a more “selected” population of patients with an acute coronary event, has subsequently confirmed the findings of the PLATO trial, although suggesting that the scissor between benefit and bleedings could be less marked than ex­ pected, and raising potential concerns on an increased rate of major bleeding complications, and in particular for certain subsets of patients, being less represented in the major ticagrelor trials.  

In the PEGASUS trial the prolonged use of ticagrelor was associated with a neutral effect on mortality despite the reduction in cardiovascular mortality and thrombotic complications, mainly due to a counter­ balanced increase in major bleeding complications, with both low-dose $(60~\mathrm{mg})$ and especially standard dose $(90~\mathrm{mg})$ [7]. Some disparities in benefits were observed according to age and race.  

Race issues have been raised for Black and Eastern patients with Ticagrelor. Mitsumida et al., [9] have suggested that ticagrelor could not offer proper antithrombotic benefits in Asian patients, being instead weighed by an excess of bleedings, a warning that was supported by the subsequent PHILO trial [27], documenting a numerical inferiority of ticagrelor with regard to death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and bleeding over clopidogrel. In fact, the PLATO-defined “net clinical benefit” in PHILO was also significantly $(\mathrm{OR}=1.61;\mathrm{CI}=1.11–2.34,p=$ 0.0145) inferior for ticagrelor, and the “number needed to harm” sug­ gested that for every 29 patients treated for 12 months with ticagrelor instead of clopidogrel, one will suffer an additional major bleeding event. Based on these conclusions, the trial was prematurely terminated, thus leaving suspended the question of ticagrelor use in this specific population [42].  

Similar constraints were also raised for elderly patients, accounting for only the $14\%$ of the PLATO population, but over $40\%$ of real-life ACS patients [43], representing a more challenging setting in vue of frailty and comorbidities and providing explanation for the higher rate of major bleedings observed in real life registries as compared to RCTs [13].  

The recent POPular Age trial, enrolling over 1000 patients with NSTE-ACS and aged over 75 years of age [28], concluded that in these patients, the use of clopidogrel leads to less bleeding events without an increase in thrombotic events as compared to the use of ticagrelor or prasugrel.  

Thus, the completion of new trials dedicated to these specific highrisk populations, paves the way to the present updated meta-analysis evaluating ticagrelor based antiplatelet regimens as compared to the conventional treatment in patients with acute or stable CAD.

[8], ticagrelor and conventional antiplatelet drugs (clopidogrel or ASA) were comparable in terms of efficacy in both ACS and stable patients, but for a reduction in myocardial infarction with Ticagrelor. Indeed, these ad­ vantages were more evident in PCI trials, while resulting non-significant among patients undergoing CABG or fibrinolysis [16,30,35], suggesting a potential role of stent thrombosis on such observation. In effect, both the PLATO and PEGASUS-TIMI 54 trial included patients treated with the older generations of drug-eluting stents or even bare metal stents, while more recent studies [15] were certainly underpowered for such endpoint.  

In addition, confirming the data of previous literature [44,45], we found a significant increase in major bleedings among ticagrelor treated patients, and especially for intracranial hemorrhage, thus pointing at the importance of an adequate patients’ selection, accounting for both ischemic and hemorrhagic risk, for the selection of the optimal anti­ platelet strategy in CAD patients. In fact, when balancing the anti­ ischemic benefits against hemorragic complications and other safety reported events, the net adverse clinical events resulted comparable between ticagrelor and clopidogrel in a large analysis enclosing 31,290 propensity-matched pairs [46].  

However, indications on the criteria for tailoring antiplatelet treatment are still lacking and should probably account both clinical factors, coronary anatomy and the revascularization strategy, but also pharmacological interactions and platelet reactivity, whose assessment has been recently re-evaluated for the optimization of DAPT [47].  



# 7. Study limitations  

The first limitation of our study derives from the synthesis of different trials. In fact, we enclosed studies evaluating a variety of subsets of patients, comprising patients with ACS or stable CAD, patients undergoing PCI, CABG or fibrinolysis and trials focused on very highrisk population such as diabetics or the elderly. In fact, our primary endpoint was conditioned by patient’s risk profile, as displayed by the positive interaction at meta-regression analysis. However, since we had no access to individual patients’ data, we could not identify the pro­ portion of patients with concomitant CAD and cerebral or peripheral vascular disease in all trials and neither we could provide data on the net clinical benefit derived from the balance between thrombotic and hemorrhagic complications. In particular, regards to cerebrovascular events, we could not evaluate separately the rate of periprocedural events, that could have been higher especially in patients undergoing bypass surgery.  

Moreover, a significant heterogeneity was observed, mainly driven by the results of smaller studies. In particular, despite the TIMI defini­ tion for major bleedings was used in the majority of the trials, the use of different criteria could have influenced the heterogeneity of our results. Nevertheless, our findings were consistent at subgroup analysis when evaluating separately stable and acute patients, and also with the con­ clusions of previous meta-analyses. Finally, a quote of the population received low-dose ticagrelor.
