As with any invasive procedure, DC has known complications, including blooming of contusions, external cerebral herniation, lack of protection against further trauma, and strangulation of cerebral tissue at the edge of the bone flap.3 Other complications such as delayed seizures are reported in $37\%$ of patients after DC, and hydrocephalus and syndrome of the trephined are also described.3,4  

Studies have reported that many of these complications are reversed with the replacement of the bone flap. This reversal is related to the restoration of normal cerebral hemodynamics, a phenomenon first described by Richaud et al.5-7 However, even 100 years since the first description of DC by Theodor Kocher, uncertainties remain regarding the indications, optimal timing, and effects of the procedure on the functional outcome of head-injured patients.1,2,6  

With this background, we studied the efficacy of a novel technique, four-quadrant osteoplastic decompressive craniotomy (FoQOsD) and compared its outcomes with conventional DC. This study was first described in 2015 by Peethambaran et al., who found that outcomes regarding survival and brain expansion were similar to conventional DC techniques.8 The hypothesis behind this procedure is that it provides for adequate surgical decompression and retains the bone flap, which therefore avoids many of the complications associated with conventional DC and later cranioplasty.  

The present study is the first randomized controlled trial comparing the outcomes of both the DC and FoQOsD techniques for patients with TBI.

# Trial Design

The study was carried out at a level 1 trauma center for 7 months from December 1, 2016 to July 31, 2017. Ethics committee approval was secured before recruitment of patients. The 2 procedures were allocated numbers 1 and 2 (1 for DC and 2 for FoQOsD) and we devised a sequence of random computergenerated numbers. Concealment of the number sequence from the operating surgeon was ensured to minimize bias. After evacuation of the hematoma, if the brain was bulging 1 cm above the inner table of the skull, lax augmentation duroplasty was performed. We routinely perform lax augmentation duroplasty in our center, which may be different from other centers. Intraoperative randomization to either procedure was performed at this stage by a third person based on the random number sequence.  

Patients aged ${>}\mathbf{I}8$ years with TBI for whom primary DC was indicated were included in the study. The indications for urgent DC included subdural hematomas with thickness ${>}\mathbf{I}$ cm, contusions with volume ${>}20~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ , and evidence of cerebral edema in the form of midline shift $({\mathrm{MLS}})>5.$ ${>}5\mathrm{mm}$ and effaced cisterns. The final decision to operate was determined by a senior consultant neurosurgeon based on clinicoradiologic correlation. Patients with minor and moderate head injury underwent surgery only when there was evidence of clinical deterioration along with radiologic evidence of increasing mass effect. We excluded those patients whose caretakers refused to become a part of the study and those who required mass closure because of unstable clinical condition or malignant cerebral edema during surgery. We also excluded patients with penetrating compound depressed fractures.  



# Surgical Technique  

Technique of Conventional Decompressive Hemicraniectomy. For unilateral DC, the patient was supine with a small rolled towel placed underneath the ipsilateral (I/L) shoulder and the head turned toward the contralateral (C/L) side.  

Once the site was prepared and draped, a standard question mark/reverse question mark incision was made starting at the level of the zygoma and curving posteriorly above the ear and then superiorly and anteriorly, stopping just behind the hairline. An adequate number of burr holes were made and underlying dura separated using a Penfield number 3 dissector. We used an osteotome or Gigli wire saw to perform the craniotomy by raising an osteoplastic flap. The anteroposterior extent of the craniotomy flap was $\geq\mathtt{I}5$ cm and lateral extension was down to the floor of the temporal fossa. The dura was opened slowly in a C-shaped fashion. After evacuation of the hematoma, we performed a lax augmentation duroplasty with autologous pericranial patch. Scalp closure was performed in layers and the bone flap was placed under a subcutaneous plane in the anterior abdominal wall.  

Technique of FoQOsD Group. The technique of FoQOsD is similar to that of DC described previously up to lax augmentation duroplasty. The bone flap is then cut into $4$ pieces using an osteotome or Gigli wire saw and loosely connected to the periosteum on all sides with silk sutures. Scalp closure was performed in 2 layers. These bone flaps would fan out in all $4$ directions when the brain expanded, preventing an increase in ICP Figure 1AeD.  



# Statistical Analysis  

The primary outcome studied was the functional status at 6 months using the Glasgow Outcome ScaleeExtended (GOS-e). The GOS-E is a global outcome scale assessing functional independence, work, social and leisure activities, and personal relationships (Figure 2). Favorable outcome was defined as patients with a GOS-e score of upper severe disability or better.  

The secondary outcomes studied included the rate of complications in each group as well the radiologic parameters (e.g., change in MLS and brain width). These outcomes were measured on preoperative and postoperative computed tomography (CT) scans using standardized scales of measurement.  



# Sample Size  

Based on the results of a pilot study conducted by us in April 2016 in which mortality was $30\%$ higher in the DC group with means of simulation to provide $80\%$ power and rounding off, the calculated sample size came to 55 per group.  

Quantitative variables were compared using an unpaired t test and paired t test for preoperative and postoperative follow-ups between 2 groups. Qualitative variables were compared using a $\chi^{2}$ test or Fisher exact test as appropriate. A $\mathrm{P}$ value of ${<}0.05$ was considered statistically significant. If the data were not normally distributed, a Mann-Whitney test was used to compare the 2 groups. Logistic regression univariate and multivariate analysis were performed to find out factors affecting final outcome. Analysis was performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Two-tailed significance was kept at 0.05.  



# RESULTS  

A total of 2500 patients with head injuries were admitted during the period of the study of whom $848$ were operated on. Of the 848 operations, 161 decompressive craniectomies were performed.  

A total of 115 patients were randomized into undergoing either procedure after $46$ were excluded (13 patients had penetrating compound skull fractures, 15 patients underwent mass closure, and for 18 we were unable to obtain consent). Fifty-nine patients underwent conventional DC and $56$ underwent FoQOsD. We lost 2 patients to follow-up, 1 from each group. These patients were lost to follow-up after the 1-month review period and were excluded from the mortality analysis as per the intention-to-treat protocol.

# Demographic Analysis  

Both groups of patients were similar in age, age-groups, and sex (Table 1). Both groups were also similar for Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score on admission (7.42 vs. 8.02; $\mathrm{P}=0.29$ ), motor score on admission (3.66 vs. 3.79; $\mathrm{P}=0.590,$ ), and pupils (anisocoria present in $54\%$ in DC group vs. $39\%$ in FoQOsD group; $\mathrm{~P~}=0.\mathrm{{I08}}$ ). The difference in anisocoria was close to statistical significance.  

The most common preoperative CT finding was cerebral contusions $(62.7\%$ vs. $55{\cdot}4\%$ ; $\mathrm{P}=0.702$ ). Opposite side contusion was present in $23.7\%$ in DC group compared with $30.4\%$ in the  

FoQOsD group $\left(\mathrm{P}=0.423\right)$ , and either subarachnoid hemorrhage or intraventricular hemorrhage was present in $37\%$ in the DC group versus $4\mathrm{{I}\%}$ in the FoQOsD group $\left(\mathrm{P}=0.4\mathrm{I}\right),$ ). There was a delay in patients reaching the operating theater (21.6 hours in the DC group and 23.5 hours in the FoQOsD group; $\mathrm{~P~}=~0.797,$ . Although not statistically significant, this delay could have been a source of bias in negatively affecting the final clinical outcome.

# Evidence of Neurologic Deterioration Caused by Reposition of Bone Flap  

We evaluated the possibility of clinical deterioration from reposition of the bone flap in the FoQOsD group (Table 4). This evaluation was achieved by comparing the percentage change in MLS and brain width on preoperative and postoperative CT scans in both groups and correlating those results with changing trends in GCS score. We assumed that if there was deterioration in GCS score in the postoperative period within the first $4^{8}$ hours, a possibility of pressure effect from the bone flap should be evaluated.  

Neurologic deterioration in the postoperative period was marginally less common in the DC group than in the FoQOsD group (6 vs. 11 patients; OR, 0.46; $95\%$ CI, $\sigma.\mathrm{{I}}^{6-\mathrm{{I}}.4}$ ; $\mathrm{P}=0.15$ ). In the FoQOsD group, there was a $0.8\%$ overall decrease in brain width on the $\mathrm{I/L}$ compared with an $8.0\%$ increase in the DC group; however, P value was not significant $\ensuremath{(\mathrm{P}=0.\mathrm{{I}}84},$ ). The percentage change in MLS was comparable between both groups $(56.2\%$ vs. $49\%$ ; $\mathrm{P}=0.68\mathrm{o}$ ), whereas the $\mathrm{C/L}$ brain width expansion in this DC subgroup was significantly higher than in the FoQOsD group $\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{I}0.6\%\$ vs. $5.8\%$ ; $\ensuremath{\mathrm{P}}=0.004$ ).  

There were no patients in the FoQOsD group in whom the bone flap was removed in the second sitting to further reduce ICP.  



# Factors that Affect Final Outcome  

Using logistic regression univariate analysis to predict individual factors that influence primary outcome in both groups, we found that the most significant factor was the presence of intraventricular hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage on preoperative CT scan in addition to hematoma or cerebral contusion (OR, 7.17; $95\%$ CI, $\mathbf{I}.364^{-}37.7$ ; $\mathrm{~P~}=~0.020,$ ) (Table 5). Other factors that influenced outcome were presence of opposite side contusion (OR, $3.838$ ; $95\%$ CI, $\mathbf{I.6I4}^{-}-9.13\mathbf{I}$ ; $\mathrm{~P~}=\mathrm{~}0.002.$ ) and anisocoria (OR, 3.235; $95\%$ CI, $\mathtt{I.490-7.026}$ ; $\mathrm{~P~}=\mathrm{~}0.003,$ ). Opposite side contusion and anisocoria were the 2 factors that were significant on both univariate and multivariate analysis.

TBI is the leading cause of death and long-term disability in the first $4$ decades of life.9-12 DC plays an important role in the management of increased ICP in select cases.1,2,12 However, absence of the bone flap is associated with a myriad of complications. There is also evidence of neurologic recovery and restoration of cerebral hemodynamics after cranioplasty.5-7

We then proceeded to randomization. Our interim analysis at 3 months follow-up was reported earlier showing that the newer technique has equivalent outcome.15  

In the present study, both groups had similar clinical, demographic, and radiologic characteristics. This factor ensured that the results could be reliably compared between the 2 groups.  

The most significant finding of this study was that the 2 groups were comparable for GOS-e score at 6 months (4.28 vs. $4\cdot3^{8}$ ; $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{o}.856$ ). Mortality at the end of 6 months was $38\%$ in the DC group versus $45{\cdot}4\%$ in the FoQOsD group (OR, 1.19; $95\%$ CI, $_{0.6-}$ 2.36; $\mathrm{~P~}=\mathrm{~o.6~}$ ). These results provide convincing evidence that FoQOsD is as efficacious as DC, with the added advantage of avoiding second surgery. Avoiding second surgery offers both psychological and economic benefits to patients and their caregivers.  

The other significant finding was that the percentage reduction in MLS and percentage increase in brain width (both $\mathrm{I/L}$ and $\mathrm{C/L}$ ) on the postoperative CT scan was equivalent in both groups $({\mathrm{I}}/{\mathrm{L}},$ $7.8\%$ vs. $5.6\%$ ; $\mathrm{C/L}$ , $\scriptstyle\mathbf{I}4\cdot7^{0}/0$ vs. $9{\cdot}3\%$ ; $\mathrm{P}=0.\mathrm{{r}}8\mathrm{{r}}$ ) (Figure 2AeE). Kenning et al.14 found similar results when they compared 2 cohorts of patients undergoing DC and hinge craniotomy. Pressure effect from the bone flap was evaluated by comparing the percentage change in brain width and MLS with changing trends in GCS score. In those patients whose GCS score had deteriorated in the first $4^{8}$ hours postoperatively, we found that percentage change in $\mathrm{I/L}$ brain width and percentage reduction in MLS was similar for both groups. However, neurologic deterioration was marginally less common in the DC group than in the FoQOsD group, although not statistically significant (6 vs. 11 patients; OR, 0.46; $95\%$ CI, $_{0.16-\mathrm{I}.4}$ ; $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{o}.\mathrm{I}5$ ).  



# Benefits of the Newer Technique  

As shown by Peethambaran et al.,8 the newer technique allows for the 3 bone pieces to fan out in different directions to accommodate the expanding brain. This process was shown radiologically in most of our patients because we undertook a follow-up CT scan on postoperative day 3, when increased edema can be expected (Figure 2C and G). The expansion is also more controlled, unlike with conventional DC, thereby reducing the chances of venous congestion and hemorrhagic infarction.17,18  

The other benefit of FoQOsD is the superior cosmetic outcome (Figure 3AeF). Using an osteoplastic flap minimizes temporalis muscle atrophy and preserves the contour of the skull. Further, in a resource-limited setting in which there are delays in cranioplasty, adding to the psychological stress, single-stage surgery can be effective. Cranial complications were comparable in both groups (cranial SSI, 3 in DC vs. 5 in FoQOsD; hydrocephalus, 2 in DC vs. 1 in FoQOsD), whereas there were 8 patients who had bone flap infection in the DC group, which was avoided in the FoQOsD group.  

When we correlated the initial motor score with mortality at 6 months, we found that patients with an initial motor score of 2 tended to do better when they underwent conventional DC $(56.2\%$ mortality in DC vs. $90.9\%$ in the FoQOsD group). These findings were also seen in those patients with an initial motor score of 3 $(4\mathbf{I}.7\%$ in DC vs. $58.3\%$ in FoQOsD). Conversely, patients with an initial motor score of 5 tended to do better with FoQOsD ( $2\mathrm{I}^{0}/\mathrm{o}$ in DC vs. $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}4\cdot2^{0}}/{\mathrm{0}}$ in FoQOsD), whereas mortality in those patients with an initial motor score of $4$ was comparable $\left(44\cdot4^{0}\%\right)$ in DC vs. $45{\cdot}5\%$ in FoQOsD). Although the evidence is not strong, we believe that there is a subgroup of patients with TBI who do better with the newer technique.  



# Limitations  

There were 2 limitations to our study. The first is that ICP was not monitored. Brain trauma foundation guidelines recommend ICP monitoring to reduce inpatient and 2-week postinjury mortality in all patients with severe TBI.19 We started ICP monitoring for all our recent cases and measured the ICP in 20 patients from each group via external ventricular drain or intraparenchymal catheter. The mean ICP in the immediate postoperative period was $\mathrm{II.7}\pm7.34\mathrm{cm}$ of water in the DC group and ${\bf\delta}_{13.69}\pm{\bf\delta}_{4.4}$ cm of water in the FoQOsD group $\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{P}=0.30\$ ). We measured ICP postoperatively for up to 72 hours in both groups, the trends being $\mathbf{II}.53\pm\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{II}.\mathbf{I}$ versus ${\bf\delta}{\bf\delta}^{0.50}\pm{\bf\delta}{\bf8}.2$ at 12 hours $\left(\mathrm{P}=0.52\right)$ ; $\mathtt{I2.I7}\pm\:9.077$ versus ${\bf{I}}5{\bf{\cdot}}{\bf{I}}2\pm9{\cdot}24$ at 24 hours $\textstyle({\mathrm{P}}=0.314,$ ); 9.45 $\pm8.63\$ versus $\mathbf{I}3.2\mathbf{I}\pm7.\mathbf{I}7$ at $4^{8}$ hours $\mathrm{(P=o.r7)}$ ); and $8.80~\pm$ $6.75$ versus $\mathtt{I}2.75\pm7.02$ at 72 hours $\left(\mathrm{P}=0.\mathrm{{I}}0\right)$ ).  

The second limitation is that MLS and brain width are best measured on three-dimensional CT scans using Brainlab software (Brainlab, Munich, Germany). This software was not available to us and therefore there could have been fallacies in measurement.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

Our study is the first to evaluate cerebral decompression techniques of FoQOsD and conventional DC through a comparative randomized controlled trial. We have found that FoQOsD is equivalent to DC in improving clinical outcomes. Further, FoQOsD has better cosmesis, avoids the psychological stress of second surgery, and avoids the complication of infection of the stored bone flap. In a select subset of patients with TBI, it can be the procedure of choice.
