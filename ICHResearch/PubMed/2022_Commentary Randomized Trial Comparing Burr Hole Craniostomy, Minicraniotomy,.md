he operative management of chronic subdural hematomas (cSDHs) is a common and frequent element of neurosurgical practice. It is a disease process that predominates in the elderly population and has demonstrated decades of time-related increase in its incidence.1 Given the relative frailty of older adults, cSDHs are significant events and associated with excess mortality for many years after hospitalization.2,3 Indeed, 1-year mortality rates have been reported between $13.7\%$ and $32\%$ .4-6 A recent study by Rauhala et $\mathrm{al}^{3}$ found that patients demonstrated $48\%$ excess mortality 20 years after cSDH diagnosis. They found that age older than 80 years and neurologic disability at admission were the most important factors determining excess mortality. Owing to an expanding older adult population combined with an incidence peak of older than 70 years, cSDHs are projected to sustain a steady increase in incidence.7 In their study, “Randomized trial comparing burr hole craniostomy, minicraniotomy and twist drill craniostomy for treatment of chronic subdural hematoma,” the authors make a significant contribution to the large body of work attempting to solve this nontrivial disease, and they set the stage for future high-powered RCTs.8  

Although surgical drainage is considered the gold standard treatment, several compelling conservative treatment options have emerged. There are several class III recommendations for the nonoperative management of cSDH, mainly targeting patients with Markwalder scores of 0 to 1 or those unsuited for surgical drainage. Corticosteroids, namely dexamethasone, are an efficacious alternative in select patient groups.9 A recent trial was terminated because of serious adverse events,10 although other studies have not reported significant steroid-related events. One potential drug candidate, atorvastatin, targets the inflammatory dysregulation hypothesized to underly the mechanism of cSDHs. A recent study showed significant differences in recovery of neurologic function and decreases in hematoma volume and the eventual need for surgery compared with placebo.11 Trials examining tranexamic acid both in the perioperative and postoperative setting as a surgical adjunct12 and as a conservative management agent13,14 are underway. Studies have proposed that mannitol may alter the dynamics of the osmotic gradient of the hematoma itself.9 However, the evidence is limited, and the requirement of long-term daily intravenous treatment limits practicality. Minimally invasive management such as middle meningeal artery embolization has demonstrated efficacy, and a recent systematic review documented a recurrence rate of $3.6\%$ across a mixed cohort of primary, recurrent, and postoperative prophylactic cSDHs.15 These results should be interpreted with caution because of the heterogeneity of protocols, surgical outcomes, and the absence of long-term functional outcomes. If reproducible, minimally invasive management may serve as a viable treatment option in select patient populations as current postoperative recurrence rates are reported to range from 5% to 30%.4,16-18 Although still early in evaluation, many of these treatments show promise, and their role as standalone treatments for select populations or as surgical adjuncts will be elucidated through further trials, many of which are currently underway.  

Operative trends across time have fluctuated based on prevailing ideas and available technologies.19 Gone are the days of “woodpecker” approaches to cSDH evacuation20 because computed tomography imaging has allowed precise characterization and localization of the lesion. The task of neurosurgical providers has narrowed to optimizing the surgical technique of extraction. Three prevailing surgical techniques are burrhole craniostomy, twistdrill craniostomy (TDC), and open craniotomy. Trials to determine the superiority of these procedures have sought to maximize the balance of efficacy, minimally invasive nature, rate of recurrence, and now long-term functional outcomes.18,21,22 Arguably, recurrence leading to reoperation poses the highest risks to patients. reoperation, which includes exposure to general anesthesia and reduced mobility secondary to recovery, are nontrivial.2,7 From an outcomes perspective, a gold standard treatment option will demonstrate a minimal risk of recurrence, minimal risk of complications, and a quick recovery period.  

One should commend the authors on the rigor in their approach to this problem. The authors conducted a randomized 1:1:1 prospective study across 4 centers to assess the complications and clinical outcomes associated with the 3 most popular surgical approaches to cSDHs.8 They found that although the TDC was the quickest procedure, it exhibited the lowest rate of “good” outcomes. The highest rates of reoperation were TDC $(19.5\%$ , $P=.07,$ while burrhole craniostomy demonstrated the lowest $(7.6\%)$ . Regarding surgical outcomes after cSDH evacuation, the definition of a “good outcome” has been difficult to ascertain, and the heterogeneity of outcomes defined in previous studies has limited meta-analysis. The author’s incorporation of the modified Rankin score and Markwalder score were critical in consolidating measurements of long-term neurologic function. It is important to note that the study was limited in statistical power and that these trends were lacking statistical significance; nevertheless; the authors have raised the bar in their study design, and their decision to measure both recurrence and standardized long-term neurologic outcomes set a standard for future prospective trials.  

Although surgical management of cSDH may be seen as a foundational skill within neurosurgery, future trials should attempt to control for technical competence. Modeling an approach from trials such as the Carotid Revascularization Endarterectomy vs. Stending Trial (CREST), one may require case review and credentialing to ensure operator volume and technical competencies sufficient to differentiate outcomes. Furthermore, there is a need to standardize the criteria for reoperation via predetermined radiographic and clinical metrics. Trials of this nature may shed light on the operative technique that strikes the balance between risk and outcomes and set the benchmark for acceptable complication rates. Follow-up studies can dissect the learning curve for operators to achieve this benchmark competency and weigh the time-to-mastery against the projected complication rates.23 In conclusion, although short of providing level I evidence, the authors present an excellent framework for future trials. Optimization of cSDH management is likely to come from large trials seeking to understand the interplay of surgical and conservative treatments.
