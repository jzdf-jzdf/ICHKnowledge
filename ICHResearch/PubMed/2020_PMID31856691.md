# Cilostazol Versus Aspirin in Ischemic Stroke Patients With High-Risk Cerebral Hemorrhage Subgroup Analysis of the PICASSO Trial  

Bum Joon Kim, MD; Sun U. Kwon, MD; Joung-Ho Park, MD; Yong-Jae Kim, MD; Keun-Sik Hong, MD; Lawrence K.S. Wong, MD; Sungwook Yu, MD; Yang-Ha Hwang, MD; Ji Sung Lee, PhD; Juneyoung Lee, PhD; Jong-Ho Rha, MD; Sung Hyuk Heo, MD; Seong Hwan Ahn, MD; Woo-Keun Seo, MD; Jong-Moo Park, MD; Ju-Hun Lee, MD; Jee-Hyun Kwon, MD; Sung-Il Sohn, MD; Jin-Man Jung, MD; Jose C. Navarro, MD; Hahn Young Kim, MD; Eung-Gyu Kim, MD; Seongheon Kim, MD; Jae-Kwan Cha, MD; an-Seok Park, MD; Hyo Suk Nam, MD; Dong-Wha Kang, MD; on behalf of the PICASSO Investigator  

Background and Purpose—Although cilostazol has shown less hemorrhagic events than aspirin, only marginal difference was observed in hemorrhagic stroke events among patients at high risk for cerebral hemorrhage. To identify patients who would most benefit from cilostazol, this study analyzed interactions between treatment and subgroups of the PICASSO trial (Prevention of Cardiovascular Events in Asian Ischemic Stroke Patients With High Risk of Cerebral Hemorrhage).  

Methods—Ischemic stroke patients with a previous intracerebral hemorrhage or multiple microbleeds were randomized to treatment with cilostazol or aspirin and followed up for a mean 1.8 years. Efficacy, defined as the composite of any stroke, myocardial infarction, and vascular death, and safety, defined as the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke, were analyzed in the 2 groups. Interactions between treatment and age, sex, presence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus, index of high-risk cerebral hemorrhage, and white matter lesion burden were analyzed for primary and key secondary outcomes. Changes in vital signs and laboratory results were compared in the 2 groups.  

Results—Among all 1534 patients enrolled, a significant interaction between treatment group and index of high risk for cerebral hemorrhage on hemorrhagic stroke $P$ for interaction, 0.03) was observed. Hemorrhagic stroke was less frequent in the cilostazol than in the aspirin group in patients with multiple microbleeds (1 versus 13 events; hazard ratio, 0.08 $[95\%$ CI, 0.01–0.61]; $P{=}0.01$ ). A marginal interaction between treatment group and white matter change on any stroke $P$ for interaction, 0.08) was observed. Cilostazol reduced any stroke significantly in patients with mild (5 versus 16 events; hazard ratio, 0.36 $[95\%$ CI, 0.13–0.97]; $P{=}0.04\$ )-to-moderate (16 versus 32 events; hazard ratio, 0.50 $[95\%$ CI, $0.29-$ 0.92]; $P{=}0.03.$ ) white matter changes. Heart rate and HDL (high-density lipoprotein) cholesterol level were significantly higher in the cilostazol group than in the aspirin group at follow-up.  

Conclusions—Cilostazol may be more beneficial for ischemic stroke patients with multiple cerebral microbleeds and before white matter changes are extensive.

Key Words: cerebral hemorrhage ◼ diabetes mellitus ◼ follow-up studies $\sqsubset$ heart rate ◼ white matter atients with a prior hemorrhagic stroke or multiple cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) are at increased risk of future intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH).1,2 Although this CMBassociated risk of ICH was found to be greater in patients who were than were not treated with aspirin,3 antiplatelet treatment has become standard for patients with noncardioembolic stroke and was beneficial even for those with a prior ICH. Cilostazol showed less hemorrhagic events than aspirin among patients with ischemic stroke. Therefore, the PICASSO trial (Prevention of Cardiovascular Events in Asian Ischemic Stroke Patients With High Risk of Cerebral Hemorrhage) was performed to investigate which antiplatelet agent may be more effective and safe to treat these patients.  

The PICASSO trial included patients with a considerable small vessel disease burden, as shown by high Fazekas scores. The trial showed that cilostazol was noninferior to aspirin in preventing a composite of major vascular events. In contrast to the previous study results, cilostazol failed to reduce cerebral hemorrhage in these high-risk cerebral hemorrhage patients. The incidence of stroke was lower, whereas the incidence of myocardial infarction was slightly higher, in the cilostazol than in the aspirin group. Nevertheless, this study was unable to determine the types of patients who would show greater benefit from cilostazol treatment or to assess how treatment affected patients’ vital signs and laboratory results at follow-up.  

Factors associated with recurrent vascular events and bleeding events include patient age, presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prior ICH, or multiple CMBs and the severity of white matter lesions. To identify patients who would show greater benefit from cilostazol treatment, patients enrolled in the PICASSO trial were subgrouped by these factors, and the interaction between treatment and subgroups on major vascular events, cerebral hemorrhage, any stroke, ischemic stroke, and any systemic bleeding was analyzed. Furthermore, this study also compared the effects of cilostazol and aspirin on vital signs and laboratory results in these patients.  



# Methods  



# Study Design  

The PICASSO trial was a multicenter randomized clinical trial performed at 67 institutions in 3 Asian countries: South Korea, Hong Kong (China), and the Philippines.4 The design and main results of this trial have been described.5 In accordance with the two-by-two factorial design of the study, eligible patients were simultaneously randomized to the antiplatelet arm and treated with cilostazol ( $100\mathrm{mg}$ BID) or aspirin $\mathrm{100mg}$ QD) or to the probucol arm and treated with probucol $500~\mathrm{mg}$ QD) or no probucol. The trial was registered at https://www. clinicaltrials.gov (unique identifier: NCT01013532). As there was no interaction between the 2 arms, the results of each arm were separately analyzed and reported. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of each participating institution, and all eligible patients or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent before participation. This report presents subgroup analysis and changes in vital signs and laboratory results in the antiplatelet arm. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.  



# Participants  

Patients were included in this study if they (1) had a history of noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack within  

180 days, (2) were aged ${>}20$ years, and (3) were at high risk for cerebral hemorrhage. Patients at high risk of cerebral hemorrhage were defined as those who had (1) a clinical history of previous ICH or (2) radiological findings of old ICH (a slit like curvilinear hypointense signal lesion on gradient echo image ${>}8~\mathrm{mm}$ ) or (3) at least 2 CMBs. CMBs were defined as small $(2{-}8\mathrm{mm})$ ), rounded, homogeneous, well-defined hypointense lesions in the brain parenchyma on gradient echo images. Key exclusion criteria included an inability to receive antiplatelet therapy (eg, bleeding diathesis) and a need for potent antiplatelet therapy (eg, patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention within 4 weeks) or anticoagulation (eg, patients with severe cardiomyopathy or congestive heart failure).  



# Follow-Up  

Based on a preplanned follow-up schedule, the patients were examined every 3 months; factors recorded included vital signs, blood test results, electrocardiography, neurological deficits, concomitant medications, adherence, and any adverse events. Vital signs and blood test results were compared at baseline and 13 months after randomization. The patients were followed up until the occurrence of any event qualifying as a primary end point, the patient withdrew consent, the investigator decided to discontinue treatment, or the end of the clinical trial.  



# Outcomes  

The coprimary efficacy and safety outcomes were prespecified at the start of the PICASSO trial. The primary efficacy outcome was the occurrence of the composite of major vascular events, including any stroke (ischemic and hemorrhagic), myocardial infarction, and vascular death. The primary safety outcome was the occurrence of cerebral hemorrhage, including spontaneous ICH and subarachnoid hemorrhage confirmed by imaging. The secondary outcomes were any stroke, ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, or other predefined vascular events. The predefined vascular events included cerebral venous thrombosis, transient ischemic attack without diffusion weighted image lesion, pulmonary embolism, symptomatic deep vein thrombosis, symptomatic peripheral artery occlusion, and any revascularization procedure. Each outcome was confirmed by the Central Independent Adjudication Committee. Any systemic bleeding events (excluding intracranial bleeding) and headache reported by the patients were analyzed as a safety outcome.  



# Subgroups and Statistical Analysis  

To better understand the effects of treatment on primary efficacy and safety outcomes and key secondary outcomes, patients were subgrouped based on prognostic factors. Key secondary outcomes assessed in the subgroup analyses included any stroke and ischemic stroke, the rates of which were considerable. Subgroup analysis was not performed on other secondary outcomes because of their low event rates. Because bleeding is an important issue in the use of antiplatelet agents occurring in a considerable proportion of patients receiving antiplatelet agents and because headache is a common side effect of cilostazol, the rate of any systemic bleeding and headache was additionally included in the subgroup analysis.  

Factors assessed in subgroup analyses included age, sex, presence of hypertension or diabetes mellitus, qualifying criteria for high risk of hemorrhage (history of ICH, radiological findings of ICH, or multiple CMBs), and the severity of white matter disease. Each patient was subgrouped to 1 of the 3 qualifying criteria for high-risk hemorrhage, which was checked by the investigator at the time of enrollment. The high-risk groups did not overlap.  

The absolute number of events was reported for each treatment group and subgroup. The interaction between treatment and each subgroup, reported as the exact number of events, was tested using a 2-sided Wald test. Because of post hoc subgroup analyses, results were not adjusted for multiple testing. Changes in vital signs and laboratory results from baseline to 13 months after randomization were compared in the 2 groups using a generalized estimation equation method. The effect on each parameter of the interaction between antiplatelet arm and visit was determined. As a post hoc analysis, $P$ for interaction $<0.10$ was used to define statistical significance for subgroup analysis. In other analyses, $P{<}0.05$ was regarded to be statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed by 2 designated statisticians (J.S.L. and J.L.), blinded to the treatment assignments, using SAS, version 9.4, software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). The study protocol and statistical analysis plan can be confirmed at https://www.clinicaltrials.gov and in the previous design article.5  



# Results  



# Study Participants  

During the study period, 1568 patients were screened. Of these, 1534 patients were enrolled and randomized, 766 to cilostazol and 768 to aspirin. Among these patients, $89.1\%$ had hypertension, $32.1\%$ had diabetes mellitus, and $57.6\%$ were aged ${>}65$ years. Fazekas scores, indicative of white matter changes, were 1, 2, and 3 in 422 $(27.5\%)$ , 643 $(41.9\%)$ , and 266 $(17.3\%)$ patients, respectively. The qualifying criteria for high risk of hemorrhage were history of ICH in 288 $(18.8\%)$ patients, radiological findings of ICH in 328 $(21.4\%)$ , and multiple microbleeds in 918 $(59.8\%)$ . The enrolled patients were randomized a mean 17 days after ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack. The mean duration of follow-up was 1.8 years (Table 1).  



# Subgroup Analysis  

There were no significant interactions between subgroups and treatment group for the primary efficacy outcome. Only a marginal interaction was observed between the index of high-risk cerebral hemorrhage and treatment group for the composite of major vascular events $(P$ for interaction, 0.10). The interaction between the index of high-risk cerebral hemorrhage and treatment group for the primary safety outcome was statistically significant $P$ for interaction, 0.03). Among patients with multiple CMBs, cerebral hemorrhage rates were significantly lower in the cilostazol than in the aspirin group (1 versus 13 events; hazard ratio, 0.08 $[95\%$ CI, 0.01–0.61]; Figure). There was no significant interaction between other subgroups and treatment on the primary safety outcome.  

In assessing key secondary outcomes, a marginal interaction was observed for Fazekas score and treatment group for any stroke $(P{=}0.08$ for interaction). Patients with mild (5 versus 16 events; hazard ratio, 0.36 $[95\%$ CI, 0.13–0.97]) and moderate (16 versus 32 events; hazard ratio, 0.50 $[95\%$ CI, 0.29–0.92]), but not severe, white matter changes had a lower rate of any stroke when treated with cilostazol than with aspirin (Figure). There was no significant interaction between subgroups and treatment group for ischemic stroke.  

The effect of treatment on the occurrence of any systemic bleeding, except intracranial, also differed according to the Fazekas score $P$ for interaction, 0.05). This is the quantitative interaction of the interaction types. Among patients with mild white matter changes, 5 in the cilostazol group and 25 in the aspirin group experienced any systemic bleeding (odds ratio, 0.20 $[95\%$ CI, 0.08–0.54]). # Follow-Up Laboratory Results  

The groups were well balanced for all variables at baseline. Thirteen months after randomization, the increase in heart rate was significantly greater in the cilostazol group than in the aspirin group $(P$ for interaction, 0.005). White blood cell count $P$ for interaction, 0.002) and LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol concentration $\mathbf{\nabla}P$ for interaction, 0.01) showed significantly greater reductions in the cilostazol than in the aspirin group. HDL (high-density lipoprotein) cholesterol concentrations decreased in both groups but more prominently in the aspirin than in the cilostazol group $P$ for interaction, ${<}0.0001$ ; Table 2).

# Discussion  

Although cilostazol failed to reduce cerebral hemorrhage in the overall population of patients at high risk of cerebral hemorrhage, subgroup analysis showed that cilostazol had less cerebral hemorrhage compared with aspirin in patients with multiple CMBs. Cilostazol significantly reduced the incidence of any stroke in patients with mild-to-moderate white matter changes but not in those with severe white matter changes. At follow-up, cilostazol was associated with a greater increase in heart rate and a lower reduction in HDL cholesterol concentrations than aspirin.  

Cilostazol was first developed as an antiplatelet agent, improving the symptoms of peripheral artery disease, mainly because of its additional vasodilation effect.6 It was also effective for diabetic patients, reducing carotid intima media thickness due to its effect against atherosclerosis (antiatherogenic effect).7 Cilostazol also attenuated the deterioration of albuminuria in patients with diabetes mellitus, mediated by its improvement in adhesion molecules.8 With these pleiotropic effects, cilostazol showed greater benefit than aspirin in diabetic ischemic stroke patients.9 By contrast, the present subgroup analysis of patients in the PICASSO trial failed to show that cilostazol improved primary and key secondary outcomes in patients with diabetes mellitus when compared with aspirin.  

Subgroup analyses of previous trials showed that patients with lacunar infarction experienced greater benefit from cilostazol treatment.9,10 Moreover, imaging markers of small vessel disease showed a significant or marginal interaction in the PICASSO trial. Cilostazol provided greater benefit to patients with multiple CMBs than to patients with prior ICH, as the rate of cerebral hemorrhage in patients with multiple CMBs was significantly lower in the cilostazol than in the aspirin group. Hypothetically, multiple CMBs may represent a more widespread chronically damaged cerebral small vessels and their blood-brain barriers,11 whereas a single ICH may be more driven by a transient spiking of blood pressure damaging a single perforator to bleed massively. As medications influence more systematically in all vessels, widespread, chronically damaged small vessels may be more prone for future hemorrhagic stroke under antiplatelet treatment. But this hypothesis may be proved by another study comparing those with ICH only, multiple CMBs, and those with both. In the same vein, elderly patients who are more prone to major bleeding following long-term use of aspirin12–14 may also favor cilostazol treatment. These results may be due to the lower rate of bleeding complications observed with cilostazol, as this agent protects the security of the blood-brain barrier, especially in patients with diffuse fragile perforators.15

Although white matter change is also a marker of small vessel disease, advanced white matter change differently affects the risks of cerebral hemorrhage and ischemic events.16 Our subgroup analysis found that cilostazol reduced any stroke rate significantly in patients with mild-to-moderate, but not severe, white matter changes. This difference may be explained by the ceiling effect in patients with severe white matter changes. These results are also in good agreement with previous results showing the limited effect of treatment in patients with severe white matter changes.17 That is, appropriately managed blood pressure effectively prevented the progression of white matter lesions but not in patients with severe white matter lesions at baseline.17  

Treatment with cilostazol plus donepezil was found to preserve cognition in patients with mild dementia but not in patients with moderate-to-severe dementia.18 Cilostazol attenuates vascular wall thickening and perivascular fibrosis, reducing the progression of white matter changes.19 However, these effects may be limited only to those without extensively damaged small vessels. Therefore, early treatment with cilostazol before small perforators are extensively affected may be effective for secondary prevention of stroke.  

After treatment for 13 months, cilostazol showed greater reductions in white blood cell counts and LDL cholesterol concentrations but lesser reductions in HDL cholesterol concentrations than aspirin. As half of the patients in the PICASSO trial were treated with probucol—a lipid-lowering agent—HDL cholesterol concentrations decreased in both groups. Because cilostazol increases HDL cholesterol, the reduction in HDL cholesterol level was lower in the cilostazol than in the aspirin group.20,21 The presence of CMBs, severe white matter changes, and lacunar infarction, all markers of small vessel disease, was associated with low HDL cholesterol concentrations.22–24 In addition to the previously demonstrated effects of cilostazol, the ability of cilostazol to increase HDL cholesterol may have had a positive effect, lowering the rate of stroke recurrence in patients with small vessel disease.  

Cilostazol failed to show superiority to aspirin in reducing the composite rate of vascular events, as cilostazol was associated with an increased rate of myocardial infarctions. After 13 months, heart rate had increased from baseline in the cilostazol group but decreased in the aspirin group. Heart rate is a critical determinant of myocardial oxygen consumption.25 Increased heart rate may have increased the burden on the heart, resulting in a higher rate of myocardial infarction in the cilostazol than in the aspirin group. Heart rates should, therefore, be monitored in ischemic stroke patients treated with cilostazol for secondary stroke prevention.  

This subgroup analysis had several limitations. First, the main trial was performed in 3 Asian countries and enrolled Asian ischemic stroke patients at high risk of cerebral hemorrhage. Caution should thus be exercised in extending these results to non-Asian populations. Second, although differences in CMB location may indicate differences in their pathomechanism, CMBs in this trial were not analyzed by location. We plan, however, to perform a separate post hoc analysis of our data to address this issue.  

In conclusion, cilostazol may better prevent future cerebral hemorrhage in patients with small, fragile cerebral vessels, as shown by the presence of multiple CMBs. Cilostazol significantly reduced stroke rates in patients with small vessel disease, especially before they were extensively affected. Cilostazol may, therefore, be more effective for patients with diffuse fragile small vessel disease, especially before they are severely affected.
