# Abstract  

What is known and objective: The benefits and risks of restarting antiplatelet therapy (APT) for patients with spontaneous intracranial haemorrhage (ICH) remain controversial. This meta-analysis was performed to explore the efficacy and safety of restarting APT for these patients.  

Methods: We followed the recommended PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews. Studies from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI and the Cochrane Library were systematically retrieved from the inception of each database to 31 July 2020. We also manually retrieved studies of reference.  

Results and discussion: In this study, seven cohort studies and one randomized controlled trial (RCT) with subjects were included. APT resumption after spontaneous ICH did not significantly increase the risk of major haemorrhagic events (HR 1.15; $95\%$ CI: 0.70-1.89; $p=.59,$ . However, it did not significantly reduce the risk of a composite endpoint concerning occlusive/thromboembolic events $(\mathsf{HR}0.98\$ ; $95\%$ CI: 0.81-1.19; $p=.83)$ and all-cause mortality (HR 0.93; $95\%$ Cl: 0.80-1.08; $p=.35.$ .  

What is new and conclusion: Restarting APT for patients with spontaneous ICH is generally safe. However, the benefits of reducing the risk of ischaemic vascular events and all-cause mortality were not apparent. More RCTs are required.  

# KEYWORDS  

antiplatelet therapy, antithrombotic therapy, intracerebral haemorrhage, meta-analysis, platelet aggregation inhibitor, spontaneous intracranial haemorrhage  

# 1  |  WHAT IS KNOWN AND OBJECTIVE  

Spontaneous, non-traumatic intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. It accounts for $10\%{-15\%}$ of all strokes, but its contribution to overall stroke mortality and disability is over-proportionally high.1 Adults with stroke due to spontaneous ICH often have a history of ischaemic vascular diseases, such as ischaemic stroke, myocardial infarction (MI) or subsequent ischaemic pathologies for which antiplatelet (AP) medicines are indicated.2,3 This is not surprising because ICH, ischaemic stroke and MI have shared risk factors, particularly, increasing age and uncontrolled hypertension.  

Platelets are essential for normal hemostasis, and they adhere and aggregate at the site of injury and contribute to vessel repair following injury. However, the uncontrolled progression of such a process may lead to clot formation, vascular occlusion and the formation and extension of atherosclerosis.4 Antiplatelet therapy (APT) has been the cornerstone for primary and secondary prevention of ischaemic vascular diseases for over 100 years. At least onethird of adults in high-income countries take oral antithrombotic (antiplatelet or anticoagulant) drugs at the onset of intracerebral haemorrhage.5 Although APT is effective in preventing ischaemic stroke and coronary atherosclerotic disease, the patient receiving APT is at an increased risk of bleeding events, such as ICH, which are often fatal.6,7  

Generally, antithrombotic drugs are immediately discontinued because of the risk of early hematoma growth in patients with ICH. Meanwhile, discontinuation of these drugs is often permanent because of the perceived risk of recurrent ICH. The risk of ischaemic vascular events will increase and may put them at risk of disabling stroke and heart diseases due to pre-existing vascular comorbidities.8,9 In the management of spontaneous ICH, whether AP agents can be restarted safely and bring overall benefits for these patients is unclear.10 Hence, the resumption of AP agents after ICH has posed a clinical conundrum. We performed a meta-analysis of available studies to evaluate the efficacy and safety of restarting APT after spontaneous ICH.  

# 2  |  METHODS  

We performed this study according to the guidelines recommended by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalysis (PRISMA),11 the Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE)12 statements and scale of assessing the quality of randomized trial.13  

# 2.1  |  Data sources and searches  

We systematically retrieved relevant literature published in PubMed, Embase, CNKI, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library, from the inception of each database to 31 July 2020. We also manually retrieved other studies for reference.  

The search terms we used to query the databases were as follows: “cerebral hemorrhage,” “intracranial hemorrhage,” “brain hemorrhage,” “antithrombotic,” “antiplatelet,” “anticoagulant,” “aspirin,” “clopidogrel,” “ticlopidine,” “cilostazol,” “dipyridamole,” “ticagrelor,” “tirofiban,” “sarpogrelate,” “rivaroxaban,” “warfarin,” “recurrent intracranial hemorrhage,” “recurrent intracerebral hemorrhage,” “major recurrent bleeding,” “hematoma expansion,” “ischemic stroke,” “thrombosis,” “systemic embolism,” and “myocardial infarction.”  

# 2.2  |  Study selection  

We included studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of APT resumption after spontaneous ICH; only peer-reviewed studies in scientific journals were included. Inclusion criteria for our study were:  

(1) studies with restarting APT after spontaneous ICH; (2) RCT or cohort studies; (3) studies with outcomes of major haemorrhagic events (ICH recurrence or hematoma growth) and major occlusive/ thromboembolic events (ischaemic stroke, systemic embolism and Ml) during the follow-up period; (4) patients $\geq18$ years of age; and (5) sample size ${}_{220}$ patients to preclude inclusion of case reports or small case series; (6) studies that can provide the precise details needed for data extraction; (7) patients with restarting AP medicines continued and did not combine anticoagulant therapy in the duration of follow-up.  

# 2.3  |  Outcome definition  

All included subjects were verified using cranial CT or MRI and the outcomes were identified according to the International Classification of Diseases (10th revision) diagnosis codes.  

# 2.4  |  Data extraction  

A single investigator (B.C.) screened the title and abstract produced by our search strategy and shortlisted studies for further review. Then, these articles were independently reviewed by two investigators (JZ. L. and L.P.) and selected based on the inclusion criteria and quality of literature. We adapted the risk of bias assessments in a previously published meta-analysis on stroke risk and generated eight specific questions to evaluate for potential selection, detection, reporting and confounding bias.14,15 Any disagreements were resolved by public discussions. The following study characteristics were extracted from the included studies: first author, publication year, country, study design, cases of each group, age and gender distribution, location of ICH, AP agents, time to resumption of AP agents, duration of follow-up and study quality.  

# 2.5  |  Quality evaluation  

The Jadad scale13 and the NOS scoring rules16 were used to evaluate the quality of the literature. The total score on the Jadad scale is seven points. A study with score lower than four is considered of low-quality. The total score of the NOs scale is nine stars, and more than six stars indicate a high-quality research. Additionally, publication bias in the included studies was assessed by the funnel plot; if the plot is symmetrical, it is inferred that publication bias does not exist.  

# 2.6  |  Data synthesis and analysis  

RevMan v5.3 was used to combine the data when at least two studies reported on the main outcome indicators. We selected the hazard ratio (HR) with $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) in the included studies as the effect parameter.We used the random-effects model and fixedeffects model to calculate the pooled HR and generated forest plots to display the single study and pooled effect parameter.Chi-square test and $I^{2}$ statistic were used to assess heterogeneity among the studies. Heterogeneity was considered significant if the $p$ -value of the chi-squared test was $<0.1$ or $I^{2}\geq50\%$ 17-19If there was no significant heterogeneity, the fixed-effects model was used to pool data across the included studies; otherwise, the random-effects model was adopted.20,21  

# 3  |  RESULTS  

# 3.1  |  Study selection and characteristics  

We retrieved a total of records relative to our initial search strategy from the selected databases and other resources. A total of 2073 studies were excluded due to duplication. The remaining 3205 titles and abstracts of the studies were screened, of which 91 studies were selected for reading full text. Eventually, eight studies met the inclusion criteria. The literature selection is presented in Figure 1.  

In these studies, seven were cohort,3227 and only one was RCT.5 We were able to calculate pooled HR concerning the outcomes of interest in all of the selected studies. Two of the included studies were from the USA,26,27 two from Denmark,24,25 two from the $\mathsf{UK}^{3,5}$ and two each from Italy22 and China.23 Eight studies were published between 2006 and 2019 and contained 5287 subjects. Of these patients, 1959 received APT, and 3328 avoided APT in the duration of follow-up.The characteristics of the eight studies are presented in Table 1.  

# 3.2  |  Quality assessment  

Based on the Jadad scale and NOS scoring rules, we have listed the final study quality scores in Table  1. The funnel plot contributes a qualitative appraisal of publication bias by observing its symmetry, with no significant publication bias concerning haemorrhagic events that we analysed (Figure 2).  

# 3.3  |  Analysis of outcomes  

# 3.3.1  |  Haemorrhagic events  

All included studies reported the data of major bleeding events (ICH recurrence and hematoma growth).3,5,22-27 Since the results from the heterogeneity test suggested that the heterogeneity among studies was high $(I^{2}=67\%$ , $p<.01\$ , the random effect model was adopted. The final results showed no statistical difference between the APT group and the non-APT group (HR 1.15; $95\%$ CI: 0.70-1.89; $p=.59\$ ). We conducted two subgroup analyses on this composite endpoint. One was concerning different median time of restarting APT (within 3 months, HR 0.88; $95\%$ CI: 0.52-1.49; $p=.64$ , vs. more than 3 months, HR 1.93; $95\%$ CI: 0.79-4.76; $p=.15$ , Figure 3A); another was concerning different follow-up time (within 2 years, HR 0.71; $95\%$ CI: 0.44-1.13; $p=.15$ , vs. more than 2 years, HR 1.70; $95\%$ CI: 0.90-3.21; $p=.10$ , Figure 3B). There were no apparent statistical differences with the two subgroups analyses.

# 3.3.2  |  Ischaemic vascular events  

There were six studies concerning major ischaemic vascular events, comprising 5074 subjects (APT and non-APT).3.5,224,25,27 We adopted the fixed-effect model for no heterogeneity suggested by the heterogeneity test among studies $(I^{2}=0.0\%$ , $p>.5)$ . Finally, HR did not demonstrate a statistical difference between the group of APT and non-APT (HR 0.98; $95\%$ CI: 0.81-1.19; $p=.83$ , Figure 4).  

# 3.3.3 I All-cause mortality  

Four studies reported all-cause mortality, which included 4022 patients (APT and non-APT).22-25 The heterogeneity test results suggested that there was no heterogeneity among the studies $(l^{2}=0.0\%,p>.5)$ , and a fixed-effect model was used. The final metaanalysis showed no statistical difference between the APT and nonAPT groups (HR 0.93; $95\%$ CI: 0.80-1.08; $p=$ .35, Figure 5).  

# 4  |  DISCUSSION  

Patients surviving the most common subtype of intracranial haemorrhage and intracerebral haemorrhage are at risk of both haemorrhagic and ischaemic vascular events. Generally, considering the risks of early hematoma growth or ICH recurrence, antithrombotic drugs (anticoagulant, antiplatelet) are immediately discontinued when patients suffer ICH.5 In this condition, due to the preexisting pathological basis of ischaemic vascular events such as atherosclerosis-thrombosis, the patients' risk of occlusive/thromboembolic events is increased. To obtain overall benefits, patients with ICH should resume antithrombotic therapy. However, the conclusions of relevant research are controversial, and clinical guidelines do not provide high-quality recommendations concerning the safety and appropriate timing of restarting APT.28-30 Clinicians have been presented with a therapeutic dilemma when managing patients with competing haemorrhagic and ischaemic pathologies.3 Hence, results from the current meta-analysis may assist in guiding clinical practice.  

In this meta-analysis of studies with more than 5200 patients with spontaneous ICH, we found that receiving AP agents for these patients did not significantly increase the risk of bleeding events, including ICH recurrence and hematoma enlargement (HR 1.15; $95\%$ CI: 0.70-1.89; $p=.59\AA,$ ). However, there were also no significant benefits in reducing major occlusive/thromboembolic events (HR 0.98; $95\%$ CI: 0.81-1.19; $p=.83\AA,$ and all-cause mortality (HR 0.93; $95\%$ CI: $0.80{-}1.08$ ; $p=.35.$ ) for these patients.  

To understand the association between using APT after spontaneous ICH and haemorrhagic events, Nielsen et $a|^{25}$ found that receiving APT after ICH in patients with atrial fibrillation did not increase the risk of recurrent ICH after 1 year of follow-up $(\mathsf{HR}0.60\$ ; $95\%$ Cl: 0.37-1.03). However, more studies, especially RCTs, are needed to confirm the long-term effects of APT in these patients. Biffi et $a|^{26}$ recruited 104 patients with lobar ICH, which is an underlying disease of cerebral amyloid angiopathy. They found that restarting APT (all aspirin) significantly increased the risk of ICH recurrence during a median follow-up time of 34.3 months (HR 3.95, $95\%$ CI 1.60-8.3, $p=.02$ ). Meanwhile, they found that baseline predictors of ICH recurrence (severity of white matter disease and cerebral microbleeds) likely played an important role in the overall risk of ICH recurrence in these patients.  

The results of the heterogeneity test suggested significant heterogeneity among the studies. We considered that many factors might have an impact on this endpoint. However, due to the data limitations in the included studies, we could only perform two subgroup analyses to explore the source of heterogeneity. The results of a subgroup analysis showed that the risk of bleeding events was not significantly different at different median follow-up times. The major risk factors for ICH recurrence include the location of cerebral haemorrhage and uncontrolled hypertension.31 Additionally, patients use various types of AP agents, and their risks of haemorrhagic events are different.32 As data continue to accumulate on predicting the future risk of ICH recurrence, clinicians can better weigh the risk of receiving APT in individualized treatment of patients with spontaneous ICH.  

The main purpose of using AP drugs is to prevent ischaemic vascular events, especially occlusive vascular diseases due to atherosclerosis-thrombosis. For patients with spontaneous ICH, whether receiving APT can effectively reduce the occlusive/ thromboembolic events will also influence the clinician's choice of prescribing AP agents. For the efficacy of restarting APT in preventing ischaemic vascular events in patients with spontaneous ICH, five studies reported HR data for this endpoint event in our meta-analysis, all of which suggested that restarting APT did not significantly reduce these events. This was unexpected. However, the actual benefits of reducing the risk of occlusive/thromboembolic events might be underestimated. The possible reasons were as follows: First, in these included observational studies, compared to patients who avoided APT, the APT group might be at a greater risk of ischaemic vascular diseases. Besides, the composite endpoint cannot assess the benefits of decreasing the risk among different subtypes of ischaemic vascular events. Additionally, considering the risk of ICH recurrence or hematoma expansion, the APT regimen may have been too conservative to effectively prevent ischaemic vascular events. There is a clear need for large prospective RCTs.

In terms of all-cause mortality, four studies reported relevant data, all of which suggested that restarting APT did not significantly reduce the risk of all-cause deaths. In our meta-analysis, restarting APT after spontaneous ICH did not significantly decrease the risk of all-cause mortality. However, the internal composition of death events could differ among different studies. It is necessary to explore the risk of different types of death events in highquality RCTs to better assess the risks or benefits of management strategies.  

In clinical practice, in addition to the risk assessment of endpoint events based on different pathological changes, the functional outcomes, defined as modified Rankin Scale (mRS), of restarting APT after ICH will also affect clinicians’ decision-­making on antithrombotic therapy. Studies by Murthy et al and Chen et al33,34 explored functional outcomes $(\mathsf{mRS}4-6)$ , which indicated that receiving APT after spontaneous ICH was generally safe.  

We followed the PRISM guidelines strictly to perform this meta-analysis.35 To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis that included any RCTs and chose HR as the effect parameter to explore the efficacy and safety of restarting APT after spontaneous ICH. Nevertheless, our study did have several limitations. First, in our meta-analysis, all but one RCT were cohort studies, leading to insufficient levels of evidence. Second, the available data in the included studies were limited. Although we conducted a subgroup analysis based on the median restart time concerning haemorrhagic events, we failed to find the optimal restart time point. The optimal restarting time still needs further research. Important influencing factors, such as age, location of ICH, ICH volume, type of AP, monotherapy or dual APT etc., prevented us from performing subgroup analysis. Third, the sample size was not large. Notably, the ongoing RCTs, REstart or STop Antithrombotic Randomized Trial in France (RESTART-F, NCT02966119) and Study of Antithrombotic Treatment After IntraCerebral Hemorrhage (STATICH, NCT03186729) will provide stronger evidence for better clinical practice.  

# 5  |  WHAT IS NEW AND CONCLUSION  

The APT resumption in patients with spontaneous ICH did not significantly increase the risk of major bleeding events. However, it also did not significantly reduce the risk of major ischaemic vascular events and all-cause mortality. Notably, large sample size, multicentre and high-quality RCTs are needed to determine the benefits and harms of APT after ICH.
