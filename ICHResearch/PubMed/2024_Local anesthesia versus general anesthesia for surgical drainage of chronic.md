# Local anesthesia versus general anesthesia for surgical drainage of chronic subdural hematoma: a systematic review and meta-analysis Comparaison de I'anesthesie locale et de I'anesthésie generale pour le drainage chirurgical de I'hematome sous-dural chronique : une revue systematique et une meta-analyse  

Christopher Mathew, MBBS $\circledcirc$ . Theodore G. L. Wong, MD, FRCPC . Rachel W. L. Leong, MBBS, MMed (Anaes)  

Received: 26 July 2023 / Revised: 20 November 2023 / Accepted: 21 November 2023 / Published online: 28 February 2024

# Abstract  

Purpose The optimal anesthetic technique for surgical drainage of chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) is still uncertain. We performed this systematic review and metaanalysis to determine if local anesthesia with or without sedation $(LA)$ or general anesthesia (GA) results in better outcomes for surgical drainage of CSDH.  

Methods We searched PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and ClinicalTrials.gov for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective or retrospective studies that compared GA vs LA for adult patients undergoing surgical drainage of CSDH and reported at least one outcome of interest. Primary outcomes of interest included total duration of surgery, recurrence rate, and length of hospital stay (LOS). Secondary outcomes included intraoperative adverse events, postoperative complications, and postoperative mortality.  

Results Eight studies (1,542 patients; 926 LA; 616 GA) were included—two were RCTs and six were observational studies. Pooling the estimates of all available studies, we found that LA was associated with a decreased mean LOS by about two days $95\%$ confidence interval [CI], -3.47 to $-0.77$ ; $P=0.0I$ ; low certainty of evidence) as well as a lower risk of postoperative complications (odds ratio, 0.31; $95\%$ CI, 0.17 to 0.58; $P=0.004$ ; very low certainty of evidence). There was no significant difference in terms of duration of surgery, recurrence rate, intraoperative adverse events, or mortality. The quality of the observational studies was poor to fair, largely because of heterogeneity among the studies. Among the RCTs, one had a low risk of bias and one was deemed to be at high risk of bias.  

Conclusions Local anesthesia with/without sedation for surgical drainage of CSDH may be associated with a shorter LOS, and lower postoperative complications. As most of our included studies were observational in nature, our results should be interpreted as summaries of unadjusted group comparisons. In view of the low certainty of evidence, higher quality evidence is required to corroborate these findings.

Chronic subdural hemorrhage is one of the most common neurosurgical conditions and a significant number of these patients are above 65 years of age. Given the burgeoning elderly population in many developed countries, the prevalence of this condition looks set to increase. Surgical evacuation is recommended for symptomatic chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH).1 Options for surgical management include burr hole drainage, twist drill craniostomy, and craniotomy.2,3 Each surgical approach can be performed under general anesthesia (GA) or local anesthesia with/without sedation (LA). The choice of anesthesia can be influenced by various factors such as patient factors, experience of the proceduralist, and local institutional practices. Elderly patients who are often frail and have significant underlying comorbidities face increased risk of perioperative complications4–6 and there has been a trend towards avoiding GA for this population in other surgeries.7,8 On the other hand, performing the surgery under LA may run the risk of intraoperative patient movement hindering surgery and potential conversion to GA. Liu et al. published a systematic review and metaanalysis on this topic but were constrained by the limited literature available. We also raise several questions regarding the validity and robustness of Liu et al.’s significant findings given that pooled analyses were done on studies with different definitions. There has been renewed concern regarding the growing problem of care of this CSDH population and the role of anesthesia,10 with several new prospective and retrospective studies published since. We thus sought to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis with updated data from new studies and more thorough analyses in the hopes of strengthening the current evidence base.  

# Methods  

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines and registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD42022333388; first submitted 1 June 2022). There were no protocol deviations during the conduct of our study.  

# Search strategy  

We searched the following databases: PubMed (MEDLINE, PubMed Central), Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and ClinicalTrials.gov. We identified relevant trials published from inception until 2 June 2022 based on their abstracts and obtained full texts to screen for inclusion based on our inclusion criteria. We performed a repeat search on 19 January 2023 to identify any trials that might have been published since our initial search.  

Authors R. L. and C. M. developed the search strategy using appropriate keywords ‘‘general anaesthesia,’’ ‘‘local anaesthesia,’’ ‘‘regional anaesthesia,’’ ‘‘burrhole,’’ ‘‘chronic subdural hematoma,’’ and ‘‘CSDH’’ joined with Boolean operators. This search strategy was reviewed and refined by our librarian (W. S. N.). The detailed search strategy can be found in the Electronic Supplementary Material eAppendix. Only English and English-translated studies were used. Abstracts and selected full texts were reviewed by the same two authors to determine suitability for inclusion in the study. We resolved any discrepancies by consultation with a third author (T. W.).  

# Selection criteria  

Inclusion criteria were RCTs and prospective or retrospective studies that directly compared at least one outcome of interest for surgical drainage of CSDH under LA with or without sedation vs GA in patients above 18 yr of age. We did not apply restrictions on the type of local anesthetic used. We excluded studies if they were not in English, had no full text available, or were limited to case reports/series. We did not include studies on acute-onchronic SDH (AoCSDH) as it often requires drainage via a craniotomy, rendering it less suitable to be done under LA. As AoCSDH is also associated with trauma, outcomes may differ from CSDH.  

# Primary outcomes  

Our primary outcomes of interest were total duration of surgery from onset of anesthesia until end of surgery, measured in minutes; postoperative recurrence rate (defined as reaccumulation of hematoma in the ipsilateral subdural space), measured as the percentage of patients; and reduction in LOS (defined as time from admission until discharge from hospital), measured in days.  

# Secondary outcomes  

Secondary outcomes comprised reduction in intraoperative adverse events, defined by number of events; number of postoperative complications, including both medical and surgical complications; defined by number of events; and reduction in mortality rate, measured as the percentage of patients.  

We also explored other post hoc outcomes such as anesthesia onset time, duration of surgery from incision to completion of dressing, intraoperative conversion rate from LA to GA, time spent in the PACU, pain on postoperative day one, time until fitness for discharge, and hospitalization cost in the studies that reported them.  

In the case of missing data, we attempted to contact the corresponding authors to obtain them. Data that we could not obtain were excluded from analysis.  

# Data extraction  

Two reviewers (R. L. and C. M.) independently extracted and compiled data in a standardized format in a Microsoft Excel version 15.0 file (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). For each study included in the systematic review, we extracted the following data, if available: number of patients in each arm, type of operative intervention performed, baseline patient demographics, method of anesthesia including type, volume, and concentration of local anesthetic used, use of systemic sedatives, and conduct of GA.  

# Quality assessment  

To evaluate the risk of bias, we used the revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool (RoB 2) for randomized studies and the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOQAS) for non-randomized cohort studies. The latter was subsequently translated to the equivalent Agency for Health Research and Quality (AHRQ) standards for better stratification, as published in the study by Shamsrizi et al.12 After independent grading by two authors (R. L. and C. M.), gradings were compared and any differences were resolved via mutual discussion. We used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach to evaluate the overall quality of evidence and recommendation for each primary outcome.  

# Data analysis and statistical methods  

Results for continuous variables, such as duration of surgery and LOS, were compared by differences in means and standard deviations (SDs). Dichotomous variables such as the incidence of postoperative recurrence of subdural hematoma were pooled across studies and analyzed with the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio. We attempted to contact the corresponding authors for any missing data. Wherever necessary, data presented as median and interquartile range [IQR] were converted to mean (SD) using Hozo’s formula.13 To account for the possibility of chance findings when evaluating multiple outcomes while minimizing the risk of type II errors, we adjusted the threshold $P$ value for our outcomes using the Bonferroni–Holm correction.14 We only performed metaanalyses for outcomes that included at least three studies, namely duration of surgery, recurrence rate, LOS, postoperative complications and mortality rate. As such, the lowest $P$ value we used to evaluate for statistical significance was $0.05/5=0.01$ .  

Data were analyzed using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK).15 We adopted random effects modelling for our metaanalysis and estimated variance in RevMan using the default DerSimonian and Laird method.16 Nevertheless, we manually corrected this for the primary and secondary outcomes using the method described by Hartung,17 Hartung and Knapp,17–19 and Sidik and Jonkam20,21 (HKSJ method), which has been shown to perform better in meta-analyses with a small number of studies and high heterogeneity.22–25 We created no funnel plot as our metaanalysis included fewer than ten studies which may reduce the power of the test.26 We used the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ statistic to determine the degree of heterogeneity among the trials for each comparison of interest27 and considered a value $>50\%$ as being indicative of significant heterogeneity.  

# Results  

# Study selection  

The initial search returned 257 articles (Fig. 1). After removing duplicates, 232 articles remained. Abstracts were screened and full texts of relevant articles were obtained to assess suitability for inclusion. A total of eight articles28–35 that met our inclusion criteria were ultimately included in our study. Of these, two were RCTs and six were observational studies. These studies comprised a total of 1,542 patients, with 926 patients in the LA arm and 616 patients in the GA arm.  

# Study characteristics  

Five28,29,31,33,34 out of the eight studies were retrospective observational studies, two32,35 were RCTs, and one was a prospective observational study with a retrospective cohort as the control.30 Six studies compared GA with LA plus sedation for surgical drainage of CSDH, while the LA group in Zhuang et al.’s retrospective cohort study28 only had subcutaneous infiltration of lidocaine at the incision site without any sedation. Blaauw et al.’s study31 did not mention the technique used for LA or GA. In terms of LA technique, Surve et al.35 used a dexmedetomidine infusion with boluses of fentanyl and midazolam for sedation, Hestin et al.32 used a remifentanil infusion and three other studies29,33,34 used either midazolam or propofol. The study by Surve et al.35 targeted a score of 4 on the Ramsay Sedation Scale, while the study by Wong et al.34 targeted a Ramsay score of 2 or 3. The other studies did not specify the depth of sedation during the procedure. Anatomical scalp blocks were performed in the studies by Ashry et al.29 and Surve et al.,35 with the former also infiltrating the operative site with local anesthetics. Four other studies28,33–35 administered local anesthetics only to the operative site. For the GA techniques, total intravenous anesthesia was used in two studies,28,32 inhalational anesthesia was used in two studies,34,35 and the type of anesthesia was not mentioned in the remaining studies. # Risk of bias assessment  

We evaluated the risk of bias for the six observational studies28–31,33,34 using the NOQAS and their equivalent AHRQ standards (Table 2). We evaluated the risk of bias for the two RCTs32,35 using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool (Fig. 2). The quality of the observational studies was poor to fair, largely because of heterogeneity among the studies. Among the RCTs, one had a low risk of bias and one was deemed to be at high risk of bias.  

Outcomes of the meta-analysis  

TOTAL DURATION OF SURGERY  

Three studies28,29,35 compared the total duration of surgery between GA and LA. There were 111 patients each in the LA arm and the GA arm. Compared to GA, LA was not associated with reduced total duration of surgery: mean difference $-28~\mathrm{min}$ $95\%$ confidence interval [CI], $-54.09$ to $-1.37$ ; very low quality evidence; $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ , $97\%$ ; $P=0.05$ by HKSJ correction) (Fig. 3). The threshold $P$ value from the Bonferroni–Holm correction that we used for this comparison was $P=0.05/3=0.017$ .  

POSTOPERATIVE RECURRENCE RATE  

Five studies28,30–32,34 examined the postoperative recurrence rate when comparing surgery done under LA with surgery done under GA. These studies comprised 850 patients in the LA arm and 540 patients in the GA arm. Compared to GA, LA was not associated with a reduced postoperative recurrence of CSDH. The odds ratio (OR) for recurrence of CSDH following surgery done under GA compared with LA was 1.09 ( $95\%$ CI, 0.80 to 1.49; low quality evidence; $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ , $0\%$ ; $P=0.48$ by HKSJ correction) (Fig. 4). The threshold $P$ value from the Bonferroni–Holm correction used for this comparison was $P=0.05/2=0.025$  

The definition of recurrence varied among the studies. Blaauw et al.31 defined recurrence as a return of clinical symptoms and imaging evidence of reaccumulation of CSDH requiring retreatment within three months of the initial diagnosis. Zhuang et al.28 also included clinical symptoms and a radiologic increase in CSDH volume warranting reoperation in their definition of recurrence. Wong et al.34 considered any symptomatic CSDH requiring reoperation as a recurrence. Finally, Certo et al.30 and Hestin et al.32 did not state what they considered to be a recurrence.  

LENGTH OF HOSPITAL STAY  

Six studies29,31–35 examined the total LOS for both the LA arm and the GA arm. There were 845 patients in the LA arm and 547 patients in the GA arm. Surgical treatment of CSDH under LA was associated with a significantly shorter mean LOS compared with GA by about two days $95\%$ CI, $-3.47$ to $-0.77$ ; low certainty of evidence; $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ , $68\%$ ; $P=0.01\$ by HKSJ correction) (Fig. 5). The threshold $P$ value from the Bonferroni–Holm correction we used for this comparison was $P=0.05/4=0.0125$ .  

We performed a sensitivity analysis based on sedation methods for this outcome. We compared studies that used opioid-based analgesics,32,34,35 such as fentanyl, remifentanil, or pethidine with studies that only used anxiolytics/sedatives,29,33 such as midazolam and propofol as part of their sedation protocol. For studies that used anxiolytics/sedatives, sensitivity analysis yielded similar results to the main outcome (mean difference [MD], $-2.29$ ; $95\%$ CI, $-3.27$ to -1.30; $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ , $0\%$ ; $P<0.001\$ ). For studies that used opioid-based sedation techniques, there appeared to be a lesser impact on the mean LOS when comparing LA vs GA (MD, $-1.69$ ; $95\%$ CI, $-4.12$ to 0.75; $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ , $74\%$ ; $P=0.17.$ ).

# POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS  

Seven28,29,31–35 studies analyzed the rates of postoperative complications, with 893 patients in the LA arm and 601 patients in the GA arm. The odds of postoperative complications for surgical drainage done under LA were lower compared with GA (OR, 0.31; $95\%$ CI, 0.17 to 0.58; very low certainty of evidence; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=41\%$ ; $P=0.004$ by HKSJ correction) (Fig. 6). The threshold $P$ value from the Bonferroni–Holm correction we used for this comparison was $P=0.05/5=0.01$ .  

The various studies had different definitions for postoperative complications. While all the studies were comprehensive in including both medical and surgical complications, the actual events recorded were different. Only Ashry et al.29 and Wong et al.34 included myocardial ischemia while Surve et al.35 included less severe complications such as snoring, throat pain, and giddiness. We performed a subgroup analysis of specific postoperative complications that were included in three or more studies. The results of this subgroup analysis seemed to suggest a lower risk of pneumonia28,32–34 (OR, 0.21; $95\%$ CI, 0.05 to 0.86; $\begin{array}{lll}{P}&{=}&{0.03}\end{array}$ ) and postoperative myocardial infarction29,32,34 (OR, 0.18; $95\%$ CI, 0.03 to 1.06; $P=0.06)$ for LA vs GA but not for seizures28,29,34 (OR, 1.33; $95\%$ CI, 0.28 to 6.40; P = 0.72) and wound infection28,29,32,34 ( OR, 1.67; $95\%$ CI, 0.38 to 7.32; $P=0.5\$ ), although none reached statistical significance.  

# MORTALITY  

Four studies31,33–35 assessed the effects of surgically draining CSDH under GA vs LA on mortality, with  

790 patients in the LA arm and 494 patients in the GA arm. Compared to GA, LA was not associated with a lower odds of mortality (OR, 0.86; $95\%$ CI, 0.12 to 6.40; very low certainty of evidence; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=33\%$ ; $\begin{array}{r}{P=0.83}\end{array}$ by HKSJ correction) (Fig. 7). The threshold $P$ value from the Bonferroni–Holm correction we used for this comparison was $P=0.05$ .  

The follow-up duration for which patients were observed for mortality ranged widely. Blaauw et al.31 included death within three months while Wong et al.34 limited it to 30 days postoperatively. The remaining two studies33,35 only looked at mortality during the hospital stay, which ranged from a mean of one day to eight days.  

# Discussion  

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a low certainty of evidence that performing surgical drainage of CSDH under LA may be associated with a shorter LOS and lower postoperative complication rates than GA. However, postoperative recurrence, intraoperative adverse events, and mortality may be similar between the GA and LA groups. Of note, intraoperative conversion from LA to GA was low at 0 to $7.9\%$ .  

The primary outcomes of interest of this study included total duration of surgery, postoperative recurrence, and LOS. The total duration of surgery has been shown to be associated with a longer postoperative stay.36 This in turn can have larger-scale implications for logistical planning and cost-savings in health care, as potentially shown by Zhuang et al.28 Length of stay as an outcome was also deemed important enough to be included in six out of eight studies and has been associated with patient satisfaction37,38 while postoperative recurrence remains a significant concern after drainage of CSDH and has attracted a lot of research into potentially modifiable factors for risk of recurrence.  

Liu et al.9 published a similar paper on this topic that included a total of four studies,30,33–35 comprising 195 patients in the GA arm and 196 patients in the LA arm. Our study included the same four studies as well as four more relevant studies (one RCT32 and three observational studies28,29,31)—three of which were published after their literature search. This resulted in considerably more patients (926 patients in LA arm $\nus616$ patients in GA arm) in our study.  

Liu et al. $\mathrm{\Delta},\mathrm{\Delta}_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{9}$ findings of a significantly shorter duration of surgery and lower rates of postoperative complications for LA compared with GA were based on studies with different definitions. For example, when comparing total duration of surgery, the authors included studies that only counted surgical time and those that also counted anesthesia time. The authors also analyzed the composite outcome of postoperative complications, which ranged from serious ones such as myocardial infarction to minor ones such as snoring. Instead, our study gave clear definitions for our outcomes and only pooled studies with the same definition for analysis. We also performed subgroup analyses for specific postoperative complications deemed to be more clinically significant and were able to show a trend towards a lower risk of pneumonia and myocardial infarction with LA.  

The total duration of surgery, which includes both anesthesia onset time and procedure duration, was similar when performing surgical drainage of CSDH under LA vs GA. Even though LA with/without sedation may not require airway manipulation unlike GA, more time may be required to titrate the sedative of choice to an appropriate depth prior to skin incision. Although Surve et al.35 reported that there was a statistically significant reduction in anesthesia onset time for LA compared with GA, the absolute difference of six minutes is unlikely to be clinically significant.  

While our study did suggest a shorter mean LOS with LA, there was high heterogeneity for this analysis. We note that both RCTs reported smaller effect sizes than our overall pooled estimate but further subgroup analysis was challenging as there were only two RCTs. In the RCT by Hestin et al.,32 patients were assessed daily using a set of clearly defined criteria for fitness for discharge. This may have led to a shorter LOS than other studies, where data on LOS was collected only when patients were physically discharged from hospital. Furthermore, it is possible that LA was chosen for patients deemed to be at higher risk in some of the retrospective studies, and yet our results suggest that mean LOS is shorter in this group, which may be a result of the lower likelihood of postoperative complications compared with GA.  

Our findings also suggest an overall lower odds of postoperative complications for LA, particularly for pneumonia and myocardial ischemia, although the certainty of evidence is very low. This may be explained by several factors such as the reduced need for airway manipulation, preserved airway reflexes, reduced time on mechanical ventilation, and potentially earlier mobilization postoperatively for LA. Local anesthesia with/without sedation may also have lesser hemodynamic lability, as shown by Surve et al.,35 which can explain a lower risk of myocardial ischemia. These patient-relevant outcomes are also interlinked, with less postoperative complications resulting in earlier fitness to discharge, and a shorter hospital stay reducing hospital-acquired infections.  

The risk of recurrence after surgical drainage of CSDH has been quoted in the literature to range from $10\%$ to $30\%$ .39–41 Whether the modality of anesthesia affects the risk of recurrence remains equivocal. A single-centre case series by Alnaami et al.42 reported that surgery for CSDH done under LA was associated with a significantly higher recurrence rate $22\%$ vs $6.5\%$ ). In contrast, a large systematic review and meta-analysis on risk factors for CSDH recurrence, comprising 2,244 patients from six studies, did not show any significant difference in recurrence rates between GA and LA.43 This was also mirrored in our study, albeit with slight variations in definitions of recurrence. As opined by Oh et al.,44 a standardized definition for recurrence would be helpful in resolving much of the heterogeneity among the published studies.  

Mortality is another important patient-relevant outcome, especially as CSDH has been described as a ‘‘sentinel health event’’ in this elderly and frail population with mortality of up to $32\%$ at one year.45 Many believe that by avoiding the physiologic disturbances associated with GA, surgery may be safer being performed under LA. Mortality has been extensively studied as an outcome of GA vs LA in other neurologic/neurosurgical patients with similar baseline characteristics such as carotid endarterectomy and endovascular thrombectomy. Nevertheless, there has been no significant difference found in mortality in GA vs LA in these procedures despite several large wellconducted RCTs and systematic reviews.46–48  

We recognize that our study has some limitations. The included studies comprised six observational studies and two RCTs. The different study designs could have introduced confounders that we were unable to account for, influencing our results. In retrospective studies, the choice of anesthesia—and thus outcomes—can be influenced by various patient and surgical factors, such as disease severity and experience of health care providers. There was also significant heterogeneity among the studies, which tempers the level of evidence we are able to provide. The main reasons for this heterogeneity among the studies are likely to be the variable sedoanalgesic techniques used and the variable definitions of outcomes (e.g., for recurrence and postoperative complications). As a result, we were also unable to perform meta-analyses or subgroup analyses for certain outcomes as there were too few studies that specifically looked at these outcomes. Lastly, three29,30,33 out of eight of our included studies included fewer than 30 patients in each arm, which gives rise to the risk for small study effects.  

Indeed, the Improving Care in Elderly Neurosurgery Initiative, a multidisciplinary work group aiming to improve the care of CSDH, has called for more wellconducted trials to address this area.10 Furthermore, patient-reported outcome measures in evaluating treatment efficacy have been gaining recognition49,50 and should not be overlooked. Future RCTs may also look to a standardized set of core outcomes for CSDH studies when it is developed, which takes opinions from health care professionals, patients, and carers.51  

In conclusion, our systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that LA for surgical drainage of CSDH may be associated with a shorter mean LOS, and fewer postoperative complications. Recurrence rates, intraoperative adverse events, and mortality may be comparable between the LA and GA groups. In view of the high heterogeneity among the studies, we were only able to provide low certainty of evidence. We call for higher-quality studies on this topic. It is highly likely that the choice of anesthesia is just one of the many factors that influence patient outcomes,52 but these results can help guide clinicians in counselling high-risk patients planned to undergo CSDH drainage.
