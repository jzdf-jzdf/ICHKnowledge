# Safety of High Dose Erythropoietin Used with Therapeutic Hypothermia as Treatment for Newborn Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy: Secondary Analysis of the HEAL Randomized Controlled Trial

# Abstract  

Objective—To assess whether high dose erythropoietin (Epo) treatment of cooled infants with neonatal hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy results in a higher risk of prespecified serious adverse events (SAEs).  

Study design—Five hundred infants born at $\not{\geq}36$ weeks of gestation with moderate or severe hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy undergoing therapeutic hypothermia were randomized to Epo or placebo on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7. Pretreatment and posttreatment SAEs were compared with adjusted generalized linear models, with posttreatment models adjusted for the presence of a pretreatment SAE. Clinical risk factors and potential mechanisms for SAEs were also examined.  

Results—The rate of experiencing at least one posttreatment SAE did not significantly differ between groups (adjusted relative risk [aRR], $95\%$ CI: 1.17, 0.92-1.49); however, posttreatment thrombosis was identified more often in the Epo group $(\mathtt{n}=6,2.3\%)$ ) than the placebo group (n $=1$ , $0.4\%$ ; aRR, $95\%$ CI: 5.09, 1.32-19.64). The rate of posttreatment intracranial hemorrhage identified at the treatment sites by either ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging was slightly elevated in the Epo group $(\mathrm{n}=61$ , $24\%$ ) but not significantly different from the placebo group $\mathbf{\rho}_{\mathrm{n}}=$ 46, $19\%$ ; aRR, $95\%$ CI: 1.21, 0.85, 1.72).  

Conclusions—A small increased risk of major thrombotic events was identified in the Epo treatment group.  

# Trial registration—NCT02811263.  

Erythropoietin (Epo) has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration as an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent indicated for the treatment of chronic anemia in children and adults since 1989.1,2 Serious adverse events (SAEs) have been identified in adults, including hypertension, thrombosis, seizures, and polycythemia.3–6 Although not Food and Drug Adiministration-approved for use in newborn infants, Epo has been widely used for the treatment of neonatal anemia without short- or long-term safety concerns within a wide range of doses (250 U/kg/dose to $1000\mathrm{U/kg/dose},$ ). $^{7-10}\mathrm{No}$ excess physical or behavioral maleffects were identified in studies of high-dose Epo for neuroprotection in infants born preterm such as the Swiss Trial of Neuroprotection $\mathrm{\Deltan}=442$ ), which used 3000 U/kg/dose $\mathrm{~x~}3$ doses in the first 2 days after birth,10 and the Preterm Epo Neuroprotection Trial (n $=936.$ ), which used 1000 U/kg/dose x 6 doses followed by $400\mathrm{U}/\mathrm{kg}3$ times a week until 32 completed weeks postmenstrual age. Similarly, small, published studies of Epo monotherapy for neuroprotection of term infants with hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) showed benefit and no safety concerns.11–13  

The High-Dose Erythropoietin for Asphyxia and Encephalopathy (HEAL) Trial (NCT02811263) was a multicenter, doubleblind trial which enrolled and randomized 500 infants born at $\not{\geq}36$ weeks of gestation with moderate or severe HIE to receive Epo or placebo in conjunction with standard therapeutic hypothermia (TH). The HEAL protocol14 and primary results15 have been previously published. Surprisingly, a higher risk of cumulative SAEs was reported in Epo-treated infants when all SAEs from birth were included.15 In an exploratory analysis, we now investigate the timing of SAEs relative to Epo dosing.  

# Methods  

# Patient Sample  

A total of 501 infants underwent randomization from January 25, 2017, through October 9, 2019, at 17 sites involving 23 hospitals. All patients enrolled and included in the modified intention to treat analysis of the HEAL Trial are included herein.15 Infants were eligible if they met all four criteria as follows: (1) born at $\not{\geq}36$ weeks of gestation; (2) one or more signs of perinatal depression including Apgar score ${<}5$ at 10 minutes; cardiorespiratory resuscitation received beyond 10 minutes of age; $\mathrm{pH}<7.00$ or base deficit $\ge15\mathrm{mmol/L}$ in an umbilical cord or infant blood gas obtained within 60 minutes of birth; (3) moderate or severe encephalopathy; and (4) TH started within 6 hours of birth and continued for 72 hours. Exclusion criteria were birth weight $\mathrm{\check{\Psi}}1800\mathrm{\Psi}\mathrm{g}$ , head circumference $\mathrm{<30~cm}$ , known genetic or congenital conditions affecting neurodevelopment, hematocrit $>65\%$ , parents considering redirection to palliative care, encephalopathy attributed to a postnatal event, guardian of diminished cognitive capacity, surviving twin undergoing TH, and patient anticipated to be unavailable for evaluation at age 2.14 Patients were randomized to receive intravenous $1000\mathrm{U/kg}$ Epogen (epoetin alfa) or a placebo on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7, in conjunction with standard TH. Informed consent was obtained from parents of all subjects in the study. The study was approved by institutional review board at each of the 17 enrolling sites, and all procedures were done in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability & Accountability Act of 1996.  

# Serious Adverse Events  

Serious adverse events were defined a priori and were chosen either because they have been reported in adult patients receiving chronic Epo or because they are known serious complications of HIE.16–21 The three SAEs reported in adults treated with Epo include thrombosis, polycythemia, and hypertension.3–6,22 For the purposes of this study, we defined the thrombosis SAE as any thrombosis treated with a course of anticoagulation, any venous or arterial thrombosis involving a major vessel not related to a central line, or any symptomatic thrombosis involving a major vessel (eg symptoms such as superior vena cava syndrome). Polycythemia was defined as central hematocrit $>65\%$ , and the hypertension SAE required medical treatment.  

The six SAEs commonly associated with HIE included disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) with clinical bleeding and requiring blood products; pulmonary hypertension requiring inhaled nitric oxide or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) defined as intraparenchymal or intraventricular blood seen on head ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) T1, T2, or diffusion weighted imaging sequences; cardiopulmonary arrest; unexpected life-threatening events; and death. We distinguished cases of ICH who were diagnosed clinically (ie, on a clinical ultrasound or MRI read by the site radiologist) from cases of ICH who were confirmed during a central review of the brain MRIs as previously described.23 Brain MRIs were performed at a median age of 5.0 days (IQR, 4.5-5.8). All SAEs were recorded prospectively, and each was reviewed throughout the study by an independent medical monitor.  

In our initial analysis reported in the New England Journal of Medicine,15 all prespecified SAEs recorded from the time of birth were included regardless of whether they occurred prior to or following the first study drug dose. In this analysis, we sought to analyze SAEs relative to the timing of the first dose of Epo administration. For each SAE, a semistructured text-based description of the event was recorded and included date-time stamps of study drug dosing and date-times of when the SAE was first identified. Because the first study drug dose was given on day 0 or 1, SAEs documented as occurring on day 0 or 1 $\mathbf{\rho}_{\mathrm{n}}=$ $173/384,45\%$ ) were reviewed by two neonatologists independently of one another. Each reviewer determined whether an SAE occurred before or after study drug dosing, and all disagreements $\mathbf{\tilde{n}}=22$ , $13\%$ ) were assessed through a consensus review.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Clinical and demographic characteristics of infants who did and did not experience an SAE were summarized through tabulation and calculation of means (SD) or medians (IQR) and compared using $\chi^{2}$ tests and two-sample ttests. Demographic and clinical characteristics were previously compared by treatment group, and no meaningful differences were identified.15 Individual items of the baseline Sarnat severity of encephalopathy exam have not been previously examined and were compared graphically using a stacked bar chart and evaluated with a $x^{2}$ test.

Days until occurrence of an individual SAE were first compared between treatment groups using simple linear regression adjusting for HIE severity. Analyses of SAEs were then stratified and reported separately according to whether they occurred prior to or following the first dosing of study drug. The relative risk (RR) of each individual SAE associated with Epo treatment was evaluated in prespecified analyses using separate logistic (log link) regression models, adjusting for HIE severity and recruitment site which were stratification factors for randomization.  

In the analysis of posttreatment SAEs, we additionally adjusted the regression model for an indicator of whether the infant had experienced any SAE prior to first study drug dose as an attempt to adjust for a strong predictor of subsequent SAEs that may be unbalanced between treatment groups. To compare total SAEs between groups, we used a quasi-poisson regression model with robust standard errors, and for individual SAEs, we used logistic regression adjusting for HIE severity and site in each model. In a sensitivity analysis, we similarly analyzed the subset of ICH SAEs identified clinically between days of life 0 through 5 that were confirmed by day 5 MRI. In a second sensitivity analysis, we repeated the analyses of SAEs by treatment group stratifying by whether they occurred after day of life 1. All randomized infants received at least one study drug dose and were included in all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.0.2 (Microsoft).  

# Results  

Maternal and infant demographics and characteristics of infants who did and did not have SAEs are compared with the overall group in Table I. Race and ethnicity were by maternal self-report. There were more sentinel events in the infants without SAEs, particularly prolapsed cord. Infants who experienced one or more SAEs were more likely to be outborn than those who had none $87\%$ vs $79\%$ ; $P=.009,$ ). Infants with one or more SAEs were more likely to have lower 5- and 10-minute Apgar scores, lower pH, higher initial base deficit, and ongoing resuscitation at 10 minutes, suggesting they were sicker at presentation. Similarly, $31\%$ of infants who experienced an SAE had severe encephalopathy on the initial Sarnat exam compared with just $15\%$ of infants with no SAEs $(P<.001)$ . All items of the modified Sarnat exam except posture were associated with having one or more SAEs ( $\cdotP<$ .001).  

In Figure 1, we examined whether there was evidence of more severe encephalopathy in the different individual components of the baseline Sarnat exam that might indicate higher severity of injury prior to enrollment. The range of severity of each aspect of the modified Sarnat exam and the distribution of severity by treatment group are shown. No meaningful differences in the severity ratings of individual Sarnat items were identified between treatment groups.  

The days on which SAEs occurred are shown in Figure 2. Major vessel thrombosis occurred later in Epo-treated infants (median, IQR: 15, 7.3-20.5) compared with placebo (median, IQR: 0, 0-2.5; $P=.04,$ ). There was no difference in the timing of other SAEs between treatment groups. However, individual SAEs occurred during different time frames. Pulmonary hypertension and DIC presented early, whereas systemic hypertension, major  

vessel thrombosis, and cardiac arrest occurred later in the hospital course. Among the 87 infants with pulmonary hypertension requiring inhaled nitric oxide or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, $89\%$ $(\mathtt{n}=77)$ ) were identified on day of life 0 or 1, and $88\%$ of the 64 DIC SAEs were identified on day of life 0 or 1. Systemic hypertension tended to be identified later (median, IQR: 7, 4.25-8.75 days), as did cardiac arrests (median, IQR: 7, 1-18 days). An ICH was diagnosed most often on day 5 when the protocolized brain MRIs were performed. The median age of death was 7 days (IQR: 4-16.25). Most infants treated with Epo $\mathbf{\bar{n}}=226$ , $88\%$ ) received all intended intravenous doses, with 25 $(9.7\%)$ Epo-treated and 14 $(5.8\%)$ placebo-treated infants dying prior to the fifth study drug dose.  

The rate of posttreatment ICH identified by the treatment sites by either ultrasound or MRI was slightly elevated in the Epo group $\mathrm{\r{n}=61}$ , $24\%$ ) but not significantly different from the placebo group $\mathrm{\check{n}}=46$ , $19\%$ , adjusted risk ratio [aRR], $95\%$ CI: 1.21, 0.85, 1.72). However, posttreatment ICH confirmed on the centrally read day 5 brain MRI occurred more frequently in the Epo group $(\mathtt{n}=50,21\%)$ ) than in the placebo group $(\mathrm{n}=30$ , $13\%$ , $\mathrm{aRR}=1.54\$ , $95\%$ CI: 1.02-2.34). Because ICH was primarily diagnosed at the time of the protocolized day 5 brain MRI, we cannot identify the exact timing of bleeding; however, $78\%$ $(\mathrm{n}=96)$ ) of the 123 site-identified ICH SAEs were identified prior to day of life 6. A subset of infants was too unstable to obtain an MRI within the prescribed time window (day 4 or 5), so the diagnosis of ICH, if present, was made after day 5.  

In Table II, we present comparisons of SAEs by treatment group according to whether they were identified before or after the first study drug dose was administered. Of the 384 SAEs identified in total, 150 $(39\%)$ were found to have been present prior to administration of the first study drug dose. DIC and pulmonary hypertension accounted for the majority $(83\%)$ of pretreatment SAEs. aRRs and $95\%$ CIs are presented in aggregate and for individual pretreatment SAEs. The RR of having an SAE prior to treatment was not significantly higher in the Epo-treated group (RR, $95\%$ CI: 1.31, 0.95-1.81). Overall, the rate of experiencing at least one posttreatment SAE did not significantly differ between treatment groups (aRR, $95\%$ CI: 1.22, 0.96-1.54) or in a model that additionally adjusted for having an SAE prior to treatment (aRR, $95\%$ CI: 1.17, 0.92-1.49). The results did not meaningfully change when stratifying SAEs by day of life 0-1 or two or greater (Table III). Although the rates of pretreatment DIC ( $13\%$ vs $8.2\%$ ) and pulmonary hypertension ( $17\%$ vs $12\%$ ) were higher in the Epo group than in the placebo group, respectively, neither aRR was statistically significant.  

Posttreatment thrombosis of a major vessel was identified more often in the Epo group (n $=6,2.3\%$ ) than in the placebo group $\mathbf{\tilde{n}}=1$ , $0.4\%$ ; aRR, $95\%$ CI: 5.09, 1.32-19.64). The thrombotic complications seen in the six patients who received Epo included two with deep medullary vein thromboses diagnosed on head ultrasound, one of whom had Herpes simplex type 1 treated with acyclovir; two patients with clots associated with umbilical vein catheters seen in the inferior vena cava and portal vein, respectively; one patient with an acute deep vein thrombosis associated with a lower extremity peripherally placed central catheter; and one patient with an intracardiac thrombus diagnosed by cardiac echocardiogram.  

There were 16 “other” recorded SAEs, none of which differed by treatment group. In addition, two infants were diagnosed and treated for pyloric stenosis, both of whom were treated with Epo.  

# Discussion  

This is the largest randomized trial of Epo administered for neuroprotection in term infants with HIE who were also treated with TH. We undertook this analysis because of the surprise finding that when all SAEs from birth were considered, there was an excess number of SAEs per infant in the Epo-treated group. To examine whether this was an Epo-specific risk, we analyzed SAEs that occurred after study drug treatment separately. In this more specific analysis, we found that the thrombosis SAE, defined as “thrombosis of a major vessel requiring anticoagulation or not related to central lines,” occurred more commonly in the Epo-treated group. Based on the descriptions of these thrombi, three of six infants had indwelling catheters that were most likely the initiating stimulus for the thrombi.  

Thrombotic complications are a well-recognized complication of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents in adult patients. These are thought to be the result of a combination of polycythemia, hypertension, thrombocytosis, platelet hyperactivity, and activation of blood coagulation in addition to underlying conditions that activate additional prothrombotic factors.22 Although polycythemia is not a factor in our neonatal HIE cohort, platelet hyperactivity, DIC, and hypercoagulability may be risk factors in these infants. Previous studies of Epo monotherapy for HIE have failed to show any increased risk, but increased thrombosis of a major vessel and ICH were evident in this larger study of cooled infants with HIE. The role of TH in combination with Epo is not known; however, most literature regarding the hematologic effects of TH in the setting of neonatal HIE suggests that cooling increases the risk of DIC and bleeding but not of thromboses.24–26  

Although no significant difference was noted in the site-identified bleeds, those identified and confirmed by centrally read MRI occurred more commonly in the Epo-treated group. It is possible that the combination of TH and Epo stimulated this result; however, the mechanism is unclear. This could also be because of the difference in sensitivity of head ultrasound with MRI, or it could be due to chance given the multiple comparisons. We did not find an association between Epo and polycythemia, seizures, or hypertension as has been described in the adult population.  

As expected, we found that sicker infants (eg, lower Apgar scores, worse initial $\mathrm{pH}$ , and clinically severe HIE) were more likely to experience one or more SAEs. Interestingly, infants with sentinel events, particularly prolapsed cord, had a lower risk of SAEs. This suggests perhaps that a more chronic exposure to hypoxia-ischemia might increase the susceptibility to adverse events.  

This study has limitations. Despite the relatively large study sample, the incidence of individual SAEs was small, particularly thrombosis of a major vessel, and the study was not powered to detect differences in individual SAEs. Despite prospective collection of SAEs throughout the study and rigorous independent monitoring and evaluation, identification  

of the start time of an SAE relative to study drug timing remains challenging. Further, it remains unclear whether adverse events in the placebo group would have been exacerbated to the level of an SAE had such infants been randomized to and treated with Epo.  

Overall, high-dose Epo treatment in infants with HIE who were treated with hypothermia was associated with a small increased risk of thrombosis, as has been described in adults receiving chronic Epo therapy for kidney disease. Furthermore, we found that Epo treatment was also associated with a small increased risk of ICH, a complication that has not been previously reported in adults or infants treated with Epo.
