# a  bstr  a c  t  

Background and purpose.  –  The  optimal  management of  patients with  tandem lesions  (TL),  or cervical internal carotid  artery (c-ICA) steno-occlusive pathology and  ipsilateral intracranial occlusion, who  are undergoing endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) remains unknown. We  sought to  establish the  feasibility of a trial  designed to  address this  question.  

Materials and methods. –  The  Endovascular Acute  Stroke  Intervention (EASI) study was a single-centre randomized trial  comparing EVT  to  medical therapy for  large-vessel occlusion stroke. Patients with  TL receiving EVT  were  randomly allocated to  acute  c-ICA  stenting or  no  stenting. The  primary outcome was the proportion of patients with  a modified Rankin Scale  (mRS)  score  of 0–2  at  90  days.  Safety outcomes were symptomatic intracranial  hemorrhage (sICH)  at  24  hours and  mortality at  90  days.  

Results. – Of 301  patients included in  EASI  between 2013  and  2018,  24 $(8.0\%)$ with  TL  were  randomly allocated to acute  stenting $(n=13)$ or  no  stenting $(n=11$ ).  Baseline characteristics were balanced. Eight $61.5\%$ ; $95\%$ CI $35.5\%{-82.3\%}$ and  7 $63.6\%$ ; $95\%$ CI $35.4\%{-}84.9\%_{\cdot}$ patients, respectively, had  a  favorable outcome (mRS  0–2; $P=1.0$ ).  One  non-stented patient  had  a symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage.  

Conclusions. –  This  pilot  trial  of patients with TL  undergoing EVT  suggests that  a  sufficiently powered larger TL  trial  comparing acute c-ICA stenting to no  stenting is  feasible.

# Introduction  

Several trials have demonstrated the benefit of endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) in patients with acute anterior circulation large-vessel occlusion (LVO) [1–7]. Patients with tandem lesions (TL), that is, LVO with a steno-occlusive lesion of the ipsilateral cervical internal carotid artery (c-ICA), represented 13 to $32\%$ of all patients in the four EVT trials [1,2,4,7] in which they were not systematically excluded. A meta-analysis of five EVT trials [8] confirmed benefit of thrombectomy in TL patients, but only one trial reported how the c-ICA was managed [9]. One approach is to traverse the c-ICA lesion, perform thrombectomy, deferring possible c-ICA treatment by endarterectomy or stenting beyond the acute phase (no acute stenting, NAS). The other approach is to acutely stent the c-ICA during EVT (acute stenting, AS). To our knowledge, no randomized data comparing AS to NAS in TL patients have yet been published, and the optimal management of the c-ICA lesion remains uncertain.  

The Endovascular Acute Stroke Intervention (EASI; NCT02157532) trial compared EVT with medical therapy in patients with LVO, and randomly allocated TL treatment when present [10]. We  report the characteristics and outcomes of EASI patients with TL randomly allocated to either AS or NAS.

# Materials and methods  



# Trial design  

EASI was an investigator-driven, single-centre, non-funded, randomized care trial [10] initiated in 2013, comparing EVT to medical therapy for patients with suspected LVO with second randomization of c-ICA treatment in TL patients. Following the presentation of MR CLEAN [1] in late 2014, EASI continued as a prospective EVT registry from 2015 to 2018, with TL patients still offered randomization of c-ICA treatment. The study was approved by our Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from patients or representatives.  



# Patients  

Patients with ${\mathsf{age}}\geq18$ years, ${\mathrm{NIHSS}}\geq8$ , onset of symptoms $\leq5$ hours or clinical symptom-imaging mismatch if $\geq5$ hours and suspected or proven MCA (M1 or M2  segments) or supraclinoid ICA occlusion were eligible. Inclusion in the TL sub-study required identification of a severe $(\geq70\%)$ ipsilateral c-ICA stenosis or occlusion on either CT or catheter angiography. Exclusion criteria were well-established target-territory infarction or hemorrhage and comorbidities associated with a poor 90-day outcome.  

Randomization and outcomes  

Randomization to EVT or medical therapy was web-based (www.medscinet.com) using adaptive stratified sampling with the following criteria:  

• thrombolysis use;   
$\bullet\leq3$ hours from onset;   
• $\mathrm{NIHSS}\le16$ .  

Patients allocated to EVT with a TL identified on vascular imaging underwent a second 1:1 randomization to either AS or NAS.  

As per care trial design, outcomes, adverse events and mortality were recorded at standard 90-day clinical follow-up in an unblinded fashion.  

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0–2 assessed in-person at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included proportion of patients with symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) on 24-hour brain imaging as per ECASS-2 criteria [11] and mortality at 90 days. Angiographic outcomes included the proportion of patients achieving TICI 2b-3 and postprocedural ICA stenosis $\geq70\%$ as determined from clinical radiology reports.  

Endovascular procedure  

At our center, EVT is generally performed using local anesthesia. The order of stenting relative to thrombectomy was left to operator preference and emboli protection devices were not used. A self-expanding c-ICA stent was usually deployed. Among patients assigned to NAS, c-ICA angioplasty to facilitate intracranial access was permitted. At the end of the procedure, c-ICA patency was verified. No strict recommendations were made regarding antiplatelet regimens. Generally, oral or intrarectal single antiplatelet agents were preferred for thrombolyzed patients (Aspirin $325\mathrm{mg~PO}$ or $650\mathrm{mg}$ PR bolus if antiplatelet naïve or Clopidogrel $300\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{PO}$ if not). A second agent was added after brain imaging at 6–24 hours. For non-thrombolyzed patients, dual antiplatelet therapy was usually given immediately post-procedure. This regimen, while fairly commonly used, is theoretically limited by the relative delay in antiplatelet effect when agents are administered orally or rectally. Antiplatelet resistance was not performed as this is not part of clinical routine at our institution.  



# Statistics  

The number of patients needed to reach a statistically significant difference between groups was not predetermined for this sub-study. All analyses are thus exploratory. Standard descriptive statistics were used, with confidence intervals calculated according to the Wilson procedure without correction for continuity. A single Fisher exact test with a two-sided $\upalpha=0.05$ was used to compare the proportions of patients with a favorable outcome. Analyses were performed by intention-to-treat (ITT) using GraphPad (http://www.graphpad.com).

# Results  

Of 301 EASI patients enrolled between April 2013 and December 2018, 24 $(8.0\%)$ with a TL were randomly allocated to AS $(n=13)$ or NAS $\left(n=11\right)$ ) (Fig. 1). All patients had a TL confirmed by catheter angiography during EVT. Baseline characteristics were similar in both groups (Table 1). Half of all patients had complete c-ICA occlusion and only one patient had a non-atherosclerotic c-ICA lesion (cardioembolism). Although not explicitly excluded, no TL patients with dissection were enrolled.  

Two AS patients crossed over to NAS because c-ICA recanalization with thromboaspiration and angioplasty was deemed sufficient by the treating physicians. In one of these patients, the etiology of the c-ICA occlusion was embolic. No patients were considered to have chronic c-ICA occlusions, for which stenting, while potentially beneficial [12], is not routinely indicated.  

Two patients $(18.2\%)$ assigned to NAS underwent c-ICA angioplasty for intracranial access while 9 $(81.8\%)$ had no targeted c-ICA revascularization. Only two $(18.2\%)$ NAS patients had delayed carotid endarterectomy and none had delayed stenting. Among the 9 remaining NAS patients who did not undergo delayed revascularization, the reason is known for 7: 3 had functional outcomes too poor (mRS 5 or 6) to warrant such a procedure, 3 had an end-of-EVT residual stenosis of $\leq70\%$ and 1 had intraprocedural re-occlusion of the $\mathbf{c}$ -ICA lesion.  

Of 13 AS patients, 9 $(69.2\%)$ also underwent angioplasty: 3 prior to and 6 following stenting. Among the 11 stented patients, most (9 or $81.8\%.$ were stented after thrombectomy. Of all 13 AS patients, $9(69.2\%)$ were administered a single oral antiplatelet agent with a delayed second agent.  

Eight of 13 AS patients $61.5\%$ ; $95\%$ CI $35.5\%{-82.3\%}$ and 7 of 11 NAS patients $63.6\%$ ; $95\%$ CI $35.4\%{-}84.9\%)$ had a favorable 90- day outcome (mRs 0–2; $\mathrm{P}=1.0$ ). (Fig. 2). One NAS patient suffered a sICH and died (Table 2). One AS patient died beyond the acute phase.

# Discussion  

To our knowledge, this is the first published randomized dataset comparing acute management of c-ICA using either AS or NAS. Acute c-ICA stenting may more effectively treat the cause of stroke (whether dissection or atherosclerotic plaque), favor intracranial clot lysis and decrease the risk of recurrence while improving cerebral perfusion. However, stent placement carries a risk of in-stent thrombosis while foregoing stenting avoids early administration of antiplatelet agents as well as potential reperfusion injury, thus possibly attenuating sICH risk, particularly in thrombolyzed patients and those with a large ischemic core. Deferring treatment also allows endarterectomy or stenting to be performed in a more controlled setting.  

An international survey found wide variability regarding the optimal management of TL patients [13] while meta-analyses [14–16] of case-series report no differences in outcomes with AS and NAS. However, these analyses did not include more recent large retrospective cases-series suggesting that stented patients receiving acute antiplatelet medications had more favorable outcomes than patients with no c-ICA intervention or angioplasty alone [17,18].  



# Study feasibility  

Approximately 5 patients were recruited per year at a single tertiary thrombectomy centre, suggesting that any future larger efficacy trial will require multi-centre involvement. However, as overall thrombectomy rates continue to increase [19], it can reasonably be expected that more tandem patients will be treated annually and thus the number of eligible patients will also increase. In this study, cross-over occurred in only 2 of 24 patients, both from AS to NAS. If a cross-over rate of $8\%$ is maintained in a larger trial, adherence to treatment allocation could be considered satisfactory. Finally, completeness of follow-up at 90 days despite an extremely pragmatic design also suggests feasibility of a larger efficacy trial.  



# Study limitations  

Our study, while demonstrating feasibility, has several limitations including the small number of patients from a single academic center and the unblinded outcome assessments. Recruitment occurred over about 5 years, during which tools and techniques for EVT have evolved. Finally, delayed stent patency was not routinely evaluated and stent thrombosis has recently been shown to be relatively frequent and associated with a worse functional outcome [20]. While these limitations preclude clinically meaningful conclusions, these data primarily confirm the feasibility of a larger trial.  



# Conclusions  

The question of how best to manage patients with TL undergoing EVT remains unanswered by existing data. Finding an answer will require a large multi-centre randomized trial, and this singlecentre pilot study suggests that a sufficiently powered larger trial comparing acute c-ICA stenting to no stenting is feasible.
