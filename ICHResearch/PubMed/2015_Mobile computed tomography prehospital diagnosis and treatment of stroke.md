# Mobile computed tomography: prehospital diagnosis and treatment of stroke  

Martin Ebinger, Jochen B. Fiebach, and Heinrich J. Audebert  

# Purpose of review  

Mobile computed tomography (CT) scanning in specialized ambulances has recently become feasible. Two randomized trials have used mobile CTs for prehospital thrombolysis. This short review summarizes the available literature on this topic and provides an outlook on potential future developments.  

# Recent findings  

Mobile CT in acute suspected stoke is feasible and helps to diagnose, triage and treat patients. It is an integral component of a novel and promising stroke research platform in specialized ambulances with telemedicine connection and neurological expertise on board.  

# Summary  

Mobile CTs can speed up stroke treatment, especially thrombolysis; they allow for selecting the most adequate hospital destinations; and they offer new means of stroke research.  

# Keywords  

prehospital thrombolysis, stroke, telemedicine  

# INTRODUCTION  

Prehospital diagnosis and treatment of stroke can be challenging. Presentations of stroke symptoms vary, they may be mimicked by other diseases and decisions have to be made under considerable time pressure. Compared with other body tissues, brain cells are extremely vulnerable to ischaemia. They die within minutes [1]. Thrombolysis with intravenous (i.v.) recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) is the only effective, evidence-based treatment of acute ischaemic stroke up to $4.5\mathrm{h}$ of symptom onset. Effects of tPA are time-dependent. With every minute delay, fewer people are saved from disability [2–4]. Therefore, American and European guidelines recommend giving tPA as fast as possible [5,6]. However, exclusion of haemorrhagic stroke by brain imaging is mandatory before tPA can be administered. Many hospitals around the world are struggling to keep the time from hospital arrival to treatment start (door-to-needle time) below 60 min [7]. A very fast in-hospital concept was developed in Helsinki and has recently been adopted by the Royal Melbourne Hospital [8,9]. One of the basic principles of this concept is to do as much as possible in the prehospital phase of a stroke emergency and as little as necessary after arrival in the hospital before tPA treatment [8].  

However, in addition to in-hospital delays, prehospital delays contribute to the total time to treatment [10,11]. Some patients choose to wait for spontaneous recovery from symptoms prior to calling emergency services. Dispatchers are often challenged by the way patients describe their symptoms. Therefore, they may assign the wrong level of urgency to an emergency call for a stroke patient that has been mistaken for something else. Special stroke algorithms may facilitate the accuracy of these tele-diagnoses [12]. In conventional emergency care, the patient’s vital parameters are usually checked after arrival at the scene and prior to transportation, usually to the nearest hospital. The nearest hospital, however, may not be the most adequate hospital. A hospital adequate for stroke patients provides Stroke Unit (or Stroke Center) care and tPA treatment 24/7. Recently, several studies have indicated that prehospital stroke care including thrombolysis in specialized ambulances equipped with computed tomography (CT) scanners is feasible. The CT aboard the ambulances does not only allow for thrombolysis but also enables accurate diagnosis and triage in the prehospital setting. This review summarizes the available literature on this topic and provides an outlook on potential future developments.  

# PREHOSPITAL DIAGNOSIS  

According to the WHO, stroke is a clinical diagnosis based on the presence of a sudden focal (or global) neurological deficit in the absence of a better explanation than stroke [13]. More imaging-based definitions have been proposed recently [14]. In any case, currently, it is not possible to reliably differentiate between haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke without brain imaging [15]. Recent technological advances provided the opportunity to integrate small CT scanners into ambulances (Fig. 1). This made CT imaging in the prehospital setting feasible.  

# PLAIN COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY  

Silke Walter et al. [16] from Homburg (Saarland), Germany, were the first to report on the use of such a specialized stroke ambulance in 2010. They reported that the differentiation between haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke was feasible in the prehospital setting. Although the diagnosis of ischaemic stroke was made in a 66-year-old patient with a sudden onset of a right sided paresis (NIHSS 16) after the exclusion of haemorrhage via CT, the diagnosis of haemorrhagic stroke was made in a 63-year-old lady with left-sided hemiparesis (NIHSS 7) [16].  

In 2012, Silke Walter et al. [17] published the results of 53 stroke patients diagnosed in their ‘mobile stroke unit (MSU)’ compared with 47 patients in the control group. The time from alarm to end of CT scanning was $34\mathrm{{min}}$ [median; interquartile range (IQR) $30{-}38\operatorname*{min}]$ , a remarkable $38{\cdot}\mathrm{min}$ difference $[95\%$ confidence interval $(95\%$ CI) 33–43] compared with the control group (median $71\mathrm{min}$ , IQR $62{-}87\mathrm{min}$ ; $P<0.0001$ ). Prehospital CT imaging supported the diagnosis in 53 patients, with $55\%$ ischaemic and $4\%$ haemorrhagic strokes. Stroke mimics were diagnosed in $23\%$ and transient ischaemic attacks in $15\%$ of all prehospital cases. However, some scans could not be performed in the ambulance due to scanner defects $(n=8)$ , dysfunction on steep streets $(n=2)$ and overweight of patients $(n=2)$ .  

The PHANTOM-S study started in 2011 [18]. Similar to the study by Walter et al. [17], a Stroke Emergency Mobile (STEMO) equipped with a CT scanner and point-of-care laboratory is used to shorten alarm-to-treatment times in acute ischaemic stroke patients, but some relevant changes have been made $[19^{\bullet}]$ . The radiologist is no longer on board the ambulance because CT scans are read remotely via teleradiology. With special fixation of the scanner and full emergency equipment, patients are transported to the hospital in STEMO, although this transport was executed in normal ambulances in the MSU model. In 2013, Weber et al. $[20^{\bf{s}\bf{s}}]$ reported on prehospital CT scans performed in 64 patients aboard the STEMO during the pilot phase of this study. Eight CT scans $(12\%)$ had motion artefacts and one case of CT dysfunction occurred. Of all scans, $95\%$ had sufficient quality for reading [21&]. The CT scanner (CereTom, eight-slice mobile CT scanner; NeuroLogica Danvers, Massachusetts, USA) in the STEMO is locked during travel and unlocked when scanning. On one occasion, the CT scanner could not be moved back into the locked position requiring transport of the patient by another ambulance $[20^{\bf{s}\bf{s}}]$ . In the week-randomized main PHANTOM-S study, which overall included more than 6000 individuals, there was not a single breakdown of the CT scanner during missions $[22^{\bf{s}\bf{s}}]$ . The median alarm-to-imaging time after STEMO deployment was $35\mathrm{{min}}$ $(30{-}42\operatorname{min})$ as opposed to $50\mathrm{min}$ (43–59) during control weeks $(P<0.001)$ $[22^{\mathbf{s}\mathbf{\overline{{]}}}}$ . Among the 1804 patients with STEMO deployment, 614 patients were diagnosed with ischaemic stroke, 45 with intracerebral haemorrhage, three with subarachnoid haemorrhage, 182 with transient ischaemic attacks and 22 with other cerebrovascular diseases $[22^{\mathbf{s}\mathbf{\overline{{]}}}}$ .

During the PHANTOM-S pilot study, only one CT angiography was performed $[20^{\bf{s}\bf{=}}]$ . However, with increasing routine of staff aboard the STEMO, the number of prehospital CT angiographies grew.

Technically, a prehospital CT perfusion scan in stroke patients is feasible, but limited results on its use have been published yet [23].  

# IMAGE TRANSFER  

Prehospital CT scanning is either accompanied by a radiologist in the ambulance [17] or an on-call radiologist connected via telemedicine $[22^{\bf{s}\bf{=}}]$ . Transmission time depends on the used mobile phone net and can be slowed when multiple persons use internet either in the geographic cell of the stroke ambulance or of the remote radiologist. During the PHANTOM-S pilot study, transfer of CT images to on-call neuroradiologists were delayed by $5{-}15\mathrm{min}$ in two cases $[20^{\bf{s}\bf{s}}]$ . With higher bandwidth of the mobile information technology (IT) connection, image transfer can be accelerated. In order to improve transmission rates, the new ’long-term evolution (LTE)’ technology allows prioritization of special users such as telemedicine providers [24].  

# PREHOSPITAL TREATMENT: THROMBOLYSIS  

The use of mobile CT scanners is a key component of the prehospital thrombolysis concept practiced in the Saarland and Berlin $[17,22^{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}}]$ . The exclusion of haemorrhage has allowed for 12 prehospital thrombolysis in the Saarland and 177 in Berlin $[17,22^{\mathbf{u}\mathbf{\cdot}\mathbf{I}}$ . In both studies, there were significant time reductions from alarm to treatment. However, thrombolysis is not the only treatment neurologists can offer to stroke patients. For instance, recent data suggest that early blood pressure lowering may have beneficial effects in haemorrhagic strokes [25].  

# BLOOD PRESSURE MANAGEMENT  

Walter et al. [17] reported that pharmacological blood pressure management was started in eight out of 53 patients before hospital arrival. In contrast, none of the 47 patients in the control groups received early blood pressure management. Analyses of prehospital blood pressure in haemorrhagic strokes are underway in the STEMO project but not yet available.  

# REVERSAL OF ORAL ANTICOAGULATION  

The detection of intracranial haemorrhages on CT scans including intracerebral, subarachnoidal or traumatic hematoma allows for reversal of oral anticoagulants using prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) [26]. Although numerous patients received PCC in STEMO, comprehensive evaluations have not yet been published.  

# PREHOSPITAL TRIAGE  

Already in the first report of prehospital CT scanning, the information gained from imaging was used to triage patients for further treatment. [16] The first patient was started on i.v. tPA before hospital arrival but then went on for mechanical recanalization in the hospital. CT scans of the patient with intracerebral haemorrhage were transmitted to the neurosurgeons in the hospital who decided against surgery.  

In 2012, Kostopoulos et al. [23] reported on prehospital triage using the information from CT imaging acquired in a ‘mobile stroke unit’ in four patients. One was admitted to a primary hospital after per-hospital exclusion of stroke; a second patient with haemorrhagic stroke was transferred to a neurosurgery department; a third patient with ischaemic stroke was admitted to a stroke unit; and a fourth patient received multimodal CT including CT perfusion imaging that revealed a subacute lacunar stroke without necessity for further aggressive treatment [23].  

The PHANTOM-S study showed that patients with cerebrovascular disease cared for in STEMO were significantly less frequently delivered to hospitals without Stroke Unit compared with the same patient group cared for in the conventional emergency system (5.5 vs. $12.7\%$ ) $[22^{\bf{s}\bf{s}}]$ Although correct diagnosis and adequate delivery are a composite of specialized neurological assessment, brain imaging and patient persuasion, prehospital CT scanning is of enormous help. Further analyses of directed admission to specialized facilities in specific stroke subtypes are currently ongoing.  

Taken together, prehospital CT imaging offers substantial improvement of accuracy when triaging patients for hospital admission. Some patients with spontaneous superficial intracerebral haemorrhage without intraventricular haemorrhage detected on prehospital CT scans may benefit from early surgery [27]. Others with malignant ischaemic stroke should also be admitted to hospitals with neurosurgery departments when hemicraniectomy may be required during the acute phase of stroke [28,29]. Patients with subarachnoid haemorrhage $n=3$ in the PHANTOM-S study) may require endovascular treatment or neurosurgery $[22^{\bf{s}\bf{s}}]$ . Despite the current lack of proven benefit, patients with large vessel occlusions, unlikely to open with i.v. tPA alone, may be referred to hospitals with neuro-interventionalists for mechanical recanalization as demonstrated by Walter et al. [16,30–32].  

# OUTCOMES AND COST-EFFECTIVENESS  

Both stroke ambulance projects used time reductions (either from alarm to therapy decision [17] or from alarm to thrombolysis $[22^{\bf*\bf\delta}])$ as the primary endpoint. Although safety (secondary haemorrhage and early mortality) was analysed in the PHANTOM-S-study as the primary safety endpoint, both studies were not designed to prove a clinical benefit of prehospital thrombolysis with regard to functional outcome. Despite the established relationship between shorter time to thrombolysis and better treatment effects, doubts were expressed whether this association holds true within the ultra-early time window of less than $60\mathrm{{min}}$ [33]. The reason for this scepticism is that virtually no patients were included in previous randomized controlled tPA trials within the so-called ‘golden hour’ and prediction of the time to treatment effects remains difficult, therefore. It is reassuring that a posthoc analysis of the PHANTOM-S study showed that patients treated within $60\mathrm{{min}}$ had an almost doubled probability of being discharged home compared with those treated later $[34^{\bf=\bf4}]$ . As prehospital stroke care in stroke ambulances is costly, more data on clinical efficacy and cost-effectiveness are desirable. Evaluations on these issues are underway.  

# LIMITATIONS OF THIS REVIEW  

So far, the available reports on prehospital use of stroke ambulances equipped with CT scanners are limited to two groups from Germany. The field of prehospital thrombolysis is new and data are just beginning to emerge. Other groups have started using specialized stroke ambulances with CT-scanning ability but have not published results so far (http://clinicaltrials.gov/show/NCT02190500; http://my.clevelandclinic.org/neurological_institute/ cerebrovascular-center/treatment-services/mobilestroke-unit.aspx).  

# FUTURE DIRECTIONS  

Prehospital CT imaging is a major leap forward to speed up time critical diagnosis and treatments in stroke. Prehospital CT imaging is also a novel opportunity to conduct stroke research at a point in time where it matters most: the ultra-early phase of stroke. Although the results of the FAST-MAG trial were neutral [35,36], prehospital imaging may help to select patients with a potential benefit from neuroprotective treatment. Several biomarkers have been studied in the PHANTOM-S trial and will be analysed in conjunction with imaging data acquired at the same early points in time during stroke progression. They aim at the differentiation between haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke and the clarification of the time from stroke onset in wake-up strokes. Currently, STEMO is integrated in the recruitment for other stroke trials such as the European WAKE-UP trial [37] and the ATCH-II trial (https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT01176565 ?term $=$ atach&rank $=2$ ). The neurological expertise aboard the specialized ambulances and the imaging possibilities combined with teleradiological and/or telemedicine support open new avenues for prehospital stroke research.  

# CONCLUSION  

Prehospital CT imaging may turn out as a milestone on the road towards improved stroke outcomes. It allows for earlier diagnosis, treatment and triage of acute stroke patients, and in addition, it forms an integral part of a novel and promising research platform to test new biomarkers and promising therapeutic agents.
