# Tranexamic acid versus placebo in individuals with intracerebral haemorrhage treated within 2 h of symptom onset (STOP-MSU): an international, double-blind, randomised, phase 2 trial  

Nawaf Yassi\*, Henry Zhao\*, Leonid Churilov, Teddy Y Wu, Henry Ma, Huy-Thang Nguyen, Andrew Cheung, Atte Meretoja, Duy Ton Mai, Timothy Kleinig, Jiann-Shing Jeng, Philip M C Choi, Phuc Dang Duc, Helen Brown, Annemarei Ranta, Neil Spratt, Geoffrey C Cloud, Hao-Kuang Wang, Rohan Grimley, Karim Mahawish, Der-Yang Cho, Darshan Shah, Thai My Phuong Nguyen, Gagan Sharma, Vignan Yogendrakumar, Bernard Yan, Emma L Harrison, Michael Devlin, Dennis Cordato, Nicolas Martinez-Majander, Daniel Strbian, Vincent Thijs, Lauren M Sanders, David Anderson, Mark W Parsons, Bruce C V Campbell, Geoffrey A Donnan†, Stephen M Davis†, and the STOP-MSU Trial Investigators‡  



# Summary  

Background Tranexamic acid, an antifibrinolytic agent, might attenuate haematoma growth after an intracerebral haemorrhage. We aimed to determine whether treatment with intravenous tranexamic acid within $2\textbf{h}$ of an intracerebral haemorrhage would reduce haematoma growth compared with placebo.  

Methods STOP-MSU was an investigator-led, double-blind, randomised, phase 2 trial conducted at 24 hospitals and one mobile stroke unit  in Australia, Finland, New Zealand, Taiwan, and Viet Nam. Eligible participants had acute spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage confirmed on non-contrast CT, were aged 18 years or older, and could be treated with the investigational product within $2\mathbf{h}$ of stroke onset. Using randomly permuted blocks (block size of 4) and a concealed pre-randomised assignment procedure, participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive intravenous tranexamic acid (1 g over ${\bf10}{\bfmin}$ followed by ${\bf18}$ over $^{81}$ ) or placebo (saline; matched dosing regimen) commencing within $2\textbf{h}$ of symptom onset. Participants, investigators, and treating teams were masked to group assignment. The primary outcome was haematoma growth, defined as either at least $33\%$ relative growth or at least $6\mathbf{mL}$ absolute growth on CT at $24\mathbf{h}$ (target range $\mathbf{18}\mathbf{-}3\mathbf{0}\mathbf{h}|$ ) from the baseline CT. The analysis was conducted within the estimand framework with primary analyses adhering to the intention-to-treat principle. The primary endpoint and secondary safety endpoints (mortality at days 7 and 90 and major thromboembolic events at day 90) were assessed in all participants randomly assigned to treatment groups who did not withdraw consent to use any data. This study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03385928, and the trial is now complete.  

Findings Between March 19, 2018, and Feb 27, 2023, 202 participants were recruited, of whom one withdrew consent for any data use. The remaining 201 participants were randomly assigned to either placebo $\scriptstyle(\mathbf{n}=98)$ or tranexamic acid $_{(\mathbf{n}=103}$ ; intention-to-treat population). Median age was 66 years (IQR 55–77), and 82 $(41\%)$ were female and 119 $(59\%)$ were male; no data on race or ethnicity were collected. CT scans at baseline or follow-up were missing or of inadequate quality in three participants (one in the placebo group and two in the tranexamic acid group), and were considered missing at random. Haematoma growth occurred in 37 $(38\%)$ of 97 assessable participants in the placebo group and 43 $(43\%)$ of 101 assessable participants in the tranexamic acid group  (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1·31 $95\%$ CI $_{0\cdot72}$ to 2·40], $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p}}=0\cdot37]$ ). Major thromboembolic events occurred in one $(1\%)$ of 98 participants in the placebo group and three $(3\%)$ of 103 in the tranexamic acid group (risk difference 0·02 $79\%$ CI $\mathbf{-0\cdot02}$ to 0·06]). By 7 days, eight $(8\%)$ participants in the placebo group and eight $(8\%)$ in the tranexamic acid group had died (aOR 1·08 $95\%$ CI 0·35 to 3·35]) and by 90 days, 15 $(15\%)$ participants in the placebo group and 19 $(18\%)$ in the tranexamic acid group had died (aOR 1·61 $[95\%$ CI 0·65 to 3·98]).  

Interpretation Intravenous tranexamic acid did not reduce haematoma growth when administered within $^\textrm{\scriptsize2h}$ of intracerebral haemorrhage symptom onset. There were no observed effects on other imaging endpoints, functional outcome, or safety. Based on our results, tranexamic acid should not be used routinely in primary intracerebral haemorrhage, although results of ongoing phase 3 trials will add further context to these findings.

# Research in context  

Optimal participant and outcome selection for antifibrinolytic trials in intracerebral haemorrhage remains challenging. The evidence to date does not support the use of tranexamic acid in intracerebral haemorrhage. Results of larger ongoing phase 3 trials and future individual patient meta-analyses will be required for definitive recommendations.  



# Introduction  

Approximately 3·4 million incident cases of intracerebral haemorrhage occur globally per year.1 Despite being a less common form of stroke, the annual number of deaths related to intracerebral haemorrhage (2·89 million) is similar to the number related to ischaemic stroke, and the condition causes a higher burden of disability than does ischaemic stroke.1 However, evidence-based therapy for intracerebral haemorrhage remains extremely limited.  

A key therapeutic target of putative treatments is haematoma growth, which is independently associated with both functional disability and mortality after intracerebral haemorrhage.2 Risk factors for haematoma growth include clinical factors, such as antithrombotic therapy, and imaging factors, such as the presence of the CT angiography spot sign.3  

Clinical trials of tranexamic acid, an antifibrinolytic agent, have suggested that it might inhibit haematoma growth after intracerebral haemorrhage, although the effects on functional outcomes have been unclear to date.4–6 Importantly, these trials also showed that tranexamic acid is safe in intracerebral haemorrhage (with no evidence of increased thromboembolic events). Given the low cost and ready accessibility of the drug, if proven to be effective, it could be a treatment that would be widely generalisable and accessible on a global scale.  

A key clinical factor associated with haematoma growth is early presentation.3 In our phase 2 STOP-AUST trial,5 in which patients with a CT angiography spot sign were treated within $4{\cdot}5{\mathrm{~h~}}$ of symptom onset, we found a nonsignificantly greater attenuation of haematoma growth when treatment with tranexamic acid was initiated early, especially among the small number of participants treated within $2\textrm{h}$ of stroke symptom onset. Delivering medical interventions routinely to patients with intra­ cerebral haemorrhage within $2\mathrm{~h~}$ of symptom onset requires optimisation of acute hospital and pre-hospital services, which has become feasible with the development of mobile stroke units.7  

We aimed to determine whether treatment with intravenous tranexamic acid within $2\mathrm{~h~}$ of symptom onset of spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage would reduce haematoma growth compared with placebo.  



# Methods  



# Study design and participants  

The Stopping Intracerebral Haemorrhage with Tranexamic Acid for Hyperacute onset Presentation including Mobile Stroke Units (STOP-MSU) trial was an investigator-led, parallel group, double-blind, random­ ised, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial conducted at 25 sites in Australia, Finland, New Zealand, Taiwan, and Viet Nam, including 24 hospital sites and one mobile stroke unit, based in Melbourne, VIC, Australia.  

Individuals were recruited if they presented with acute spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage that was confirmed on non-contrast CT, were aged 18 years or older, and were able to be treated with the investigational product within $2\textrm{h}$ of stroke onset. Key exclusion criteria were a baseline Glasgow Coma Scale score of less than 8; brainstem haemorrhage; intracerebral haematoma volume of more than $70~\mathrm{mL}$ as measured by the ABC/2 method;8 use of heparin, low-molecular weight heparin, GPIIb/IIIa antagonist, or oral anticoagulation (eg, warfarin/vitamin K antagonists, factor Xa inhibitor, thrombin inhibitor) within the previous $72\mathrm{~h~}$ ; and bleeding known or suspected to be secondary to trauma, aneurysm, vascular malformation, or other secondary causes. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is in the study protocol, which has been published previously9 and is in the appendix (pp 20–62).  

Depending on local legal and regulatory provisions at each site, consent could be obtained in either written or oral form from the patient or a person with decisionmaking responsibility. In some jurisdictions, recruitment into the trial under emergency treatment provisions was approved. In such cases, the patient or person responsible were subsequently approached to provide consent to continue follow-up in the study. Where early rapid clinical deterioration occurred leading to death or initiation of palliative care, the Human Research Ethics Committee granted a waiver for the requirement of consent to continue follow-up to avoid distress to the person responsible and avoid the risk of introducing systematic bias by exclusion of participants with poor clinical outcomes. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Royal Melbourne Hospital. The study was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03385928.  



# Randomisation and masking  

Using a concealed pre-randomised assignment pro­ cedure to minimise potential communication-related delays (either blinded treatment packs or envelopes, depending on the site), participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either intravenous tranexamic acid or placebo, stratified by treating centre and using randomly permuted blocks (block size of 4). The treatment allocation table was prepared by the study statistical team and was not available to the investigators. At most centres, the investigational product was distributed in pre-randomised externally indistinguishable sealed treatment packs containing either tranexamic acid or $0\cdot9\%$ saline in non-identical vessels. Following enrolment, the lowest numbered sealed treatment pack was handed to an external person (ie, not involved in study-related evaluations, such as a paramedic or emergency department nurse) who was unmasked to treatment assignment, who would prepare and administer the study drug while maintaining masking of the participant, treating team, and study team to treatment allocation. For some sites where packaged kits were not acceptable due to local workflows, randomisation envelopes were provided. At these sites, after enrolment, the lowest numbered randomisation envelope was opened by an external unmasked person (as with sites using kits). The unmasked person would then obtain the appropriate ampoules of tranexamic acid or saline from hospital stock and administer these while maintaining masking of the participant, treating team, and study team.  



# Procedures  

Following enrolment and selection of the appropriate prerandomised investigational product, participants were treated with either intravenous tranexamic acid ${1\mathrm{~g~}}$ over $10\mathrm{min}$ followed by ${1\mathrm{g}}$ over $^{8\mathrm{h}}$ (tranexamic acid group) or intravenous normal saline over $10\mathrm{min}$ followed by normal saline over $^{8\textrm{h}}$ (placebo group). The dose of tranexamic acid was chosen in line with previous studies in intracerebral haemorrhage.4–6 The volume of diluent (normal saline) was not mandated to allow study sites to use their existing clinical workflows. The final preparations of active study drug and placebo were indistinguishable.  

Haematoma volume was measured using non-contrast CT and a validated semi-automated planimetric method10 at a central imaging laboratory (Christchurch, New Zealand), at baseline (ie, at presentation) and follow-up (at $24\textrm{h}$ after baseline, within a target range of $18{-}30\mathrm{~h~}$ after the baseline scan). Investigations including electrocardiography (baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ ) and standard baseline laboratory blood investigations were performed and collected as part of routine clinical care. Blood pressure management was according to the discretion of the treating physician and based on local guidelines. Sex data were collected by self-report. Ethnicity and race data were not collected.  

Investigators and designated study personnel were responsible for monitoring participants for adverse events occurring up to 90 days after symptom onset, as detailed in the protocol (appendix pp 20–62). The investigators also determined whether deaths occurring during the study were due to study treatment. The final centralised telephone assessment at 90 days included an assessment of functional outcome with the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), as well as adverse events and survival. This assessment was conducted by a clinical assessor who was masked to the treatment group assignment. Pre-stroke mRS was evaluated by study investigators and designated study personnel at the baseline assessment.

The primary efficacy outcome of the study was the presence or absence of intracerebral haematoma growth by $24\mathrm{~h~}$ (target range 18 to $30\mathrm{~h~}$ ) as defined by either at least $33\%$ relative growth or at least $6\mathrm{mL}$ absolute growth from baseline on CT.12 In line with the primary study estimand, any participant-related intercurrent event that prevented follow-up CT scanning resulted in the outcome being imputed as growth present, as per the composite strategy prespecified in the statistical analysis plan. This typically related to participant clinical deterioration and a decision being made for palliative care before collection of the outcome measure. For participants who required surgical haematoma evacuation due to haematoma expansion after randomisation, preoperative CT scanning was used to measure follow-up haematoma volume. Surgical haematoma evacuation was considered a participant-related intercurrent event within the primary

study estimand definition, and hence was counted towards the primary outcome as a growth in haematoma. In situations where an intercurrent event (patient-related or system-related) led to early collection of the primary outcome (ie, $^{<18\mathrm{h}}$ from baseline), growth was imputed if it was observed as occurring despite the early measurement. In cases where an intercurrent event (patient-related or system-related) led to delayed collection of the primary outcome (ie, ${>}30\mathrm{~h~}$ but $<36$ h from baseline), absence of growth was imputed if the growth was not observed on the delayed assessment. Any systemrelated intercurrent events that prevented collection of the primary outcome measurement according to these rules was handled using a treatment policy strategy in line with the intention-to-treat principle.  

As the primary estimand analysis was conducted under an assumption of missingness at random, the sensitivity of the results to plausible departures from this assumption was explored.13 According to this approach, the data and missingness were modelled jointly using a pattern-mixture model, modelling the differences between missing and observed data. Assumptions about the missing data were expressed via the parameter δ, which measures the degree of departure from missingness at random. Results were investigated over a range of values of δ.  

Secondary efficacy outcomes were haematoma growth assessed by $24\mathrm{~h~}$ (target 18–30 h) after baseline using the following definitions: at least $33\%$ relative growth or at least $6~\mathrm{mL}$ absolute growth in intracerebral haematoma volume or any increase in intraventricular volume, using the same strategies for intercurrent events as the primary estimand);14 absolute intracerebral haematoma growth; relative intracerebral haematoma growth; absolute intraventricular haematoma growth; and absolute intracerebral plus intraventricular haematoma growth. Intercurrent events affecting the absolute and relative haematoma growth estimands were handled utilising a principal stratum strategy, whereby participant-related intercurrent events that prevented the collection of the follow-up CT scan led to those participants being excluded from this analysis. Furthermore, any intercurrent events that led to the follow-up scan being performed less than $18\mathrm{~h~}$ after baseline or more than $30\mathrm{~h~}$ after baseline (up to a maximum of $36\mathrm{~h~}$ ) were included in the analysis, with a subsequent sensitivity analysis conducted on the population where imaging was performed strictly within $18{-}30\mathrm{h}$ after baseline.

Secondary safety outcomes were deaths within 7 days and 90 days and the occurrence of major thromboembolic events within 90 days (defined as ischaemic stroke, myocardial infarction, or pulmonary embolism). # Statistical analysis  

The statistical analysis plan was finalised before database lock and unblinding, and it is available in the appendix $\mathrm{(pp~~63-81)}$ . Originally, we estimated a sample size of 124 participants (62 per treatment group) to provide $80\%$ power to detect haematoma growth of at least $33\%$ or at least $6~\mathrm{mL}$ in $39\%$ of the placebo group compared with $14\%$ of the tranexamic acid group, with a two-tailed significance level of 0·05. Following the recruitment of 49 participants, and based on the faster than anticipated recruitment, the trial executive committee made a fully blinded decision to increase the sample size to 180 participants (90 participants per group) to detect an absolute difference in the primary outcome of $20\%$ ( $39\%$ in the placebo group vs $19\%$ in the tranexamic acid group) under identical power and two-tailed significance level settings. The anticipated proportion of participants with haematoma growth in the placebo group was estimated on the basis of the study by Al-Shahi Salman and colleagues.3 The effect size was consistent with the effect estimates obtained in our original STOP-AUST trial.5 Adaptive sample size re-estimation, using the meth­ odology of Mehta and Pocock,15 was planned on the basis of the outcomes of 144 participants with the possibility to increase the sample size to a prespecified maximum of 326 participants. This was performed as planned, and no increase to the original sample size of 180 participants was recommended. Based on the observed rate of potentially non-evaluable primary endpoint assessments, the trial executive committee set a final target sample size of 200 to allow 180 evaluable primary endpoints, and the trial was concluded following the recruitment of 202 participants. For full details about the power analysis and sample size calculation see the appendix (pp 45, 68).  

The primary efficacy outcome, secondary functional outcomes, and secondary safety outcomes were assessed in all participants under the intention-to-treat principle (ie, all participants randomly assigned to treatment who did not withdraw consent). Participants with missing scans were considered to be missing at random and so not assessable for these endpoints). For the secondary haematoma growth outcomes, all participants in the ITT population without intercurrent events preventing measurement of follow-up haematoma volume, as specified within the intercurrent event handling strategies, were included. In the per-protocol sensitivity analyses, the per-protocol population included all participants who met inclusion criteria, received at least $50\%$ of study drug, and had no protocol violations. Additionally, a further sensitivity analysis was performed for the absolute and relative haematoma growth outcomes in participants who underwent follow-up scanning strictly within $18{-}30\mathrm{h}$ .  

The primary analysis was a comparison of the proportion of participants with haematoma growth (defined as either $23\%$ relative growth or $\ge6\mathrm{mL}$ absolute growth from baseline) between the tranexamic acid group and the placebo group using binary logistic regression, adjusted for baseline haematoma volume. As recommended by the US Food and Drug Administration’s guidance on covariate adjustment for randomised controlled trials, we report the effect as an adjusted odds ratio (aOR), with adjustment for baseline haematoma volume, and standardised unconditional risk difference, with corresponding $95\%$ CIs.16 The difference between groups in haematoma growth using the alternative definition of at least $33\%$ relative growth, at least $6\mathrm{mL}$ absolute growth, or any increase in intraventricular haematoma volume was also tested using a similar model. We estimated the differences in absolute and relative haematoma growth between groups using quantile regression, with the primary effect reported as a difference in medians with corresponding $95\%$ CIs, adjusted for baseline haematoma volume. We also report the effect at the 25th and 75th percentiles of growth with corresponding $95\%$ CI.  

We did subgroup analyses for the primary outcome on the following prespecified subgroups: time from symptom onset to treatment ${\left\lvert{\le1}\right\rvert}$ h vs ${>}1\mathrm{~k~}$ ), baseline intracerebral haematoma volume $\mathrm{\K}{<}30\mathrm{mL}$ $\nus\ge30\mathrm{mL}$ ), anatomical location (deep vs lobar vs cerebellar), baseline intraventricular haemorrhage (present vs absent), Glasgow Coma Scale score $(>12\nus8{-}12^{\cdot}$ ), age ${<}70$ years vs ${\geq}70$ years), sex (male vs female), and site (mobile stroke unit vs hospital). Additionally, given the finding of a baseline systolic blood pressure by treatment interaction in the TICH-2 study,4 we included a similar post-hoc subgroup analysis (baseline systolic blood pressure; $\leq170\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}\nus>170\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg})$ .  

We tested dichotomous functional outcomes on the mRS using binary logistic regression, adjusted for age and baseline haematoma volume. For ordinal comparison of 90-day mRS across the range of values (mRS 5–6 comb­ ined) we used assumption-free Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney generalised ORs (adjusted for age and baseline haematoma volume) because the proportional odds assumptions were not satisfied.17 We tested the mean difference between groups in utility-weighted mRS using a linear regression model, adjusted for age and baseline haematoma volume. We used utility weights for indivi­ dual mRS categories per the recommendations of Chaisinanunkul and colleagues.18

We compared the incidence of major thromboembolic events between groups using unadjusted binary logistic regression. We tested mortality outcomes using binary logistic regression, adjusted for age and baseline haematoma volume. Outcomes were reported as standardised risk differences, with corresponding $95\%$ CIs. Deaths within 7 and 90 days were also reported as aOR $(95\%$ CI). We did a post-hoc time-to-death analysis using Cox hazard regression to estimate the mortality hazard ratio (HR) and corresponding $95\%$ CI, with adjustment for the same covariates as the mortality logistic regression analyses.  

As a sensitivity analysis, we did a per-protocol analysis for all estimands. We did a second sensitivity analysis of the absolute and relative haematoma growth estimands including all participants who underwent follow-up brain imaging strictly within $18{-}30\mathrm{h}$ of the baseline scan.  

Differences in all endpoints were tested independently at the two-tailed 0·05 level of significance. We did no formal adjustment for multiplicity of testing for the secondary endpoint analyses because their purpose was to supplement evidence from the primary analysis to more fully characterise the treatment effect. Results from the secondary analyses should be interpreted within this context.  

Statistical analyses were done using Stata SE (version 18.0) and R (version 4.1.1).  

An independent data safety monitoring board (DSMB) considered two independent primary safety variables (mortality and major thromboembolic events) after the random assignment of the first 49 participants and subsequently following random assignment of 100 and 150 participants. The DSMB conducted three interim safety analyses in line with the study protocol and no recommendations to terminate or modify the course of the trial based on safety considerations were made.

# Discussion  

In this randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial, we found no difference in haematoma growth between participants treated with tranexamic acid versus placebo within $2\textrm{h}$ of intracerebral haemorrhage symptom onset.  

Additionally, no differences between the treatment groups were observed for a number of alternative imaging endpoints, including a definition of haematoma growth incorporating any intraventricular haematoma growth into the standard growth definition. No differences were observed in functional outcomes at 90 days.  

Haematoma growth, using the primary definition, occurred less frequently in the STOP-MSU trial, particularly in the placebo group (in 37 $[38\%]$ of 97 participants in the placebo group vs 43 $[43\%]$ of 101 in the tranexamic acid group), than in our previous phase 2 STOP-AUST trial,5 where the CT angiography spot sign was used for participant selection within $4\cdot5\mathrm{h}$ of symptom onset (26 $[52\%]$ of 50 in the placebo group vs 22 $[44\%]$ of 50 in the tranexamic acid group). Growth occurred more frequently than was seen in the larger TICH-2 trial,4 which did not use imaging markers of haematoma growth and recruited participants up to $^{8\mathrm{~h~}}$ after symptom onset (304 $[29\%]$ of 1058 in the placebo group vs 265 $[25\%]$ of 1054 in the tranexamic acid group); and was similar to what was observed in the TRAIGE trial6 (34 $[41\%]$ of 82 in the placebo group vs 36 $[40\%]$ of 89 in the tranexamic acid group), which used a combination of markers of haematoma growth up to $^{8\mathrm{h}}$ after symptom onset.  

Median absolute haematoma growth was $1\cdot2\mathrm{mL}$ in both groups, which is a small absolute increase. The small median absolute growth of haematoma in participants in the placebo group in our trial was an unexpected finding, particularly compared with the $3\cdot4~\mathrm{mL}$ (IQR $0\cdot0{-}16\cdot0)$ median growth observed in the placebo group in STOP-AUST.5 Other similar trials have predominantly reported absolute growth as mean (SD) rather than median (IQR), which restricts our ability to make direct comparisons. With this consideration in mind, the TICH-2 trial4 reported a mean haematoma growth of 4·90 mL (SD 16·0) at $24\mathrm{h}$ in the placebo group, which was slightly but significantly reduced by $1\cdot37~\mathrm{mL}$ in the group treated with tranexamic acid (mean growth of $3\cdot72\mathrm{mL}$ [SD 15·9]).4 The TRAIGE trial6 reported a mean haematoma growth of $7\cdot6\mathrm{mL}$ (SD 15·6) in the placebo group and $6\cdot6~\mathrm{mL}$ (SD 16·5) in the tranexamic acid group, with no significant difference observed. A smaller trial of tranexamic acid in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage on non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants $\mathrm{(TICH{-}NOAC)^{19}}$ found an incidence of haematoma growth of $45\%$ (14 of 31) in the placebo group and $38\%$ (12 of 32) in the tranexamic acid group, with median absolute growth of $1{\cdot}8~\mathrm{mL}$ (IQR 0·1–8·7) and $3\cdot3\mathrm{mL}$ $(0\cdot6{-}8\cdot8)$ , respectively. However, this trial was discontinued prematurely due to exhausted funding.19  

Our findings have important implications for the design and interpretation of future trials of tranexamic acid in intracerebral haemorrhage. A large phase 3 trial, to include 5500 participants, of tranexamic acid versus placebo, to be given within $4{\cdot}5{\mathrm{~h~}}$ of symptom onset, is being conducted by the TICH investigators,20 with a primary endpoint of mortality at 7 days. This endpoint was chosen on the basis of the reduced early mortality with tranexamic acid observed in TICH-2. However, notably, we did not observe any signal of a reduction in early mortality in our smaller phase 2 trial.  

The substantial proportion $62\%$ [60 of 97] in the placebo group, and $57\%$ [58 of 101] in the tranexamic acid group) of participants who did not have haematoma growth in our trial, and in similar trials, and the relatively small median absolute haematoma growth we observed $[1\cdot2\mathrm{mL}$ ; despite selecting patients using the known risk factor of early presentation), suggest that optimal patient selection for such trials remains challenging. Notably, the proportion of participants in both groups who had smaller intracerebral haematoma volumes at follow-up than at baseline might have contributed to the relatively small median growth overall. To account for the possibility that some of this reduction in haematoma volume might have been related to intraventricular extension of the haemorrhage, we also included an outcome measuring absolute growth in intracerebral plus intraventricular haematoma volume.  

In both STOP-AUST5 and STOP-MSU, we aimed to select patients at an increased risk of haematoma growth to test our underlying hypothesis. However, accumulating evidence suggests that any effect of tranexamic acid on haematoma growth might be very small in absolute terms.4–6 Therefore, on the one hand, individuals who have substantial haematoma growth might not benefit in a meaningful way from antifibrinolytic therapy. On the other hand, individuals who do not have haematoma growth (eg, approximately $50\%$ of participants in both STOP-AUST5 and STOP-MSU did not have growth by the standard growth definition) might dilute any potential observable effect of antifibrinolytic therapy. We attempted to investigate whether tranexamic acid might have had an effect at the more extreme levels of growth by analysing absolute growth at the 25th and 75th percentiles (as well as the median) but did not see any significant effects in these analyses. Whether other markers, including non-contrast CT markers of haematoma growth, would provide a more optimally enriched target group in addition to the early time window should be explored in future studies. However, prospective acquisition and interpretation of more complex imaging markers, such as the CT angiography spot sign, might not be feasible within the ultra-early time window (ie, $2\mathrm{{h}}$ of symptom onset).  

Additional factors might have contributed to our findings. Participants in STOP-MSU tended to have relatively small and deep haematomas, with a median baseline haematoma volume of $11{\cdot}1\mathrm{mL}$ (IQR 4·3 to 23·2) in the placebo group and $9\cdot5~\mathrm{mL}$ (5·8 to 17·0) in the tranexamic acid group. By comparison, in the STOP-AUST trial,5 the median baseline haematoma volume was $15\cdot6\mathrm{mL}$ (IQR 7·9 to 33·4) in the placebo group and $13\cdot8\mathrm{mL}$ $(7\cdot8$ to 32·0) in the tranexamic acid group. More than $80\%$ of participants had haematomas in deep locations, compared with approximately $70\%$ in STOP$\mathrm{AUST^{5}}$ and approximately $60\%$ in TICH-2.4 The high proportion of participants with deep haematomas in STOP-MSU might reflect earlier clinical presentation because of the proximity of the lesion to the subcortical motor tracts. Because only a few participants had haematomas in cortical locations, a benefit of tranexamic acid  on an analysis of this subgroup was inconclusive. A differential treatment response based on haematoma location is supported by a 2019 study suggesting differences in functional coagulation between lobar and deep intra­ cerebral haemorrhages.21 However, these findings require further replication and validation. An alternative explanation for the relatively small baseline haematoma volumes observed in our trial is that of earlier imaging in patients with haematomas that grew at a later timepoint. However, the small median absolute haematoma growth we observed at $24\mathrm{h}$ does not support this hypothesis.  

The optimal measure of haematoma growth and its implementation within clinical trials remains uncertain. For example, a recent multicentre retrospective analysis of over 2000 individuals with intracerebral haemorrhage demonstrated that the strongest association between haematoma growth and functional outcome was seen in those with more extreme haematoma growth $1\mathord{\left/{\vphantom{12}}\right.\kern-\nulldelimiterspace}>66\%$ relative growth or ${>}12\cdot5\mathrm{mL}$ absolute growth).22 Moreover, these associations were significant only in patients with baseline haematoma volumes smaller than $30~\mathrm{mL}$ , suggesting that baseline haematoma volume above this threshold conveys a negative prognosis, even in the absence of further haematoma growth.22 Such patients might be more likely to benefit from surgical approaches, including minimally invasive haematoma evacuation, rather than pharmacological therapies.  

Reassuringly, we found that intravenous tranexamic acid was safe in our participants, with no observed difference in mortality or major thromboembolic events between groups. This finding is consistent with previous trials in intracerebral haemorrhage4–6 and trauma.23,24  

In addition to tranexamic acid, there is substantial ongoing interest in the haemostatic agent recombinant factor VIIa. In contrast to tranexamic acid, which inhibits fibrinolysis by blocking the lysine binding sites on plasminogen, recombinant factor VIIa enhances thrombin generation on the surface of activated platelets and promotes coagulation.25,26 Two previous trials of recombinant factor VIIa in intracerebral haemorrhage showed significantly reduced hematoma growth, but no consistent clinical benefit.27,28 Further analyses suggested that patients treated within $2\cdot5\mathrm{{h}}$ of symptom onset, with baseline haematoma volumes smaller than $60\mathrm{mL}$ and intraventricular haematoma volumes smaller than $5\mathrm{mL}$ , might be an optimal target population.29 Based on these data, the ongoing phase 3 FASTEST trial will test recombinant factor VIIa in individuals presenting within $2\textrm{h}$ of symptom onset, including individuals managed by mobile stroke units.30 Notably, the high cost of recombinant factor VIIa might limit its implementation in some healthcare settings. Additionally, the  previous phase 3 trial showed an increase in arterial thromboembolic events in participants treated with recombinant factor VIIa $80\upmu\mathrm{g/kg}$ which is the dose being used in the FASTEST trial.28  

Our study has some limitations. First, the sample size of the study did not allow us to draw definitive conclusions on important secondary functional outcomes. This limitation is particularly relevant given the suggestion that tranexamic acid might potentially have effects on outcomes in intracerebral haemorrhage that are independent of haematoma growth attenuation, including effects on perihaematomal oedema or inflammation.31 The relatively small sample size also restricts the conclusions that can be derived from subgroup analyses. Resource and funding limitations restricted our ability to conduct a larger phase 3 study, but such studies are ongoing.20 Second, despite optimisation of clinical workflows, there was a median delay of approximately 26 min between baseline CT scan and initiation of treatment. Given the highly dynamic nature of haematoma growth within the ultra-early timeframe, some haematoma growth probably occurred during this period of delay. However, within the logistical confines of a randomised controlled trial, it is unlikely that this delay could be further minimised. Third, given the pragmatic nature of the trial, we did not prospectively record participant blood pressure readings in the acute phase beyond the baseline blood pressure. Given the emerging importance of early blood pressure reduction in the management of intracerebral haemorrhage, particularly its potential beneficial effect on haematoma growth,32 future studies should investigate whether an interaction exists between the effect of tranexamic acid and control of blood pressure in the ultra-early time window. Finally, given the pragmatic nature of the trial, the hyperacute nature of the condition being investigated, and resource limitations, a detailed screening log was not available.  

Our study also has important strengths. Despite the substantial challenges imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic, we completed this pragmatic phase 2 trial with minimal missing data. We prospectively recruited over 200 participants with ultra-early intracerebral haemorrhage and had a median onset to treatment time of approximately $105~\mathrm{min}$ and baseline imaging to treatment time of approximately $26~\mathrm{min}$ , which, to our knowledge, are the fastest reported times to date for such trials. This achievement was supported in part by the mobile stroke unit paradigm, but we found that recruitment of patients with intracerebral haemorrhage within $2\textrm{h}$ of onset in emergency departments was also feasible.  

In summary, we found that intravenous tranexamic acid did not reduce haematoma growth when administered within $2\textrm{h}$ of intracerebral haemorrhage symptom onset. Results of ongoing larger trials using tranexamic acid and recombinant factor VIIa will be important to add further context to these findings and establish whether antifibrinolytic or haemostatic therapy has a future role in acute intracerebral haemorrhage.
