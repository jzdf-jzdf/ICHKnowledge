# Anemia From Inflammation After Intracerebral Hemorrhage and Relationships With Outcome

BACKGROUND: Baseline anemia is associated with poor intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) outcomes. However, underlying drivers for anemia and whether anemia development after ICH impacts clinical outcomes are unknown. We hypothesized that inflammation drives anemia development after ICH and assessed their relationship to outcomes.  

METHODS AND RESULTS: Patients with serial hemoglobin and iron biomarker concentrations from the HIDEF (High-Dose Deferoxamine in Intracerebral Hemorrhage) trial were analyzed. Adjusted linear mixed models assessed laboratory changes over time. Of 42 patients, significant decrements in hemoglobin occurred with anemia increasing from $19\%$ to $45\%$ by day 5. Anemia of inflammation iron biomarker criteria was met in $88\%$ . A separate cohort of 521 patients with ICH with more granular serial hemoglobin and long-term neurological outcome data was also investigated. Separate regression models assessed whether (1) systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) scores related to hemoglobin changes over time and (2) hemoglobin changes related to poor 90-day outcome. In this cohort, anemia prevalence increased from $30\%$ to $71\%$ within 2 days of admission yet persisted beyond this time. Elevated systemic inflammatory response syndrome was associated with greater hemoglobin decrements over time (adjusted parameter estimate: $-0.27$ $[95\%$ CI, $-0.37$ to −0.17]) and greater hemoglobin decrements were associated with poor outcomes (adjusted odds ratio per $19/\mathrm{dL}$ increase, 0.76 $[95\%$ CI, 0.62–0.93]) independent to inflammation and ICH severity.  

CONCLUSIONS: We identified novel findings that acute anemia development after ICH is common, rapid, and related to inflammation. Because anemia development is associated with poor outcomes, further work is required to clarify if anemia, or its underlying drivers, are modifiable treatment targets that can improve ICH outcomes.

# RESEARCH PERSPECTIVE  

# What Is New?  

This study provides novel insights into how anemia development is related to inflammation and is common and rapid in the initial days after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). Anemia development following ICH relates to poor long-term ICH outcomes.  

# What Question Should Be Addressed Next?  

Further work is needed to assess whether anemia during an ICH hospitalization is a modifiable treatment target that can improve ICH outcomes.

As iron decreases, ferritin levels increase as an acute phase reactant of inflammation and as iron retaining macrophages secrete ferritin.9–11 This leads to impaired erythropoiesis coupled with shortened red blood cell (RBC) half-life,12 resulting in hemoglobin decrements and anemia over time. Yet it is unknown whether similar serial iron or hemoglobin changes occur in ICH, a critical illness known to be inflammatory in nature.13,14 Because ICH carries the highest morbidity and death of all stroke subtypes,15,16 yet has a paucity of therapies that can improve long-term outcomes, a better characterization of the processes driving anemia after ICH, the kinetics of its development, and its relationship to long-term ICH outcomes are critical steps needed to assess whether anemia (or its underlying drivers) are modifiable treatment targets in ICH. Subsequently, we sought to characterize temporal hemoglobin changes after ICH and to investigate whether iron biomarkers following ICH would reflect patterns consistent with anemia from inflammation. We also assessed relationships of these acute laboratory changes with longterm ICH outcomes.  

# METHODS  

The data supporting the findings of this study are available upon reasonable request.  

Patients with ICH enrolled in the double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled HI-DEF (High-Dose Deferoxamine in Intracerebral Hemorrhage) phase 2 trial were evaluated. The Hl-DEF trial enrolled patients with supratentorial spontaneous ICH from centers in the United States and Canada between 2013 and 2018 to receive iron chelator, deferoxamine (62 kg/kg/day for 5 days), or placebo within 24 hours of ICH onset.17 The purpose of the trial was to determine whether treatment with deferoxamine was of sufficient promise to improve ICH outcomes, given the known neurotoxicity of cerebral iron from hematoma breakdown products, as well as obtain biomarker data for these patients. This study was stopped after enrollment of 42 patients to modify deferoxamine dosing and to create trial modifications, which supported the subsequent iDEF (Intermediate Dose Deferoxamine in Intracerebral Hemorrhage) trial.18  

An external, single-center observational ICH cohort study (ICHOP [Intracerebral Hemorrhage Outcomes Project) was separately investigated. Patients with spontaneous ICH consecutively admitted to a large, comprehensive stroke referral center between 2009 and 2018 were enrolled in ICHOP. Study coordinators and physicians prospectively collected and adjudicated data elements including baseline medical and radiographic ICH characteristics, laboratory results, vital sign data, and outcomes.1 ICHOP patients with available serial hemoglobin data were assessed (Figure  1). Patients with secondary etiologies of ICH, ischemic stroke with hemorrhagic transformation, or traumatic ICH were excluded from both HI-DEF and ICHOP cohorts.  

# Laboratory Assessments  

In Hl-DEF, laboratory assessments of hemoglobin and serum iron concentrations (iron, transferrin, ferritin) were performed per study protocol at 2 time -points: (1) preinfusion baseline and (2) day 5 after infusion completion. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin concentrations $<129/\mathsf{dL}$ for women and $<139/\mathsf{dL}$ for men per World Health Organization definitions. Anemia from inflammation was assessed at these time points using the following standard serum iron biomarker diagnostic criteria: low iron $(<60\upmu\mathrm{g/dL})$ , low transferrin saturation $(<20\%)$ , and increased ferritin concentrations $(>100\mu\mathrm{g/L})$ .11 In ICHOP, daily complete blood counts with hemoglobin concentrations were obtained per clinical care. The following prespecified complete blood count time points were assessed: baseline, day 2, day 4, day 7, and discharge (if after day 7) to assess more granular kinetics on how hemoglobin changes over time. Given the absence of iron laboratory biomarker data from ICHOP to directly assess criteria for anemia from inflammation, baseline inflammation was assessed using admission systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) scores (body temperature ${<}36$ or ${>}38~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , heart rate ${\tt>}90$ beats per minute, respiratory rate ${>}20$ breaths per minute, white blood cell count ${<}4000$ or ${>}12000/\mathrm{mm}^{3}$ ).19,20

# Standard Protocol Approvals and Patient Consent  

The HI-DEF and ICHOP studies were separately approved by the Institutional Review Board at each enrolling site. Informed consent was obtained from all patients or family when appropriate.17 The Hl-DEF trial was registered via ID number at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01662895).  

# Statistical Analysis  

Intergroup differences between HI-DEF and ICHOP cohorts were assessed using Mann–Whitney $U$ or $t$ tests for continuous variables and the $\chi^{2}$ or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Temporal changes of serum hemoglobin, iron, transferrin, and ferritin (between baseline and day 5) across the overall Hl-DEF cohort were assessed using generalized linear mixed models after adjusting for baseline laboratory concentration, age, sex, race, and ICH severity (ICH volume and intraventricular hemorrhage presence). Sensitivity analyses were performed adjusting for renal function as well as excluding patients with pneumonia complications. These adjusted models were extended to assess a potential differential effect of treatment (deferoxamine versus placebo) as well as poor 90-day outcome on the change in referenced laboratory values.

# RESULTS  

Baseline characteristics of the Hl-DEF $(\mathsf{n}{=}42)$ and ICHOP cohorts $(\mathsf{n}{=}521$ ) are seen in Table 1. It was notable that the ICHOP patient cohort was more racially diverse than the Hl-DEF cohort. Expectedly, patients in ICHOP had more brainstem and infratentorial hemorrhages given Hl-DEF exclusion of these patients. However, other demographic variables, baseline medical history, and ICH characteristics (volume and intraventricular hemorrhage) were comparable between cohorts. Average baseline hemoglobin concentrations were 13.9 and $13.29/\mathsf{dL}$ , with baseline anemia being seen in $19\%$ and $30\%$ in HI-DEF verSus ICHOP cOhorts, respectively.  

In the Hl-DEF cohort, we noted low baseline concentrations of iron $(59~\upmu9/\mathrm{dL}$ [interquartile range, 40–86]), transferrin $(245~\mathrm{ng/mL}$ [interquartile range, 219–260]), transferrin saturation $(18.1\%$ [interquartile range, $13.4\mathrm{-}$ 28.5]), and elevated baseline concentrations of ferritin $(132\upmu\mathrm{g/L}$ [interquartile range, 56–235]). Among the $19\%$ of Hl-DEF patients anemic at baseline, $88\%$ met iron biomarker criteria consistent with an anemia from inflammation. When assessing specific laboratory concentration changes in this cohort, we identified significant, concurrent decrements of hemoglobin, iron, and transferrin serum concentrations between baseline and day 5 after ICH. Conversely, we identified significant increments of ferritin over that same period (Table  2). These relationships were not altered when adjusting for renal function or excluding those with pneumonia complications (Table  S1). The prevalence of anemia in this cohort increased from $19\%$ to $45\%$ at 5-day follow-up, with $82\%$ of patients with anemia at follow-up having iron biomarkers consistent with anemia of inflammation. No significant differences in anemia development was seen at 5-day follow-up between patients receiving deferoxamine versus placebo ( $40\%$ versus $50\%$ ; $P{=}0.75$ ). And there were no interactions of deferoxamine on iron laboratory changes compared with placebo (adjusted time parameter estimate for deferoxamine, $-19.81$ $\left[-35.03\right.$ to $-4.59]$ versus placebo, $-16.45$ $\left[-31.68\right]$ to $-1.21]$ ; interaction $P{=}0.75$ ).

In our larger external ICHOP cohort, we identified 521 patients with available serial hemoglobin data. Patients excluded due to missing serial hemoglobin measurements were primarily sicker patients who encountered early death and had greater baseline ICH severity, greater baseline SIRS score, and shorter hospital lengths of stay (data not shown). In our inclusion cohort, baseline anemia was seen in $30\%$ . This anemia prevalence increased to $71\%$ during the hospitalization. Using a linear model with random effects, we modeled the association between hemoglobin change and time. In our adjusted analyses, we identified that hemoglobin concentrations significantly decreased over time (adjusted time parameter estimate: $-0.03\left[-0.04\right.$ to $-0.02]$ ; $P_{<0.0001}\$ ), and these changes occurred primarily in the initial 2 days following hospitalization (Figure  2). However, continued decrements, albeit more gradual ones, were identified at later time points. To assess whether baseline inflammation had a potential role in these hemoglobin decrements over time, we assessed relationships of baseline SIRS score with subsequent hemoglobin decrements (as the outcome variable). In our linear regression models, we identified that higher baseline SIRS scores were associated with larger hemoglobin decrements over time (adjusted time parameter estimate: $-0.27$ $\left[-0.37\right]$ to −0.17]; $P{<}0.0001^{\cdot}$ ). Finally, we confirmed our HI-DEF findings that greater hemoglobin decrements are associated with poor 90-day outcomes (adjusted odds ratio per 1g/dL increase, 0.76 [0.62–0.93]) independent to ICH severity and baseline SIRS score (Figure 2). Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II, hospital length of stay, RBC transfusions, and neurosurgical procedures did not impact any of our models.

# DISCUSSION  

Anemia is recognized to play a role in poor long-term neurological outcomes across different acute brain injuries22–26 including ICH.1–4,27 ICH is a unique critical illness as it is a life-threatening hemorrhage despite its volume of hemorrhage being small (often less than a teaspoon amount of volume) and localized intracranially. Thus, measurements of low hemoglobin concentration in these patients are not directly impacted by ICH blood loss itself or systemic extracranial bleeding complications,4 yet appear to consistently associate with worsened outcomes. This is largely posited to be due to impaired cerebral oxygen delivery in patients with anemia and ICH with prior work showing that lower static, single hemoglobin measurements, both at baseline1–4 and at nadir concentrations,4 relate with poor outcomes. And though it is known that hemoglobin concentrations do not remain static during critical illness,28,29 it has been unclear how hemoglobin changes over time in ICH and what the underlying drivers for this anemia may be in this patient population. Through data from 2 separate ICH studies, our work provides a novel characterization of anemia after ICH in attempts to address this knowledge gap. Specifically, we identified that hemoglobin concentrations decrease rapidly in the initial days after ICH leading to the majority $(71\%)$ of patients ultimately meeting criteria for anemia. Furthermore, we identified that these acute decremental changes of hemoglobin in turn relate to poor long-term neurological outcomes. Finally, using serial iron biomarker data and assessments of inflammation, concurrent decrements of hemoglobin, iron, transferrin, and converse increments of ferritin over time suggest that inflammation is a relevant driver of anemia development in these patients.

This is thought to promote evolutionarily developed efforts to decrease circulating iron, which normally facilitates bacterial proliferation and etiology.30,31 This serum iron decrement is accompanied by decrements in transferrin, the primary circulating binding protein of iron. Meanwhile, serum ferritin, representing secreted iron from iron-filled macrophages and an inflammatory acute-phase reactant, conversely increases. Additionally, RBC half-life is shortened,i2 and erythropoiesis response to anemia is also suppressed.32 This type of multimodal impairment of RBC survival and erythropoiesis is known to be a major driver of anemia development and maintenance in critical illness.6,1 Ourstudy recapitulates these wel-known iron biomarker characteristics seen with inflammation in a novel patient population with ICH. However, it is worth mentioning that while certain studies have suggested that anemia of inflammation can manifest acutely,7,8 the inflammatory impairment of erythropoiesis has been thought to be a more prolonged and chronic process.10 Thus, we cannot exclude that the kinetics of the hemoglobin decrements identified in our ICH cohorts over the initial days were in part due to other superimposed unmeasured processes seen in critical illness like hemolysis, phlebotomy, hemodilution, fluid resuscitation, or other sources of blood loss or clearance. Yet it is worth mentioning that we adjusted for ICH severity, systemic medical disease severity, length of stay, and neurosurgical procedures, all known surrogate markers of diagnostic and interventional “burden” over an ICH hospitalization.21 Because our hemoglobin decrements occurred in the initial 2 days after ICH, it is less likely that phlebotomy practice on admission would contribute to the extent of hemoglobin decrement seen over this time. Furthermore, our iron biomarker data identified that the majority of patients with anemia $(88\%)$ at baseline presentation met iron biomarker criteria for an anemia of inflammation, suggesting that these processes were present before any hospital-­ related complications or procedures. At the very least, our data suggest that inflammatory processes present at baseline may be driving the anemia “maintenance” seen at later time points during the ICH hospitalization. Whether it is the primary driver for the acute hemoglobin decrements seen in the initial 2 days after ICH requires clarification. More provocatively, if the anemia seen at baseline in patients with ICH truly reflects a slower, chronic onset of anemia of inflammation, future work will need to focus on whether inflammatory processes and their subsequent anemia development precedes ICH onset itself and can be a trackable biomarker for ICH risk following inflammatory processes such as infection or even cause it, as prior work has suggested a potential environmental causal role of infection on incident stroke risk.33–37  

Our findings were clinically significant as we identified that hemoglobin decrements over time related to poor long-term neurological outcomes across both our ICH cohorts. These relationships were independent to both ICH severity and baseline inflammation. Although ICH and its severity is tied to the activation of inflammation, prior multicenter observations have shown that baseline inflammation relates to downstream medical complications, but does not independently predict poor ICH outcomes.20 Thus, it is possible that our findings similarly reflect the impacts that baseline inflammation has on additional medical complications like anemia development, which in turn impacts ICH outcomes. However, separate work has identified relevant associations of specific inflammatory biomarkers, ferritin and hepcidin, on poor ICH outcomes.38–40 While we did not identify any clear relationship of ferritin on poor ICH outcomes in our study, it was notable that there were potential trends in the associations of low transferrin concentrations and poor ICH outcomes from our Hl-DEF data. Thus, larger-scale work collectively assessing specific iron biomarkers, inflammation, and anemia with ICH outcomes are needed to clarify the direct drivers of the poor outcomes seen in these studies.  

Similarly, it remains to be determined whether relationships of anemia itself with poor ICH outcomes are causal. While we adjusted for ICH severity and systemic medical disease severity, and performed analyses excluding certain medical complications like pneumonia, it is still possible that greater hemoglobin decrements are associated with other hospital complications or specific unmeasured factors related to or independent of ICH that would generate inflammation and also be related to poor ICH outcomes (ie., infection, sepsis, thrombosis).41–43 Further human observational work is required to clarify clinical risk factors for anemia development, as this may elucidate modifiable biological pathways or preventable complications (infections, thrombosis) that could prevent anemia development and potentially improve outcomes apart from anemia correction. Additionally, these studies need to be paired with preclinical translational reductionist models to disentangle overlapping inflammatory and anemic processes to clarify whether these directly cause poor ICH outcomes and whether its correction can conversely improve outcomes.  

While speculative in ICH, it is plausible that anemia can directly cause poor ICH outcomes. Because patients with acute brain injury are critically dependent on optimal cerebral oxygen delivery to prevent secondary brain injury,2,22,23 adequate hemoglobin concentrations may be critical to prevent such secondary injuries from occurring. This concept may be particularly important in ICH, even compared with other, similar acute brain injuries like subarachnoid hemorrhage or traumatic brain injury where liberal RBC transfusion trials are underway.44,45 In ICH, aggressive blood pressure correction is rapidly implemented and maintained for most of the ICH hospitalization as clinical standard of care for these patients.46,47 It could be envisioned that this is deleterious in certain scenarios, specifically in the context of anemia as cerebral oxygen delivery is dependent on blood flow and arterial oxygen concentration. Therefore, in settings where reflexive sympathetic surges of blood flow are prevented iatrogenically through strict blood pressure control, the brain becomes primarily dependent on the oxygencarrying capacity of blood (ie, hemoglobin) to deliver oxygen to the brain. Thus, processes leading to anemia could very well aggravate secondary brain injury, as we have separately identified that hemoglobin concentrations appear to relate with neuroimaging magnetic resonance imaging ischemic lesion presence (a radiographic biomarker of secondary brain injury).48  

Though our study strengths included the use of a multicenter trial with rigorous protocolized blood sampling time points and accurate outcome data paired with a larger observational, confirmatory data set attesting to the generalizability of the data, there are several limitations worth noting. First, as stated previously, our outcome data may have been subject to unmeasured confounding or selection bias. Patients from both cohorts studied needed to be able to survive beyond the initial days following ICH. Thus, it is unknown how our findings generalize to patients with the most severe ICH who die early. While we attempted to account for baseline disease severity across our analyses, there may be specific unmeasured factors that occur over a hospitalization course that may be related to inflammation, acute anemia development, and poor long-term outcomes (ie, procedural complications, fluid resuscitation, hemodilution, sepsis, infections) that will need to be explored in future work. Second, our Hl-DEF data set with available iron biomarker assessments was small and was confounded with deferoxamine therapy. Reassuringly, we determined that deferoxamine did not have a differential impact on iron concentration changes in our HI-DEF data set and identified generalizable findings across our HI-DEF and ICHOP cohorts. However, similar iron biomarker data were not available clinically from the ICHOP cohort. Thus, larger-scale, laboratory-based work paired with hospital course risk factors is required to identify etiologies of anemia, both at baseline and follow-up, as these may not be overlapping entities and will require different diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Additionally, we did not have MRI neuroimaging data or physiologic measurements of cerebral tissue oxygenation to assess how anemia or an anemia of inflammation relates to relevant radiologic or physiologic (oxygenation) outcomes of interest. Future observational work will be needed to clarify whether anemia and its relevant drivers can create detectable secondary brain injury burden that then can impact clinical outcomes. Finally, these studies will need to be paired with preclinical models to disentangle the complex overlap of disease severity, inflammation, anemia, and outcomes to establish whether anemia causes poor outcomes and whether it (or its underlying processes) can be modified to improve outcomes.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

Anemia is common and develops rapidly after ICH. Aggregate concentrations and changes in hemoglobin, iron, transferrin, and ferritin over time suggest novel findings that anemia at baseline and follow-up after ICH is related to inflammation. Given that patients with ICH with poor long-term outcomes had greater acute decrements in hemoglobin over time in our cohort, further work will be required to determine inflammation’s role in anemia and ICH outcomes, which may clarify potential therapeutic targets to consider for patients with ICH.
