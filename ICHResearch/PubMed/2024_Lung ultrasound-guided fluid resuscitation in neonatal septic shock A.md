# Lung ultrasound–guided fluid resuscitation in neonatal septic shock: A randomized controlled trial

# Abstract  

This randomized controlled trial aimed to determine whether lung ultrasound–guided fluid resuscitation improves the clinical outcomes of neonates with septic shock. Seventy-two patients were randomly assigned to undergo treatment with lung ultrasound-guided fluid resuscitation (LUGFR), or with usual fluid resuscitation (Control) in the first $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ since the start of the sepsis treatment. The primary study outcome was 14-day mortality after randomization. Fourteen-day mortalities in the two groups were not significantly different (LUGFR group, $13.89\%$ ; control group, $16.67\%$ ; $p{=}0.76$ ; hazard ratio 0.81 $[95\%$ $\operatorname{CI}0.27{-}2.50]$ ). The LUGFR group experienced shorter length of neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stays (21 vs. 26 days, $p{=}0.04)$ and hospital stays (32 vs. 39 days, $p{=}0.01\$ ), and less fluid was used in the first $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ (77 vs. $106\mathrm{mL/kg}$ , $p{=}0.02,$ ). Further, our study found that ultrasound–guided fluid resuscitation can significantly reduce the incidence of acute kidney injury ( $25\%$ vs. $47.2\%$ , $p{=}0.05,$ ) and intracranial hemorrhage (grades I–II) within $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ ( $13.9\%$ vs. $36.1\%$ , $p{=}0.03,$ ). However, no significant difference was found in the resolution of shock within $^{1\textrm{h}}$ or $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ , use of mechanical ventilation or vasopressor support, time to achieve lactate level $<2\mathrm{mmol/L}$ , and the number of participants developing hepatomegaly in the first $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ .  

Conclusion: Lung ultrasound is a noninvasive and convenient tool for predicting fluid overload in neonatal septic shock. Fluid resuscitation guided by lung ultrasound can shorten the length of hospital and NICU stays, reduce the amount of fluid used in the first $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ , and reduce the risk of acute kidney injury and intracranial hemorrhage.  

Trial registration: Registered in Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital: 2021-IIT-156-EK, date of registration November 13, 2021. And ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06144463 (retrospectively registered).  

What is Known:   
• Excessive fluid resuscitation in neonates with septic shock had worse outcomes.   
What is New:   
• Lung ultrasound should be routinely used to guide fluid resuscitation in neonatal septic shock.

# Introduction  

Neonatal sepsis is one of the important causes that endanger the lives of newborns, and the mortality rate caused by severe infection remains high at between 20 and $40\%$ [1]. Guidelines and recommendations on managing pediatric septic shock, which have been published and updated since 2002 [2, 3], suggest that the initial treatment for pediatric septic shock focus on early diagnosis, timely administration of antibiotics, and recovery of hemodynamic status using fluid resuscitation and vasopressor [3]. Rapid bolus fluid resuscitation is of fundamental importance to the management of neonatal septic shock [3]. However, aggressive fluid administration in neonates with septic shock has recently been questioned [4]. Accumulating pediatric studies suggest that excessive fluid resuscitation in patients with septic shock had worse outcomes in neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) [4]. Additionally, the study also showed that fluid overload (FO) prolonged NICU hospitalization and increased the difficulty of advanced life support and rates of mortality [4]. Detecting FO can be particularly challenging in young children, especially in lung fluid status [5]. However, it has been reported that the sensitivity and specificity of using chest X-ray to detect FO are poor [5]. Therefore, a convenient and reliable technology for assessing excessive fluid resuscitation is urgently needed in neonates with septic shock.  

Lung ultrasound has gained popularity in the medical care for patients in NICU [6–8]. It is a noninvasive, radiation-free, and affordable technique, making it a well-fitted and competent procedure for disease diagnosis among neonates in a critical care setting [9, 10]. There is an increasing demand for less-invasive techniques for monitoring, assessing, and treating neonatal patients, and ultrasound technology is an ideal option for hemodynamic management of neonatal septic shock [6]. In particular, lung ultrasound can be a valuable tool in the management of cardiopulmonary conditions in acutely ill neonates and children and can sensitively detect varying degrees of pulmonary edema and extravascular lung water (EVLW) [8, 11]. Importantly, the utility of B-lines on lung ultrasound as a sign of EVLW has been confirmed in previous studies [8, 11–13]. However, previous studies investigating the application of ultrasonography in shock have focused on adults; for newborns, research is quite limited [14]. Besides, its benefit in reducing the mortality of neonates with septic shock remains unclear. Given the background, we conducted a randomized clinical trial involving neonates with septic shock to examine whether lung ultrasound–guided fluid resuscitation (LUGFR) would improve clinical outcomes for neonates with septic shock.  

# Materials and methods  

# Study design and setting  

This randomized controlled trial was conducted in a university-affiliated tertiary hospital from January 2022 to August 2023. This trial has been registered in Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital, registration number 2021-IIT156-EK, date of registration November 13, 2021. The study protocol and informed consent process were approved by the institutional ethics committee of the hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of the participants.  

# Participants  

In this randomized controlled trial, neonates (postnatal age $<28$ days for term birth, corrected gestational age $<37$ weeks $+28$ days for preterm birth) diagnosed with septic shock who were admitted to the NICU of Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital between January 2022 and August 2023 were enrolled. Septic shock was diagnosed based on the recommendations of the American College of Critical Care Medicine Clinical Practice [3] (Supplementary 1). A convenience initial sample of 92 children was enrolled in this pilot study.  

# Randomization and blinding  

The 92 enrolled participants were randomly assigned to either the control group who received usual fluid resuscitation treatment for septic shock or the LUGFR group who underwent lung ultrasound–guided fluid resuscitation. The specific details of random grouping and blind method are presented in online supplementary material (Supplementary 1).  

# Intervention  

Subjects were randomly assigned to control group or LUGFR group. The process of fluid resuscitation in newborns with septic shock can be found in online supplementary materials (Supplementary 1). For the control group, patients received usual fluid resuscitation treatment for septic shock. For the LUGFR group, therapy was applied based on the same treatment guidelines, and initial lung ultrasonography was performed after enrollment as baseline assessment of lung. The lung ultrasound was conducted using the Doppler ultrasound diagnostic instrument (Philips CX50) at a probe frequency range of 8 to $12~\mathrm{MHz}$ [14]. Lung ultrasonography was conducted using the 10-region method that included upper anterior, lower anterior, lateral, and upper posterior and lower posterior walls on both sides of the lung, which was focused on the condition of A-lines, B-lines (including confluent B-line and compact B-lines), lung consolidation, and pleural effusion [15–17].  

Shortly after every fluid resuscitation, same rapid lung ultrasound was performed. If ultrasonography showed EVLW or pulmonary edema was further aggravated (such as reduction of A-lines, increase of B-lines and lung consolidation), limited fluid resuscitation was performed: adjust the use of vasoactive drugs, and if shock was unsolved, continue fluid resuscitation ( $10\mathrm{mL/kg}$ , isotonic saline), extend the resuscitation time from 15 to $30\mathrm{min}$ , and carefully monitor vital signs during resuscitation. If lung ultrasound showed sizable confluent B-lines, or even compact B-lines, treatment was adjusted in a timely manner and fluid resuscitation was terminated. Thus, for each subject in LUGFR group, we compared the latest lung ultrasound with baseline and former ones, to modify the fluid resuscitation. Other points for initial fluid resuscitation of LUGFR group were the same as usual fluid resuscitation, including limits of cumulative fluid used, bacterial cultures, and conventional treatments.  

The trials lasted for $^{6\mathrm{~h~}}$ since the start of the sepsis treatment. For both groups, further decisions regarding the administration of fluids, glucocorticoids, monitoring, use of mechanical ventilation, subsequent treatment after the trial finished, or other treatments to achieve therapeutic endpoints (Supplementary 1) for resolution of shock were made by the treating team according to the protocol, which was based on the ACCM guidelines and clinical assessment [3] (Supplementary Fig. 1).  

# Data collection and outcome measures  

Baseline demographic information and clinical characteristics of the eligible participants were collected, including sex, gestation, birth weight, postnatal age, perinatal factors, and etiology. Baseline vital signs include heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and capillary refill time, and laboratory parameters include oxygen saturation, lactate, $\mathrm{pH}$ , and ${\mathrm{PaO}}_{2}$ -to- $\mathrm{{FiO}}_{2}$ ratio. Vital signs were recorded hourly for $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ after randomization. Besides, data regarding cumulative fluid used, vasoactive support, mechanical ventilation, laboratory parameters, and other supportive therapies were collected. Adverse events up to $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ after randomization were also recorded, including arrhythmia, congestive respiratory failure, persistently high lactate levels, and other undefined events.  

The participants were followed for 14 days or until discharge or death occurred. The primary study outcome was all-cause mortality within 14 days. Secondary outcomes included length of stay in the hospital, receipt and duration of mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support, cumulative fluid used, time to achieve lactate level $<2\mathrm{mmol/L}$ , the development of hepatomegaly in the first $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ , and the incidence of intracranial hemorrhage (grades I–II) in $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ , and the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) and the need of diuretic use to correct overt FO. The definitions of hepatomegaly and AKI are presented in online supplementary material (Supplementary 1).  

# Data analysis  

# Sample size and statistical power  

Using a baseline mortality of $40\%$ in the control group and a one-sided $\upalpha$ value $<0.05$ indicating statistical significance, it was estimated that a sample size of 72 patients would yield the statistical power of $80\%$ to detect a $28\%$ absolute reduction in mortality in 14 days. An interim analysis was planned and performed after enrollment of $50\%$ of the estimated sample size was achieved using a two-sided symmetric O’BrienFleming design [18].  

# Statistical analysis  

For both the LUGFR and control groups, chi-squared tests with Yates correction or Fisher’s exact tests were used to explore the distribution differences between categorical variables, and the $t$ -test was applied to test the means of the continuous variables between groups. The time to death between groups was examined using Kaplan-Meier survival curve estimates and log-rank tests. Cox-proportional hazard regression models were constructed to evaluate the effects of treatments on 14-day all-cause mortality. Changes in physiological variables and acid–base parameters over the first $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ were compared using the Mann–Whitney test. All analyses were performed using R software (version 3.5.3). All statistical tests were two-sided, and $p$ values $<0.05$ were considered to be statistically significant.  

# Results  

# Baseline characteristics  

Initially, 92 neonates with septic shock fulfilled the inclusion criteria. After screening with exclusion criteria, 77 patients were enrolled and underwent randomization (LUGFR group, $n=38$ ; and control group, $n=39$ ). Ultimately, 72 neonates were followed up and included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1. Demographic information and clinical characteristics at baseline were similar between the two groups. The perinatal factors $(p=0.13)$ (Table 1), laboratory parameters (Table 1), baseline vital signs (Supplementary Table 1), and pathogens (Supplementary Table 1) were also not significantly different between the two groups (all $p$ values $>0.05$ ).  

# Study outcome analysis  

Fourteen days after randomization, the primary outcome (i.e., death from any cause) occurred in 5 of 36 $13.89\%)$ patients in the LUGFR group and 6 of 36 $(16.67\%)$ in the usual-care group $(p=0.76)$ (Table 2). Survival time did not differ significantly between the groups $(p=0.77$ [log-rank test]) (Supplementary Fig. 2). There were no significant between-group differences in 14-day mortality according to Cox proportional-hazards analysis after adjusted baseline covariates of sex, gestation, birth weight, systolic blood pressure, and lactate level (hazard ratio 0.81 $[95\%$ $\operatorname{CI}0.27\substack{-2.50}]$ ). Between-group mortality was similar in all predefined subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 3).  

For secondary outcomes (Table 2), the lengths of both NICU and hospital stay were shorter in the LUGFR group than those in the control group (21 days [IQR 14–26] vs. 26  days [IQR, 18–34]; $p{=}0.04$ ; 32  days [IQR, 24–40] vs. 39 days [IQR, 27–54], $p{=}0.01\$ ). Fluid volume used in the first 6 h was less in LUGFR group $\langle77.00\mathrm{mL/kg}$ [IQR 55.75 to 116.50] vs. $106.00\mathrm{mL/kg}$ [IQR 81.00 to 130.25]; $p{=}0.02,$ ), but the cumulative fluid used in first hour $(p=0.85)$ and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ $(p=0.17)$ did not differ significantly between the two groups. It is noteworthy that the incidence of AKI $25\%$ vs. $47.2\%$ , $p{=}0.05,$ and the need of diuretic use to correct overt fluid $(2.0\mathrm{mg/kg}$ [IQR 1.0 to 3.0] vs. $3.0\mathrm{mg/kg}$ [IQR 2.0 to 4.0]; $p{=}0.05,$ ) were significantly reduced in children undergoing ultrasound–guided fluid resuscitation. The incidence of intracranial hemorrhage (grades I–II) in the LUGFR group was significantly reduced within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ ( $13.9\%$ vs. $36.1\%$ , $p{=}0.03,$ . However, there were no significant differences between the LUGFR and control groups in the resolution of shock within $^{1\mathrm{~h~}}$ $(p=0.78)$ or $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ $(p=0.63)$ , use of mechanical ventilation $(p=0.74)$ or vasopressor support $(p=0.16)$ , time to achieve lactate level $<2\mathrm{mmol/L}$ $(p=0.29)$ , and the number of patients developing hepatomegaly $(p=0.78)$ . Laboratory values at 1, 6, 24, and $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ post-randomization were similar between the two groups (all $p{>}0.05,$ ) (Table 3).  

# Discussion  

Neonatal septic shock is an important cause that endangers the life and health of newborns in NICU, which often causes high mortality and serious sequelae [19]. Intravenous infusion therapy can effectively promote hemodynamic stability in the initial treatment of septic shock [20]. However, more studies have shown that FO during initial resuscitation of septic shock can often lead to more severe prognosis. In recent years, studies have emphasized the use of ultrasonography for fluid resuscitation in newborns with septic shock. Ultrasonography is commonly used to predict fluid responsiveness and cardiopulmonary function and detect signs of extravascular fluid in patients who experience shock [15, 21]. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to investigate the use of lung ultrasound in fluid resuscitation for neonatal septic shock. In our randomized controlled trial, compared with usual fluid resuscitation, LUGFR did not improve the primary outcome of 14-day all-cause mortality in newborns with septic shock. However, neonates with ultrasound–guided fluid resuscitation had shorter hospital stay and NICU stays, less fluid use in the first $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ , and a lower risk of AKI and intracranial hemorrhage.

Evidence-based guidelines on using point-of-care ultrasound for severely ill neonates and children are now available [22]. Lung water of different contents will manifest unique patterns in ultrasound images [23, 24]. Bedside cardiopulmonary ultrasonography can evaluate lung content in neonates with patent ductus arteriosus and predict the possibility of hemodynamic changes in persistent patent ductus arteriosus [25]. With the FALLS-protocol brought forward in 2012, Lichtenstein et al. indicated that the use of lung ultrasonography monitoring could prevent FO during fluid resuscitation of patients with shock [26]. EVLW is a key variable in heart failure management and prognosis. In pulmonary congestion, the presence of both air and water creates a peculiar echo fingerprint [27]. Lung ultrasound shows B-lines, comet-like signals arising from a hyperechoic pleural line with a to-and-fro movement synchronized with respiration [27]. Increasing EVLW accumulation alters the normal, no-echo signal into a black-and-white pattern or a white lung pattern with coalescing B-lines. In this sense, lung ultrasound assessment of EVLW using B-lines provides an excellent alternative [26]. The study by Zong et al. suggested that the B-lines can serve as a sensitive, quantitative, noninvasive, and real‐time indicator of EVLW in a rabbit model of lung water accumulation [28]. Under lung ultrasonography, the presence of pulmonary B-lines has been shown to detect an early increase in EVLW and correlates well with pulmonary edema on chest radiograph [21]. Therapy guided by lung ultrasound and the presence of B-line are of great significance for lung disease in low birth weight infants [24, 29].  

Ultrasonography is especially suitable for use in the smallest patients and in critical care settings and is an ideal medical tool for hemodynamic management of neonatal septic shock [10]. Lung ultrasound is a promising bedside modality for the estimation of EVLW, but has not been validated against objective measures in children [10]. As a hands-down and noninvasive procedure to ascertain fluid responsiveness, inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter measurement in respiratory changes is widely used in adult shock patients [30, 31]. However, measurement of IVC diameter appears to be not applicable to neonatal septic shock given the physical characteristics of neonates.

Due to the fragile germinal matrix vessels in the brain of premature infants, rapid hemodynamic fluctuations can often cause neonatal intraventricular hemorrhage [32, 33]. Inappropriate FO can stress the germinal matrix vessels and lead to intraventricular hemorrhage [34]. Our study found that ultrasound–guided fluid resuscitation can significantly reduce the incidence of intraventricular hemorrhage. A large number of previous studies have shown that FO can increase the risk of AKI and venous congestion in neonates with septic shock [4, 34, 35]. Our study also confirmed that fluid control under the guidance of lung ultrasound can effectively reduce the risk of AKI and the use of diuretics.  

Although the 14-day mortality rate did not improve, lung ultrasound significantly shortened the length of hospital and NICU stay of neonates with septic shock in our setting. However, our study had some limitations. First, although we determined that a sample of 72 patients would provide the study with $>80\%$ power to detect a $28\%$ absolute reduction in 14-day mortality, our sample size may have impacted the effect size of the short-term benefit(s). Second, although we used the 10-region method to monitor and evaluate newborns with septic shock, we did not assess lung ultrasound scores to further explore the relationship between lung ultrasound scores and neonatal septic shock and its complications. The combination of lung ultrasound scores and clinical features as a means of monitoring and evaluating neonatal septic shock is the focus of future prospective research [17]. And larger multicenter prospective studies are required to confirm the results of the present study. Third, although bias was minimized by appropriate randomization, bias may have occurred during patient allocation, data collection, or outcome measurement due to the unconcealed nature of the study interventions. In addition, the clinical parameters recorded were not sufficiently comprehensive, and certain factors, such as the type of bacteria and use of antibiotics, were omitted.

# Conclusion  

Results of this single-center, randomized clinical trial indicated that the use of LUGFR did not improve 14-day mortality in neonates with septic shock. However, the use of lung ultrasonography significantly shortened the length of hospital and NICU stay and fluid use in the first $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ , and reduced the risk of intraventricular hemorrhage and AKI. Data from the present study may provide a basis for conducting larger randomized controlled clinical trials to explore the use of LUGFR in the prognosis of neonates with sepsis or septic shock. Further studies are required to identify optimal physiological targets and fluid resuscitation approaches for the initial treatment of neonatal septic shock.
