# Does Initiation of Prophylactic Antiseizure Medication Improve Neurological Outcomes in Patients With Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage?  

A Critically Appraised Topic  

Nikita Chhabra, DO,\* Amy Z. Crepeau, MD,\* Bart M. Demaerschalk, MD, MSc,\* Molly G. Knox, MD,\*   
William David Freeman, $MD,\dagger$ Cristina Valencia Sanchez, MD, PhD,\*   
Lisa A. Marks, MLCS, $AHIP,\ddagger$ and Cumara B. O’Carroll, MD, MPH\*  

Objective: The objective of this study was to critically assess current evidence regarding the role of prophylactic antiseizure medication in patients presenting with acute intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH).  

Methods: The objective was addressed through the development of a structured critically appraised topic. This included a clinical scenario with a clinical question, literature search strategy, critical appraisal, results, evidence summary, commentary, and bottom-line conclusions. Participants included resident neurologists, a medical librarian, and content experts in the fields of epilepsy, stroke neurology, neurohospitalist medicine, and neurocritical care.  

Results: A randomized clinical trial was selected for critical appraisal. The trial assessed whether prophylactic levetiracetam (LEV) use reduced the risk of acute seizures in patients with ICH, as defined by clinical or electrographic seizure, captured by continuous electroencephalogram 72 hours after enrollment. A total of 42 patients were included in the final analysis (19 in the LEV group and 23 in the placebo group). There was a significantly higher occurrence of seizures in the placebo versus LEV group (LEV $16\%$ vs placebo $43\%$ , $P=$ 0.043). There were no differences in functional outcomes between the groups at 3, 6, or 12 months $(P>0.1$ ).  

Conclusions: The role of prophylactic treatment with antiseizure medication in ICH remains unclear.  

Key Words: critically appraised topic, evidence-based medicine, intracerebral hemorrhage, antiseizure drugs, prophylaxis, functional outcome, preventative  

(The Neurologist 2023;28:422–425)  

# CLINICAL CASE  

A 72-year-old man with a past medical history of deep vein thrombosis on anticoagulation with warfarin presents to the emergency department after being found down by his wife. A prompt neurological assessment is conducted which reveals a right face, arm and leg hemiparesis, and global aphasia. A computed tomography head reveals a large volume of left hemispheric intraparenchymal hemorrhage with midline shift. Treatment is initiated which includes reversal of anticoagulation, blood pressure management, and consulting neurosurgery and neuro-intensive care unit colleagues. There is no clear evidence of clinical seizures, but discussion arises as to whether initiating prophylactic antiseizure medication (ASM) is appropriate.  

# BACKGROUND  

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a devastating diagnosis, accounting for up to $15\%$ of all stroke diagnoses and resulting in a $40\%$ to $50\%$ mortality rate.1 ICH can be complicated by the development of seizures. Clinical and electrographic seizures have been demonstrated to occur in up to $30\%$ of patients with ICH.2,3 Clinical seizures in the setting of ICH have been shown to be associated with worse functional outcomes, with one study demonstrating worse modified Rankin score [mRS; odds ratio (OR): 1.79; CI: 1.12-2.86, $P=0.015]$ and increased risk of death (OR: 3.26; CI: 1.98-5.39; $P<0.001\$ ) at 90 days.4 It is well accepted and stated in the $2022\mathrm{ICH}$ guidelines by the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association that if clinical or electrographic seizures are detected, ASM should be initiated to reduce mortality, improve functional outcomes, and prevent recurrent seizures.5  

Less clear is the role of prophylactic initiation of ASM in ICH. Previous literature addressing this topic is conflicting and often combines patients who are being treated preventatively and therapeutically for seizures, using various endpoints. $6{-}\dot{11}$ For example, Naidech et $\mathrm{al^{10}}$ evaluated 98 patients with ICH who received either prophylactic or therapeutic ASM treatment with phenytoin or levetiracetam (LEV) and found that patients who received phenytoin had increased occurrence of fever $(P~=~0.03)$ , lower National Institute of Health Stroke scale (NIHSS) at 14 days $(P=0.003\$ ), and worse mRS at 14 days, 28 days, and 3 months. Notably, LEV was not associated with these outcomes. When specifically looking at patients who were treated with prophylactic phenytoin, there was an increased risk of poor outcome (OR: 9.8; CI: 1.4-68.6, $P=0.02\$ .10 A placebo-controlled randomized trial by Gilad et al, 2011, demonstrated that there was no difference in seizure occurrence over 1 year when evaluating patients prophylactically being treated for seizures with valproic acid versus placebo in the setting of ICH $(P=0.8)$ ).7 Initial results by Battey et $\mathrm{al}^{6}$ were promising, as this retrospective observational cohort study demonstrated  

The Neurologist $\bullet$ Volume 28, Number 6, November 2023 that prophylactic or therapeutic ASM treatment was associated with reduced 90-day mortality in supratentorial and lobar hemorrhage; however, when the analysis was restricted to patients surviving $>5$ days from ICH, the association did not persist, ultimately concluding that this association was not clinically significant. As described, the literature supporting the use of prophylactic ASM in ICH remains unclear and muddled by poor-quality evidence and various endpoints. This is further highlighted by the latest $2022\mathrm{ICH}$ guidelines, which recommend against the initiation of prophylactic ASM given the lack of supporting data.5 A high-quality randomized control trial (RCT) addressing this question is lacking, and, therefore, the choice of prophylactic ASM use in ICH is often left to expert judgment based on individual clinical scenarios.  

Given the unclear impact of seizure prevention on longterm morbidity and mortality in patients with ICH, there remains great interest in understanding the optimal management strategy of ICH-related seizures in the neurology community.  

# CLINICAL QUESTION  

In adult patients with ICH, does the use of prophylactic ASM compared with placebo lead to improved functional outcomes?  

# SEARCH STRATEGY  

The literature search was conducted in the Ovid MEDLINE database between January 1946 and September 2022 using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords. MeSH terms included: cerebral hemorrhage, anticonvulsants, and treatment outcome. Keywords included: intracerebral hemorrhage, antiseizure agents, neuro$\log^{*}$ outcome\*, preventative, prevention and treatment outcome\*. MeSH terms and keywords were combined using the Boolean operators of “AND” and “OR” and then limited to English language articles resulting in 17 references for review (\* indicates truncation of word or phrase). A trial by PeterDerex et al,12 “Safety and efficacy of prophylactic LEV for the prevention of epileptic seizures in the acute phase of ICH (PEACH): a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial” was selected.  

# EVIDENCE, RESULTS, AND CRITICAL APPRAISAL  

# Evidence  

The study reported by Peter-Derex et al, 2022,12 best addressed our clinical question and was chosen as it was determined to be the highest level of evidence of the manuscripts reviewed. This study was a parallel-group, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial that was conducted in 3 stroke units in France. The primary objective was to assess whether prophylactic LEV would reduce the risk of acute seizures in patients with ICH. This was measured by the occurrence of a clinical or electrographic seizure as captured by continuous electroencephalogram (cEEG) over 72 hours after enrollment. The study also included a number of secondary objectives, which included: the number of early (day 0 to 30) and late (day 30 to $12\mathrm{mo},$ ) clinical seizures, change in ICH volume and mass effect defined as midline shift at 72 hours, change in NIHSS between inclusion and 72 hours, 1 month, and 3 months, change in mRS between inclusion and 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months, and quality of life (QoL) as assessed by Stroke Impact Scale at 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months. Safety measures included frequency of treatment-related side effects, anxiety, and depression as evaluated by the Hospital  

Anxiety and Depression Scale at 1 month and 3 months, along with all-cause mortality at 12 months. Patients were included if they were 18 years of age or older and presented with spontaneous supratentorial ICH within 24 hours of symptom onset. Patients were excluded if they presented with an NIHSS of $\geq25$ , were suspected or confirmed to have secondary ICH, had a history of epilepsy or premorbid antiseizure drug use, were pregnant or breast-feeding, had a history of severe depression or psychosis, had a premorbid terminal illness, had a known allergy to LEV, or had an occurrence of a clinical seizure between inclusion and initiation of treatment. Once patients were consented and enrolled, they were randomized 1:1 to the LEV or placebo group, stratified by center and NIHSS on presentation $\mathit{\check{\Psi}}<15$ or $>15$ ) through a central randomization software. Patients, all clinicians (neuroradiologists, neurophysiologists, and acute care physicians), and those assessing adverse outcomes were blinded to group assignment. Notably, unblinding was allowed if a clinical seizure occurred during the treatment period $(12\mathrm{mo})$ , to allow for adjustment in treatment accordingly, although this did not exclude the patients from the final analysis.  

Once the patient was included and randomized, the patient was initiated on treatment (LEV vs placebo) and cEEG within 24 hours of enrollment. All patients received LEV $500~\mathrm{mg}$ twice a day, through intravenous route for 48 hours, followed by oral (if able to swallow) for 30 days, followed by a 2-week taper. cEEG was an 11-electrode montage with placement through 10 to 20 systems; there was no video component. cEEG data were retrospectively analyzed by a blinded boardcertified neurophysiologist, with confirmation by a consensus committee of 5 other blinded neurophysiologists if there were suspected seizures. Repeat brain imaging was completed at 72 hours to assess for hematoma expansion and midline shift and was analyzed by a blinded neuroradiologist.  

It was calculated that a sample size of 52 per arm was required to achieve $80\%$ power with an alpha of $5\%$ , with an assumed loss to follow-up of $10\%$ . In terms of statistical analyses, the primary and secondary endpoints were performed according to the modified intention-to-treat principle (included all patients who were randomly assigned to treatment and had cEEG) and safety analysis was performed in the intention-totreat population (all patients who received at least one of either of the treatments, irrespective of the dose). Primary outcome analysis was completed through a logistic regression model, with adjustment for randomization stratification factors (NIHSS and center).  

# RESULTS  

A total of 50 patients were enrolled (mean age: 72, $37\%$ women) but ultimately due to multiple exclusions (largely due to cEEG logistical issues), only 42 patients were finally included (19 in the LEV group and 23 in the placebo group). At baseline, there were multiple differences between the two groups. Patients in the LEV group were older, had higher premorbid mRS, higher likelihood of premorbid hypertension and diabetes, were more likely to be on antiplatelet therapy, and were more likely to have lobar hemorrhage. Patients in the placebo group were more likely to have higher NIHSS and larger hematoma volume on presentation. All patients had continuous electroencephalogram monitoring.  

Regarding the assessment of the primary outcome, no patient had a clinical seizure within the first 72 hours; therefore, all seizures reported and included for analysis were electrographic (only noted on cEEG). There was a significantly higher

occurrence of seizures in the placebo versus LEV group [LEV 3/19 patients, $16\%$ vs placebo 10/23 patients, $43\%$ ; effect size 0.16 (CI: 0.03-0.94), $P=0.043]$ ].  

There were no significant differences in any secondary outcome between the two groups, including hematoma expansion or midline shift at 72 hours, difference in NIHSS at 72 hours, 1 month, and 3 months, difference in mRS at 3 months, 6 months, or 12 months, and difference in QoL at 3 months, 6 months, or 12 months (all $P>0.1$ ). In addition, there were no differences between groups when assessing safety outcomes, including no difference in all-cause mortality, anxiety and depression, and adverse events (all $P>0.1$ ).  

# APPRAISAL  

This study is the first RCT to date to address the specific question of whether the use of prophylactic LEV reduces the occurrence of a seizure, either clinical or electrographic, in patients with acute ICH. The results clearly reveal a significant difference between the LEV and placebo groups, showing that patients in the LEV group had significantly fewer seizures. It is important to highlight that all seizures were electrographic, as no clinical seizures occurred within the first 72 hours. Despite this difference in the occurrence of seizures, all secondary outcomes, including change in hematoma volume and midline shift at 72 hours, all measures of functional outcome (mRS, NIHSS, and QoL) across all time points were not statistically different between the two groups. In addition, all conclusions must be interpreted within the context that all endpoints, including the primary outcome, were underpowered as goal enrollment was not achieved.  

The strengths of this study include its clear randomization protocol, well-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the blinding of patients, neuroradiologists, neurophysiologists, acute care physicians, and all involved in assessing adverse events. In addition, the study authors had well-defined primary and secondary outcomes, with objective and standardized methods of measurement. It is notable that the authors specifically screened for anxiety and depression as an adverse event given the active drug of use in this study was LEV, an ASM known for causing behavioral and neuropsychiatric side effects.  

Limitations of the study include poor representation of women, small sample size, and the fact that only patients from France were included, thereby limiting overall generalizability. These data are also not generalizable to patients with severe ICH, as those presenting with $\mathrm{NIHSS}>25$ were excluded, which one could argue maybe the patients with the highest risk of seizures. Inclusion and subgroup analysis of this group would have been of tremendous interest. Notably, there were differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups, with some differences being factors that may influence the risk of development of seizures. For example, the placebo group had higher NIHSS and hematoma volume at baseline, which may have increased the risk of seizure development. In addition, specific locations of hemorrhages were not disclosed. It is unclear whether the location of hemorrhages was significantly different between groups, specifically a question of whether one group had a higher percentage of patients with areas of hemorrhage that have a propensity to be highly epileptogenic, which in turn may influence the incidence of and number of total seizures. It also remains unclear whether there were truly no clinical manifestations of the electrographic seizures as there was no video component captured. Therefore, subtle motor and nonmotor manifestations of electrographic seizures were potentially missed (ie, confusion, aphasia, and subtle twitching). All patients received LEV $500~\mathrm{mg}$ and, therefore, it remains unclear whether this was an adequate dose as average weight and creatinine clearance by the group were not reported. Finally, given that the goal of prevention of seizures with an ASM is to improve functional outcomes, the primary outcome should have included a measure of functional outcome rather than an assessment of seizure occurrence.  

# CLINICAL BOTTOM LINES  

The role of prophylactic treatment of seizures in ICH remains unclear. Although there was a clearly reduced occurrence of electrographic seizures in the LEV group, this did not impact radiographic or functional outcomes in both the short and long term.   
There is a high frequency of seizures in patients with ICH, and, therefore, cEEG should be considered routinely in patients with fluctuating mental status and unexplained altered mental status, as it can potentially result in more aggressive management and escalation of treatment with ASMs.   
 There remains a need for an adequately powered RCT that uses functional outcome measures as a primary outcome. As the role of prophylactic treatment with ASMs in ICH remains unclear, it is up to treating clinicians to use their discretion based on individual clinical factors.  

# DISCUSSION  

# Epilepsy, Vascular Neurology, Neurohospitalist, and Neurocritical Care Commentary  

The RCT by Peter-Derex and colleagues was conducted to determine whether prophylactic treatment with LEV prevents seizures in acute ICH. The results clearly show a significant difference between the LEV and placebo groups, demonstrating that patients in the LEV had significantly fewer seizures. Despite this difference in the occurrence of seizures, all secondary outcomes, including change in hematoma volume and midline shift at 72 hours, and all measures of functional outcome (mRS, NIHSS, and QoL) were not statistically different between the two groups. Importantly, all conclusions must be considered within the context that all endpoints, including the primary outcome, were underpowered as goal enrollment was not met. All in all, the question of whether or not prophylactic treatment of seizures in ICH is warranted remains unknown, as although there was a clearly reduced occurrence of seizures in the LEV group, this did not impact radiographic or functional outcomes in both the short and long term.  

We acknowledge that this trial brings into question whether our threshold for consideration of cEEG monitoring in patients with ICH should be lowered, as $30\%$ of all patients in this study had electrographic seizures. If these patients were continuously monitored and the care team was alerted to seizure occurrence, a clinical evaluation by video or bedside examination may have determined that the electrographic seizures had subtle clinical manifestations, which may have encouraged more aggressive treatment by clinicians. However, further study is warranted to determine how the recognition and treatment of electrographic seizures impact clinical outcomes.  

As discussed, this trial only detected electrographic seizures, and although the treatment of clinical seizures with ASM has proven to improve functional outcomes, this relationship is less clear for electrographic seizures.13,14 Data by Vespa et al3 demonstrate that $28\%$ of their patients with ICH developed

electrographic seizures, which was associated with worse neurological outcomes as measured by NIHSS and an increase in midline shift. Therefore, logic may support aggressive treatment of all seizures in patients with ICH. However, data looking at the treatment of nonconvulsive status epilepticus in the critically ill has demonstrated that higher or greater doses of antiepileptic drugs do not improve outcomes, and the treatment with benzodiazepines (a medication often used in seizures) increased the risk of death, despite the severity of illness.15 Given conflicting data, the treatment of recurrent electrographic seizures is not addressed by guidelines published by the Neurocritical Care Society or the American Epilepsy Society. However, consensus expert opinion often recommends that these seizures should be treated similarly to convulsive status epilepticus, with the avoidance of anesthetics and intubation, as again, the outcomes of untreated non-convulsive status epilepticus remain unknown16,17  

We acknowledge that there may be differences in expert opinion regarding the recommendation for prophylactic ASM for ICH, and, therefore, the development of institution-specific practice algorithms may be favored. The implementation of these guidelines is aimed at reducing medication errors between care transfers and reducing the number of patients who end up on long-term ASMs for unclear indications.  

It should also be noted that the study protocol dictated the use of cEEG in all patients presenting with ICH for 72 hours, a practice which is often not feasible in resource-limited settings or supported by data, and, therefore, not pursued in many stroke units. The authors of the study also note that the use of cEEG for all patients with ICH is not part of their routine practice. Importantly, it should be acknowledged that all seizures captured in the study would not have been detected if cEEG was not used, as all seizures were electrographic. Therefore, this practice and result may not reflect real-world practice.  

Future RCTs may also consider the application of a predictive score to help predict seizure occurrence in patients with ICH. One such tool includes the 2HELPS2B score18 which is a clinical risk score that combines 5 electroencephalogram features and a history of seizure to assign a score between 0 and 7, with higher scores indicating a higher risk of seizures (risk of seizure was $5\%$ with a score of 0 and $95\%$ with a score of 6 or 7). This original publication included 543/5427 patients with ICH but also included various other pathologies. Validation of this score in the ICH population, or creation of a similar score specifically for the ICH population, may be a helpful clinical tool to help predict those with the highest risk of seizure which may help inform the decision regarding prophylactic ASD use.  

Overall, the role of prophylactic treatment with ASMs in ICH remains unclear and is left up to the expert opinion of the treating clinician based on individual clinical factors. There remains a need for an adequately powered, RCT, which uses functional outcome measures as the primary outcome to help answer this important question.
