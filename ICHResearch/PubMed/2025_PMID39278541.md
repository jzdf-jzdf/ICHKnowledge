# The Effects of Glibenclamide on Cognitive Performance, Quality of Life, and Emotional Aspects Among Patients With Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: A Randomized Controlled Trial  

Isabela Costola Windlin', Bruno Braga Sisnando da Costa', Joao Paulo Mota Telles', Leonardo B. Oliveiral2, Edwin Koterba1, Vitor Nagai Yamaki1, Nicollas Nunes Rabelo1, Davi Jorge Fontoura Solla1, Manoel Jacobsen Teixeira1, Eberval Gadelha Figueiredo1  

- BACKGROUND: Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is associated with a high incidence of long-term cognitive impairment, decreased quality of life (QoL), and psychiatric disorders. The effects of glibenclamide on such outcomes in the setting of aSAH are unknown.  

- OBJECTIVE: To assess the impact of glibenclamide in patients with aSAH on cognitive performance, QoL, and emotional aspects.  

- METHODS: Patients identified with aSAH were randomly allocated to receive $5~\mathrm{mg}$ of glibenclamide for 21 days or placebo, starting within 96 hours of the ictus. After 6 months, patients were evaluated with Montreal Cognitive Assessment test (cognitive performance), Medical Outcomes Short-form Health Survey (QoL), and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale and Screen for Post-traumatic Stress Symptoms (emotional aspects).  

- RESULTS: The mean Montreal Cognitive Assessment score was $22.5\pm6.2$ No statistically significant difference was found between groups, with a mean score of $21.7\pm6.4$ in the Glibenclamide group and $23.4\pm6.2$ in the placebo group $(P=0.392)$ . A score $\mathbf{<}23$ was observed in 16 patients $(35.6\%)$ and its frequency was similar between groups $(P=0.900)$ . The most frequently impaired domains were Attention $(\mathsf{N}~=~21/45;~46.7\%)$ and Visuospatial (18/45; $40.0\%$ . Impairment of each domain was similar between groups $(P>0.05)$ . In each domain, the mean score was similar between groups $(P>0.05)$ . The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale scores did not differ between groups $(P>0.05)$ . The mean Screen for Post-traumatic Stress Symptoms score as well as the mean scores of its domains were similar between groups $(P>0.05)$ .  

- CONCLUSIONS: Glibenclamide did not improve cognitive performance, QoL, and emotional aspects after 6 months of follow-up of aSAH survivors.

Glibenclamide, an antidiabetic sulfonylurea, is a promising therapy in the setting of acute brain injury.8 Its potential benefit comes from the inhibition of SUR1-TRPM4 receptors, which might exert a protective effect on the vascular endothelium, mitigating edema and secondary hemorrhages. It is also thought to inhibit neuronal death, promote neurogenesis, and have antiinflammatory effects.9-11  

Clinical trials failed to demonstrate benefit of glibenclamide on functional outcomes of patients with aSAH, as well as on functional outcomes of other neurological conditions such as ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and traumatic brain injury.12-14 Our group published the Glibenclamide in Aneurysmatic Subarachnoid Hemorrhage trial, randomizing $78$ patients to glibenclamide or placebo after aSAH. There was no difference between groups in terms of functional outcome, mortality, or vasospasm.15  

However, we are unaware of trials that analyzed the impact of glibenclamide on cognitive performance, QoL, and emotional aspects in the setting of acute brain injury. Therefore, this randomized controlled study aims to assess whether the administration of this drug improves such outcomes within 6 months among patients with aSAH.  



# MATERIALS AND METHODS  



# Ethics, Design, and Registration  

This research included patients who participated in the clinical trial entitled Glibenclamide in Aneurysmatic Subarachnoid Hemorrhage, in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials checklist (Figure 1). The trial protocol was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03569540) and it is also detailed in a previous publication.16 The results of the primary outcome and secondary outcomes other than those analyzed in the present study were also published.15  



# Patients  

Patients admitted to the neurocritical care unit from July 31, 2017 to May 1, 2020 were enrolled. We obtained written standardized informed consent forms from the patients or from their legal representatives. Inclusion criteria were 1) radiological evidence of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging and aneurysmal origin confirmed by digital subtraction angiography, computed tomography angiography, or magnetic resonance angiography; 2) age between 18 and 70 years; and 3) definitive treatment (either clipping or coiling) within 96 hours of ictus. Exclusion criteria were 1) inadequate administration of the medication through 21 days, 2) current use of glibenclamide prior to study initiation, 3) pregnancy, 4) known liver or kidney disease, 5) functional impairment before bleeding (any grade), 6) Glasgow Coma Scale score 3 or Hunt and Hess grade V at admission, 7) other comorbidity that limits survival (e.g., cancer, acute respiratory distress syndrome), 8) alcohol or drug abuse, and 9) current use of anticoagulant drugs (e.g., warfarin). For the outcomes analyzed in the current report, 10) death before the evaluation in 6 months was an additional exclusion criterion.  



# Randomization and Intervention  

Patients were randomly allocated (1:1) to receive $5~\mathrm{mg}$ of glibenclamide or $5~\mathrm{mg}$ of placebo. Intravenous glibenclamide is not available in Brazil, and $5~\mathrm{mg}$ is the standard initial dose in our institution—a small dose to prevent significant hypoglycemia. A computer-generated sequence randomly designated patients to either treatment, in blocks of 10 (5 treatment and 5 placebo). All patients began treatment within 96 hours of ictus, as soon as they underwent definitive treatment of their aneurysm (clipping or coiling) due to the potential harm of early postoperative hypoglycemia. Doses were administered once daily orally or through feeding tubes. Treatment was continued until the 21st day after the ictus.  



# Patient Monitoring  

Laboratorial monitoring included blood glucose every 6 hours as well as daily urea, creatinine, and liver enzymes. Hypoglycemia $\left(<\mathrm{70}\mathrm{mg/dL}\right)$ was immediately corrected with intravenous glucose, according to our institutional protocol. Patients with recurrent hypoglycemia for $_{24^{-}4^{8}}$ hours during medication use had treatment suspended and, if glycemia was normalized after suspension, they were withdrawn from the study intervention. The experimental treatment drugs were administered between lunch and dinner, around $4~\mathrm{PM}$ , to avoid fasting and hypoglycemia.  



# Outcomes  

At the end of the treatment (21st day), each patient’s functional status was evaluated according to the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). The primary outcome of the original trial was the distribution of the 6-month mRS score. In this report, we analyzed the following secondary outcomes: 1) cognitive performance, 2) QoL, and 3) emotional aspects.  

Cognitive performance was assessed by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). We obtained the means scores $\pm$ standard deviation, as well as frequencies and percentages of impaired domains. QoL was assessed by Medical Outcomes Short-form Health Survey (SF-36). Emotional aspects were assessed by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and by the Screen for Post-traumatic Stress Symptoms (SPTSS).15  



# Statistical Methods  

We estimated a sample size of 80 patients, which would yield $80\%$ power at the $5\%$ significance level (2-sided) to detect a treatment effect equivalent to a $7\%$ absolute increase in the proportion of patients with a more favorable functional outcome. This calculation was based on an ordinal analysis of the mRS score, assuming the treatment effect follows a proportional odds model (which was confirmed by the parallel lines test).  

Data were analyzed according to the modified intention-to-treat principle. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentages. Continuous data are presented as mean $\pm$ standard deviation or median and interquartile range, according to normality tests (Shapiro-Wilk’s). Mann-Whitney U test, chisquared test, and t-test were employed for hypotheses testing, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using International Business Machines Corporation Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics for Windows (version 24.0, IBM Corp.). The analysis was blinded for group allocations.

The educational level was higher in the placebo group (0.001).

# DISCUSSION  

This randomized controlled trial assessed whether the administration of glibenclamide improved the cognitive performance, QoL, or emotional aspects of patients with aSAH. For these purposes, the MoCA, SF-36, HADS, and SPTSS assessments were used after 6 months of follow-up. We did not find statistically significant difference of these outcomes between glibenclamide and control groups.

Although no significant effects of glibenclamide on these outcomes were found, there was a high prevalence of these symptoms in both groups. Such results are indicative that, in general, a neurological event such as SAH has intense repercussions in the different spheres of the patient’s life. In fact, psychological problems are associated not only with deficits but also with subjective perceptions of cognitive impairment, contributing even more to a negative experience of the subject in the rehabilitation process.17  



# Cognitive Performance  

Regarding the possible benefits of glibenclamide, Jiang et al.18 observed that the medication brought improvements in motor skills and spatial memory in rats in studies of traumatic brain injury. However, no such neuroprotective effects were identified in the present study.  

The absence of significant results regarding the MoCA suggests a possibility that, despite being sensitive for identifying impairment, it is not very specific. The study that translated and validated the MoCA in Brazil suggests the use of a cutoff score less than or equal to 25.19 However, this research adopted the cutoff score of 23, as suggested by a recent meta-analysis.20 Adopting this lower cutoff score offers a better balance between specificity and sensitivity.  



# Quality of Life and Emotional Aspects  

In this study, there was also no significant difference in the groups in relation to all domains of the scale that assessed the QoL of the participants (SF-36). However, a clinical trial that tested the use of glibenclamide in patients with hemispheric stroke was able to identify an improvement in survival and QoL in subjects who received the drug.

Despite the absence of statistical difference between the groups regarding emotional symptoms (anxiety and depression) and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, a higher prevalence than that found in the general population was observed. Researchers describe that there is an association among PTSD, development of anxiety and depression, and length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU). In fact, all patients in this study were hospitalized for at least 14 days in the ICU, and, therefore, they are also susceptible to the emotional impacts of this hospitalization and may even develop the so-called Postintensive Care Syndrome. Some authors warn about a difficulty in making this diagnosis in neurological patients who have gone through the ICU, as some neuropsychological symptoms can be confused with symptoms that determine criteria for Postintensive Care Syndrome.23  

Even so, the presence of PTSD symptoms is associated with decreased QoL in patients who had SAH, even in the absence of cognitive or physical sequelae.24 Therefore, this topic demands attention and care during recovery from an event such as aSAH.  



# Limitations  

This study presents some limitations. The sample size is not extensive, and recruitment did not achieve the initial calculated sample size. Furthermore, the analysis is restricted to secondary outcomes, albeit prespecified.  

Studies using other dosages or routes for medication are needed. An earlier introduction of the drug might prevent an early upregulation of receptors and therefore improve outcomes.25 More sensitive instruments for the assessment of specific cognitive functions and that consider education can bring greater specificity and detail to the study of cases. Another limitation is the follow-up, as longer follow-ups might demonstrate other cognitive trends.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

In this randomized controlled trial, glibenclamide failed to demonstrate improvement of cognitive performance, QoL, and emotional aspects among aSAH survivors. Due to their large prevalence and burden, cognitive performance, QoL, and emotional aspects should be considered as primary endpoints in future trials of aSAH.
