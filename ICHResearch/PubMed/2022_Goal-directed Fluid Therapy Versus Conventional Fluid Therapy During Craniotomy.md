# Goal-directed Fluid Therapy Versus Conventional Fluid Therapy During Craniotomy and Clipping of Cerebral Aneurysm: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Trial  

Summit D. Bloria, DM,\* Nidhi B. Panda, MD,\* Kiran Jangra, DM,\* Hemant Bhagat, DM,\* Banashree Mandal, DM,\* Ketan Kataria, DM,\* Rajeev Chauhan, DM,\* Ankur Luthra, DM,\* Shiv Lal Soni, DM,\* Narender Kaloria, DM,\* Shalvi Mahajan, DM,\* Shamik Kr Paul, DM,\* Shailesh Gupta, DM,\* Sanket Agrawal, DM,\* and Navneet Singla, Mch†  

Background: Fluid imbalance is common after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage and negatively impacts clinical outcomes. We compared intraoperative goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT) using left ventricular outflow tract velocity time integral (LVOTVTI) measured by transesophageal echocardiography with central venous pressure (CVP)-guided fluid therapy during aneurysm clipping in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage patients.  

Methods: Fifty adults scheduled for urgent craniotomy for aneurysm clipping were randomly allocated to 2 groups: group G $(\mathtt{n}=25$ ) received GDFT guided by LVOT-VTI and group C $(\mathtt{n}=25\$ ) received CVP-guided fluid management. The primary outcome was intraoperative mean arterial pressure (MAP). Secondary outcomes included volume of fluid administered and several other intraoperative and postoperative variables, including neurological outcome at hospital discharge and at 30 and 90 days.  

Results: There was no difference in MAP between the 2 groups despite patients in group $\mathbf{G}$ receiving lower volumes of fluid compared with patients in group C $2503.6\pm534.3$ vs. $3732.8\pm676.5\mathrm{mL}$ , respectively; $P<0.0001$ ). Heart rate and diastolic blood pressure were also comparable between groups, whereas systolic blood pressure was higher in group $\mathrm{\bfG}$ than in group $\mathrm{^C}$ at several intraoperative time points. Other intraoperative variables, including blood loss, urine output, and lactate levels were not different between the 2 groups. Postoperative variables, including creatinine, duration of postoperative mechanical ventilation, length of intensive care unit and hospital stay, and incidence of acute kidney injury, pneumonitis, and vasospasm were also comparable between groups. There was no difference in neurological outcome at hospital discharge (modified Rankin scale) and at 30 and 90 days (Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale) between the 2 groups.  

Conclusion: Compared with CVP-guided fluid therapy, transesophageal echocardiography–guided GDFT maintains MAP with lower volumes of intravenous fluid in patients undergoing clipping of intracranial aneurysms with no adverse impact on postoperative complications.

Approximately $3\%$ of the middle-aged human population harbors an intracranial aneurysm.1 Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is caused by the rupture of an intracranial aneurysm. Following aneurysmal rupture, aSAH patients are at risk of hypovolemia from multiple causes including decreased fluid intake because of impaired consciousness, diuretic use, cerebral salt wasting syndrome, and preoperative fasting.2 Hypovolemia can lead to hypotension, low cardiac output, and subsequent hypoperfusion of vital organs. In a recent study, a high proportion of aSAH patients presenting for aneurysm coiling procedures were dehydrated and had low cardiac output states.2 aSAH patients may also be at risk for hypervolemia because of overzealous fluid resuscitation; this increases the risk of pulmonary complications and can worsen preexisting neurogenic pulmonary edema with adverse impacts on mortality and morbidity.2–4 Hence, it is vital to provide judicious fluid therapy to aSAH patients, and goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT) may be beneficial in achieving this aim.2,5  

The intraoperative period is considered a critical part of patient management after aSAH as there can be large hemodynamic variations during surgery which may adversely affect cerebral perfusion.6–8 Careful hemodynamic and fluid management is one of the most important goals during craniotomy for aneurysm surgery as both hypovolemia and hypervolemia increase the risk of morbidity after aSAH. Hypovolemia can lead to decreased cerebral blood flow and is considered one of the main risk factors for the development of delayed cerebral ischemia.9 Hypovolemia can also adversely impact systemic organ perfusion and lead to systemic acidosis and acute kidney injury (AKI). Hypervolemia is also not without risk as it may lead to pulmonary edema and cerebral edema.8 Thus, GDFT might play a significant role in avoiding the complications of both hypovolemia and hypervolemia after aSAH.  

There are several techniques that can be used to manage GDFT, including assessment of stroke volume variation, pulse pressure variation, and pulmonary artery catheterization; each has advantages and disadvantages. Stroke volume variation may not accurately estimate volume status in cardiac surgical patients or in those with sepsis or severe arrhythmias,10 w hereas pulmonary artery catheterization is an invasive technique associated with potentially life-threatening complications that cannot be justified in most patients. Therefore, for this study, we chose transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) as a reliable and relatively noninvasive tool to assess intravascular volume status. TEE has similar effectiveness to pulmonary artery catheterization for the determination of volume status, and additional advantages because it provides realtime information about the structure and functioning of the heart.11,12  

Although other techniques have been used to guide GDFT in aSAH patients, there is insufficient evidence to support the use of intraoperative TEE to guide GDFT during neurosurgery. Therefore, we planned to compare intraoperative GDFT guided by TEE-measured left ventricular outflow tract velocity time integral (LVOT-VTI) with central venous pressure (CVP)-guided fluid therapy in patients undergoing clipping of intracranial aneurysms. LVOT-VTI, the integral of all velocities flowing across the aortic outlet during systole, is measured using pulse-wave Doppler to trace the velocity envelope at the aortic outlet and has been reported to be a reliable surrogate of fluid responsiveness in mechanically ventilated patients.13  

We hypothesized that GDFT would be associated with improved control of mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) compared with CVP-guided fluid therapy during intracranial aneurysm surgery. The primary aim of this study was comparison of intraoperative MAP with fluid replacement guided by GDFT and CVP. Secondary aims were comparison of other intraoperative variables (including blood loss, urine output, and lactate levels), postoperative variables (including ventilator days, intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital length of stay, in-hospital mortality), and neurological outcomes assessed by the modified Rankin score (mRS) at discharge and the extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS-E) at 30 and 90 days.  

# METHODS  

This prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted following Institutional Ethics Committee Approval (INT/IEC/2017/1448) and registration with Clinical Trial Registry, India (CTRI/2018/02/011862). Adult patients (aged from 18 to 65 y) scheduled for urgent craniotomy for clipping of an intracranial aneurysm at a tertiary level hospital between April 2018 and November 2018 were eligible for recruitment into the study following written informed consent from participants or their next of kin (for those unable to provide consent). Exclusion criteria included patient/relative refusal, contraindications for TEE probe insertion (esophageal surgery/stricture/lesions, coagulopathy), poorly controlled hypertension (known cases of hypertension noncompliant with medications and signs of left ventricular hypertrophy with/without strain pattern on electrocardiogram), patients with severe cardiac pathology (stunned myocardium, malignant arrhythmias, or known valvular heart disease or coronary artery disease), and patients with endorgan dysfunction including renal and hepatic disorders. All patients were assessed with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) preoperatively to rule out cardiac pathology; any patient found to have structural or functional heart defects was also excluded from the study.  

# Anesthesia Technique  

Following arrival in the operating room, standard monitors were attached to the patient and an infusion of $0.9\%$ saline was started and continued throughout surgery at a rate of $2\mathrm{{mL}/\mathrm{{kg}/h}}$ . A standard anesthesia protocol was followed in all the patients. Anesthesia was induced with propofol (1 to $2\mathrm{mg/kg}$ ) and fentanyl $(2\upmu\mathrm{g/kg)}$ , followed by vecuronium $(0.1\mathrm{mg/kg)}$ to facilitate tracheal intubation with an appropriately sized endotracheal tube. Anesthesia was maintained with propofol (50 to $150\upmu\mathrm{g}/$ $\mathrm{kg/min)}$ and fentanyl $(1\upmu\mathrm{g/kg/h})$ infusions adjusted to maintain state entropy values at 40 to 60; intermittent doses of vecuronium $\mathrm{1~mg}$ every $20\operatorname*{min}$ ) were administered as required. Arterial and central venous lines were inserted after intubation. Mechanical ventilation was adjusted to maintain a partial pressure of carbon dioxide of between 32 and $35\mathrm{mmHg}$ . In accordance to the institutional protocol, mannitol $20\%$ $(1~\mathrm{gm/kg})$ was administered to all the patients.  

Baseline volume status was assessed by TTE measurement of inferior vena cava (IVC) collapsibility index (in the subcostal window), the “kissing papillary muscle sign” (in the parasternal short-axis), and LVOT-VTI variation (in an apical 5-chamber view). Hypovolemia was defined as the presence of any of the following: IVC diameter ${<}2.1\mathrm{cm}$ with $>50\%$ collapse, presence of the “kissing papillary muscle sign,” and LVOT-VTI variation $>12\%$ .14–17 All hypovolemic patients received a bolus (5 to $10\mathrm{mL/kg}$ of $0.9\%$ saline) to achieve IVC collapsibility index $<50\%$ or velocity time integral variations $<12\%$ .

The random numbers were kept in opaque sealed envelopes numbered sequentially, and the envelopes were opened before the induction of anesthesia. Intraoperative fluid administration was guided by LVOT-VTI or CVP according to group allocation.  

Group G: LVOT-VTI was monitored with a Vivid E9 TEE machine (GE Healthcare, Australia/New Zealand) using an adult 6Tc TEE probe (Model no- KN100107; GE Medical Systems, France) inserted to achieve a deep transgastric longaxis view. The first reading after probe insertion (before the start of surgery) was defined as baseline, and LVOT-VTI was then measured every 30 minutes (and additionally during any episode of hypotension) until the end of the surgery. Fluid was administered according to the protocol outlined in Figure 1. LVOT-VTI ${<}15\mathrm{cm}$ or variation $>12\%$ defined fluid responders who received a fluid bolus $(5\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{kg})$ of $>5$ minutes. Patients with LVOT-VTI $>15$ cm and velocity time integral variation of $<12\%$ were considered nonresponders and were administered phenylephrine only if they developed hypotension (MAP ${<}70\mathrm{mmHg}$ ).  

Group C: The right subclavian vein was cannulated and fluid therapy guided according to CVP, aiming to maintain CVP 8 to $10\mathrm{cm}\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}$ . Fluid administration and hypotension were also managed according to the protocol outlined in Figure 1.  

In both the groups, phenylephrine ( $100\upmu\mathrm{g}$ intravenous [IV] bolus) was administered if MAP remained $<70\mathrm{mmHg}$ for 5 minutes after administration of a second fluid bolus. If repeated phenylephrine boluses were required $\left(>3\right)$ times), norepinephrine infusion was initiated and titrated according to blood pressure. During any hemorrhagic complication, including intraoperative aneurysm rupture, background fluid administration was increased based on blood loss and, in the event of major blood loss, fluid was replaced with hydroxyethyl starch or blood products as per our institutional protocol to maintain hematocrit level of around $30\%$ . Allowable blood loss was calculated using the following formula:  

(Initial hematocrit − Target hematocrit) Total blood volume $\times$ Initial hematocrit  

Any patient experiencing massive blood loss $(>1$ estimated blood volume) was excluded from the study.

# Outcomes  

The primary outcome of the study was intraoperative MAP in the 2 study groups. Secondary outcomes included other intraoperative variables (volume of fluid administered, number of episodes of MAP $<70$ $\mathrm{mmHg}$ , number of rescue vasopressor boluses, urine output, blood loss, blood product transfusion, and serum lactate levels at baseline and at the end of surgery) and postoperative variables including ventilation days, ICU and hospital length of stay, incidence of vasospasm, AKI and pneumonitis, and neurological outcomes.  

# Statistical Analysis  

The sample size was calculated using online software (www.openepi.com/SampleSize/SSMean.htm) and data from a previous study investigating GDFT in supratentorial neurosurgery.19 Assuming a baseline MAP of $80\pm15\mathrm{mmHg}$ and an $\upalpha$ error of 0.05, we calculated that a sample size of 44 (22 per group) would be required to identify a $9\mathrm{mmHg}$ difference in MAP between groups with $80\%$ power. Allowing for dropouts, we enrolled 25 participants in each group.  

The normality of the data was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Data was presented as mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ when normally distributed and as median (interquartile ranges) in case of non-normal distribution. Categorical data was expressed as count $(\%)$ . Continuous data with normal distribution were compared between study groups with paired or nonpaired $t$ tests, and nonnormally distributed data with the Mann-Whitney $U$ test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test for unpaired and paired results, respectively. Categorical data were compared using the $\chi^{2}$ test or Fisher exact test, as applicable. Data analysis was performed with SPSS, version 22.0 and Microsoft

# RESULTS  

Sixty-one consecutive patients were assessed for eligibility; 11 met predefined exclusion criteria leaving 50 who were eligible for inclusion in the study (Fig. 2). Demographic characteristics, and clinical and radiologic aSAH grades were similar between groups (Table 1). The mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ time from ictus to surgery was $1.72\pm1.95$ days and $2.65\pm0.89$ days in groups $\mathbf{G}$ and C, respectively $(P=0.114)$ .  

There was no difference in intraoperative MAP (the primary outcome) between groups (Fig. 3). Heart rate and DBP were also similar between groups, whereas SBP was higher in group $\mathbf{G}$ compared with group C at 30, 120, 150, and 180 minutes $\scriptstyle{P=0.045}$ , 0.006, 0.043, 0.045, respectively) after the start of surgery (Fig. 3). Mean fluid volume administered during surgery was $32.9\%$ lower in group $\mathrm{\bfG}$ compared with group $\mathrm{^C}$ $2503.6\pm534.3$ vs. $3732.8\pm676.5$ mL, respectively; $P<0.0001\$ ). Other intraoperative variables, including blood loss, volume of blood products administered, urine output, and lactate levels at baseline and end of surgery were similar between the 2 groups (Table 1). There was also no difference in the number of hypotensive episodes (group G, 5 vs. group C, 7; $P{=}0.187.$ ). One patient in each group required rescue boluses of phenylephrine, but no patient in either group required norepinephrine infusion (Table 1).

# DISCUSSION  

In this prospective randomized controlled study, we compared intraoperative GDFT guided by TEE-measured LVOT-VTI with conventional CVP-guided fluid therapy in aSAH patients undergoing surgical clipping of a cerebral aneurysm. GDFT resulted in lower volume of IV fluid administration to maintain MAP (which was similar between groups) compared with CVP-guided therapy. GDFT was associated with higher SBP at various times during surgery, although DBP was similar between groups. The number of hypotensive episodes and use of vasopressors were also comparable between GDFT and CVP groups.  

Patients are at high risk of fluid imbalance after aSAH. Though hypovolemia is common because of decreased fluid intake, vomiting, diuretic use, and the cerebral salt wasting syndrome,2 aSAH patients may also become hypervolemic secondary to excessive fluid administration. Perioperative fluid management is crucially important in aSAH patients, and can be challenging. Diastolic dysfunction has been reported in $57\%$ of aSAH patients,20 placing them at higher risk for developing pulmonary edema during high volume fluid administration, and $17\%$ to $20\%$ have systolic dysfunction and may not respond adequately to the fluid loading. Perioperative fluid therapy has been shown to impact outcomes in non-neurological surgery.22 Various factors govern perioperative fluid management after aSAH, including preoperative hydration status, blood loss, hemodynamic parameters, and fluid replacement decisions made by the anesthesiologists.23 Both hypovolemia and hypervolemia can increase the risk of multiorgan dysfunction and failure. Hypovolemia may lead to decreased cerebral blood flow and delayed cerebral ischemia after aSAH in addition to reducing systemic tissue perfusion leading to acidosis and risk of AKI, whereas hypervolemia can result in pulmonary edema, acute respiratory distress syndrome, cerebral edema, delayed recovery, and increased morbidity.7,9,24 Hence, it is vital to carefully manage fluid therapy in aSAH patients.  

Several monitoring modalities are available to guide perioperative fluid therapy. CVP is an established static method to assess fluid status and guide fluid replacement, while stroke volume variation, pulse pressure variation, pulmonary artery catheterization, and LVOT-VTI are dynamic methods. Echocardiographic-based techniques such as TEE have advantages over other modalities because they are able to assess real-time functional activity of the heart in addition to guiding fluid and vasopressor/inotrope management. In this study, we used TEE to guide GDFT because it is considered one of the most desirable perioperative choices, particularly when LVOT-VTI is used to guide fluid administration.25 TEE allows real-time visualization of the impact of fluid and inotropes on myocardial contractility and, unlike monitoring devices incorporating pulse contour analysis, can be used in patients with arrhythmias. In contrast, techniques using CVP, IVC collapsibility index, arterial pulse contour analysis, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure are indirect modalities for the estimation of left ventricular preload. TEE has the additional advantage of a short learning curve.  

We chose LVOT-VTI and LVOT-VTI variation to guide fluid management in the GDFT arm of this study because, unlike some other methods, LVOT-VTI is a reliable surrogate of fluid responsiveness in mechanically ventilated patients.13 Several studies have reported the utility of LVOT-VTI to guide fluid management.11,12,25 Broch et $\mathrm{al}^{25}$ demonstrated that TEE-measured LVOT-VTI variation predicted fluid responsiveness with $62\%$ sensitivity and $83\%$ specificity in patients with normal left ventricular function. In addition, subaortic LVOT-VTI variation has been reported to predict fluid responsiveness in critically ill patients receiving low tidal volume mechanical ventilation.26 The algorithm that we used for GDFT in this study (Fig. 2) was designed to use the minimal volume of IV fluid to maintain normovolemia and normotension.  

Similar algorithms have previously been reported for use in neurosurgical patients.19,27 We used $0.9\%$ saline in both groups in this study in accordance with our institutional protocol for fluid therapy in neurosurgical patients. As the need for frequent assessment of circulating volume is important in aSAH patients during aneurysm clipping surgery, we monitored LVOT-VTI variation and CVP every 30 minutes and during episodes of hypotension.  

The higher SBP in the patients having GDFT compared with CVP-guided fluid replacement may be because LVOT-VTI is primarily a measure of myocardial contraction, which contributes directly to SBP, whereas DBP depends on vessel wall compliance.28 Hence, LVOTVTI–guided fluid therapy has a greater impact on SBP. The importance of targeting SBP rather than MAP in neurosurgery is a matter of debate. Brown et al29 conducted an online survey among members of the Neurocritical Care Society to identify blood pressure targets in aSAH patients. When augmenting blood pressure in the management of delayed cerebral ischemia, most respondents preferred to target SBP or a combination of SBP and MAP with fewer targeting MAP alone $(\mathtt{n}=51$ , 52, and 24, respectively). Similarly, if blood pressure lowering was required before aneurysm treatment, most respondents targeted SBP $(\mathrm{n}=89\$ ). As per the current study, SBP is better maintained when fluid administration is guided by LVOT-VTI compared with CVP.  

Our finding that GDFT allowed maintenance of MAP with lower overall fluid input compared with CVP-guided therapy could be advantageous; unmonitored reductions in fluid input can compromise vital organ perfusion, leading to anaerobic metabolism and systemic lactate production.21 However, we found no difference in serum lactate levels or urine output between GDFT and CVP groups, or any differences in postoperative serum creatinine or incidence of AKI. Similarly, unmonitored fluid overload also has complications. In a cohort study, Vergouw et $\mathrm{al}^{30}$ found that higher volume fluid administration to aSAH patients within the first 72 hours of admission to a neurointensive care unit was associated with a higher occurrence of delayed cerebral ischemia, though GDFT (with transpulmonary thermodilution) significantly reduced fluid input and overall fluid balance without negatively affecting preload or Glasgow Coma Score scale in that study.  

We did not observe any differences in major postoperative complications or neurological outcomes between GDFT and CVP-guided fluid therapy groups. However, as we only used GDFT during surgery we are unable to determine any impact of the application of GDFT throughout the perioperative period. Although long-term neurological outcomes after aSAH are impacted by various factors, intraoperative fluid management is an important factor. Even though of short duration, meticulous intraoperative fluid management is crucial because of the significant intraoperative hemodynamic fluctuations that can contribute to adverse aSAH outcomes.  

This study has the strength of being a prospective, randomized study, but it also has limitations. First, relatively low numbers of patients were enrolled into the study. Though adequate for the assessment of the primary outcome (intraoperative MAP), it is unlikely that 50 participants were sufficient to identify differences in the many secondary outcomes that we report. Second, patients with cardiac pathology detected on preoperative TTE were excluded from the study, so our findings cannot be extrapolated to this patient cohort. Finally, GDFT was used only during the intraoperative period; postoperative fluid management was at the discretion of the intensivist managing the patients in the neurosurgical ICU. Thus, we are unable to determine any impact of the use of GDFT throughout the perioperative period until IV fluids were discontinued.  

# CONCLUSION  

TEE-guided GDFT maintained intraoperative MAP with significantly lower fluid volumes compared with fluid management guided by CVP monitoring, with no adverse impact on postoperative complications. Larger randomized controlled trials powered to detect effects in clinical outcomes, and with GDFT utilized throughout the perioperative period, are required to confirm any superiority of GDFT over conventional fluid management approaches in aSAH patients.
