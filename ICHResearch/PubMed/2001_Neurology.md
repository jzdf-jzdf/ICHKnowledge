# Rebleeding leads to poor outcome in ultra-early craniotomy for intracerebral hemorrhage  

L.B. Morgenstern, MD; A.M. Demchuk, MD, FRCPC; D.H. Kim, MD; R.F. Frankowski, PhD; and J.C. Grotta, MD  

Article abstract—Background: A modest benefit was previously demonstrated for hematoma evacuation within 12 hours of intracerebral hemorrhage onset. Perhaps surgery within 4 hours would further improve outcome. Methods: Adult patients with spontaneous supratentorial intracerebral hemorrhage were prospectively enrolled. Craniotomy and clot evacuation were commenced within 4 hours of symptom onset in all cases. Mortality and functional outcome were assessed at 6 months. This group of patients was compared with patients treated within 12 hours of symptom onset using the same surgical and medical protocols. Results: The study was stopped after a planned interim analysis of 11 patients in the 4-hour surgery arm. Median time to surgery was 180 minutes; median hematoma volume was $40\mathrm{mL}$ ; median baseline NIH Stroke Scale score was 19 and Glasgow Coma Scale score was 12. Six-month mortality was $36\%$ and median Barthel score was 75 in survivors. Postoperative rebleeding occurred in four patients, three of whom died. A relationship between postoperative rebleeding and mortality was apparent $(p=0.03)$ . Rebleeding occurred in $40\%$ of the patients treated within 4 hours, compared with $12\%$ of the patients treated within 12 hours $(p=0.11)$ . There was a clear correlation between improved outcome and smaller postsurgical hematoma volume $(p~=~0.04)$ . Conclusions: Surgical hematoma evacuation within 4 hours of symptom onset is complicated by rebleeding, indicating difficulty with hemostasis. Maximum removal of blood remains a predictor of good outcome.  

NEUROLOGY 2001;56:1294–1299  

Case fatality rates for intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) approach $50\%$ ,1 and deaths from ICH comprise $11\%$ of stroke deaths, with a high predilection for racial and ethnic minorities.2 Acute surgical evacuation of spontaneous supratentorial ICH remains controversial.3 A randomized pilot study of craniotomy and hematoma extraction vs best medical therapy within 12 hours of symptom onset demonstrated a modest mortality benefit for surgery.4 We hypothesized that even earlier clot evacuation may prevent the local edema formation and inflammation seen in animal models.5,6 We also believed early surgery would forestall deleterious consequences of ICH, such as early hematoma growth. In one study,7 deterioration due to hematoma expansion was found within the first 20 hours in $38\%$ of medically treated patients with ICH. To test the feasibility and safety of ultra-early surgical clot evacuation in patients with ICH we performed a single-center open-label pilot study of surgery within 4 hours of symptom onset as a comparison with the previous 12-hour trial.4  

Methods. The methods of the Surgical Treatment for Intracerebral Hemorrhage (STICH) single-center trial have been reported elsewhere.4 The current study used the same protocol, except that the surgical group was treated within 4 hours of symptom onset instead of 12 hours. All patients in the current report were treated with surgery. Comparison is made to the medical and surgical treatment groups reported previously.4 The STICH protocol was undertaken at Hermann Hospital in Houston, TX. Hermann Hospital is the academic teaching hospital for the University of Texas, Houston, and serves as a referral center for acute stroke cases in southeast Texas. This study was approved by the University of Texas, Houston Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects. Patients or their next of kin were required to sign a written informed consent prior to participation in this study.  

Between March 1997 and May 1999, all patients meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria were offered participation in the study. Patients were included with spontaneous, supratentorial IC $\mathrm{;H>}20\mathrm{mL}$ (lobar or deep extending outside the thalamus) identified by noncontrast CT. Volume was estimated by the $({\bfA}\times{\bfB}\times{\bfC})/2$ method.8 Surgery commenced within 4 hours of the time that the patient was last seen without symptoms in each case. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score had to be ${>}4$ . Patients were excluded for 1) known or suspected cerebral aneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, brain tumor or head trauma; 2) low level of function prior to the current event (Rankin score ${>}2$ ); 3) coagulopathy (prothrombin time ${>}15$ seconds, elevated partial thromboplastin time, platelet count $<100,000$ per $\mathrm{mm^{3}}$ , current anticoagulant use, or thrombolytic use within 24 hours); 4) serious concomitant medical condition that would significantly increase medical risk or preclude 6-month survival; and 5) ventricular extension greater than one half of one lateral ventricle or one third of both lateral ventricles.

A standard frontal craniotomy was performed in all cases and a cortisectiomy was made in the middle frontal gyrus. For deep hemorrhages, a transcortical approach was made to the basal ganglia using the operating microscope, and the clot gently evacuated using suction and irrigation. Any blood that had extended into the ventricle was not disturbed. Hemostasis was attempted with bipolar coagulation, and the hematoma cavity was lined with Gelfoam (Pharmacia and Upjohn, Peapack, NJ) or Surgicel (Johnson $^+$ Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) depending on the surgeon’s preference. Thrombolytic agents were not employed.  

Patients with GCS scores ${\leq}9$ were intubated in the emergency department and taken to the operating room. Mannitol was given, 50 to $_{100~\mathrm{~g~}}$ , and hyperventilation instituted. Severe hypertension was controlled in the operating room by reducing mean arterial pressure (MAP) $10\%$ to $20\%$ for a target MAP of 100 to 120. Abrupt decrements in blood pressure were avoided. Postoperatively, hyperventilation was reversed slowly over 24 to 36 hours. Blood pressure was also allowed to slowly rise to higher levels, appropriate for the individual patient, after the initial 24 to 36 hours. Intracranial pressure (ICP) monitors were used in patients with a GCS score ${<}9$ , and patients were treated with a standard ICP protocol to maintain ICP $<21$ cm $_\mathrm{H_{2}O}$ . In all cases, MAP and ICP were controlled following surgery to provide maximum time for surgical hemostasis to ensue. IV steroids were not used. Postoperatively, the patients were cared for by the University of Texas, Houston, stroke team in a neurologic intensive care unit and later in a dedicated stroke unit. These patients were cared for by same group of physicians who had treated the patients who underwent the original 12-hour trial.  

Data were prospectively obtained on demographic variables, ICH location and volume, time to surgery, admission GCS score, blood pressure, NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, 6-month mortality, and Rankin and Barthel scores. Target enrollment was planned for 20 patients as a pilot comparison with the patients randomized to surgery within 12 hours $({\bfn}=17\$ ) or medical therapy $({\bfn}=17\$ ) in the STICH trial. Head CT scans were performed on all patients within 24 hours after surgery. Rebleeding was identified when the postoperative CT volume was either greater than the preoperative volume or there was a $<5$ mL difference in the pre- and postoperative CT blood volume measurements. We planned an interim safety analysis midway through 20 patients. Because we had previously demonstrated a mortality rate of $29\%$ in medically treated patients and $18\%$ in 12-hour surgery patients,4 we planned to terminate the trial if mortality in the patients undergoing surgery within 4 hours exceeded $30\%$ at the interim analysis.  

Because the sample sizes were small, descriptive statistics reported include medians, minimums, and maximums of continuous variables and proportions for binomial and nominal variables. Proportions between groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test and distributions of continuous variables between groups are compared using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney shift test for two-group comparisons and its generalization, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for the three-group comparisons.9 Significance levels reported for two-group tests are two-sided exact $p$ values. Spearman’s rank order correlation was used to estimate the association between modified Rankin ordinal scores at 6 months and postsurgical residual volumes using upper one-sided significance tests. One-sided significance levels for rank correlations are reported, because we assumed that as residual volumes increase, Rankin scores will also increase. Computations of significance levels were done using StatXact 4 for Windows, Version 4.0.1 (CYTEL Software, Cambridge, MA).10

Four patients had rebleeding, three of whom died. Those who bled had a median time to surgery of 172 minutes, whereas those who did not bleed had a median time to surgery of 182 minutes $(p=0.61)\$ ).

Discussion. This pilot study found significant safety concerns with craniotomy performed within 4 hours of spontaneous supratentorial ICH. In contrast to the 12-hour surgical protocol, rebleeding with ultra-early evacuation may be associated with increased mortality. In a nonrandomized report of putamenal clot evacuation in 100 patients within 7 hours of symptom onset, Kaneko et al.11 reported only $7\%$ mortality and complete recovery or only minor disability in $50\%$ . These authors point out the critical need to identify the bleeding lenticulostriate branch and coagulate it to achieve hemostasis. This vessel may be only 100 to $200~\upmu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter. Surrounding arterioles and venules are also subject to bleeding and must be assiduously addressed. The authors warn that if hemostasis is not achieved or if damage to the lenticulostriate branch occurs rebleeding may lead to poor outcome.12 Although topical thrombogenic substances such as thrombin are frequently employed for systemic trauma, their use inside the brain has not been studied. It seems that the critical window for rebleeding is the first few hours after symptom onset, because rebleeding was rare in our patients who underwent surgery within 12 hours. This was confirmed by investigators who demonstrated that the most critical time for rebleeding is the first few hours following initial ICH.7,13 We had hoped that ultra-early surgery might have prevented this early hematoma expansion by coagulating the bleeding vessel. Instead, it appears that it is difficult to achieve hemostasis in the initial 4-hour period after symptom onset, even with a direct therapeutic intervention.

More aggressive lowering of blood pressure needs to be examined as a means to lessen rebleeding in patients with very high perioperative MAP. Very high blood pressure on the first day has been identified as a poor prognostic sign.14 Imaging techniques such as CT with contrast may also provide an important clue to the ICH group with ongoing bleeding and help guide therapy.15 Such imaging techniques should be considered in future studies to identify the group with a high risk for rebleeding.  

This study demonstrated the feasibility of ultraurgent triage and surgery for patients with ICH. With aggressive education of emergency department, surgical, and operating room personnel, these patients were cared for with the same expediency as candidates for thrombolytic therapy for acute ischemic stroke. Although the time course of permanent damage resulting from ICH remains unknown, it is clear that early cerebral edema and increased intracranial pressures lead to deterioration and herniation in a sizeable percentage of patients.1 Efforts to remove the toxic effects of blood products and reduce pressure may be most effective at a critically early time.  

Although the patients treated surgically within 4 and 12 hours did not have better outcome than medically treated patients, patients with the smallest postoperative hemorrhage volumes had the best outcomes. This finding is similar to data from studies that found that volume is the best predictor of ICH outcome16 and argues that removal of hemorrhage may ultimately be beneficial. The best strategy to remove the hemorrhage is uncertain. Six randomized, controlled trials (including a total of 414 patients) have examined surgical benefit for ICH.4,17-21 No clear benefit was established for surgery in any of the studies, or in meta-analyses.22,23  

Other less-invasive surgical procedures may avoid the complications of craniotomy and still allow removal of the hematoma. In one series of CT-guided stereotactic surgery for ICH, the rebleeding occurred in only 6/158 patients, possibly due to the late completion of the hematoma evacuations ( $76\%$ were done more than 3 days after symptom onset).24 One randomized trial did demonstrate a modest benefit for ultrasound-guided endoscopic hematoma evacuations of small to moderate size hematomas.20 Endoscopic surgery is more invasive than a stereotactic approach and can potentially cause more complications. The theoretical problem with stereotactic or endoscopic surgery for ICH is that it takes a long time to completely evacuate the clot. As figure 2 demonstrates, the more blood that is removed and the less that remains postoperatively, the better the outcome. Whether it is critical to remove this blood early and completely, as can be achieved with craniotomy, or whether it is sufficient to extract the blood more slowly with stereotactic surgery, has yet to be determined. The adjunctive application of direct thrombolytic agents into the hematoma cavity may improve stereotactic hematoma evacuation without compromising safety.25 Other researchers have focused on the critical role of intraventricular hemorrhage in poor outcome from intracranial bleeds. Ventricular drainage with concomitant thrombolytic use was recently suggested to improve survival.26

The limitations of this report include the small sample size. Single-center studies rely on the methods of a limited group of surgeons and may not be easily generalized. We compared the patients who underwent surgery within 4 hours as a third arm with the patients randomly assigned to surgery within 12 hours and those given medical treatment. Although the protocol was the same, the enrollment was not contemporaneous or randomized, and differences in personnel and medical and surgical technique may have influenced our results despite our best efforts. We attempted to standardize our protocol for blood pressure and ICP management, but individual variations may provide some explanation for rebleeding. Following surgery, an elevated blood pressure or reversing hyperventilation could result in arterial bleeding, through increased hydrostatic pressure and augmented cerebral blood flow, respectively. This trial was stopped for qualitative safety concerns. Formal stopping rules were not employed. The trial may have been stopped prematurely.  

Physicians employing craniotomy for hematoma evacuation within the first few hours after ICH symptom onset should use extreme caution due to the risk of rebleeding. Early surgery for ICH as single therapy cannot be recommended based on the currently available data. A rational future strategy for improving outcome of ICH may still include early clot evacuation. However, this will need to be combined with careful attention to hemostasis. Perhaps smaller clots are best treated with stereotactic aspiration, whereas large clots may require open craniotomy. Clot evacuation might be coupled with pharmacotherapy targeting the inflammatory response shown to develop around the hematoma and leading to delayed cellular death in experimental animal models6,27 Further research is needed to investigate possible combination therapies to limit the impact of this devastating disease.

# Fever in subarachnoid hemorrhage Relationship to vasospasm and outcome

Article abstract—Objective: To investigate the causes of fever in subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) and examine its relationship to outcome. Background: Fever adversely affects outcome in stroke. Patients with SAH are at risk for cerebral ischemia due to vasospasm (VSP). In these patients, fever may be both caused by, and potentiate, VSP-mediated brain injury. Methods: The authors prospectively studied patients admitted to a neurologic intensive care unit with nontraumatic SAH, documenting Hunt–Hess grade, Fisher group, Glasgow Coma Score, bacterial culture data, daily transcranial Doppler mean velocities, and maximum daily temperatures. Patients were classified as febrile (temperature above $38.3~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for at least 2 consecutive days) or afebrile (no fever or isolated episodes of temperature above $38.3^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ ). VSP was verified by either transcranial Doppler or angiographic criteria. Rankin scale scores on discharge were dichotomized into good (0 to 2) or poor (3 to 6) outcomes. Results: Ninety-two consecutive patients were studied. Thirty-eight patients were classified as febrile. No source for infection was found in 10 of 38 $26\%$ patients. In a multivariate analysis, three variables independently predicted fever occurrence: ventriculostomy (OR, 8.5 [CI, 2.4 to 29.7]), symptomatic VSP (OR, 5.0 [CI, 1.03 to 24.5]), and older age (OR, 1.75 per 10 years [CI, 1.02 to 3.0]). Poor outcome was related to fever (OR, 1.4 per each day febrile [CI, 1.1 to 1.88]), older age (OR, 1.64 per 10 years [CI, 1.04 to 2.58]), and intubation (OR, 21.8 [CI, 5.6 to 84.5]). Conclusion: Fever in SAH is associated with vasospasm and poor outcome independently of hemorrhage severity or presence of infection.

Cerebral vasospasm (VSP) is a cause of morbidity and mortality in subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH).1 Critically ill patients with SAH commonly have fever, a factor known to worsen neurologic injury during ischemia.2 Experimentally, fever potentiates ischemic damage,3 whereas induced hypothermia by itself has been suggested as a potent neuroprotective strategy.4-7 In patients with ischemic stroke, high temperatures are associated with larger infarct size, increased mortality, and poor outcome.8 Hypothermia has shown promise as a therapeutic strategy in patients with traumatic brain injury or stroke.9,10  

Fever in patients with SAH may have various causes. Nosocomial infection is always the first consideration. Patients with decreased level of arousal are especially prone to infectious complications such as aspiration pneumonia. However, patients with SAH also may develop fever of noninfectious origin. Blood in the CSF induces fever in experimental models.11 Vasospasm causes brain injury that is potentially associated with central fever.
