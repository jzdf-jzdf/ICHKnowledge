# Intracranial pressure monitoring in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage  

Ching-Jen Chen, MD,1 Dale Ding, MD,2 Natasha Ironside, MBChB,3 Thomas J. Buell, MD,1 Andrew M. Southerland, MD, MSc,4,5 Fernando D. Testai, MD, PhD,6 Daniel Woo, MD,7 and Bradford B. Worrall, MD, MSc,4,5 for the ERICH Investigators  

Departments of 1Neurological Surgery, 4Neurology, and 5Public Health Sciences, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, Virginia; 2Department of Neurosurgery, University of Louisville, Louisville, Kentucky; 3Department of Neurosurgery, NewYork-Presbyterian/ Columbia University Medical Center, New York, New York; 6Department of Neurology, University of Illinois, Chicago, Illinois; and 7Department of Neurology and Rehabilitation Medicine, University of Cincinnati, Cincinnati, Ohio  

OBJECTIVE  The utility of ICP monitoring and its benefit with respect to outcomes after ICH is unknown. The aim of this study was to compare intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) outcomes in patients who underwent intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring to those who were managed by care-guided imaging and/or clinical examination alone.  

METHODS  This was a retrospective analysis of data from the Ethnic/Racial variations of Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ERICH) study between 2010 and 2015. ICH patients who underwent ICP monitoring were propensity-score matched, in a 1:1 ratio, to those who did not undergo ICP monitoring. The primary outcome was 90-day mortality. Secondary outcomes were in-hospital mortality, hyperosmolar therapy use, ICH evacuation, length of hospital stay, and 90-day modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, excellent outcome (mRS score 0–1), good outcome (mRS score 0–2), Barthel Index, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL; measured by EQ-5D and EQ-5D visual analog scale [VAS] scores). A secondary analysis for patients without intraventricular hemorrhage was performed.  

RESULTS  The ICP and no ICP monitoring cohorts comprised 566 and 2434 patients, respectively. The matched cohorts comprised 420 patients each. The 90-day and in-hospital mortality rates were similar between the matched cohorts. Shift analysis of 90-day mRS favored no ICP monitoring $(\mathsf{p}<0.001)$ ). The rates of excellent $(\mathsf{p}<0.001)$ and good $(\mathsf{p}<0.001)$ outcome, Barthel Index $(\mathsf{p}<0.001)$ , EQ-5D score $(\mathsf{p}=0.026)$ , and EQ-5D VAS score $(\mathsf{p}=0.004)$ at 90 days were lower in the matched ICP monitoring cohort. Rates of mannitol use $(\mathsf{p}<0.001)$ , hypertonic saline use $(\mathsf{p}<0.001)$ , ICH evacuation $(\mathsf{p}<0.001)$ , and infection $(\mathsf{p}=0.001)$ were higher, and length of hospital stay $(\mathsf{p}<0.001)$ was longer in the matched ICP monitoring cohort. In the secondary analysis, the matched cohorts comprised 111 patients each. ICP monitoring had a lower rate of 90-day mortality $(\mathsf{p}=0.041)$ ). Shift analysis of 90-day mRS, Barthel Index, and HRQoL metrics were comparable between the matched cohorts.

# Methods  

# Patient Identification, Ethical Approval of the Study, and Informed Consent  

This study was approved by the institutional review board at each participating site, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient (or a legally authorized representative when a patient was medically unfit to provide consent) enrolled in the study. De-identified patient data from each respective site were pooled for analysis. This study follows the guidelines set forth by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.  

# Data Sources and ICP Monitoring  

The Ethnic/Racial variations of Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ERICH) study methods have been previously described in detail.22 Briefly, the ERICH study is a multicenter, prospective, case-control study designed to recruit 1000 non-Hispanic whites, 1000 non-Hispanic blacks, and 1000 Hispanics with spontaneous ICH. Matched ICH-free controls were recruited, via random digit dialing, for the identification of genetic and epidemiological risk factors associated with ICH and its outcomes. Participants were derived from 19 United States sites comprising 42 hospitals. All participants or designated proxies underwent a standardized data collection protocol, including a personal interview and medical chart abstraction. ICH was defined as a spontaneous, nontraumatic, abrupt onset of severe headache, altered level of consciousness, or focal neurological deficit that was associated with a focal collection of blood within the brain parenchyma, as evident on neuroimaging.22 Warfarin-associated ICH and peripartum ICH were included in the definition. Venous sinus thrombosis-associated hemorrhages, hemorrhagic conversions of ischemic cerebral infarcts, and hemorrhages attributable to vascular malformations, aneurysms, or tumors were excluded. Patient eligibility for inclusion in the ERICH study was reviewed by the study neurologists based on clinical presentation and neuroimaging.  

All ICH patients with available data regarding the use of ICP monitoring were included in this study. Patients were categorized into the no ICP monitoring cohort if invasive ICP monitoring was not performed during the entirety of the acute hospitalization, and these patients were managed based on care-guided imaging and/or clinical examination. Patients were categorized into the ICP monitoring cohort if invasive ICP monitoring was performed at any point during the acute hospitalization, and these patients were managed with the aid of ICP data that were available to the treating physicians. Decisions regarding the use of ICP monitoring were made at the discretion of the treating physicians.  

# Outcomes  

The primary outcome was mortality at 90 days. The secondary outcomes were in-hospital mortality, central nervous system (CNS) infection, excellent outcome (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score 0–1) at 90 days, good outcome (mRS score 0–2) at 90 days, mRS at 90 days, Barthel Index (on a scale of 0 to 100; higher scores indicate less disability) at 90 days, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) at 90 days, as measured by the EQ-5D (EuroQol Group; on a scale of $-0.11$ to 1; higher values indicate better HRQoL), and EQ-5D visual analog scale (EQ-5D VAS; on a scale of 0–100; higher values indicate better HRQoL) self-report questionnaires, mannitol use, hypertonic saline use, surgical ICH evacuation (including craniotomy, decompressive craniectomy, or any minimally invasive procedure for evacuation of hematoma), and length of hospital stay.6,13,15  

# Statistical Analysis  

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 14.2, StataCorp LLC). Patients were dichotomized into 2 cohorts based on the use of ICP monitoring (no ICP monitoring vs ICP monitoring). Baseline demographics, clinical, and radiographic characteristics were compared between the 2 cohorts. The Student t-test or Wilcoxon ranksum test was used to compare continuous variables, where appropriate. A Pearson chi-square or Fisher exact test was used to compare categorical variables, where appropriate.  

To adjust for baseline differences, the 2 cohorts were matched, without replacement, in a 1:1 ratio with a caliper of 0.2 using propensity scores derived from baseline characteristics comparisons with $\mathrm{p}<0.10$ . The matching was performed using the PSMATCH2 package developed for Stata. Univariable binary logistic, ordered logistic, linear, and Poisson regression analyses were performed on both the unmatched and matched cohorts to assess the relationships between ICP monitoring and the primary and secondary outcomes. The findings from the binary logistic, ordered logistic, linear, and Poisson regression analyses were then adjusted for covariates of the matched cohorts with $\mathrm{p}<0.10\$ . A secondary analysis of ICH patients without intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) was performed. Patients with IVH were excluded from this secondary analysis, and the ICP monitoring and no ICP monitoring cohorts were matched, without replacement, in a 1:1 ratio with a caliper of 0.2 using propensity scores derived from baseline characteristics comparisons with $\mathrm{p}<0.10$ . Statistical significance was defined as $\mathrm{p}<0.0\dot{5}$ , and all tests were 2-tailed. Missing data were not imputed.

# Discussion  

The onset of spontaneous ICH can raise a patient’s ICP; the extent of the increase depends on a multitude of factors, including the degree of pre-existing brain atrophy, presence of encephalomalacia from prior strokes, and hematoma volume and location. A study of 57 ICH patients who underwent ICP monitoring reported intracranial hypertension $(\mathrm{ICP}{>}20\mathrm{mmHg})$ in $70\%$ , although it was less frequent in older patients and those with infratentorial ICH.10 However, the same study did not find an association between intracranial hypertension and post-ICH outcomes at 12 months. In contrast, ICP elevation $\mathrm{(ICP>20~mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg})$ was found in only $14\%$ of ICP recordings $(\mathtt{n}=472)$ ) among 11 patients with obstructive hydrocephalus secondary to IVH and ICH volume $\leq30~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ who underwent ICP monitoring with external ventricular catheters.23 The inconsistent rates of ICP elevation in ICH patients may be a product of differential pressure gradients that have been hypothesized to exist between the vicinity of a hematoma and a distant location.3 As such, ICP measured in ICH patients may not accurately represent the loco-regional mass effect caused by the hematoma and perilesional edema, and evidence to support clinical decision-making based on the measured ICP is lacking.7,18,19  

Current management strategies for elevated ICP in ICH patients are derived from TBI guidelines.2,9 However, in the absence of published data supporting the benefit of invasive ICP monitoring in the management of ICH, the role of this intervention remains unknown. Herein, we present the first study to compare the outcomes of ICH in patients who underwent ICP monitoring to outcomes in those who were managed based only on care-guided imaging and/ or clinical examination. Based on our matched analysis of a large, multicenter, multiethnic cohort, we found that ICP monitoring was not associated with reduced mortality rates in ICH patients at 90 days or during acute hospitalization. Furthermore, functional outcomes and HRQoL metrics at 90 days appeared to be worse in ICH patients who were managed with ICP monitoring. The lack of effect of ICP monitoring on survival after ICH should not be considered surprising, since a randomized controlled trial comparing ICP monitoring to treatment based on imaging and clinical examination in patients with severe TBI similarly failed to show a benefit from ICP monitoring.4 However, we are unable to ascertain the reasons for the poorer functional outcomes and HRQoL in the ICP monitoring cohort. We cannot determine the contribution of disparities in baseline characteristics and neurological presentation between patients who underwent ICP monitoring and those who were managed without ICP monitoring to the observed differences in functional outcome and HRQoL. Although poorer function outcomes may be attributable to higher rates of CNS infection in the ICP monitoring cohort, other factors, such as surgical ICH evacuation, may contribute to the higher infection rates in the ICP monitoring cohort. Provided that our matching process sufficiently accounted for the baseline differences between the ICP monitoring and no ICP monitoring cohorts, treatment factors related to the presence or absence of ICP data could have influenced the interim follow-up outcomes. Specifically, patients who underwent ICP monitoring may have been managed in such a way (e.g., increased administration of sedatives, prolonged intubation) that blunted neurological and clinical recovery in the post-ICH period.

Although surgical intervention and medical therapies for ICH have not yet been proven to result in better clinical outcomes to date, patients in the ICP monitoring cohort were more likely to be administered hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol or hypertonic saline) and undergo surgical ICH evacuation.8,11,14,17 The length of hospital stay was also longer in patients who underwent ICP monitoring. Although the costs of acute hospitalization were not calculated in the ERICH study, taken together, these findings suggest that the use of ICP monitoring may increase the economic burden of ICH care without delivering a measurable benefit to patient outcomes. Additionally, it appears that both the medical and surgical management of patients in the ICP monitoring cohort were more aggressive, although we are unable to determine from the present analysis whether the similar mortality rates and worse functional outcomes and HRQoL metrics of these patients are attributable to shortcomings of hyperosmolar therapy and surgical treatment for ICH.8,14,17,21  

The secondary analysis of ICH patients without IVH who were managed with versus without ICP monitoring found a lower rate of 90-day mortality in the matchedcohort analysis. However, ICP monitoring was also associated with a lower likelihood of functional independence at 90 days. In addition, in-hospital mortality, functional outcomes, and HRQoL metrics were comparable between the matched cohorts. ICH patients with IVH may require external ventricular drain placement to relieve acute hydrocephalus. Since we are unable to account for differences in the need for ventriculostomy in the overall cohort of ICH patients, we performed a subgroup analysis of those without IVH to address this confounding factor. We found that while ICP monitoring may be associated with reduced mortality among ICH patients without IVH, survivors who underwent ICP monitoring did not appear to have better functional outcomes. Therefore, we did not find sufficient evidence to support the routine use of ICP monitoring in patients with ICH.  

We acknowledge that our study has several limitations that may affect the generalizability of our findings. Because the ERICH study was not specifically designed to evaluate outcomes associated with ICP monitoring, details pertaining to this procedure (i.e., type of ICP monitor used, location of device placement relative to the hematoma, timing and duration of monitoring, ICP values and waveforms, cerebral perfusion pressure measurements) were not captured. ICP values captured by parenchymal monitors may differ from those measured by ventriculostomy catheters, and these discrepancies may result in management variability across different institutions and clinicians. Consequently, the lack of standardized protocol for ICP monitoring (i.e., parenchymal monitor vs ventriculostomy, monitor placement ipsilateral vs contralateral to the hematoma) among ICH patients may have resulted in site-specific under- or over-treatment of ICH patients. Additionally, complications related to ICP monitoring, including hemorrhage, were not recorded. The decision to perform ICP monitoring was made at the discretion of the treating physician and institution, and thus, our results may have been influenced by selection, treatment, and referral biases. Despite propensity-score matching, there were other unmeasured severity variables which may influence patient selection for ICP monitoring, including but not limited to clinical herniation, midline shift, and perihematomal edema, which may be worse in the ICP monitoring cohort. This may be indicated by the higher rates of ICH evacuation and longer lengths of hospital stay in the matched ICP monitoring cohort, despite greater use of hyperosmolar therapy in this cohort. Although the presence of hydrocephalus can be associated with elevated ICP, the definition of hydrocephalus-induced intracranial hypertension is ambiguous, and the threshold for CSF drainage is clinician- and institutiondependent. The aforementioned data were not captured in the ERICH database. Furthermore, the presence of IVH may not be associated with hydrocephalus or elevated ICP. Therefore, we attempted to balance the presence of IVH, and possibly acute hydrocephalus, in our matched primary analysis, and we subsequently removed patients with IVH from the secondary analyses. Since ventriculostomy may be used for either ICP monitoring or CSF drainage, the findings of the study should not be interpreted as a suggestion to avoid ventriculostomy for the purpose of CSF drainage in ICH patients presenting with acute hydrocephalus. Although comfort care rates were comparable between the cohorts, patients who were destined to have worse outcomes may not have been selected to undergo ICP monitoring. Although recommendations for ICP management in ICH patients are outlined in the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines, differences in the implementation of treatment strategies and in the aggressiveness of controlling intracranial hypertension among physicians and institutions exist. Case-specific indications for the use of hyperosmolar therapy and referral to neurosurgery for ICH evacuation could not be determined from the available data. Therefore, details regarding how ICP measurements influenced intervention could not be inferred. Furthermore, approaches to the management of ICH patients who did not undergo ICP monitoring may also vary among physicians and institutions, and were assumed to follow guidelines set forth by the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association.9  

# Conclusions  

Routine ICP monitoring in patients with spontaneous  

ICH does not appear to confer a benefit with respect to mortality or functional outcomes, after controlling for baseline severity variables associated with monitoring. Although reduced mortality was observed in the subgroup of ICH patients without IVH who underwent ICP monitoring, these patients did not have improved functional outcomes. Our findings also suggest that ICP monitoring is associated with increased use of hyperosmolar therapy, increased rate of surgical ICH evacuation, and prolonged hospital stay. Therefore, this study did not provide evidence to support the routine utilization of ICP monitoring in ICH patients.
