# Effect of desmopressin on hematoma expansion in antiplatelet-associated intracerebral hemorrhage: A systematic review and meta-analysis

# abstract  

The purpose of this study was to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis on the effect of desmopressin on hematoma expansion (HE) in antiplatelet-associated intracerebral hemorrhage (AA-ICH). Secondary outcomes examined were the rate of thrombotic complications and neurologic outcome.  

Three databases were searched (Pubmed, Scopus, and Cochrane) for randomized clinical trials and controlled studies comparing desmopressin versus controls in adult patients with AA-ICH. The MantelHaenszel method was applied to calculate an overall effect estimate for each outcome by combining stratum-specific risk ratio (RR). Risk of bias was computed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (42020190234).  

Three retrospective controlled studies involving 263 patients were included in the meta-analysis. Compared to controls, desmopressin was associated with a non-significant reduction in HE $19.1\%$ vs. $30\%$ ; RR:0.61; $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ , 0.27–1.39; $\ensuremath{\mathrm{~P~}}=0.24$ ), a similar rate of thrombotic events $5.5\%$ vs. $9.9\%$ ; RR:0.47; $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ , 0.17–1.31; $\mathrm{~P~}=~0.15$ , and significantly worse neurologic outcome $\mathrm{'mRS}\geq4\$ $66.3\%$ vs. $50\%$ ; RR:1.36; $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ , 1.08–1.7; $\mathrm{P}=0.008$ ). Qualitative analysis of included studies for each outcome revealed low to moderate risk of bias.  

The available literature does not support the routine use of desmopressin in the setting of AA-ICH. Until larger prospective trials are performed, the administration of desmopressin should be judiciously considered on a case-by-case basis.

# 1. Introduction  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is the most devastating type of stroke [1,2]. Patients on antiplatelet medications have an increased risk of ICH, and antiplatelet-associated ICH (AA-ICH) carries higher mortality rate [3]. The association between AA-ICH and worse outcome is uncertain, but to a certain degree mediated by hematoma expansion (HE) [4]. Studies on platelet transfusion (PT) in AA-ICH failed to demonstrate any beneficial effect of PT compared with standard of care [5]. Desmopressin (DDAVP) is a peptide that increases the endothelial release of Von Willebrand factor, enhances platelet adhesion and reduces bleeding time [6–8]. Recent uncontrolled reports in AA-ICH have shown an improvement in platelet function in ICH after administration of DDAVP [9,10]. Under these premises, the current guidelines from the Neurocritical Care Society suggest considering a single dose of DDAVP $(0.4\mathrm{mcg/kg})$ in AA-ICH [11]. The scarcity of controlled studies on the topic deserves a more thorough investigation to update the current evidence. Therefore, the aim of this study is to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis on the effect of DDAVP on HE in ICH. Secondary end-points examined are the rate of thrombotic events, and neurologic outcome.  

# 2. Methods  

# 2.1. Protocol  

This systematic review was registered via PROSPERO [12] (registration number 42020190234) and conducted and presented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement [13]. Ethical approval was not required because data was synthetized from previously published studies.  

# 2.2. Study design  

This is a systematic review and meta-analysis aimed at assessing the effect of DDAVP on hematoma expansion in AA-ICH. Additional outcomes were 1) the effect of DDAVP on thrombotic events and 2) the association between administration of DDAVP and neurologic outcome.  

# 2.3. Search strategy  

Three major databases (Pubmed, Scopus, and Cochrane) were searched from inception to 18 May 2020, without linguistic or geographical limitation using the following keywords ‘‘intracerebral hemorrhage” or ‘‘intraparenchymal hemorrhage” or ‘‘ICH” in conjunction with ‘‘desmopressin” or ‘‘DDAVP”. The search strategy is available in supplementary material. Reference lists were searched manually for additional sources.  

# 2.4. Eligibility criteria  

All articles identified from the literature search were screened by two authors (AL and FEA), independently, as per the following inclusion criteria: 1) adult population with spontaneous ICH and prior to admission documented use of antiplatelet therapy, 2) interventions comparing DDAVP to controls, and 3) clinical outcome focused on a) hematoma expansion b) thrombotic events c) neurological outcome. Thrombotic events included deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarct, and ischemic stroke. Randomized clinical trials and controlled observational cohort studies were included in the review. Detailed information on participants, interventions, comparisons, outcomes and types of studies (PICOS) are provided in Table 1. The relevance of the studies was assessed using a hierarchical approach based on title, abstract, and full manuscript.  

# 2.5. Assessment of study quality  

Risk of bias was computed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [14] by two authors (AL and FEA), independently. Disagreement between quality of the study was resolved by consensus.  

# 2.6. Data extraction  

For each included study, the following variables were extracted independently by two authors (AL and FEA): lead author, publication date, study design, number of participants for each arm, DDAVP dose, rate of hematoma expansion in the two groups, rate of thrombotic events in the two groups, and neurological outcome in the two groups. If any of the above variables were not available in the full text publication, further information was sought by correspondence with the lead author of the study.  

# 2.7. Statistical analysis  

The Mantel-Haenszel method was applied to calculate an overall, unconfounded, effect estimate of treatment with DDAVP for each specific outcome by combining stratum-specific risk ratio (RR). Stratum-specific RRs were calculated within each stratum of the confounding variable and compared with the corresponding random effect estimates in the whole group. A Z test was carried out to assess the significance of the RR. The $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ was calculated by $\boldsymbol{\chi2}$ test to assess variability due to heterogeneity rather than chance. A substantial heterogeneity was assumed with $\mathrm{I}^{2}>50\%$ $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) for hematoma expansion, thrombotic events, and neurologic outcome was calculated with the Wilson method and placed in forest plots. A p value $<0.05$ was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.3 [15].  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Study selection  

A total of 186 studies were identified through database searching, including Pubmed $(\mathtt{n}=80)$ ) Scopus ${\mathrm{~(n~=~}95\mathrm{~'~}}$ ), and Cochrane ${\mathrm{~(n~=~11)~}}$ . After removing 31 duplicates, the remaining studies $\prime\mathrm{n}=155\$ were screened for eligibility. A total of 141 records were excluded by title $\left(\mathtt{n}=97\right.$ ) and by abstract $[\mathrm{n}=44\AA]$ ). As a result, 14 studies were assessed with full-text review. Among those, 9 articles were excluded due to protocol only $(\mathtt{n}=3)$ [16–18], performance of a review only $\left({\mathfrak{n}}=3\right)$ [11,19,20], pilot study ${\mathrm{\Omega}}({\mathrm{n}}={\mathrm{1}})$ [21], not desired population $(\mathtt{n}=1$ ) [21] and lack of control group $(\mathtt{n}=1)$ [9]. Finally, this meta-analysis included 3 articles that met the inclusion criteria. The detailed PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1.  

# 3.2. Study characteristics  

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in table 2. No randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were identified; all studies were single center retrospective cohort analyses $[22-$ 24]. The dose of DDAVP ranged from 0.3 to $0.4~{\upmu}{\mathrm{g/kg}}$ . The rate of hematoma expansion was evaluated in all the studies [22–24]. Hematoma expansion was defined as an increase of $33\%$ from baseline hematoma volume in one study [23], absolute increase of $3\mathrm{mL}$ from baseline hematoma volume in one study [24], and increase of $33\%$ from baseline hematoma volume or absolute increase of $6~\mathrm{mL}$ in one study [22]. Thrombotic events were evaluated in two studies [23,24]. Mengel et al. included deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarct [23]. Feldman et al. included thromboembolic events, myocardial infarct, and ischemic stroke [24]. Neurologic outcome was evaluated in two studies [22,23]. Both studies defined unfavorable outcome as $\mathrm{mRS}\geq4$ , one at discharge [22] and one at three months [23]. In the study of Feldman et al., the corresponding author was contacted and data pertaining to spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage $\textstyle({\mathtt{n}}=51$ ) was obtained [24]. Attempts were also made to contact the corresponding authors of other included studies, as needed, to obtain additional data pertinent to the outcomes of this review.

# 4. Discussion  

# 4.1. Summary of evidence  

This meta-analysis shows that in patients with AA-ICH, the administration of DDAVP is associated with a non-significant trend for limiting hematoma expansion, does not increase the rate of thrombotic complications, and may be associated with worse neurologic outcome.  

Expansion of initial hematoma represents a potentially modifiable marker of poor prognosis in ICH. Albeit several risk factors have been identified, including baseline ICH volume, use of anticoagulants, shorter time to CT scan, and spot sign on CT angiography, specific therapeutic strategies to prevent HE remain elusive [25]. Intensive blood pressure reduction has demonstrated questionable effect on HE [26,27]. The use of antiplatelet therapy has been proven to be associated with HE, and worse outcome [3,4]. However, reversal of antiplatelet therapy in AA-ICH remains of uncertain benefit [11].  

In the present review, the pooled data of available controlled cohort studies demonstrated no beneficial effect of DDAVP on HE. There is no consensus in clinical practice about the reversal of antiplatelet agents in patients presenting with spontaneous ICH. The use of desmopressin in AA-ICH has attracted attention recently because of its beneficial mechanism of action on platelet function, readily availability, affordability, and relative safe drug profile when tested in systemic major bleeding [28]. Some anecdotal and clinical evidence has suggested a possible efficacy in improving platelet activity after AA-ICH [9]. However, its translation to clinical practice remains uncertain. The present metaanalysis failed to demonstrate a beneficial effect of DDAVP on preventing HE. Nevertheless, this evidence is based on retrospective cohort studies, while large randomized trials are lacking. Furthermore, a subgroup analysis relative to the type of agent reversed or the severity of ICH could not be performed due to the paucity of studies and lack of available data. Given the above considerations, our results don’t indicate a clear benefit on the routine administration of DDAVP to prevent HE in AA-ICH. However, the trend of limiting HE, albeit not significant, encourages larger prospective trials on the topic, perhaps targeting selected subgroups of patients with higher potential for response, such as patients on dual antiplatelet therapy and moderate to large baseline hematoma volume. Meanwhile, judicious use of DDAVP should be considered on a case-by-case basis in non-traumatic AA-ICH.  

Timing of administration of DDAVP remains uncertain. Recent studies have demonstrated that prevention of early hematoma growth may improve functional outcome [4,26]. Therefore, administration of DDAVP in AA-ICH, when indicated, should be considered in the ultra-early stage of ICH.  

In accordance with the evidence in systemic major bleeding [29], this meta-analysis also demonstrated that DDAVP in AA-ICH has a safe profile in terms of associated thrombotic events. As under any circumstances of reversal of antithrombotic agents, attention needs to be paid to the underlying indication for antiplatelet therapy. The collateral thrombotic risk may be low in case of use of antiplatelet therapy for secondary stroke prevention, but possibly devastating in the instance of a fresh cardiac stent. Detailed indications for platelet administration in the patients included in this study were not available.  

Surprisingly, we found an association between the administration of desmopressin and unfavorable neurologic outcome. This result needs to be interpreted with caution given the small sample size, moderate risk of bias, and not primary outcome of this review. In fact, not all of the included studies were controlled for known severity markers of ICH nor for known predictors of HE. It is plausible to consider that, because of the retrospective design, the administration of DDAVP occurred in more severe cases of ICH, where the benefit of reversing the effect of antiplatelets outweighed the thrombotic risk. This may account for the shift towards worse outcome in the DDAVP group. As a testament to this hypothesis, one of the two studies evaluating neurologic outcome reported significantly higher markers of ICH severity and need for neurosurgical procedures and mechanical ventilatory support in the DDAVP group [23]. A possible additional explanation lies in the effect that DDAVP exhibits on electrolytes, primarily sodium. The collateral induction of hyponatremia represents a known risk factor for development of perihematomal edema and hyponatremia is known to be associated with worse outcome in ICH [30,31]. One study did not find any significant difference in the sodium level after administration of DDAVP compared to controls [24]. However, evaluation of hyponatremia was not performed in either studies investigating neurologic outcome.  

In summary, this meta-analysis does not support the routine administration of DDAVP in AA-ICH. Future studies should have a prospective design and blind randomization, potentially guiding the administration of DDAVP by platelet function assays, and targeting subgroups of patients with higher potential for benefit, such as patients on dual antiplatelet therapy and moderate to large baseline hematoma volume. The collateral thrombotic events, as well as the repercussion of DDAVP on natremia should be strictly monitored, and the outcome adjusted for baseline ICH severity.  

# 4.2. Limitations  

The limited number of studies constitutes the main limitation of this meta-analysis, resulting in moderate heterogeneity of the pooled data for the primary outcome and overall moderate risk of bias. Additionally, propensity matching couldn’t be performed due to the limited number of studies and low number of subjects. Furthermore, in all of the included studies, some patients received platelet transfusion. Although platelet transfusion appeared to be equally distributed in both groups in most of the studies, this may have affected the results. Finally, platelet function assays were not tested before DDAVP administration. Thus, it was not possible to determine with certainty if all the subjects included in the studies had indeed an abnormal platelet function.  

# 5. Conclusion  

The available literature does not support the routine use of DDAVP to decrease the risk of hematoma expansion in AA-ICH. Until larger, prospective, controlled studies are performed, the  

administration of DDAVP should be judiciously considered on a case-by-case basis in the setting of non-traumatic AA-ICH.
