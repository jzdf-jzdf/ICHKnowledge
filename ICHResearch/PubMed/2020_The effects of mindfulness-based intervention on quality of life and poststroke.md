# Abstract  

Objective: Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) is a devastating disease that can lead to poststroke depression (PSD) and greatest impact on the quality of life (QOL) of patients. Mindfulness meditation was viewed as one of the effective ways to reduce PSD in patients with cancer. The present study tried to investigate whether mindfulness meditation has potential benefits in PSD and QOL for sICH patients in China.  

Methods: Two hundred and two patients in West China Hospital, Sichuan University, enrolled from January 2017 to December 2018 were included in a randomized controlled trial. After removing missing values, there were 67 in control group and 67 in intervention group. Patients in intervention group received 2-month mindfulnessbased cognitive therapy, and patients in control group received stress management education (ie, an active control).  

Results: The results suggested that the significant differences of depression, trait mindfulness, social well-being, emotional well-being, and total score of QOL were found in intervention group from time 1 to time 2. Physical well-being and the score of NIH stroke scale experienced significant changes in both control group and intervention group over time.  

Conclusions: Mindfulness-based intervention has positive effects on sICH patients' depression, social well-being, and emotional well-being. However, the change of trait mindfulness over time could not explain these positive effects. Future studies could explore the mechanism of mindfulness-based intervention on sICH patients' depression and QOL and clarify the boundaries of the positive effects of mindfulness-based intervention.

mindfulness-based intervention, poststroke depression, quality of life, spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage  

# 1 | INTRODUCTION  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) accounts for $10\%$ to $15\%$ of all stroke incidences worldwide with an incidence of 24.6 per $100000.^{1-3}$ It is still increasing because of the rising use of anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs and an aging population. It is the most fatal subtype, with $35\%$ to $52\%$ of the patients who died within the first month.3,4 And most survivors suffer from the following consequences of sICH: poststroke depression (PSD), physical function restrictions, and impaired cognition.  

PSD is a common and serious complication of sICH. At least onethird of all sICH survivors report PSD.6,7 Clinical and research efforts in sICH recovery focus on motor disability but largely neglecting PSD. Left untreated, PSD can increase the morbidity, mortality, and disability of sICH and seriously affect their quality of life (QOL). Mindfulness meditation has been found to be effective in reduce depression when compared with a control condition and is advocated in a range of medical settings, including cancer.8 Both sICH and cancer can lead to depression, disability, and high fatality rate. It is surmised that mindfulness meditation may have potential benefits in sICH patients.  

In current clinical and research contexts, mindfulness meditation is typically described as nonjudgemental attention to experiences in the present moment.9 It has been suggested that mindfulness meditation includes at least three components that interact closely to constitute a process of enhanced self-regulation: improved attention control, enhanced emotion regulation, and altered self-awareness (diminished self-referential processing and enhanced body awareness). Many studies in neuroscience have investigated changes in brain activation that are related with mindfulness mediation. These studies found changes in multiple aspects of mental function in beginner and advanced mediators, healthy participants, and patient populations.10-13 However, the methodological quality of many meditation research studies is still relatively low,9 and the researchers in psychological field claimed that misinformation and poor methodology related with mindfulness studies may lead public consumers to be harmed, misled, and disappointed.14 For example, the issue of biased sample (ie, wealthy, white, and western participants) and the lack of active control group decrease the internal and external validity of mindfulness intervention studies. Tang et al reported that many researchers are enthusiastic mindfulness meditators themselves.9 Although their insider perspective may be important for a deep understanding of mindfulness intervention, the experimenter effect may cause serious bias for the intervention study.  

Thus, the present study tried to improve the previous studies at the following aspects: (a) using randomized controlled trial (RCT) design to investigate the efficacy of mindfulness mediation on PSD and QOL, (b) conducting power analysis to make sure enough statistical power, (c) including active interventions in control groups (ie, stress management education) that can control for confounding variables such as social interaction with the group and therapists, (d) using single-blind-control to avoid the experimenter effect, (e) investigating sICH patients in western China (economically backward region) to help fix the issue of biased sample (wealthy, white, and western participants) in most mindfulness-based intervention studies.15  

# 2 | METHODS  

# 2.1 | Participants and procedures  

Before selecting participants for the present study, we conducted power analysis in R programming to decide how many participants are needed to acquire enough statistical power. Considering that the effect size of mindfulness-based intervention for health outcomes ranged from 0.73 to 1.37 (pretreatment to posttreatment Cohen $d),^{16-18}$ around 10 to 27 participants per group would be needed to acquire $95\%$ statistical power at 0.05 confidence level. Thus, we aimed to select at least 60 participants in the present study. Ethics approval was granted by the West China hospital Sichuan University Institutional Human Research Ethics Committees (Verified in 2019, No. 489). All participants signed consent forms before study inclusion.

Patients were recruited from West China Hospital Sichuan University between January 2017 and December 2018. The inclusive criteria were as follows: (a) 3 to 6 months post-sICH, (b) confirmation of sICH by neurological signs and computer tomography, (c) aged 40 and older, (d) NIHSS $\le20$ at time 1, and (e) being able to understand and speak Chinese. The exclusion criteria were (a). intracranial hemorrhage cases due to trauma, tumor apoplexy, ruptured aneurysm, cavernous hemangioma, or arteriovenous malformation; (b) recurrent sICH; and (c) cognitive deficits.  

As is shown in Figure 1, three hundred and eleven patients are satisfied with our inclusion criteria. All patients were sent invitation about the mindfulness meditation project. Ninety-six patients declined to participate in the project, and 13 patients did not respond to the invitation. Thus, 202 patients were randomly assigned to control group $(\mathsf{n}=101)$ or intervention group. After confirmation of participation, all participants completed consent forms and pretest questionnaires. Then three professional therapists progressed to intervention group. Participants in the intervention group received mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), which is a widely used mindfulness meditation procedure.19 MBCT is officially endorsed by the American Psychiatric Association for preventing relapse in remitted patients who have had three or more previous episodes of depression.14 MBCT therapists provided support to participants in learning to respond adaptively to thoughts, feelings, and experiences. The MBCT project consists of eight 2-hour group sessions over consecutive weeks. During the intervention sessions, 31 participants withdrew from the MBCT project, and three participants lost to follow-up. In the control group, participants received stress management education (SME), which is viewed as an active control and did not contain any mindfulness components. SME consists of 8-week 2-hour group sessions as well. It was delivered in a group lecture format. The lectures covered topics relevant to stress, stress physiology, insomnia, exercise, and nutrition. Twenty-three participants withdrew from the control group. After 8 weeks of intervention, both groups completed posttest questionnaires. It is worth noting that 11 participants in the control group were excluded from the data analysis according to their pretest scores, namely, those who have similar pretest scores with the 34 missed patients in the intervention group. This is because of making sure the equality between the intervention groups and the control group. Thus, a total of 134 patients (67 in control group and 67 in intervention group) were included in the final data analysis.

possible range of the 20-item scale is 0 to 60. The Cronbach $\alpha$ of CES-D in the present study was 0.88 and 0.82, respectively.

Trait mindfulness was measured by using mindful attention awareness scale (MAAS), which has been tested in a Chinese sample in the previous study.21 MAAS has a single-factor structure with 15 items rated from 1 (almost never) to 6 (almost always). # 2.2.3 | The functional assessment of cancer therapy-brain  

Functional assessment of cancer therapy-brain (FACT-Br) was used to measure the general QOL of the patients. FACT-Br consists of five dimensions, including physical well-being, social well-being, emotional well-being, functional well-being, and concerns that correspond to patients with brain tumors. Patients were asked to respond on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much). The internal consistency of FACT-Br was 0.86 and 0.93 in the present sample. The internal consistency for physical well-being, social wellbeing, emotional well-being, functional well-being, and additional concerns was 0.67 to 0.88 at time 1 and 0.70 to 0.83 at time 2, respectively.  

# 2.2.4 | NIH stroke scale  

The NIH stroke scale (NIHSS) is a common diagnostic method for quickly assessing the severity of a stroke experienced by a patient. The scale contains 11 elements, related to specific function (eg, level of consciousness). These elements are each evaluated with a score between 0 and 4, and some elements only have a scale from 0 to 2. The higher the score, the more impaired that specific function is. In general, the total score of 21 to 42 stands for severe stroke, 16 to 20 stands for moderate to severe stroke, 5 to 15 stands for moderate stroke, 1 to 4 means minor stroke, and 0 means no stroke symptoms.  

# 2.2.5 | Demographics and other features  

Besides depression, trait mindfulness, QOL, and stroke, we also obtained sex, marital status, ethnicity, occupation, education level, and hematoma size from hospital records.  

# 2.2.6 | Data analysis  

R 3.5.1 was used to preprocess data and conduct t test or correlation analysis. Specifically, independent sample t test was conducted to examine the significant differences of the main variables (eg, depression, trait mindfulness, QOL, and stroke) between control group and intervention group at baseline. Paired sample t test was used to examine the differences of the outcome variables between pretest and posttest in control group or intervention group. The effect size of t test (ie, Cohen $d)$ was calculated by using effsize package (https:// cran.r-project.org/web/packages/effsize/index.html). We also conduct correlation analysis to test the associations between variables in pretest and posttest.

# 4 | DISCUSSION  

The present study found the significant differences of depression, trait mindfulness, social well-being, emotional well-being, and total score of QOL in intervention group from time 1 to time 2. Physical well-being and NIHSS experienced significant changes in both control group and intervention group over time. Change in the NIHSS negatively correlated with change in the physical well-being. Change in the depression has negative relationships with change in the QOL, change in the emotional well-being, and change in the functional well-being.  

In general, the results of the present study supported the effectiveness of mindfulness meditation (ie, MBCT in the present study). First, we observed the significant changes of depression, social wellbeing, emotional well-being, and total score of QOL in the intervention group over time, and such changes were not found in the control group. Consistent with our study, previous studies have suggested that mindfulness meditation was effective to alleviate anxiety,18 stress reactivity,18 recurrent depression,22 and cancer.23 A longitudinal and quasi-experimental study found intensive mindfulness meditation enhanced positive emotions from pretraining to 1-month follow-up,24 which is consistent with our results (ie, MBCT enhanced social wellbeing and emotional well-being) as well. In their study, Orzech et al did not include active interventions in control groups (ie, they used wait-list controls); thus, the enhancing effect of mindfulness mediation may be attributed to confounding variables such as social interaction with the group and therapists.24 The current study used both RCT and active interventions in control group; we may acquire more solid conclusions compared with the study of Orzech et al. Blanke, Riediger, and Brose used experience-sampling methodology to examine the association between mindfulness and everyday affective experience; they found participants experienced more positive affect when they attentive to the present moment (ie, high level of mindfulness).25 Evidences from neuroscience also verified the effect of mindfulness on emotions,26-28 but the mechanism of it still stays fuzzy. Although some researchers proposed theoretical models to explain why mindfulness mediation promotes positive emotions,29 further research needs to use longitudinal, randomized, and actively controlled research designs and larger sample sizes to advance the understanding of it.  

No enhancing effects of MBCT were found on physical wellbeing, functional well-being, brain specific, and NIHSS in the present study. In fact, these variables experienced positive changes from time 1 to time 2 in both groups. This is reasonable, considering both groups received surgery and corresponding medical care. In addition, we found that trait mindfulness (ie, one's predisposition to be mindful in daily life) experienced an increasing trend in intervention group over time, but not in control group, suggesting the positive effect of MBCT on trait mindfulness. We initially expect that all paired correlations between the change of trait mindfulness, depression, and QOL reach statistically significant level and examine the mediation effect of trait mindfulness on the relationship between mindfulness meditation and depression/QOL, but only the change of depression and QOL has significant correlation. Thus, the hypothesis of mediation effect was not supported. Perhaps the change of emotional well-being, social well-being, and depression contributed more to the change of QOL in the intervention group.30 Previous study found individuals with higher levels of pretreatment trait mindfulness would benefit more from mindfulness meditation than those with lower levels of trait mindfulness.31 In the current study, we balanced the level of pretest trait mindfulness; it is not feasible to test the moderating effect of trait mindfulness on the relationship between mindfulness meditation and QOL/depression. Kiken et al found that state mindfulness in meditation practice over time increases trait mindfulness, which alleviates psychological distress.32 However, we did not find this mediation effect in the present study, suggesting that the mechanism of mindfulness meditation on sICH patients still needs more empirical evidence.  

Some limitations of the present study should be noted. The first limitation was the use of self-report scales to measure mindfulness. Because patients may not know exactly which aspects of mental states should be taken into account when making personal assessments.14 Second, MBCT was chosen as mindfulness-based intervention, but MBCT incorporated aspects of cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT). Thus, it is not completely sure that the positive effects of intervention are attributed to mindfulness but not to traditional CBT. Third, the patients in the present study might not be fully representative of the greater patient population with sICH, because patients who were not feasible to accept MBCT have been excluded. Fourth, the follow-up investigation was not performed, and we did not know whether the effectiveness of mindfulness intervention is temporary or not. Despite these limitations, the present study used RCT and an active control to examine the positive effects of mindfulness-based intervention on sICH patients. It provides preliminary evidence of the efficacy of mindfulness meditation to clinicians working in the neurosurgical context. Future studies should explore the mechanism of mindfulness-based intervention on sICH patients' depression and QOL and clarify the boundaries of the positive effects of mindfulness-based intervention. More importantly, researchers and the public overlooked the potential adverse effects of mindfulness.14 Clinicians and researchers should be encouraged to address the potential adverse effects stemming from mindfulness practices.  

# 5 | CONCLUSION  

Mindfulness-based intervention has positive effects on sICH patients' depression, social well-being, and emotional well-being. However, the change of trait mindfulness over time could not explain these positive effects.
