# Beta‑Blockade in Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: a Systematic Review and Meta‑Analysis  

Aravind V. Ramesh $1^{*}\oplus_{,}$ Charis F. K. Banks2, Peter E. Mounstephen2, Kate Crewdson3 and Matt Thomas4  

2020 Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature and Neurocritical Care Society  

# Abstract  

Background/objective:  Sympathetic nervous system activation after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is associated with complications and poor outcome. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we investigate the effect of beta-blockers on outcome after aSAH.  

Methods:  The review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42019111784). We performed a systematic literature search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, published conference proceedings, and abstracts. Eligible studies included both randomized controlled trials and observational studies up to October 2018, reporting the effect of beta-blocker therapy on the following outcomes in aSAH: mortality, vasospasm, delayed cerebral ischemia, infarction or stroke, cardiac dysfunction, and functional outcomes. Studies involving traumatic SAH were excluded. Citations were reviewed, and data extracted independently by two investigators using a standardized proforma.  

Results:  We identified 819 records with 16 studies (four were randomized controlled trials) including 6702 patients selected for analysis. Exposure to beta-blockade either before or after aSAH was associated with a significant reduction in unadjusted mortality ( $\mathfrak{R}0.63,95\%\subset|0.42\substack{-0.93,p=0.02)}$ . A significant reduction in unadjusted mortality was also seen in prospective trials of post-event beta-blockade (RR 0.51, $95\%\thinspace\mathsf{C}\mathsf{I}\thinspace0.28-0.93,p=0.03$ ). Statistically significant differences were not seen for other outcomes investigated.  

Conclusions:  In adult patients with aSAH, beta-blocker therapy is associated with a mortality benefit. Studies are generally of a low quality with considerable clinical heterogeneity. Prospective large interventional trials with patient centered outcomes are required to validate this finding.  

Keywords:  Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Intracranial aneurysm, Adrenergic beta-antagonists, Beta-blockers, Mortality  

# Introduction  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) accounts for $5\%$ of all stroke, typically affecting a younger patient group than other forms of cerebrovascular injury [1]. The reported case fatality rate is $25-50\%$ , and the majority of survivors have neurocognitive impairment. Only $35-55\%$ of patients are able to live independently 12 months after ictus [2]. Only one-third of survivors in work at the time of their bleed return entirely to work at 2–4 years [3]. The economic impact of aSAH in the UK is estimated to be £510 million annually [4].  

There are both intra-cranial and extra-cranial complications after aSAH. Among the former are cerebral edema, hydrocephalus, seizures, re-bleeding, and delayed cerebral ischemia, while the latter include neurogenic pulmonary edema and cardiomyopathy, cardiac arrhythmias, immunodepression and infection, thromboembolism, fever, and anemia [5, 6]. Complications have important effects on outcome [7, 8].  

Activation of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) occurs after aSAH and is greater in more severe bleeds [9]. SNS activation is linked to intra- and extra-cranial complications, particularly delayed cerebral ischemia (with or without vasospasm) and neurogenic cardiomyopathy [9–13]. Modulation of the sympathetic response therefore represents a therapeutic opportunity. However, the evidence to date does not provide a definitive answer on the effectiveness of beta-blockade, let alone answers to pertinent questions such as drug, dose, timing, and targets [14–17].  

A previous meta-analysis on the effect of beta-blockade in aSAH assessed the impact of pre-admission betablocker therapy on neurogenic stunned myocardium, cardiac-related complications, cerebral vasospasm, and mortality, concluding that there was no statistically significant effect of beta-blocker use on outcomes after aSAH [17]. Three retrospective studies were included, with a total of 68 of 698 patients exposed to beta-blockers; no single outcome was addressed by all three trials. Despite the lack of statistical significance, there was a suggestion of clinical benefit favouring beta-blockers. No information on type or dose of beta-blockers was available, and there was no information on functional outcomes.  

The primary objective of this systematic review is to assess the impact of beta-blocker exposure (pre-admission and/or post-event) on mortality after aSAH. The secondary objectives were to analyze the separate effects of pre- and post-event exposure on mortality, and to determine the effect of any beta-blocker exposure on functional outcome, cardiomyopathy, and delayed cerebral ischemia.  

# Methods  

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following the ‘Preferred Reporting Items for a Systematic Review and Meta-analysis’ statement [18] and was prospectively registered on the International prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO—ID: CRD42019111784).  

# Data Sources and Search Strategy  

The databases of Cochrane Central, MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the trials registries of ClinicalTrials.gov, US National Institutes of Health, and the World Health Organisation International Clinical Trials Registry Platform were searched in October 2018 to identify studies meeting the eligibility criteria below. The search strategy (Supplemental Table 1) was designed by an independent academic librarian in MEDLINE without date or language restrictions and then adapted to other databases.  

# Eligibility Criteria  

We included randomized clinical trials and observational studies with the following inclusion criteria: adults older than 18  years of age with confirmed diagnosis of aSAH on neuroimaging or cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and exposure to beta-blockade either prior to admission or after admission. Studies involving patients with traumatic SAH  as well as animal studies were excluded.  

# Study Selection  

Two reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of articles. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were independently assessed by the same reviewers; disagreements were resolved through referral to a third reviewer.  

# Data Extraction and Study Appraisal  

Two reviewers independently extracted data from eligible articles using a standardized data extraction form. The primary outcome assessed was all-cause mortality at the longest specified time point. Secondary outcomes assessed where possible included mortality at other time points, vasospasm, delayed cerebral ischemia, infarction or stroke as a result of SAH, markers of cardiac dysfunction (electrocardiographic and echocardiographic abnormalities or troponin), and functional outcomes (measured through validated scales such as the Glasgow Outcome Scale or the modified Rankin Scale).  

For categorical data, numbers of patients with an outcome in individual categories were assessed, while continuous data were extracted as means and standard deviations (SDs). All relevant sources were used for data extraction including full-text journal articles, abstracts, and clinical trial registry entries. Where both an abstract as well as a journal article were available for the same piece of work, the journal article was used as the primary publication as it had been peer reviewed. Authors were contacted for raw data on their primary outcome when only summary statistics were reported in the primary publication.  

Risk of bias assessment for each study was performed independently by two reviewers with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer. For randomized control trials, the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool [19] was used to assess methodological quality. For observational studies, a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Assessment Form was used [20].  

# Data Synthesis and Analysis  

Where there were three or more studies reporting an outcome of interest, a quantitative synthesis using metaanalysis was performed. Where necessary fourfold table values were imputed according to the method of Di Pietrantonj [21]. Results were pooled and analyzed using a random-effects model due to anticipated significant inter-study heterogeneity. Risk ratios were calculated for dichotomous outcomes with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided $\mathsf{p}$ values. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Chi-squared test and I [2] statistic. Sensitivity analyses of randomized controlled trials only (primary outcome) and excluding studies assessed as having a high risk of bias (primary and secondary outcomes) were performed. Where data were too heterogeneous to pool, a narrative synthesis was used.  

We pre-specified subgroup analysis comparing postevent beta-blockade versus therapy pre-admission. All statistical analysis was performed using Revman v5.3 [22].  

# Results  

A total of 819 citations were initially identified by the search strategy, with 16 studies involving 6702 participants included in qualitative data analysis [15, 16, 23– 36] (Fig. 1). Raw data were not provided by the authors for three studies [27, 33, 36].

# Study Characteristics  

Study and participant characteristics are provided in Supplemental Table  2. Eight studies [16, 24–26, 28, 29, 32, 34] were prospective, four of which were randomized trials [24, 28, 29, 34]. Settings included North America (eight studies) [15, 16, 23, 30, 31, 33, 35, 36], Europe (six studies) [24–27, 29, 32], and Asia (two studies) [28, 34]. The majority were single-center studies (14 out of 16), and study publication dates ranged from 1976 to 2016. Eight studies looked at pre-admission beta-blocker therapy alone [15, 16, 23, 27, 31, 33, 35, 36]; the other eight looked at post-event therapy [24–26, 28–30, 32, 34]. Of the eight studies reporting on the effect of pre-admission beta-blocker therapy, only one study specified that beta-blocker therapy was stopped on admission [15], the remaining seven did not specify [16, 23, 27, 31, 33, 35, 36]. Of the eight studies reporting on post-event betablockade, only one study reported whether study patients were on prior beta-blocker therapy [30], the remaining seven did not specify [24–26, 28, 29, 32, 34]. Five of the studies reporting on post-event therapy used non-selective beta-blockade (four studies  used propranolol) [24,  

26, 28–30] with the other three using a B1 selective betablocker (atenolol, landiolol, and metoprolol) [25, 32, 34]. Three of these studies did not specify time to post-event beta-blockade, four specified within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ and one within three days. Results of study quality assessment are shown in Table  1 (Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized studies) and Table  2 (Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for nonrandomized studies).  

# Outcomes  

# Mortality  

Seven studies reported quantitative data for mortality [16, 24, 28, 31, 32, 34, 35]. Mortality data were not provided by authors of one study [27]. Beta-blockade (both pre-admission and post-event studies combined) was associated with a significant decrease in all-cause mortality at the longest specified time point (RR 0.63, $95\%$ CI 0.42–0.93, $p{=}0.02$ , $I^{2}{=}0\stackrel{.}{.}$ )(Fig.  2). Specified time points ranged from 28 days to three months; one study reported in-hospital mortality, and three studies did not specify a time point.

Mortality with beta-blockade appeared reduced in the sensitivity analysis (three studies; RR $0.64$ , $95\%$ CI $0.24{-}1.76$ , $p{=}0.39$ , $\dot{I^{2}}{=}24\%$ ) [16, 24, 34] and in prospective randomized trials only (three studies; RR 0.61, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI0}.29\substack{-1.27}$ , $p{=}0.19$ , $I^{2}=16\%$ ) [24, 28, 34] although neither reached statistical significance (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).  

In subgroup analysis, post-event beta-blocker therapy was associated with a significant decrease in mortality (three randomized trials and one non-randomized trial, RR 0.51, $95\%$ CI 0.28–0.93, $p{=}0.03$ , $I^{2}{=}9\%$ ) [24, 28, 32, 34] (Fig. 3). Time points ranged from 28 days [24] to three months [34]; two studies did not specify a time point [28, 32]. There was no statistically significant difference when pre-admission beta-blockade alone was considered (three studies, RR 0.83, $95\%$ CI 0.45–1.52, $p{=}0.54$ , $I^{2}{=}0\%$ ) [16, 31, 35] (Supplementary Fig. 3). Only one study specified a mortality time point at 30 days [16], one study reported in-hospital mortality [31], and one study did not specify a mortality time point [35].  

# Vasospasm  

Four studies reported quantitative data for cerebral vasospasm [15, 28, 30, 35]. Two of the studies used transcranial Doppler values for diagnosis, and two did not specify a diagnostic method. Beta-blocker therapy may be associated with a reduced incidence of cerebral vasospasm (RR 0.59, $95\%$ CI 0.26–1.32, $p{=}0.2$ , $I^{2}{=}80\%$ , Supplementary Fig.  4), but this did not reach statistical significance. A similar direction of effect was seen on sensitivity analysis when two studies with high risk of bias were removed, but statistical significance was again not reached (RR 0.67, $95\%$ CI 0.21–2.19, $p{=}0.51$ , $I^{2}{=}90\%$ , Supplementary Fig. 5).  

Two studies reported on the incidence of vasospasm associated with post-event beta-blocker therapy: one was a randomized controlled trial which suggested reduced vasospasm with beta-blockade compared with placebo (OR 0.17, $p<0.01$ ) [28], while the other was a retrospective study that did not show a significant effect on vasospasm with post-event beta-blockade (Calculated OR from study data 1.32, CI 0.73–2.38) [30].  

# Adverse Cardiac Events  

Seven studies looked at adverse cardiac events defined as at least one of electrocardiogram changes, troponin elevation or LV dysfunction on transthoracic echocardiography [16, 23, 28, 33, 35, 36]. Quantitative data were available for four of these studies [16, 23, 28, 35]. Betablockade may be associated with a decrease in adverse cardiac events (RR 0.51, $95\%$ CI 0.17–1.54, $p{=}0.23$ , $I^{2}=64\%$ , but this did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 6). Three out of the four studies were assessed as poor quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa scoring system.

In subgroup analysis, the single randomized controlled trial measuring adverse cardiac events reported a significant reduction in adverse cardiac events with post-event beta-blockade (OR 0.28, $p{=}{<}0.01$ ) [28], but pre-admission beta-blocker therapy (three out of the four studies) [16, 23, 35] did not have a significant effect (RR 0.71, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}0.23\substack{-2.22,p=0.56,I^{2}=41\%}$ , Supplementary Fig. 7).  

# Delayed Ischemic Neurological Deficit  

Three studies reported on ischemic stroke after SAH, either as delayed ischemic neurological deficit, delayed infarction, or stroke [30, 34, 35]. Beta-blockade may be associated with increased incidence of stroke (RR 1.19, $95\%$ CI 0.78–1.81, $p{=}0.41$ , $I^{2}{=}0\%$ ) although this did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig.  8). Two of these three studies reported on infarction occurring during hospitalization; the other study reported outcomes within 3 months of SAH.  

# Functional Outcome  

Three comparative studies all looking at post-event betablockade reported a functional outcome measure [24, 30, 34]. One reported Cerebral Performance Category (grades 1 or 2; good cerebral performance or moderate cerebral disability with sufficient function for activities of daily living) at hospital discharge with 89/130 $(68.5\%)$ in the beta-blocker group compared to 61/73 $(83.6\%)$ in the control group [30]. One reported Glasgow Outcome Scale at 3  months: 16/28 $(57.1\%)$ in the beta-blocker group, and 18/28 $(64.3\%)$ in the control group were rated category 5 (mild or no disability, able to return to work or school) [34]. One used an unvalidated clinical grading method and reported 80/111 $(72.1\%)$ in intervention group against 48/93 $(51.6\%)$ as being alert with or without minor neurological deficit at one year [24]. The same study reported return to work at one year of 85/111 $(76.6\%)$ in the intervention group and 51/93 $(54.9\%)$ control group.  

# Safety  

Four studies reported on adverse events attributable to study medication of which two reported no adverse events related to the study medication [16, 24, 32, 34]. One study described a significantly lower heart rate in the beta-blocker group, but there was no difference in blood pressure or requirement for vasoactive drugs [34]. One study reported a higher rate of ventricular and supraventricular ectopy in those on beta-blockers pre-admission, but there was no mention of whether pre-admission drugs were continued or discontinued after hospital admission [16].  

# Discussion  

In our systematic review of adult patients with aSAH, beta-blockade was associated with lower all-cause mortality at the longest specified time point (RR 0.63, $95\%$ $\operatorname{CI}0.42–0.93$ , $p{=}0.02,$ ). The direction of effect was maintained when limiting analysis to higher-quality studies, although statistical significance was not reached. Interestingly, the benefit appeared to be restricted to beta-blockade after aSAH (treatment) (RR 0.51, $95\%$ CI 0.28–0.93, $p{=}0.03,$ ) compared with pre-admission beta-blockade (prophylaxis) (RR 0.83, $95\%$ CI 0.45–1.52, $\scriptstyle{p=0.54},$ .  

Observational data links increased SNS activity and the post-ictus catecholamine surge to both poor neurological outcome [9] as well as cardiopulmonary complications such as neurogenic stunned myocardium and neurogenic pulmonary edema [6]. Our meta-analysis of mortality, as well as further pre-specified subgroup analysis suggesting specific benefit in the context of post-event beta-blockade, is consistent with this theory of sympathetic attenuation as a potential therapeutic strategy post-SAH. This result is also consistent with data in other forms of acute neurological injury, for example, a recent meta-analysis of beta-blockade in traumatic brain injury also suggesting mortality benefit [37].  

Despite this, consistent with previous meta-analysis [17] we did not demonstrate a statistically significant benefit of beta-blockade on adverse cardiac events such as neurogenic stunned myocardium although the clinical effect favoured the use of beta-blockade. The only study assessing post-event beta-blockade in this context reported benefit and as with mortality it may be that it is sympathetic attenuation post-injury rather than preinjury that is beneficial. The significant clinical heterogeneity between studies reporting on this outcome, with variability in definition and diagnostic thresholds, means this result should be interpreted with caution. Long-term community beta-blocker therapy is often prescribed for significant cardiac conditions such as hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and dysrhythmias, all serious comorbidities that impact negatively on survival in critical illness. It is therefore possible that any potential benefit of pre-event beta-blockade might be offset by the mortality effect of preexisting disease in this group of patients.  

Vasospasm is a feared complication of SAH, with a risk of cerebral ischemia and infarction [38]. The effect of beta-blockade on both vasospasm and cerebral infarction is uncertain. The heterogeneity of definitions and study design, the management of pre-admission beta-blockers after ictus, and the confounding effect of comorbidity as outlined above means studies included in this review do not provide definitive answers for these outcomes. Nor is there sufficient detail to provide estimates of the impact on functional outcome following SAH.  

The major concern with using beta-blockers in acute brain injury remains hypotension, which may account for the potential increased risk of permanent ischemic deficit. A similar mechanism was proposed to account for findings in the POISE trial of preoperative betablockade in non-cardiac surgery [39]. Although hypotension was not reported as an adverse effect in any study in this review, the majority did not report on adverse events. Safety should not be assumed particularly as haemodynamic management remains a cornerstone of acute brain injury management in intensive care, where the loss of autoregulation renders brain parenchyma at risk from extremes of blood pressure [40, 41].  

Our systematic review has several limitations. The studies which comprise this review are largely observational studies with only five prospective interventional trials (four randomized trials). However, analysis of the prospective trials alone supports the pooled analysis of the primary outcome. The included studies span a 40-year time period, during which both clinical and research practice in intensive care have changed substantially. A significant number of the included studies were judged to be of poor quality, and there was substantial clinical heterogeneity across outcome definitions, study design, and inclusion/exclusion criteria. All of the above results in small numbers suitable for meta-analysis of key outcomes and the introduction of bias into further analysis. We have not attempted to adjust for potential confounders given this heterogeneity, which further limits the certainty of our conclusions. Finally, we have not assessed publication bias as there were fewer than ten studies included in the meta-analysis for the primary outcome [19].  

Overall, the evidence contained in this review provides a low certainty of clinical effect due to bias and the potential for confounding. However, effect sizes are large and estimated with reasonable precision and with the exception of cerebral ischemia discussed above are consistent in demonstrating a potential benefit. These potential benefits and risks of beta-blockers parallel findings in other settings include traumatic brain injury, and there is a plausible physiological basis for effect. As such, further research investigating the effect of beta-blockers on mortality, morbidity, and function after aSAH appears justified.  

# Conclusions  

In adult patients after aSAH beta-blockade is associated with a mortality benefit without significant adverse effects. The effect appears to be largely a consequence of post-event use of beta-blockers. Given the poor quality of existing studies and limited numbers of patients enrolled in prospective studies, larger multicenter randomized controlled trials are required to validate these findings.
