# Blood pressure targets after successful reperfusion in mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke: an updated systematic review and meta‑analysis of randomized clinical trials  

Edgar Paulo da Silva Neto1 $\bullet$ Lucas Fernandes Ferreira2 · Ana Luisa Carvalho de Cardozo Hernandez2 · Eberval Gadelha Figueiredo3 · João Paulo Mota Telles2 $\circledcirc$

# Abstract  

Background  This study sought to determine whether intensive blood pressure (BP) control for patients with successful reperfusion following acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is beneficial, compared to conventional BP management.  

Methods  PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases were systematically searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the subject. The studied outcomes included dependency or death at 90 days (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] 3–6); severe disability at 90 days (mRS 3–5); mortality at 90 days; and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. Odds ratios (OR) with $95\%$ confidence intervals were used to compare the treatment effects for categorical outcomes. We employed a fixed-effect model for analyses with low heterogeneity $(I^{2}<25\%)$ and a random-effects model for analyses with higher heterogeneity.  

Results  A total of 1519 patients were included, with $50\%$ ( $n=760,$ ) receiving intensive BP control (systolic $\mathrm{BP}<140\mathrm{mmHg},$ ). Functional disability or death at 90 days was significantly higher in the intensive group $(54.9\%)$ compared to the conventional treatment group $(44.1\%)$ $(\mathrm{OR}=1.51\$ ; $95\%$ Confidence Interval [CI]: 1.15–1.96; $p{=}0.003$ ; $I^{2}{=}29\%,$ ). Severe functional disability (mRS 3–5) was significantly higher in the intensive group $30.6\%$ vs. $43.5\%$ , $\mathrm{OR}=1.75$ ; $95\%\mathrm{CI}=1.36{-2.25}$ ; $p<0.0001$ ; $I^{2}{=}0\%$ ). There was no difference in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage $(\mathrm{OR}=1.13\$ ; $95\%\mathrm{CI}=0.76-1.67$ ) or mortality $\mathrm{\DeltaOR}=1.22\$ ; $95\%\mathrm{CI}=0.9{-}1.64\%$ ).  

Conclusions  Intensive BP control is harmful in patients who underwent EVT for AIS and achieved successful reperfusion.   
It yields higher rates of functional dependence, with no differences in mortality or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage.  

Keywords  Ischemic stroke $\cdot$ Thrombectomy $^{\ast}$ Reperfusion $^{\ast}$ Blood pressure  



# Introduction  

Blood pressure (BP) management is a key component of acute care for stroke patients due to large vessel occlusion [1, 2]. Although the association of EVT and intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) is superior to IVT alone for accidents with large vessel occlusion in acute ischemic stroke (AIS), almost half of the patients still have poor functional results [3–5]. Due to impaired cerebral autoregulation, BP control during AIS is essential to avoid secondary neurological injuries, such as intracranial bleeding and cerebral edema, and unfavorable clinical outcomes [6].  

However, the ideal BP values in patients who have undergone EVT in AIS and who have had successful recanalization are still debatable. Recent randomized controlled trials have addressed this question, but no meta-analysis has yet summarized the results of these trials. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to compare intensive versus conventional BP control in patients with a final modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) score of 2b, 2c, or 3.  



# Methods  



# Eligibility criteria  

The inclusion in this meta-analysis was restricted to studies that met the following eligibility criteria: (1) patients with acute ischemic stroke, (2) patients undergoing endovascular therapy with successful reperfusion, defined as a mTICI score $\geq2\mathrm{b}$ , (3) randomized and controlled clinical trials, (4) studies with available data on any outcome of interest with direct comparison between intervention and control groups, (5) studies targeting specific BP goals for post-EVT hypertension management. Pressure targets were qualified as intensive (systolic $\mathrm{BP\le140\mathrm{mmHg}}$ ) or conventional $(\leq180\mathrm{mmHg})$ .  

On the other hand, the following were excluded: (1) studies assessing patients with acute ischemic stroke with unsuccessful reperfusion $\mathrm{(mTICI<2b)}$ ) after endovascular therapy, (2) observational studies, (3) studies involving patients without systemic arterial hypertension after EVT, (4) studies without defined BP targets for hypertension treatment in reperfused patients.  



# Search strategy and data extraction  

The PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases were systematically searched in September 2023. The search terms included (“acute ischemic stroke” OR “acute ischaemic stroke”) AND (“blood pressure” OR “systolic pressure”) AND (“endovascular thrombectomy” OR “mechanical thrombectomy” OR “endovascular therapy”). References of all matching studies, previous systematic reviews, and metaanalyses were screened to identify relevant papers. Duplicate studies were removed using Zotero, and the selected articles were manually reviewed. Two authors (ES and JPMT) independently extracted the data following predefined search criteria and quality assessment. In addition to the outcomes of interest, the authors also extracted information for subgroup analysis, population characteristics, and study design. Discrepancies between the authors were resolved by a third author (EGF). This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD42023467480) in September 2023.  



# Endpoints and subanalyses  

The outcomes included in the analysis were as follows: dependency or death at 90  days, defined by a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 3–6; severe disability at 90 days among survivors, characterized by an mRS score of 3–5; mortality at 90 days; mean change in NIHSS score; symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), defined according to the criteria set forth in the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study III [7].  



# Quality assessment  

The risk of bias and the assessment of quality for randomized studies were conducted using the Rob-2 tool from the Cochrane Collaboration. Each trial was categorized as having “high risk,” “low risk,” or “uncertain risk” in each of the five domains: selection, performance, detection, attrition, and reporting bias [8]. Two independent authors (ES and JPMT) completed the risk of bias assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus after discussing the reasons for discrepancies. Funnel plots of individual study weights compared to point estimates were analyzed to detect evidence of publication bias.  



# Statistical analysis  

This meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the guidelines of the Cochrane Collaboration’s Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalysis (PRISMA) statement [9].  

Odds ratios (OR) with $95\%$ confidence intervals were used to compare the treatment effects for categorical outcomes. We employed a fixed-effect model for analyses with low heterogeneity $(I^{2}<25\%)$ and a random-effects model for higher heterogeneity analyses. Mean differences were used to compare continuous outcomes. Statistical analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.4.1 (Cochrane Center; The Cochrane Collaboration).  



# Results  



# Study selection and characteristics  

The initial search identified 862 results. After removing duplicate records and ineligible studies not related to the topic of interest—based on title and abstract screening—8 studies remained and were reviewed based on the inclusion criteria. Of these, a total of 4 studies, all comprising randomized controlled trials (RCTs), were included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 1). Two studies were excluded because they evaluated BP control during the thrombectomy (“early intervention”).  

A total of 1516 patients with acute ischemic stroke who underwent successful endovascular therapy with reperfusion were included, of whom 760 received intensive BP control, and 871 were male. The study characteristics are reported in Table 1. There were no significant discrepancies in the follow-up duration of patients among the studies, and, in most, Nicardipine, a calcium channel blocker, was the leading antihypertensive agent used for BP control. All patients included in this study had achieved successful recanalization through endovascular therapy.

# Quality assessment  

Bias risk was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials [8]. All five domains of the BEST-II, BP-TARGET, ENCHANTED2/MT, and OPTIMAL-BP trials showed a low risk of bias. These publications collectively exhibited an overall low risk of bias according to the Rob-2 tool (Fig. 4), although the fact that trials were not blinded certainly entails some performance bias. The analysis of the funnel plot (Fig. 5) for publication bias assessment is limited due to the small number of trials. However, there was no definitive evidence of publication bias.  



# Discussion  

This meta-analysis of four trials demonstrated that intensive BP control after mechanical thrombectomy $(<140\mathrm{mmHg}$ , systolic) was associated with statistically significant poorer functional results compared to conventional pressure targets $(<180{-}185\mathrm{mmHg}$ , systolic).  

We found that intensive BP control goals were associated with a higher risk of severe disability, although there was no difference in mortality between groups. As BP elevation seems to be part of an autoregulatory response to cerebral ischemia, intensive BP lowering might decrease blood flow to the oligemic area and exacerbate the ischemic injury, leading to worse functional outcomes.  

Furthermore, the intracranial pressure may be higher than previously due to progressive cytotoxic edema, requiring greater BP to maintain adequate cerebral perfusion. Besides, in OPTIMAL-BP and BP-TARGET trial, the intensive BP control groups had more frequent hypotensive events, which might be harmful for a recently reperfused cerebral tissue. Other trials investigating optimal pressure goals in different stroke scenarios, such as RIGHT-2, ENOS, CATIS, and ENCHANTED (the latter evaluating patients submitted to intravenous thrombolysis), also failed to find an improvement in functional outcomes with more strict pressure control [10–13].  

In addition to higher disability, intensive BP control did not reduce the incidence of symptomatic hemorrhage. These findings differ from previous observational studies, which had shown an association between elevated systolic BP and worse functional outcomes or intraparenchymal hemorrhage occurrence [14–19]. Perhaps this observation could have been related to a physiologic response in individuals with larger stroke volumes and/or more severe ischemia, leading to a greater elevation in systolic blood pressure [20, 21].  

Regarding selected BP goals, all four selected trials did not surpass $140\mathrm{mmHg}$ systolic pressure as a threshold for the intensive control group; however, the exact target varied between the trials. The ENCHANTED2/MT trial used a more strict goal of $<120\mathrm{mmHg}$ in the intensive group. The BP-TARGET trial used a $100{-}130\mathrm{mmHg}$ systolic pressure range for the intensive treatment group.

The OPTIMAL-BP and BEST-II trials both targeted a systolic $\mathrm{BP}<140\mathrm{mmHg}$ for the intensive group; however, BEST-II also included groups targeting different thresholds $(<160\mathrm{mmHg}$ and $<180\mathrm{mmHg}$ ). As to the control group, most trials used a similar goal of $140{-}180~\mathrm{mmHg}$ for systolic pressure, except for the BEST-II trial. Therefore, it seems that lowering systolic pressure below $140\mathrm{mmHg}$ is harmful, but there is still insufficient evidence regarding the ideal goal, although BEST-II suggests no benefit in lowering $\mathrm{BP}<160\mathrm{mmHg}$ [22].  

It is also important to take into account the feasibility of systolic pressure control when considering clinical practice. In some patients, reaching lower pressure goals can be challenging, even with high medication doses, leaving the patient prone to a higher variation range that could lead to worse outcomes [23]. Indeed, BP variability in the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after successful reperfusion seems to correlate more with symptomatic intraparenchymal hemorrhage than absolute BP levels [24].  

Furthermore, this study included only patients with successful reperfusion (mTICI score $\geq2\mathrm{b}$ ), since patients who do not achieve reperfusion would be more adequately managed with the same pressure target as those not submitted to EVT.  

This paper presents a few limitations. The original articles included in this meta-analysis did not adopt a single protocol of intensive BP control, with values for this category ranging from 100 to $140\mathrm{mmHg}$ (systolic), which entails some heterogeneity. The included papers did not analyze patients with low initial ASPECT scores. Therefore, the results do not necessarily apply to patients with pre-procedure extensive infarcted tissue, which may be more subject to reperfusion injury and hemorrhage. Individual patient data metaanalyses could provide additional information, particularly in analyzing the influence of baseline BP levels, time until treatment, and other clinical aspects on treatment outcomes.  

Furthermore, the selected first-choice antihypertensive treatment was a calcium channel blocker in all trials, except for the ENCHANTED2/MT [25]. Nonetheless, a significantly different result would not be expected with other therapies since a class-specific mechanism is not presently solidified to explain a worse outcome with the use of other drugs and all the trials had a minor sample of patients using different antihypertensive agents.  

Regarding this paper’s strengths, all four selected studies were randomized trials with a low risk of bias. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to pool results from these high-quality randomized controlled trials, further solidifying the evidence on this subject. We believe that future studies on the subject could attempt to evaluate individualized BP targets, guided by baseline BP values, collateral status, hemodynamic assessment using transcranial Doppler ultrasound, and advanced neuroimaging.  



# Conclusion  

Intensive blood pressure control is harmful in patients who underwent endovascular therapy for acute ischemic stroke and achieved successful reperfusion. It yields higher rates of functional dependence, with no differences in mortality or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage.
