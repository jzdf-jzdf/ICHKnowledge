# Early Versus Late Initiation of Direct Oral Anticoagulants After Ischemic Stroke in People With Atrial Fibrillation and Hemorrhagic Transformation: Prespecified Subanalysis of the Randomized Controlled ELAN Trial  

Roman Rohner , MD\*; Markus Kneihsl , MD, PhD\*; Martina B. Goeldlin , MD, PhD; Arsany Hakim , MD; Mattia Branca , Ph Stefanie Abend , BSc; Waldo Valenzuela Pinilla , PhD; Sabine Fenzl, MD; Beata Rezny-Kasprzak, MD; Daniel Strbian , MD, PhD; Sven Trelle , MD; Maurizio Paciaroni , MD; Götz Thomalla , MD; Patrik Michel , MD; Krassen Nedeltchev, MD; Thomas Gattringer , MD, PhD; Else C. Sandset , MD, PhD; Leo Bonati , MD; Diana Aguiar de Sousa , MD, PhD; P.N. Sylaja , MD; George Ntaios , MD; Masatoshi Koga , MD, PhD; Zuzana Gdovinova , MD, PhD; Robin Lemmens , MD, PhD; Natan M. Bornstein , MD; Peter Kelly , MD, PhD; Mira Katan , MD; Thomas Horvath, MD; Jesse Dawson , MD†; Urs Fischer , MD, MSc†; for the ELAN Investigators  

BACKGROUND: Whether hemorrhagic transformation (HT) modifies the treatment effect of early compared with late initiation of direct oral anticoagulation in people with ischemic stroke and atrial fibrillation is unknown.  

METHODS: This is a post hoc analysis of the ELAN trial (Early Versus Late Initiation of Direct Oral Anticoagulants in PostIschaemic Stroke Patients With Atrial Fibrillation). The primary outcome was a composite of recurrent ischemic stroke, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, major extracranial bleeding, systemic embolism, or vascular death within 30 days. Secondary outcomes were the individual components, 30- and 90-day functional outcome. We estimated outcomes based on HT, subclassified as hemorrhagic infarction (HI) or parenchymal hemorrhage (PH) on prerandomization imaging (core laboratory rating) using adjusted risk differences between treatment arms.  

RESULTS: Overall, 247 of 1970 participants ( $12.5\%,$ ) had HT (114 HI 1, 77 HI 2, 34 PH 1, 22 PH 2). For the primary outcome, the estimated adjusted risk difference (early versus late) was $-2.2\%$ $95\%$ CI, $-7.8\%$ to $3.5\%$ ) in people with HT (HI: $-4.7\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $-10.8\%$ to $1.4\%]$ ; PH: $6.1\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $-8.5\%$ to $20.6\%]$ ) and $-0.9\%$ $95\%$ CI, $-2.6\%$ to $0.8\%$ ) in people without HT. Numbers of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage were identical in people with and without HT. With early treatment, the estimated adjusted risk difference for poor 90-day functional outcome (modified Rankin Scale score, $_{3-6)}$ was $11.5\%$ $95\%$ CI, − $-0.8\%$ to $23.8\%$ ) in participants with HT (HI: $7.4\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $-6.4\%$ to $21.2\%]$ ; PH: $25.1\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $0.2\%$ to $50.0\%]$ ) and $-2.6\%$ ( $95\%$ CI, $-7.1\%$ to $1.8\%$ ) in people without HT.  

CONCLUSIONS: We found no evidence of major treatment effect heterogeneity or safety concerns with early compared with late direct oral anticoagulation initiation in people with and without HT. However, early direct oral anticoagulation initiation may worsen functional outcomes in people with PH.

# Clinical Perspective  

# What Is New?  

•	 In this post hoc analysis of the ELAN trial (Early Versus Late Initiation of Direct Oral Anticoagulants in Post-Ischaemic Stroke Patients With Atrial Fibrillation), we estimated the treatment effect heterogeneity on safety and efficacy of early compared with late initiation of direct oral anticoagulants in people with ischemic stroke with and without hemorrhagic transformation. Hemorrhagic transformation was subclassified as hemorrhagic infarction or parenchymal hemorrhage.   
•	 We identified no major treatment effect heterogeneity or safety concerns in people with and without hemorrhagic transformation.   
•	 Outcome was similar in people with and those without hemorrhagic infarction, but we observed a $25\%$ higher risk for poor 90-day functional outcome in participants with parenchymal hemorrhage and early compared with late direct oral anticoagulation treatment.  

# What Are the Clinical Implications?  

•	 Early initiation of direct oral anticoagulants $_{(<48}$ hours after ischemic stroke for minor and moderate stroke, 6–7 days for major stroke) in people with hemorrhagic transformation does not increase rates of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage and might be beneficial in people with hemorrhagic infarction types 1 and 2.   
•	 In people with parenchymal hemorrhage, early anticoagulation treatment may worsen functional outcomes, but confirmation based on data from ongoing trials is needed.

# METHODS  

The data that support the findings of this study will be accessible on reasonable request and after approval by the ELAN trial steering committee. Data can be requested through urs. fischer@insel.ch.  

This study is a post hoc analysis of the international, multicenter, randomized controlled ELAN trial,9 which was designed to compare early $\mathord{\left(<48\right.\kern-\nulldelimiterspace}$ hours after ischemic stroke for minor and moderate stroke, 6–7 days for major stroke) with late (3–4 days for minor, 6–7 days for moderate, 12–14 days for major stroke) initiation of DOACs in people with acute ischemic stroke and AF. The ELAN trial was conducted from November 2017 to September 2022 and included ${>}2000$ participants at 103 stroke centers in Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. The study protocol, main results of the trial, and information on data collection have been published previously.10,12,13  

In brief, participants were eligible if they presented with an acute ischemic stroke and had a history of AF or were diagnosed with AF during the index hospitalization. Individuals with transient ischemic attacks or a particularly high risk of intracranial bleeding (eg, multiple cerebral microbleeds) were not included in the study. Participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the early treatment group (the intervention group) or the late treatment group (the guideline-based control group). In the present study, we included all participants from the ELAN trial with available prerandomization brain imaging (either magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] or computed tomography [CT]). Participants with insufficient imaging quality to assess HT were excluded from the study.  

The study protocol received approval from all relevant ethics committees, and written informed consent was given by participants, next of kin or other legal representatives, or an independent physician. All study data were gathered by local investigators and collected in a secuTrial database hosted by the Clinical Trials Unit Bern.  

# Neuroimaging Assessment  

All neuroimaging studies obtained during the trial period were transferred to the imaging core laboratory at the Support Center for Advanced Neuroimaging (Inselspital Bern) by the local site investigators. The core laboratory consisted of 2 boardcertified neuroradiologists (S.F., B.R.K.) who were supported by a neurologist with ample experience in neuroimaging (M.K., magnetic resonance–based ratings) and a neuroradiology resident proficient in neuroimaging diagnostics (R.R., CT-based ratings).  

Prerandomization imaging studies were assessed for the presence and extent of HT with either plain CT scans or magnetic resonance–based images (1.5 or 3 T) that included susceptibility-weighted sequences. If both modalities were available, magnetic resonance–based images were analyzed because of their higher sensitivity for petechial HT.14  

HT was classified according to the ECASS (European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study) II criteria.15 ECASS II distinguishes between 2 types of petechial hemorrhagic infarction (HI) without mass effect: HI 1, characterized by small petechiae, and HI 2, characterized by confluent petechiae. Parenchymal hemorrhage (PH) was also categorized into 2 types: PH 1 denotes a hematoma occupying ${<}30\%$ of the infarcted tissue, and $\mathsf{PH}2$ refers to intracerebral hemorrhages affecting ${\ge}30\%$ of the infarct area.15 We also documented intracerebral hemorrhages occurring remotely from the infarcted tissue (Figure S1).  

According to the ELAN trial protocol, people with PH or remote hemorrhages were intended to be excluded from participation.12 The decision on whether a patient fulfilled the HT inclusion criteria was made at the discretion of the local site investigators. For this post hoc analysis, all prerandomization images underwent re-evaluation by the ELAN trial imaging core laboratory. For cases in which HT was identified or discrepancies between 2 raters occurred, a senior, board-certified neuroradiologist with special expertise in neurovascular imaging (A.H.) and 16 years of neuroimaging experience reviewed the images and had the deciding vote. All raters were blinded to clinical data.  

# Outcomes  

The primary outcome used in this study was the same as in the main ELAN trial analysis and comprised the composite of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), major extracranial bleeding, recurrent ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, or vascular death within 30 days after stroke. sICH was defined as any occurrence of intracranial bleeding that resulted in clinical deterioration and had to be determined by the treating physician.12 Secondary outcomes included the same composite end point at 90 days after stroke and the individual components of the primary outcome, as well as functional outcome according to the modified Rankin Scale at 30 and 90 days after stroke.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Statistical analyses were performed (by M.B.) with Stata version 17.0 (2021) and R version 4.3.1 (2023) according to a statistical analysis plan written by other authors (M.K., R.R., A.H., M.B., S.A., J.D., and U.F.). For the primary analysis, participants were categorized according to the presence of HT on prerandomization imaging (any HT versus no HT) as assessed by the imaging core laboratory. Treatment allocation (early versus late initiation of anticoagulation) was determined according to a modified intention-to-treat strategy, as described previously.10 Descriptive statistics were used to analyze and compare baseline clinical characteristics. Categorical variables are presented as number and percentage, and continuous variables are presented as mean and SD or median and upper and lower quartile.  

For binary outcomes, we performed univariable and multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression to account for the small number of outcome events. Ordinally scaled outcomes were assessed with ordinal logistic regression. Multivariable regressions were adjusted for age (per year), hypertension (binary), infarct location (anterior versus posterior circulation), infarct size (minor versus moderate versus major), prestroke modified Rankin Scale score, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score at admission, and acute treatment (intravenous thrombolysis or endovascular treatment) using the inverse probability weighting method. The results of the models were displayed with relative and absolute differences using the odds ratio (OR) with $95\%$ CI and marginal risk differences with $95\%$ CI, respectively. The marginal risk differences were obtained from the predicted responses from the logistic model for both groups from which the difference was then calculated.  

# Sensitivity Analysis  

We performed 3 sensitivity analyses. First, participants with HT were dichotomized into those with HI (HI 1 and HI 2) and those with PH (PH 1 and PH 2). Individual participants with intracranial hemorrhage outside the infarcted brain tissue (remote hemorrhage) were included in the PH 2 group. Second, participants were stratified according to imaging modality (CT and MRI). In the third sensitivity analysis, we excluded all participants who had undergone thrombolysis or thrombectomy but had no follow-up images before randomization available. The efficacy and safety of early compared with late DOAC treatment stratified by the presence of HT (primary analysis), its subgroups, and the imaging modality (sensitivity analyses) were presented with adjusted ORs (aORs) and adjusted risk differences (aRDs). Owing to the small number of outcome events and consequently the limited statistical power, this study provides hypothesis-generating evidence, presenting point estimates and $95\%$ CIs without reporting $P$ values. Furthermore, the interaction between the presence and absence of HT and the randomization group was calculated with the firth logistic regression model including the variables and their interaction, whereas the different ORs were derived from the subset of the data separately.  

# Interrater Reliabilities and Procedures for Handling Missing Data  

Interrater reliabilities for the presence of HT and its subtypes on prerandomization neuroimaging among different raters in the core imaging laboratory were assessed with the Gwet AC1 and Cohen κ.  

The statistical analysis plan prespecified performance of multivariate multiple imputation by chained equations if the total number of missing outcomes in 1 group was ${>}5\%$ . This was not required for the present analysis given the high data completeness (missingness $<5\%$ ).  

# RESULTS  

For this post hoc analysis of the ELAN trial, 43 of 2013 participants $(2.1\%)$ had to be excluded because of insufficient brain imaging quality. Another 37 participants were excluded from the primary analysis because their primary outcome was missing (24 participants died as a result of a nonvascular cause during follow-up, 12 withdrew consent, and 1 was lost to follow-up), leading to a final study cohort of 1933 participants with ischemic stroke and AF (median age, 77 years; $45.5\%$ female; Figure 1). Of all included participants, 247 ( $12.5\%)$ showed HT on prerandomization imaging, of whom 114 had HI 1, 77 had HI 2, 34 had PH 1, and 22 had PH 2. Core imaging laboratory interrater reliabilities for the presence of HT were 0.79 (0.70–0.87) using the Gwet AC1 and 0.63 (0.49–0.76) using the Cohen κ.  

# Baseline Characteristics  

Age, prestroke disability, and comorbidities were well balanced between the subgroups. The median $\mathrm{CHA_{2}DS_{2}}$ -VASc score was 5 points in most groups. Median National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores before randomization in people with HT were 6 (interquartile range, 2–12 with early treatment) and 5 (interquartile range, 2–11) with late treatment. In participants without HT, median National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores were 2 (interquartile range, $^{1-6)}$ with early treatment and 3 (interquartile range, $^{1-6)}$ with late treatment. Based on infarct size, HT was frequently observed in participants with major $(30.4\%,$ early treatment; $22.7\%,$ late treatment) and moderate ( $(14.8\%,$ early treatment; $14.4\%,$ , late treatment) stroke. Participants with minor strokes showed low rates of HT $1.6\%$ , early treatment; $1.9\%,$ , late treatment). At the screening visit, single antiplatelet therapy (mostly aspirin) was used in 53 participants $(40.5\%)$ with HT in the early and in 57 $(49.1\%)$ in the late treatment arm. In the non-HT group, 420 participants $(49.3\%)$ were treated with single antiplatelet in the early group and 500 $(57.4\%)$ ) in the late treatment group (Table 1; Table S1).

For participants with HI, the aOR for poor functional outcome at 90 days (early versus late treatment) was 1.37 $(95\%$ CI, 0.76 to 2.47) with an estimated aRD of $7.39\%$ $(95\%$ CI, $-6.39\%$ to $21.18\%$ ). # DISCUSSION  

In this post hoc analysis of data from the ELAN trial, we investigated the estimated treatment effect heterogeneity on safety and efficacy of early compared with late, guideline-based initiation of DOACs in participants with HT. We found neither major safety concerns nor any treatment effect heterogeneity between people with and those without HT and ischemic stroke randomized to early compared with late DOAC initiation. Outcome in both treatment arms was similar in people with and those without HI, but we observed a $25\%$ higher risk for a poor 90-day functional outcome in participants with PH and early compared with late DOAC treatment.  

The randomized controlled Swedish TIMING trial (Timing of Oral Anticoagulant Therapy in Acute Ischemic Stroke With Atrial Fibrillation) and the Swiss ELAN trial have recently described numerically lower rates of event recurrence in people with ischemic stroke and AF who underwent early compared with late DOAC initiation.9,10 No safety concerns were reported in either of the trials9,10 or in pooled data analyses of prospective observational studies and small randomized controlled trials.6,8,16 However, there is a lack of data on early DOAC initiation in people with HT, a condition that leads most clinicians to postpone anticoagulation treatment because of concerns about bleeding complications.6,8–11,16–18 This post hoc analysis of data from the ELAN trial10 on the timing of DOAC initiation in people with asymptomatic HT provides no evidence that delaying DOAC initiation is safer or more effective in people with HT than early initiation of anticoagulation. In contrast, delaying DOAC treatment is likely to increase event recurrences. For people with HI, the number needed to treat (based on the adjusted risk difference point estimate) was 21 to avoid 1 outcome event within 30 days after the index stroke. No symptomatic intracranial bleeding complications were observed in participants who underwent early DOAC treatment. These data suggest that early DOAC initiation $(<48$ hours after ischemic stroke for minor and moderate stroke, 6–7 days for major stroke) is reasonable in people with HI.

However, not all HT subgroups appear to benefit from earlier anticoagulation treatment. Although people with PH are known to have an unfavorable poststroke prognosis,19 a fact reinforced by our findings $(61\%$ of participants with PH had a 90-day modified Rankin Scale score of 3–6), our results indicate that early DOAC treatment, as opposed to late initiation, independently raises the probability of a poor 90-day functional outcome by $25\%$ . These findings are surprising given the absence of symptomatic intracranial bleeding in participants with PH. In this context, recent observations of a $20\%$ rate of HT progression in people after early poststroke DOAC initiation should be taken into consideration.8 Although in that study HT worsening did not lead to abrupt clinical deterioration and the sample size was too small to report on outcomes,8 subclinical HT progression with associated local effects that may affect regeneration processes represents the most likely pathomechanism behind our findings.20–22  

Notably, PH 2 and remote hemorrhage were the primary contributors to the heightened risk of unfavorable poststroke outcomes in the PH group if treated early (risk difference for PH 2/remote hemorrhage, $38\%$ favoring late treatment). In the early phase after stroke, the initiation of DOAC treatment is likely to be harmful in people with PH 2/remote hemorrhage. However, our results may not endorse the avoidance of early DOAC therapy in people with PH 1. Rather, they point toward a subgroup of people for whom an individualized benefitrisk assessment might be appropriate. It should be borne in mind that, according to the ELAN trial study protocol, PH was an exclusion criterion and was identified only from the present post hoc analysis by the imaging core laboratory. This underscores the challenges associated with determining PH within acute stroke populations. Moreover, the selection of erroneously included participants with PH might therefore not be representative for a routine stroke population, and we observed imbalances between the PH groups (early versus late; Table S5). Our results must therefore be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis generating.  

Nevertheless, the effect of early DOAC treatment in people with ischemic stroke and PH warrants further attention and appears to be a promising target for subanalyses of ongoing trials that regularly include participants with PH (ie, OPTIMAS [Optimal Timing of Anticoagulation After Acute Ischemic Stroke], NCT03759938) and a planned individual participant data meta-analysis from 4 trials (TIMING, ELAN, OPTIMAS, and START [Optimal Delay Time to Initiate Anticoagulation After Ischemic Stroke in Atrial Fibrillation]).9,10,23,24  

Moreover, the substantial proportion of misclassifications of HT subtypes (ie, underestimation of HT severity) by local investigators underscores the importance of careful neuroimaging interpretation in clinical practice.  

Further limitations may have influenced our results. The present study was a post hoc analysis of a trial10 that was not originally designed to assess the impact of asymptomatic HT on outcome. Consequently, we lacked follow-up brain imaging investigations and, as a result, cannot provide information on the proportion of people with subclinical progression of HT. Infarct volumes were unavailable, and because infarct size is the primary determinant of HT, the absence of volumetric adjustments may have led to some imprecision in our analysis. Volumetric analyses, particularly in people with HT, are complex and time-consuming processes.25 Nevertheless, we were able to address infarct size by categorizing it into 3 groups (minor, moderate, major), which should have minimized any potential bias. MRI is the gold standard for identifying small petechial HI because it has a higher sensitivity than CT.14 In the present analysis, MRI-based images were available in $43\%$ of participants. Therefore, in people who had undergone CT but not MRI, small HI might not have been detected, which should be considered in the interpretation of our results. However, results remained unchanged in sensitivity analysis stratified by imaging modality (CT and MRI). In severely affected people with acute ischemic stroke, a clinical deterioration might be difficult to assess, and sICH could have potentially been missed in few cases. However, the liberal definition of sICH, encompassing any intracranial hemorrhage that leads to a clinical worsening, along with the meticulous review of all bleeding events and all serious adverse events by an independent clinical event committee,12 should have avoided a substantial underreporting of sICH in the present study. For this study, the most recent imaging before randomization was used to classify HT. According to the ELAN trial protocol,12 prerandomization follow-up imaging after thrombolysis or thrombectomy was strongly encouraged but not mandatory. Consequently, in some participants $(\mathsf{n}{=}112)$ , no images were available after reperfusion treatment and before randomization. However, in a sensitivity analysis excluding these participants with missing follow-up images, study outcomes remained unchanged.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

Among people with ischemic stroke and AF, we did not observe any major treatment effect heterogeneity or identify any major safety concerns according to the presence of HT and early compared with late DOAC initiation. Whereas outcome in both treatment arms was similar for people with and without HI, for participants with PH, early DOAC treatment increased the risk for a poor 90- day functional outcome. Our results suggest that early initiation of DOACs in people with HI is acceptable on the balance of risks and benefits. In people with PH, the potential negative impact of early treatment on functional outcomes remains to be confirmed and requires further data from ongoing trials.
