# Mechanistic Evaluation of Diffusion Weighted Hyperintense Lesions After Large Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage: A Subgroup Analysis of MISTIE III

# Abstract  

Background:  Ischemic lesions on diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) are common after acute spontaneous intracer‑ ebral hemorrhage (ICH) but are poorly understood for large ICH volumes $(>30\mathrm{mL},$ . We hypothesized that large blood pressure drops and effect modification by cerebral small vessel disease markers on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are associated with DWI lesions.  

Methods:  This was an exploratory analysis of participants in the Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus Alteplase for Intrac‑ erebral Hemorrhage Evacuation phase 3 trial with protocolized brain MRI scans within 7 days from ICH. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to assess biologically relevant factors associated with DWI lesions, and relationships between DWI lesions and favorable ICH outcomes (modified Rankin Scale 0–3).  

Results:  Of 499 enrolled patients, 300 had MRI at median 7.5 days (interquartile range 7–8), and 178 $(59\%)$ had DWI lesions. The incidence of DWI lesions was higher in patients with systolic blood pressure (SBP) reduction $\geq80\mathrm{mm}\ H_{9}$ in first $24\mathsf{h}$ $(76\%)$ . In adjusted models, factors associated with DWI lesions were as follows: admission intraventricular hematoma volume $(p=0.03^{\cdot}$ ), decrease in $\mathsf{SBP}\geq80\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ from admission to day 1 $(p=0.03)$ , and moderate-tosevere white matter disease $(p=0.01$ ). Patients with DWI lesions had higher odds of severe disability at 1 month $(p=0.04)$ , 6 months $(p=0.036)$ , and 12 months $(p<0.01$ ). No evidence of effect modification by cerebral small vessel disease on blood pressure was found.  

Conclusions:  In patients with large hypertensive ${\mathsf{ICH}},$ white matter disease, intraventricular hemorrhage volume, and large reductions in SBP over the first $24\mathsf{h}$ were independently associated with DWI lesions. Further investigation of potential hemodynamic mechanisms of ischemic injury after large ICH is warranted.  

Keywords:  Intracerebral hemorrhage, Blood pressure management after intracerebral hemorrhage, White matter disease  



# Introduction  

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), the most devastating type of stroke, is associated with significant mortality and disability [1]. Acute ischemic lesions occurring in the first few weeks after ICH are common [2]. Emerging data have implicated preexisting cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) as an underlying etiology, regardless of hematoma location [3, 4]. Treatment interventions, particularly blood pressure control, performed in the hyperacute period of ICH may also play a role [5–7], although blood pressure reduction in the ATACH-2 trial did not support an association in small-to-moderate ICH [8]. An individual, pooled analysis of 1,752 patients in several large trials/studies, including the Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus Alteplase for Intracerebral Hemorrhage Evacuation phase 3 (MISTIE III) trial, showed independent association of initial systolic blood pressure (SBP) as well as cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) and white matter disease with diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) hyperintense lesions, but no relationship with change in SBP over the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ [4].  

The occurrence of DWI lesions after ICH is still not well understood. Prior studies evaluating DWI lesions after ICH mostly included small ICH volume, which may limit generalizability of these findings. DWI lesions are known to occur both in the immediate vicinity of the index hematoma and geographically distant from the ICH. However, DWI lesions are usually evaluated excluding the perihematomal region, and it is uncertain whether lesions at each location have different mechanisms. DWI lesions may result from an occlusive small vessel arteriopathy. Therefore, it is conceivable that acute blood pressure reduction may exacerbate the association of SVD and DWI lesions, especially in the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ when blood pressure alterations in ICH are most pronounced. In this study, we hypothesized that blood pressure changes play a significant role in the occurrence of DWI lesions in large ICH, that perihematomal DWI lesions and remote lesions have different mechanisms, and that CSVD modifies the association between blood pressure changes and DWI lesions. We addressed these unresolved questions by leveraging data from a multicenter trial with moderate-to-large ICH and protocolized neuroimaging.  



# Methods  



# Study Design and Population  

This is an exploratory post hoc analysis of data from the MISTIE III trial [9]. The study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT01827046. MISTIE III was a multicenter, double-blind, randomized study comparing imageguided, catheter-based removal of blood clot with intrahematoma alteplase ( $1.0~\mathrm{mg}$ alteplase every $^{8\textrm{h}}$ for up to nine doses) or standard medical care. The trial enrolled patients aged 18  years or older with spontaneous, nontraumatic, supratentorial ICH of $30~\mathrm{mL}$ or more, historical modified Rankin Scale of 0–1, symptom onset within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of diagnostic computed tomography (CT) scan, Glasgow Coma Scale $<14$ , or National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale $\mathrm{(NIHSS)}>6$ . A conventional angiogram, CT angiogram, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or magnetic angiogram was obtained prior to enrollment to rule out aneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, or other vascular anomaly. Inclusion criteria required $\mathrm{SBP}<180\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ sustained for $^{6\textrm{h}}$ recorded closest to the time of randomization. All centers managed arterial blood pressure based on American Heart Association guidelines, although specific goals were not required and adherence was not monitored [10, 11]. Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) infratentorial hemorrhage; (2) macrovascular cause of hemorrhage, hemorrhagic conversion of an ischemic infarct, or recurrence of a recent $(<1$ year) hemorrhage; and (3) uncorrected coagulopathy. The study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Medical Institutions Review Board.  



# Neuroimaging Acquisition  

Patients meeting the following criteria were included in the DWI lesion analysis: (1) one of two protocolized MRI scans obtained during hospitalization, on day of randomization or, at 7–10  days after randomization, with the later MRI used if two were performed; (2) MRI with adequate quality sequences for assessment of DWI lesions and CSVD features including T1, T2-weighted, axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), T2-weighted gradient-recalled echo (GRE), or axial susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), and DWI sequences.  

Enrollment required ICH volume to be stable at least $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ later by a second CT (using the ABC/2 method) [12]. We reported the presence of subarachnoid hemorrhage and hydrocephalus on CT scan at stability. All MRI scans were performed on $1.5~\mathrm{T}$ to $3.0\mathrm{~T~}$ scanners. MRIs were reviewed by two independent board-certified neurologists and a neurosurgeon (S.M.C., W.C.Z., and Y.L.) blinded to outcomes. Disagreements were adjudicated by W.C.Z.  



# Measurements  

Diffusion weighted imaging lesions were defined as lesions with hyperintense signal on DWI and hypointense on ADC, relative to the normal-appearing brain. The DWI and ADC sequences were reviewed with other MRI sequences to assure DWI lesions were distinct from the hematoma and exclude T2 shine through. Perihematomal DWI lesions were defined as DWI lesions outside a 1-cm rim around the hematoma, but within the region of perihematomal edema. DWI lesions were considered remote if outside the area of perihematomal edema. DWI lesions were assessed for size $(\geq20\mathrm{mm},<20\mathrm{mm})$ [13, 14] and location: (1) ipsilateral or contralateral to the hematoma, and (2) perihematomal or remote.  

We also evaluated white matter hyperintensities, lacunar infarcts, enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS), CMBs, and cortical superficial siderosis. White matter disease was defined as lesions that were hyperintense on FLAIR and T2-weighted images and not present or with a faint hypointensity on T1-weighted sequences and were rated using the Fazekas score [15]. Moderate-to-severe white matter disease was defined as Fazekas score 2–3. CMBs were defined as round signal voids with associated blooming around $2{-}10~\mathrm{mm}$ identified on T2-weighted GRE or SWI sequences. Lacunar infarct was defined as a round or ovoid, subcortical, or deep, fluid-filled cavity $3{-}15~\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter, assessed on FLAIR. EPVS were defined as fluid-filled spaces that follow the typical course of a vessel as it penetrates gray or white matter. EPVS were evaluated at two locations including basal ganglia and centrum semiovale. Cortical superficial siderosis was defined by a distinct pattern of hypointense blood-breakdown products on T2-weighted GRE or SWI images representing hemosiderin deposition limited to cortical sulci. The mean interrater agreement for all MRI features in $10\%$ of randomly selected MRI scans was 0.82. All classifications were prespecified according to the STRIVE consensus [13].  

Blood pressure measurements included SBP, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP) on admission, maximum and minimum SBP, DBP, and MAP on day 1 and day 6.  



# Outcomes  

The primary outcome was the presence of DWI on MRI performed within the first 10  days after randomization. Secondary outcomes were blinded assessments of mortality and poor outcome defined as a modified Rankin Score greater than 3 at 1, 6, and 12 months.  



# Statistical Analysis  

The relationship between DWI lesions, ICH volume, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) volume, and blood pressure variables was explored graphically using scatterplots. We used the Mann–Whitney $U$ -test for continuous variables because the data were not normally distributed. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s $\chi^{2}$ test.  

We used multivariable logistic regression to assess factors associated with DWI lesions, excluding patients who had cerebral angiography prior to MRI to avoid confounding (DWI lesions caused by procedure). In the first analysis, we evaluated factors associated with DWI lesions in any location. For the second multivariable analysis (included in supplementary file), we evaluated factors associated with DWI lesions at each location separately and excluded patients with DWI lesions in both locations. Baseline variables with a predetermined significance of $p<0.1$ for the outcome of interest were entered into logistic regression models and a backward elimination strategy was used to develop the multivariable model optimizing area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Multicollinearity evaluation was performed for each model. We analyzed admission SBP and change in SBP from admission to $24\mathrm{~h~}$ (ΔSBP) as both continuous and categorical variables. ΔSBP was calculated by subtracting the first SBP reading at the time of presentation to the emergency department and the lowest SBP recorded in the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after symptom onset. We analyzed heterogeneity of the effect of CSVD features on MRI (moderate-to-severe WMHs and CMBs) on occurrence of DWI lesions and on 1-year outcome by including an interaction term between admission SBP (and ΔSBP) as continuous variables and each imaging feature that was significant in the univariate analysis (severe WMH vs. not severe; CMBs present vs. absent), in regression models adjusted for appropriate covariates. We also performed prespecified subgroup analyses, stratified by the location of DWI lesions, perihematomal and remote. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 14.0, College Station, TX). All analyses were two-tailed, and significance level was determined by $p<0.05$ . The alpha with Bonferroni correction for the second part of the analysis with multiple comparisons included in the supplementary tables, remote versus perihematomal lesions, was 0.025.  



# Results  



# Patient Characteristics  

A total of 499 patients were enrolled in the MISTIE III trial, of whom 300 $(60\%)$ had an MRI scan at a median of 7.5  days (interquartile range 7–8; see Fig. supp-1 for inclusion flow chart). Of these, $34\%$ were lobar hematomas and $66\%$ were deep hematomas. Table  1 shows characteristics stratified by presence or absence of one or more ischemic DWI lesions. DWI lesions were found in 178/300 $(59\%)$ patients. Of these, 141/178 $(79\%)$ had perihematomal lesions, 96/178 $(54\%)$ had remote lesions, and 60/178 $(63\%)$ had DWIHLs in both locations. Similarly, DWI lesions were ipsilateral to the hematoma in $59\%$ (105/178) of patients, contralateral only in 64/178 $(36\%)$ and present bilaterally in $5\%$ (9/178).  

Patients with and without DWI lesions had similar demographics and comorbidities with the exception of prior anticoagulation use being higher among those without DWI lesions ( $9.8\%$ vs. $3.9\%$ ). Patients with DWI lesions had larger ICH volumes, higher NIHSS, and a nonsignificant tendency to having deep ICH location and IVH. All blood pressure variables were significantly higher in patients with DWI lesions compared with those without, including admission MAP, SBP, and DBP. Table 2 shows the imaging characteristics of patients with and without one or more DWI lesions. Half of the DWI lesions were $\geq20~\mathrm{mm}$ and $13\%$ were $<20~\mathrm{mm}$ . $37\%$ had lesions of both sizes. The median time from symptom onset to MRI was 7.0 (5.4–7.9) days. Patients with DWI lesions had a greater burden of moderate-to-severe white matter disease $(p=0.01)$ and greater frequency of microbleeds $(p<0.01)$ . Other MRI features of CSVD were not different between groups.

To better understand the relationship between blood pressure and DWI lesions, we evaluated categorical admission SBP and $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}$ . There was a higher incidence of DWI lesions $(82\%)$ in patients with $\mathrm{SBP}\geq220~\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ compared with patients with lower SBP $(p<0.01)$ , and in patients with $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}$ change $\geq80~\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ $(76\%)$ compared with patients with lower $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}$ $(p<0.01\$ ; see Figs.  1 and 2). We also evaluated if the $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}$ that occurred on day 1 was persistent through the first week and for association of ΔSBP from day 1 to day 6 with DWI lesions. We report minimal difference between SBP on day 6 compared with day 1, and no significant association with DWI lesion presence versus absence (Table 1; Fig. 3).  

In the multivariable logistic regression model, factors associated with DWI lesions were admission IVH volume (adjusted Odds Ratio [aOR] 1.76; $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 1.05–2.96, $p{=}0.03,$ ), $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}\geq80~\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ (aOR 2.67 [1.13–6.32], $p{=}0.03$ ), and moderateto-severe white matter disease (aOR 2.21 [1.19–4.13], $p{=}0.01\$ ). ICH volume and history of hypertension were almost significant with $p$ values of 0.05 (Table 3). A sensitivity analysis was also performed adding the treatment variable (medical vs. surgical treatment), and there were no significant changes in the results. A second sensitivity analysis was performed using admission $\mathrm{SBP}{>}220\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ instead of $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}\geq80\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ , as $49/50$ patients who presented with a $\mathrm{SBP}{>}220\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ had a drop $\mathrm{of}{\geq}80\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ . We found that admission $\mathrm{SBP}{\geq}220\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ is significantly associated with DWI lesions in the multivariable analysis adjusted for the same variables (aOR 3.65 [1.20–11.03], $p{=}022$ ).

# Secondary Outcomes  

Presence of a DWI lesion was associated with higher odds for severe disability at 1  month (aOR 2.29 [1.03–5.07], $p{=}0.04,$ , 6  months (aOR 1.79 [1.03–3.11], $p{=}0.036)$ , and 12  months (aOR 1.95 [1.12–3.38], $p{<}0.01\$ ) in fully adjusted logistic regression models (Table  4). There was no significant association between DWI lesions and mortality at any timepoint.  



# Subgroup Analyses by Location, Perihematomal Versus Remote  

Perihematomal only DWI lesions were more common (81/178), than remote only (36/178). Figure  4 shows an example of the two types of lesions. Demographics, comorbidities, and ICH characteristics by location are reported in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2. Compared with patients without DWI lesions, patients with perihematomal DWI lesions were younger with deep location ICH, significantly larger ICH volume, higher NIHSS at enrollment, and higher admission SBP and $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}.$ Patients with only remote DWI lesions were more likely to be African American with lobar ICH location and higher admission SBP compared with patients without DWI lesions.  

In the multivariable logistic regression model, there were no factors associated with perihematomal DWI lesions. For remote DWI lesions, the only factor was ΔSBP $20{-}39\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ (aOR 12.9 [2.9–56], $p<0.01\$ ) and $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}{\geq}80~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ (aOR 13.3 [2.67–66], $p<0.01\$ . Perihematomal lesions were associated with severe disability at one month (aOR 4.2 [1.2–15.3], $p{=}0.02^{\cdot}$ , but it did not reach statistical significance at 6  months (aOR 1.98 [1.02–3.88], $p{=}0.04,$ and 12  months (1.98 [1.02–3.87], $p{=}0.04)$ (Supplemental Table  3). Remote only DWI lesions were not associated with mortality or functional outcomes in adjusted models. A sensitivity analysis was also performed adding the treatment variable (medical vs. surgical treatment), and there were no significant changes in the results.  



# Discussion  

In this post hoc exploratory analysis of the MISTIE III data set comprising patients with large ICH, factors associated with DWI lesions were larger admission hematoma volume, $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}{\geq}80\mathrm{mmHg}$ and moderate-to-severe white matter disease. A $\Delta\mathrm{SBP}{\geq}80~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ resulted in twofold higher odds of having DWI lesions as compared with patients with ΔSBP decrease of less than $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ . The presence of DWI lesions was independently associated with higher odds for early and late neurological disability but not mortality. MRI features of white matter disease did not modify the effect of SBP changes on DWI lesions, when measured as either an elevation $>220\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ on admission, or as an early reduction $>80~\mathrm{mm}$ Hg.

# Blood Pressure Changes Associated with DWI Lesions  

The association of acute blood pressure lowering with DWI lesions has been previously reported [2, 5–7]. One recent pooled analysis of several cohorts suggested that change in blood pressure in the acute phase of ICH is not linked to DWI lesions. However, this study included patients with ICH who had small-to-moderate ICH volumes and did not assess blood pressure thresholds [4]. In the present study, we investigated the relationship between categorical SBP and DWI lesions; we found that admission $\mathrm{SBP}{\geq}220\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ and a reduction in the $\mathrm{SBP}\geq80\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ , were both associated with DWI lesions. These findings in conjunction with the results of a secondary analysis of the ATACH-2 trial, in which intensive BP lowering in patients who initially presented with an SBP of $250\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ or higher, was associated with poor outcomes [16], suggests that a “one-size fits all” approach to BP management in the acute phase of ICH may indeed be harmful. # Location of DWI Lesions  

Diffusion weighted imaging lesions occurring in the perihematomal location were more common than remote in our cohort. Gioia et  al. [18] also reported a higher frequency of perihematomal lesions compared to remote lesions ( $38\%$ vs. $14\%$ ) in a small study with median ICH volume of $9.8~\mathrm{mL}$ . Similar to our study, the authors reported a significant correlation between hematoma volume and perihematomal DWI lesions, but no association between maximal volume of perihematoma tissue reaching ischemic thresholds and maximal SBP drop at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ in either remote or perihematomal lesions [18]. In contrast, we found that in large ICH, both lesion locations were associated with higher admission SBP and with maximal change over first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ in univariate analysis, although the latter association only remained significant in the multivariable analysis for remote lesions. In the prior study, median maximal SBP drop during the first $24{\mathrm{h}}{\mathrm{was}}<30{\mathrm{mmHg}}$ which was not significantly different between groups and may have limited the impact of blood pressure change on ischemic lesions. In our study, we report significant differences in median SBP during first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ between the group with DWI lesions $(60\mathrm{mmHg})$ vs. without $(50~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg})$ and a significant association of SBP reduction with remote DWI lesions fo $\mathbf{r}{>}80\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ . Garg et  al. [6] similarly reported that remote DWI lesions were associated with a greater mean decrease from baseline BP values within $96\mathrm{~h~}$ from symptom onset. These data support the concept that perihematomal DWI hyperintensity (with associated decreased apparent diffusion coefficient) likely represents cytotoxic edema that is not associated with blood pressure dependent ischemia. Remote DWI hyperintensities, however, especially in patients with large ICH volumes may be influenced by the amplitude of SBP elevation around symptom onset and the magnitude of SBP reduction in the first $24\mathrm{{h}_{i}}$ , especially at relative extremes of SBP where impaired cerebral autoregulation may play a role [19]. Modest BP lowering after acute ICH on the other hand appears to be associated with well-preserved perihematomal perfusion and blood flow.  

Perihematomal DWI lesions were associated with higher odds for early neurological disability, and remote DWI lesions were not. This may reflect lack of sample power, remote lesions versus perihematomal $\overset{\cdot}{n}=36$ vs. $n=81$ ); however, it seems premature to conclude that blood pressure reduction is inconsequential based on a small number of remote DWI lesions not significantly impacting clinical outcomes.  



# MRI Features of CSVD Associated with DWI Lesions  

Several studies have identified an association between DWI lesions and CMBs and white matter disease [2, 20, 21], suggesting that DWI lesions may result from an occlusive small vessel arteriopathy and contribute to the vascular-related brain damage in ICH. The MISTIE III cohort included both lobar and deep ICHs, and likely included patients with both hypertensive and cerebral amyloid angiopathy, both of which are associated with MRI features of CSVD. In this study, we found an independent association between DWI lesions and moderateto-severe white matter disease, but also with a history of hypertension present in nearly all patients with DWI lesions. Both factors support the contribution of an underlying microangiopathy to DWI lesion formation in ICH. Because white matter disease and CMBs frequently co-exist, it is possible that residual confounding obscured an association with DWI lesions. We did not find significant effect modification of SVD (white matter disease or CMBs) on the effect of blood pressure on DWI lesions. This is perhaps not surprising due to the lack of association of either SVD or blood pressure changes with perihematomal DWI lesions, the most common location, and the low number of remote lesions where there were weak associations with white matter disease and CMBs. Gioia et al. similarly found that remote DWI lesions were associated with leukoaraiosis although perihematomal lesions were not [18]. Other studies have also reported that white matter disease did not modify the effect of BP lowering on overall outcomes in ICH [22, 23].  

This study has several limitations. First, our results may not be generalizable due to stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria of the MISTIE III trial. Second, although 300 patients had an MRI scan, this study was not powered to study subgroups such as patients with remote or perihematomal lesions only and included a smaller number of patients with remote DWI lesions $(n=36)$ . It remains unclear whether remote DWI lesions would be associated with disability in an adequately powered analysis, or whether these may represent a reversible phenomenon. Most patients who presented with a $\mathrm{SBP}{>}220\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ also experienced a SBP drop $\mathrm{of}{>}80\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ , therefore it remains unclear if the association with DWI lesions was driven by severe hypertension or the significant drop in SBP. Given no radiological signs of posterior reversible encephalopathy on MRI, it seems more likely that DWI lesions were driven by the significant drop in SBP.  

Third, we cannot exclude selection bias for MRI scans although we did not find significant clinical differences between patients with and without an MRI (See Supplementary Table  4). Strengths of this study include multicenter international representation of sites, relatively large sample size, protocolized time for MRI, and detailed characterization of DWI lesions.  

Lastly, as this is an exploratory analysis, we did not perform a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons for all the variables included in the tables, which increases the risk of type I error.

In predominantly hypertensive patients with large ICH, DWI lesions were common and independently associated with greater admission hematoma volume, initial SBP elevation, magnitude of SBP lowering in the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , and presence of moderate-to-severe white matter disease on MRI. DWI lesions resulted in higher odds of both early and late disability.
