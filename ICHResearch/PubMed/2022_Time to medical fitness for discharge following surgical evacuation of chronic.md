# Original Article  

# Time to medical fitness for discharge following surgical evacuation of chronic subdural haematoma with general or regional anaesthesia: a prospective randomised trial  

R. Hestin,1 H. Kamga Totouom,1 T. Gaberel,2 J. J. Parienti3 and J. L. Hanouz4  

1 Anaesthetist, 4 Professor, Department of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care 2 Professor, Department of Neurosurgery, 3 Professor, Department of Clinical Research and Biostatistics, Caen University Hospital and Caen Normandy University, Caen, France  

# Summary  

General or regional anaesthesia can be used for chronic subdural haematoma evacuation, but no study has compared these types of anaesthesia in terms of peri-operative outcomes. This single-centre, prospective, randomised study included adult patients $\mathrm{(age\geq18}$ years) undergoing surgical chronic subdural haematoma evacuation. Patients were randomly allocated to general (target-controlled total intravenous anaesthesia with propofol and remifentanil) or regional anaesthesia (bilateral scalp block with remifentanil sedation if required). The primary outcome measure was duration of hospital stay, based on the day patients were judged by an investigator blinded from the allocation group to be medically fit for discharge. Secondary outcomes included: rate of regional anaesthesia failure; rate of intra-operative and postoperative adverse events at 24 hours; and pain scores at 24 hours. Data from 60 patients were analysed (30 general anaesthesia and 30 regional anaesthesia). Median (IQR [range]) time until patients were judged medically fit for discharge was 3 (3–3 [2–10]) days and 3 (2–5 [2–15]) days for general and regional anaesthesia, respectively $(\mathsf{p}=0.700).$ . Regional anaesthesia failed in two patients. There were more intra-operative adverse events in patients who received general anaesthesia (25 vs. 11, respectively; $\mathsf{p}=0.001$ ). The occurrence of postoperative adverse events was similar for general and regional anaesthesia (16 vs. 13 patients, respectively; $\mathrm{~p~}=~0.605;$ . In patients requiring chronic subdural haematoma evacuation, general and regional anaesthesia are comparable in terms of duration of time until medically fit for discharge and occurrence of postoperative complications. The rate of intra-operative adverse events (mainly arterial hypotension) is greater with general anaesthesia.

# Introduction  

Chronic subdural haematoma (CSDH) is a common neurosurgical condition which occurs typically in older patients [1], with the peak age for occurrence being between the ages of 80 and 89 y [2]. The overall incidence in the general population of CSDH is estimated as 1.7–20.6 per 100,000 people per year, with an expected increase according to the demographic shift towards an ageing population [1, 2]. Patients who are asymptomatic can be managed with conservative medical treatment, but once clinical or radiological signs of cerebral compression are observed, surgical evacuation is required. This is the most common neurosurgical operation in older patients with a reported incidence of 58–77 per 100,000 per year in patients aged ${>}70$ y; this compares with a rate of 5 per 100,000 per year in the general population [1–3]. In the USA, CSDH evacuation has been projected to be the most common neurosurgical operation by the year 2030 [3].  

The peri-operative care of patients requiring surgical CSDH evacuation can be challenging. First, there are specific age-related changes in physiology and pharmacology. Second, older patients often have multiple comorbidities and are receiving chronic drug treatments [4–6]. Third, pre-operative evaluation can be difficult due to cognitive impairment related to CSDH [7–9]. Finally, emergency and unscheduled surgery are associated with increased peri-operative risks [10].  

Surgical CSDH evacuation can be performed under general anaesthesia or local anaesthesia with sedation [11, 12]. General anaesthesia for surgical CSDH evacuation provides a secure airway, pain control and immobility but increases the risk of peri-operative cardiovascular and respiratory complications, especially in older patients. Local anaesthetic infiltration or scalp block with sedation can be associated with intra-operative anxiety, agitation, patient discomfort and compromise of the airway, especially if sedation is inadequate [13, 14]. These complications may result in suboptimal surgical conditions requiring unplanned conversion to general anaesthesia.  

No study has compared general and regional anaesthesia in terms of postoperative outcomes following surgical CSDH evacuation. The aim of this study was to compare time to medical fitness for discharge between patients receiving general or regional anaesthesia, in addition to describing global postoperative complications.  

# Methods  

Ethical approval was gained and the study was registered before patient enrolment. The study followed the consolidated standards of reporting trials (CONSORT) guidelines [15]. All patients aged ${\geq}18$ y who were admitted with CSDH and required surgical evacuation (according to neurosurgical decision) were eligible. The diagnosis of CSDH was based on cerebral computed tomography (CT). We did not study patients meeting any of the following criteria: refusal to participate; pre-operative agitation precluding regional anaesthesia; history of disability with a modified Rankin score $>2$ ; pregnant and breastfeeding women; associated intracranial lesions on cerebral CT scan; and/or allergy to local anaesthetics. If possible, written informed consent was obtained from the patient before random allocation. Otherwise, according to the French law, informed consent from a legal surrogate was obtained. Written informed consent was then obtained subsequently from the patient as soon as possible before hospital discharge according to the patient’s cognitive status. Patients were randomly allocated to general or regional anaesthesia according to a computer-generated randomisation list. The patient, surgeon and attending anaesthetist could not be blinded to the allocation. However, the principal investigator in charge of recording postoperative data (including the primary outcome) was blinded to the allocation group.  

Standardised intra-operative care for all patients was as follows. No premedication was given. In the operating theatre, an intravenous cannula was inserted and patients were monitored with continuous five-lead electrocardiography, pulse oximetry and automated blood pressure measurement (every $2.5~\mathrm{{min}}$ during induction of anaesthesia and every 5 min during surgery). Dexamethasone $8~\mathrm{mg}$ was given intravenously either at the induction of general anaesthesia or before regional anaesthesia as prophylaxis against postoperative nausea and vomiting. In the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU), droperidol $1.25~\mathrm{mg}$ intravenously was given in the event of nausea or vomiting.  

Patients allocated to receive general anaesthesia were monitored with bispectral index (Covidien, Dublin, Ireland) in addition to standard monitoring. Patients were preoxygenated with $100\%$ fraction of inspired oxygen fraction $(\mathsf{F}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2})$ until the end-tidal oxygen fraction reached $90\%$ . General anaesthesia was then induced and maintained using target-controlled propofol and remifentanil infusions. Atracurium was administered to facilitate tracheal intubation and its effect was monitored by accelerometry at the thumb with train-of-four stimulations of the ulnar nerve repeated every $60~\mathsf{s}$ . Following tracheal intubation, the patients’ lungs were ventilated with the following targets: controlled ventilation mode; $\mathsf{F}_{1}\mathsf{O}_{2}$ to maintain ${\mathsf{SpO}}_{2}>{\mathsf{Q}}6\%.$ tidal volume $6{-}8~\mathrm{m}|.\mathrm{kg}^{-1}$ of ideal body weight; positive endexpiratory pressure (PEEP) 5 $\mathsf{cmH}_{2}\mathsf{O};$ inspiratory to expiratory ratio 1:2; and respiratory rate set to maintain endtidal carbon dioxide partial pressure $4.0{-}4.7~\mathsf{kPa}$ . In the intra-operative period, propofol effect-site concentration was modified in order to maintain the bispectral index $40-$ 60. Arterial hypotension (defined as a mean arterial pressure $(\mathsf{MAP})<60\mathsf{mmHg})$ was treated by ephedrine 9 mg intravenously and $250{-}500~\mathrm{m}$ crystalloid fluid challenge administered over $<5$ min. Ephedrine could be repeated up to a maximal dose of $30~\mathrm{mg}$ . If refractory hypotension occurred, a titrated continuous intravenous infusion of norepinephrine was used to maintain ${\mathsf{MAP}}\geq$ $60\mathrm{mmHg}$ .  

Patients allocated to regional anaesthesia had scalp blocks performed in the operating theatre by a trained anaesthetist. Bilateral infiltration of the supra-orbital, auriculotemporal, lesser occipital and greater occipital nerves with lidocaine $1\%$ was performed according to anatomical landmarks as described previously [16], to a maximum lidocaine dose of $4\mathrm{mg.kg}^{-1}$ . A target-controlled intravenous infusion of remifentanil $(0.5{-}1.5~\mathsf{ng.m}|^{-1})$ was allowed to provide analgesia and patient comfort during scalp block performance. If the scalp blocks failed, then general anaesthesia was performed.  

At the end of the surgical procedure, all patients were admitted to PACU. Pain was evaluated using a verbal rating scale (VRS) where 0 represented no pain and 10 the worst pain imaginable. If the VRS was $\geq5$ , 3 mg oxycodone was administered intravenously and then repeated every 5 min until the VRS was $\leq3$ . On the surgical ward, a nurse visited the patient every 4–6 h and on demand. Pain was measured using a VRS at rest and following mobilisation until postoperative day 3 or hospital discharge. Ward-based analgesia consisted of oral paracetamol (1 g every 6 h) and if the VRS score was $>3$ , additional analgesia was provided through either intravenous parecoxib $.40\mathrm{~mg},$ , intravenous nefopam $(20~\mathrm{mg})$ , oral tramadol $100~\mathrm{mg})$ and/or oral oxycodone $(5~\mathrm{mg}.$ ) according to the attending anaesthetist’s preference. Oral hydration was authorised immediately postoperatively with a light diet started on postoperative day 1. Postoperative nausea and vomiting were treated with intravenous ondansetron. Patients were encouraged to ambulate within $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ of arrival on the surgical ward. Patients were discharged from hospital at the discretion of the attending surgeon.  

We recorded patient characteristics, history of comorbid conditions, ASA physical status and chronic treatments. A Mini-Cog test was performed pre-operatively by the principal investigator [17]. Intra-operative data recorded included: duration of surgery (from skin incision to completion of surgical wounds dressing); type of surgery (craniotomy or burr hole craniostomy); total remifentanil dose; occurrence of arterial hypotension $(\mathsf{MAP}\leq60\mathsf{mmHg}$ for $\geq3{\mathrm{~min}}.$ ); occurrence of bradycardia (heart rate $<50~\mathrm{bpm};$ ; occurrence of arterial hypertension (systolic arterial pressure $>170\mathrm{mmHg}$ or a diastolic pressure $>90\mathrm{mmHg})$ ; occurrence of apnoea; occurrence of desaturation $({\mathsf{SpO}}_{2}<{\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{2\%}})$ ; and occurrence of hypothermia (temperature $<36\mathrm{~\ensuremath~{~\circ~}~}_{\mathrm{C}})$ . In PACU, data recorded included: duration of stay; maximal pain on VRS; Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale; occurrence of desaturation; need for tracheal re-intubation; and occurrence of arterial hypertension, nausea and vomiting, urinary retention and acute surgical complication requiring surgery. Postoperative data were recorded by the principal investigator on the surgical ward until postoperative day 3 or discharge. Data recorded included: maximal pain using a VRS at postoperative day 1; need for opioid administration; occurrence of surgical incision infection or disunion; CSDH recurrence; need for revision surgery; and occurrence of postoperative confusion (assessed by the Confusion Assessment Method). Any other postoperative medical complications were recorded based on the electronic patient records and confirmed by an experienced surgeon and anaesthetist.  

The primary outcome was time until medically fit for discharge as measured from the day of surgery. Each day at midday, the primary outcome was evaluated by the principal investigator (who was blinded to mode of anaesthesia) using a medical checklist. All of the following criteria had to be present if a patient was to be declared medically fit for discharge: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) 15; absence of neurological dysfunction; no complications observed on the postoperative cerebral CT scan; recovery of the same degree of cognition as that preceding the onset of symptoms related to CSDH; no ongoing medical problems; no pain or pain controlled without opioids; blood pressure and heart rate in accordance with baseline measurements; no nausea and vomiting; and no surgical complications. Secondary outcomes were as follows: rate of regional anaesthesia failure defined as the occurrence of pain, agitation, apnoea and/or the need for general anaesthesia; rate of intra-operative adverse events (arterial desaturation, arterial hypotension, bradycardia and hypothermia); rate of postoperative adverse events (nausea and vomiting, arterial hypertension, bleeding, need for emergent cerebral CT or surgery); and maximal postoperative pain on postoperative day 1.  

A preliminary audit (unpublished data from 50 patients) showed that the mean (SD) duration of hospitalisation following surgical evacuation of CSDH was 6 (3) days. We hypothesised that regional anaesthesia would reduce the length of stay by 2 days. Based on a unilateral test $(\upalpha=0.05$ , $\upbeta=0.80$ ), 30 patients in each group were required. There was no planned interim analysis, and the primary analysis was performed according to an intention-to-treat principle. The distribution of quantitative continuous and discrete variables was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual analysis of quantiles-quantiles plots. The primary endpoint was compared between groups using a Mann–Whitney Utest. The difference between the groups and its confidence interval was calculated. Data were compared using a Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data, and a Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney  

U-test for quantitative data. A value of $\mathsf{p}<0.05$ was considered significant. Analyses were carried out using R software version 3.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).  

# Results  

In total, 139 patients were screened between 1 July 2018 and 30 November $2019,$ with data from 60 patients included in the analysis (30 general anaesthesia and 30 regional anaesthesia, Fig. 1). Patient characteristics at baseline are shown in Table 1.  

Median (IQR [range]) time until medically fit for discharge was 3 (3–3 [2–10]) vs. 3 (2–5 [2–15]) days for patients allocated to general and regional anaesthesia, respectively $(\mathsf{p}=0.35\$ . Median (IQR [range]) duration of total hospital stay was 4 (3–5 [2–76]) vs. 5 (36 [2–20]) days for patients allocated to general and regional anaesthesia, respectively. No patients were admitted to the intensive care unit in the immediate postoperative periods or at any time during their hospital stay.  

Intra-operative details are shown in Table 2. Surgical procedures were similar in both groups, although patients who received regional anaesthesia received less remifentanil and had a shorter duration of stay in PACU.  

Secondary outcomes are shown in Table 3. Two patients in the regional anaesthesia group required general anaesthesia due to regional block failure. Median (IQR [range]) maximal pain scores on postoperative day 1 were 4 (2–5 [0–9]) vs. 3 (1–4 [0–8]) for patients allocated to general and regional anaesthesia, respectively $(\mathsf{p}=0.21).$ . The number of adverse events was greater in patients who received general anaesthesia (median (IQR [range]) 1 (1–2 $[0{-}3])\mathsf{vs.}0(0{-}1[0{-}3]);\mathsf{p<0.001})$ .  

# Discussion  

In patients who underwent surgical evacuation of CSDH, we found no difference between general and regional anaesthesia in terms of time to become medically fit for discharge and incidence of postoperative medical and surgical complications. Our results did suggest that the occurrence of intra-operative arterial hypotension and duration of stay in PACU were lower with regional anaesthesia, but the study was not powered to investigate these outcomes and thus these findings should only be seen as hypothesis-generating.  

Surgical CSDH evacuation can be performed under general anaesthesia or local anaesthesia with sedation [11– 14]. General anaesthesia may be associated with a greater incidence of intra-operative adverse events, which are known to increase postoperative morbidity [18, 19]. On the other hand, local anaesthesia plus sedation can result in intra-operative discomfort, pain and agitation producing hazardous surgical conditions and requiring general anaesthesia [13, 14]. Analysis of large databases of elective general surgical cases suggest that regional anaesthesia may be associated with early hospital discharge and reduced peri-operative complications [20]. However, there are few data relating to patients having surgical CSDH evacuation.

We did not confirm our hypothesis that regional anaesthesia for surgical evacuation of CSDH leads to a reduction in this time for patients to become medically fit for discharge. We chose this measure as the primary outcome because it is an indicator of hospital efficiency, quality of care and management of the patient’s medical conditions. A reduction in the number of in patient days should decrease the risk of infection and medication-associated adverse effects [7, 21].  

Only one prospective randomised study has compared general anaesthesia with local anaesthesia plus sedation for surgical CSDH evacuation [13]; this showed that sedation plus local anaesthetic infiltration resulted in a decrease in hospital duration of stay compared with general anaesthesia. However, the hospital duration of stay was very short (1 day and 2 days in the sedation and general anaesthesia groups, respectively) and the patient population was not representative of patients with CSDH (mean age 58 y and $90\%$ ASA physical status 1 or 2) [1–3]. In our study, the time to become medically fit for discharge and total duration of hospital stay were 3 days and 4 days, respectively. Following CSDH evacuation, hospital duration of stay has been shown to range from 1 to 15 days [4–8, 13, 18]. Hospital duration of stay may depend on many factors, not only related to patients but also to healthcare systems and social disparities. In order to minimise non-medical factors, we focused on a daily medical evaluation of patient readiness for discharge from hospital.  

Our results suggest that the overall rate of intraoperative adverse events was lower in patients who received regional anaesthesia. This was related mainly to a higher rate of intra-operative hypotension with general anaesthesia. This may be due to several factors. First, patient $\mathsf{age},$ comorbidities and chronic drug treatments may all increase the adverse effects of anaesthetic drugs. Second, the definition of hypotension we used $\mathsf{\Gamma}(\mathsf{MAP}\leq60\mathsf{mmHg}$ for $>3\min$ ) included hypotension immediately following induction of general anaesthesia. However, it has been shown that even brief periods of intra-operative hypotension are associated with increased postoperative morbidity [22]. Only one study has reported intra-operative haemodynamic data recorded during surgical CSDH evacuation [13]. This study compared general anaesthesia with local anaesthetic infiltration plus dexmedetomidine sedation. The incidence of hypotension $(\mathsf{MAP}<60\mathsf{mmHg})$ was $7\%$ in the general anaesthesia group and $3\%$ in the local anaesthesia group. Surprisingly, intra-operative MAP was  

$100–110\mathrm{mmHg}$ in the general anaesthesia group despite the anaesthetic drugs used (thiopental and isoflurane). However, methodological issues and patient population preclude further analysis of these findings.  

We did not show any difference in the incidence of postoperative surgical complications with the use of general or regional anaesthesia. Our overall surgical complication rate was $20\%$ which is in line with previous studies [1, 2, 4, 6–8]. In contrast with surgical complications, postoperative medical complications receive little attention in the published literature and the high heterogeneity in reporting methodologies limits extrapolation of the findings [7]. Dowlati et al. showed that $42\%$ of patients have major inhospital complications but did provide details as to the type of complication [8]. Stubbs et al. showed that neurological and non-neurological medical postoperative complications occurred in $20\%$ and $58\%$ of patients, respectively [7]. Furthermore, there was a strong association between the occurrence of any medical complications and hospital duration of stay. In our study, medical complications occurred in $43\%$ and $30\%$ of patients receiving general and regional anaesthesia, respectively, of which the most frequent was postoperative confusion. It remains unknown whether regional anaesthesia may decrease the incidence of postoperative cognitive dysfunction compared with general anaesthesia [23]. In specific patients, such as those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, regional anaesthesia decreases the occurrence of respiratory complications [24]. Further studies are required to understand the epidemiology and possible prevention of postoperative medical complications following surgical CSDH evacuation.  

Several limitations of our study should be taken into account. First, duration of hospital stay was shorter than that anticipated, which decreased substantially the power of the study. It can be hypothesised that the daily visit of the principal investigator may have unintentionally influenced the ward healthcare team. Second, the primary outcome is an overall indicator depending, at least in part, on healthcare efficiency, a patient’s medical history and social parameters. However, due to randomisation and daily evaluation of patient’s readiness to discharge, we think that the impact of non-medical factors has been minimised. Third, we only recorded data during hospitalisation but did not evaluate long-term cognitive function, surgical and medical complications. However, we chose in-hospital outcomes because they may be more dependent on intraoperative care than long-term outcomes. In addition, studying long-term outcomes requires specific, very large sample size studies. Finally, the sample size of the present study precludes any definitive conclusion regarding the incidence of postoperative surgical and medical complications because some events were rare. Further study should focus on the two most frequent medical complications observed in this population, which were postoperative confusion and respiratory complications.  

In conclusion, in patients requiring CSDH evacuation, general and regional anaesthesia are comparable in terms of duration of time until medically fit for discharge and occurrence of postoperative complications.
