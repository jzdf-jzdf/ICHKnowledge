# from placebo for ischemic stroke but increased intracranial bleeding  

Cloud GC, Williamson JD, Thao LTP, et al. Low-dose aspirin and the risk of stroke and intracerebral bleeding in healthy older people: secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial. JAMA Netw Open. 2023;6:e2325803.  

![](images/28efd357b5e190508f5ca8f4ef15319840dfd7a14cc25b38a804a0fbc0368a88.jpg)  

Question: In healthy, community-dwelling, older adults, what is the effect of aspirin for primary prevention compared with placebo on stroke and bleeding events?  

![](images/01cbc7f07c8b839c43f052353a7bcca8450e690028468ca8793a285a94b568ff.jpg)  

Design: Secondary analysis of a randomized placebo-controlled trial (ASPREE [Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly]). The trial was stopped early for lack of benefit in the primary outcome of disability-free survival.\*  

![](images/1829e08bd2ea29adcb24c81455eb3bb9795350b760c7ef5242ea0ab4a27eba16.jpg)  

Blinding: Treatment allocation concealed; blinded (participants, clinicians, and outcome adjudicators).†  

Setting: 34 sites in the USA and 16 sites in Australia.\*  

![](images/b8672196381d0970cac5f7f6a883d2284c9b45195293aa90a9bc24ac86d0ef3e.jpg)  

Participants: 19 114 community-dwelling adults aged $\geq70$ years or ${\ge}65$ years for Black or Hispanic participants living in the USA (median age, $74~\mathrm{y};$ $56\%$ women; $91.3\%$ White) who had no documented cardiovascular (CV) or cerebrovascular disease. Key exclusions: chronic illness that could limit survival to ${<}5$ years, dementia, physical disability, anemia, high risk for bleeding, or contraindication to aspirin.  

![](images/e106440e161ccdaba862005935aea12075af04e3f98a615bc3aef6d8ca4a46cf.jpg)  

Interventions: Enteric-coated aspirin, $100\mathrm{mg/d}$ $(n=9525\$ ), or placebo $(n=9589)$ ).  

![](images/f5bec232dcf6d1c8372efbe6560bf2db347e76e6df579b126d976b293eccf1c5.jpg)  

Funding: National Institute on Aging and National Cancer Institute; National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia; Monash University; Victorian Cancer Agency. Bayer Pharma (Germany) provided the study drug and placebo.

Bottom line: In healthy older adults, low-dose aspirin did not differ from placebo for ischemic stroke but increased risk for intracranial bleeding.  

Commentary: Primary prevention strategies that are implemented for the general public are designed to reduce events in all risk groups, and such interventions are more effective than no intervention because they prevent a large number of events in the entire population. The prevention paradox refers to the minimal benefit that a primary prevention intervention has on a person at low or moderate risk while conveying a large benefit in the population as a whole (1). The trial by Cloud and colleagues is a prime example of this paradox with a twist—there was no benefit at all. The study shows that in adults aged ${>}65$ years, aspirin did not provide any benefit with respect to primary prevention of stroke and increased risk for intracranial bleeding.  

The American Heart Association (AHA) primary prevention guidelines for stroke recommend aspirin for cardiovascular (CV) prophylaxis (including, but not specifically for, stroke) for people with $>10\%$ risk in 10 years if the benefits outweigh the risks (2). More recently, the AHA primary prevention guidelines for CV disease suggest that low-dose aspirin be considered in adults aged 40 to 70 years who have high risk for atherosclerotic CV disease but no increased bleeding risk (3). The guidelines do not recommend aspirin for primary prevention in adults aged ${>}70$ years.  

The results of the study by Cloud and colleagues show that aspirin should not be used at all in individuals aged ${>}70$ years or Black or Hispanic  

individuals $\ge65$ years. The potential lack of generalizability of the results and the lower-than-expected event rates may call into question the power of the study and its conclusions. Yet, the lack of any benefit and the potential risk for intracranial hemorrhage, however low, should preclude aspirin for primary prevention. These findings should be considered when updating recommendations of aspirin for primary stroke prevention.
