# Abstract  

Background: Recurrent stroke rates after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) vary widely between observational studies due to differences in ICH etiology and risk for thromboembolic events. We therefore aimed to assess the patient characteristics and prognosis after deep and lobar ICH in a well-characterized, multicenter, hospital-based cohort.  Methods: Patients were prospectively documented in 13 German neurological stroke centers. Of 744 patients with spontaneous ICH discharged alive, 516 $(69.4\%)$ gave informed consent and 496 $(66.7\%)$ could be followed up by central telephone interview over a mean duration of 2 years. Results: In patients with deep ICH, the Kaplan-Meier estimate for stroke during the first year was $5.8\%$ $95\%\mathsf{Cl}2.9\ –8.7)$ and the overall annual rate (calculated over a 3-year period) was $2.9\%$ ( $95\%$ CI 1.6–4.1). In patients with lobar ICH, the Kaplan-Meier estimate for stroke during the first year was $7.8\%$ $(95\%\mathsf{Cl}3.1-12.5)$ and the overall annual rate was $7.2\%$ ( $95\%$ CI 3.8–10.6). At the last followup before recurrent stroke or end of study, 141 patients $(28.4\%)$ overall received antiplatelet agents, and 12 $(2.4\%)$ received oral anticoagulation. No difference could be found for recurrent ICH under antiplatelet agents versus no antithrombotic medication. Conclusion: The risk of recurrent stroke after lobar ICH remains high beyond the first year, whereas it decreases after 1 year in patients with deep ICH. Antiplatelets are prescribed in a considerable number of patients even though the risk-benefit ratio after ICH remains unknown.

# Introduction  

Patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) have the highest in-hospital mortality of all stroke subtypes. While many observational studies have assessed in-hospital mortality and short-term outcome, there is a wide variation in the reported risk of recurrent stroke (between 0 and $24\%$ , overview by Hanger et al. [1]) and longterm prognosis. This is most likely due to case-mix variations, in particular patient age and ICH etiology. In addition, most of these studies were retrospective and included only limited patient numbers. Furthermore, there is a considerable uncertainty about the risk of antithrombotic therapy in ICH survivors who are at high risk of subsequent ischemic cardiovascular events [2-4]. We therefore aimed to assess the baseline characteristics, long-term risk of recurrent stroke, and mortality in patients with spontaneous ICH admitted to acute stroke units in Germany and to determine the risk of recurrent stroke and mortality under antiplatelets and anticoagulants in deep versus lobar ICH.  

Statistics  

Baseline characteristics of all ICH patients were stratified by lobar versus deep ICH and reported as percentages or mean (median). Statistical comparisons were carried out via Fisher’s exact test, Person’s $\chi^{2}$ or Mann-Whitney U test for categorical and continuous variables, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed by SPSS 17.0.  

# Methods  

This study was part of an observational cohort study conducted by the German stroke study collaboration. A diagnosis of ICH was made prospectively based on cerebral imaging at admission in all patients. From September 2002 to December 2006, 903 patients with ICH were recruited consecutively by 13 participating Neurology Departments with an Acute Stroke Unit (not all centers participated during the whole study period; the mean time of recruitment was 34 months) with standardized case report forms which included demographic information, vascular risk factors, comorbidities, neurological deficits measured by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), premorbid modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and mRS at discharge, presumed etiology, length of hospital stay, and medication at discharge. Starting in July 2005, we also assessed previous antiplatelet therapy and anticoagulation including the international normalized ratio at admission. Lobar ICH was defined as selective involvement of the cerebral cortex and underlying white matter. Deep ICH was defined as bleeding into the basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem or cerebellum. After central double-keystroke input of all baseline data, data quality was monitored via automated consistency checks, and queries were sent to the documenting center. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Essen.  

Patients (or surrogates) were asked to provide informed consent for participation in the long-term follow-up study. If patients consented, a central structured follow-up interview via telephone (or paper questionnaire upon request) was performed biannually including current antithrombotic medication, functional disability scales (Barthel index, mRS), screening for recurrent cerebrovascular events or cause of death. In case of a suspected recurrent cerebrovascular event, confirmation was sought from the treating general physician or hospital. Local citizen registries were checked if any patient could not be reached for follow-up and uncertain causes of death were confirmed on death certificates.  

We excluded 58 patients with ICH due to trauma $(\mathrm{n}=22)$ , vascular malformations (angioma $=5$ , aneurysm $=12$ , cavernoma $=$ 7), coagulopathies $(\mathrm{n}=12)$ ), or vasculitis $(\mathrm{n}=0)$ . Of the remaining 845 patients, 101 patients $(12.0\%)$ died during hospital stay in the documenting center. Of 744 patients discharged alive, 516 $(69.4\%)$ gave informed consent for long-term follow-up which could be obtained in 496 patients $(66.7\%)$ , while 11 patients $(1.5\%)$ later withdrew consent and 9 patients $(1.2\%)$ were lost to follow-up. Biannual follow-up was stopped due to lack of funding in June 2007, resulting in a mean follow-up duration of 23.5 months (maximum 56 months). Patients without follow-up had a significantly higher NIHSS at admission (mean 10.3 vs. 8.9), a higher $\mathrm{mRS}$ score at discharge (mean 3.5 vs. 2.9) and a lower rate of hypercholesterolemia (17.0 vs. $23.7\%$ ). No significant differences were found with regard to age, previous stroke, recurrent cerebral ischemic events during hospital stay, cardiovascular comorbidity, hypertension, diabetes, smoking or alcohol abuse.  

# Results  

The 845 documented patients with spontaneous ICH were 17–96 years old (mean $69.6~\pm11.8$ years) and 377 $(44.6\%)$ were women. Delay between symptom onset and hospital admission was ${<}3\mathrm{h}$ in $45.8\%$ , 3 to ${<}6\mathrm{h}$ in $21.4\%$ , $6{-}24\mathrm{h}$ in $18.3\%$ and ${>}24\mathrm{h}$ in $14.5\%$ of patients (no information available in 13 patients). The primary imaging was CT in 663 $(78.6\%)$ and MRI in 180 patients $(21.3\%$ ; no information available in 2 patients). The greatest axial diameter of hemorrhage was ${<}1$ cm in 31 patients $(3.9\%)$ , 1 to ${<}2\mathrm{cm}$ in 179 $(22.2\%)$ , 2 to ${<}4~\mathrm{cm}$ in 332 $(41.2\%)$ and $\geq4$ in 263 $(32.7\%$ ; no information available in 40 patients). 271 patients $(33.8\%)$ had lobar ICH, 509 $(63.5\%)$ had deep ICH, and 22 $(2.7\%)$ had ICH of undetermined origin (no information available in 43 patients). The majority of deep ICH were lenticulostriate (407, $80.0\%\cdot$ , 65 $(12.8\%)$ were thalamic, 24 $(4.7\%)$ were cerebellar, and 29 $(5.7\%)$ were located in the brainstem (16 with two localizations reported). Intraventricular blood was present in 294 $(36.5\%)$ patients (no information available in 39 patients). Twenty-three patients received surgical hematoma evacuation, and 75 patients were mechanically ventilated. Of 305 ICH patients with information on prior antithrombotic medication (the questions on previous antithrombotic medication were entered into the case report form only later during the study), 61 patients $(19.9\%)$ had been on antiplatelets and 41 $(13.4\%)$ on anticoagulation ( $92\%$ on oral anticoagulants, $8\%$ on highdose heparin). The median international normalized ratio at admission was 3.3 (1st and 3rd quartile, 2.1 and 4.0, respectively). # Discussion  

The major finding from this large multicenter prospective cohort study is that the risk of recurrent stroke remains high beyond 1 year after lobar ICH, whereas the rate declines after the first year following deep ICH. This reflects risk factor differences as well as different underlying pathophysiologies. Hypertensive vasculopathy is regarded as the most important mechanism in deep ICH, whereas cerebral amyloid angiopathy is the most frequent cause in elderly patients with lobar ICH. While control of elevated blood pressure can reduce the risk of recurrent ICH [5, 6], no known treatment exists for cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Deep ICH was nearly twice as frequent as lobar ICH, which is in line with other hospital-based studies [7]. In contrast, a population-based study from Italy found a similar frequency of deep and lobar ICH, which can be attributed to the higher age of patients included in population-based studies [8].

Although we were unable to determine the etiology in about $30\%$ of recurrent strokes, ischemic stroke seemed to occur more frequently than recurrent hemorrhagic stroke after both lobar and deep ICH. Likewise, in the majority of previous observational studies in ICH patients, the risk of ischemic stroke was similar or higher compared with the risk of recurrent ICH [1, 9-13]. In those studies which stratified lobar versus deep ICH, the absolute annual stroke risk widely varied from $0.6\%$ up to $2.1\%$ after deep ICH, and from $1.1\%$ up to $12.1\%$ after lobar ICH [7, 9, 14-16].  

Antiplatelet prescription in our cohort was common after both lobar and deep ICH. However, the effect of antiplatelets on the risk of recurrent stroke or cardiovascular death remains unknown due to a probable bias by indication. In addition, the rate of recurrent ICH was low even under antithrombotic treatment. In the absence of any randomized controlled trial, a previous populationbased study in 417 ICH survivors reported no significant effect of antiplatelet medication on recurrent hemorrhage [9]. In another study in 104 patients with lobar ICH, aspirin was not associated with ICH recurrence in univariate analyses, but when adjusting for baseline clinical predictors, it independently increased the risk of ICH recurrence [15]. Likewise, the risk for recurrent ICH under anticoagulation has been reported only from small, retrospective, case series [4, 9, 17-19]. Given this scarce information, no treatment recommendations exist for patients with deep or lobar ICH who are also at risk for thromboembolic events. According to a Markov model by Eckman et al. [2], patients with deep ICH who are at high risk for thromboembolic stroke might benefit from long-term anticoagulation. On the other hand, patients with lobar hemorrhage should never be started on anticoagulation. In this analysis, a very high annual ICH recurrence rate of $15\%$ after lobar ICH was assumed on the basis of only one prospective study in 71 consecutive survivors of lobar ICH [16]. This rate is much higher than the 2-year recurrence rate of $15.7\%$ reported by Biffi et al. [15] and the combined stroke rate of $7.2\%$ year found in the present study. Although the bleeding risk under aspirin is probably smaller compared with anticoagulation, the relative risk reduction for thromboembolic events in patients with atrial fibrillation is also lower [20]. Dabigatran was shown to have a markedly reduced risk of intracranial hemorrhage compared with warfarin in patients with atrial fibrillation [2i]. Whether this remains true in patients with previous ICH needs to be shown.  

Our study has several limitations. Not all ICH survivors could be followed up (mainly due to missing consent), and patients without follow-up were more severely disabled. Nevertheless, $95\%$ of patients who provided informed consent could be followed. Because more severe neurological deficits (measured on the NIHSS) were associated with a lower rate of recurrent stroke (data not shown) our study might overestimate the true rate of stroke recurrence. Furthermore, the exact etiology remained unknown in about $30\%$ of recurrent strokes and $13\%$ of deaths, which precluded an analysis of the exact rate of ischemic or recurrent hemorrhagic strokes. In addition, due to the observational nature of the study, the stroke recurrence rates under antithrombotic medication are likely confounded by indication.  

In conclusion, the risk of recurrent stroke is higher in patients with lobar ICH compared with deep ICH and remains elevated beyond the first year, whereas it decreases after deep ICH. Antiplatelets are prescribed in a considerable number of patients even though the riskbenefit ratio for any antithrombotic medication after ICH remains unknown.
