# Safety and Efficacy of Pharmaco-invasive Approach Using Streptokinase Compared With Primary Percutaneous Coronary Angiography  

El-Zahraa M. Sultan, MSC,\* Ahmed A. Elberry, MD,† Hoda Rabea, $PHD,\ddagger$ and Hesham B. Mahmoud, MD§  

Background: Fibrin-specific fibrinolytics are preferred when they used in STEMI patients (pharmaco-invasive approach). However, streptokinase is still the most common used thrombolytic agent in Egypt because of its cheaper cost.  

Methods: 266 STEMI patients were randomly assigned to undergo PPCI or pharmacoinvasive (using streptokinase). Primary end point (death, shock, congestive heart failure, or reinfarction up to $30\mathrm{d}$ ) and secondary end point (ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, or nonintracranial bleeding) were followed for 30 days after reperfusion. In pharmaco-invasive arm, urgent coronary angiography was performed in case of failed reperfusion. Based on the reperfusion time from symptoms onset, patients in both arms were divided into; early (≤3 hrs) and late reperfusion ${>}3$ hrs).  

Results: No statistical significant difference regarding left ventricular ejection fraction, end diastolic and end systolic diameter in both arms. Early PPCI $(\leq3\mathrm{~hrs})$ ) had highest ejection fraction values $(56.9\pm7.5)$ . Myocardial wall preservation was best achieved in early pharmaco-invasive $(\leq3\mathrm{hrs})$ . There was no statistical significant difference in TIMI flow results between all subgroups (early and late of both arms) $(P{=}0.750)$ ). Suction devices and IV Eptifibatide were less frequently used in the pharmaco-invasive comparing to PPCI arm; ${\itP}=0.000$ and $P{=}0.006,$ ) subsequently. No statistical significant difference regarding complication incidence in both arms $_{\mathrm{\it~P=0.518,}}$ ). Radial access was more commonly used in the pharmaco-invasive arm $(P=0.015^{}$ .  

Conclusion: Utilizing streptokinase in early re-perfused patients by PI approach $(\leq3{\mathrm{~hrs}})$ ) seems safe and efficient when PPCI delay $(>120\mathrm{min}$ from symptom onset) is the other option.  

Key Words: pharmaco-invasive (PI), primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI), reperfusion, ST-Elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), streptokinase

According to ACCF/AHA Guideline regarding the choice of fibrinolytic agent for management of STEMI, fibrin-specific agents are preferred when they are available.2 However, streptokinase is still the most common used thrombolytic agent in Egypt because of its cheaper cost compared to other fibrin-specific agents.  



# METHODS  

The aim of this study is to assess the safety and the efficacy of PI approach (using streptokinase) in acute STEMI patients. This is an open-label prospective study. It was conducted in Beni-Suef university hospital between October 2014 and January 2018. Two hundred sixty-six STEMI patients were assigned to undergo either PPCI or PI (using streptokinase), then followed up for 30 days after reperfusion (Fig. 1). Patients who arrived at the university hospital (PCI-capable hospital) from $8{:}00\mathrm{AM}$ to $5{:}00~\mathrm{PM}$ underwent PPCI, while those who arrived from $5{:}00\mathrm{PM}$ to 8:00 am were subjected to the PI strategy. Moreover, patients who first reached a non-PCI-capable hospital in Beni-Suef city received streptokinase were then transferred to the university hospital (PCI-capable hospital) for coronary angiography (CA). For 130 patients, we have performed standard PPCI within 12 hours from onset of symptoms or within 12 to 24 hours of detection of evidence of ongoing ischemia. PI arm included 136 patients. CA/PCI was done in stable patients after successful fibrinolysis and before discharge (not $^{<3}$ hrs after streptokinase). Urgent CA (rescue PCI) after fibrinolysis was performed in case of hemodynamic instability (Cardiogenic shock or acute severe heart failure (HF) that develops after initial presentation), electrical instability, worsening ischemia, or failed reperfusion or reocclusion. Successful reperfusion assured by ST-elevation resolution for $>50-70\%$ at 90 minutes after fibrinolytic therapy, decreased/disappeared chest pain or reperfusion arrhythmia. If resolution was $<50\%$ , urgent CA/rescue PCI was done. PI failed in 7 patients, and they were transferred for rescue PCI. Based on the reperfusion time, patients in both arms (PPCI and PI) were divided into 2 subgroups; early reperfusion $\mathord{\left[\leq3\right.}$ hrs from symptoms onset) or late reperfusion ${>}3$ hrs from symptoms onset). Patients presented 24 hours after onset of symptom, with no evidence of ongoing ischemia or with current or previous diagnosis of congestive HF were excluded. Also, those who had contraindication to streptokinase were excluded from the PI approach.  



# Discharge and Follow-up Plan  

Patients were usually discharged within 48 hours after reperfusion. They were followed up for 30 days, looking for primary end point (death, shock, congestive HF, or reinfarction up to $30\mathrm{~d~}$ ) and secondary end point (ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, or nonintracranial bleeding).  



# Statistical Analysis  

Data were analyzed using the software, Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS Inc., Released 2009, PASW Statistics for Windows, version 18.0: SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Frequency distribution as percentage and descriptive statistics in the form of mean and SD were calculated. $\chi^{2}$ , t test, 1-way ANOVA, and correlations were done whenever needed. $P$ values of less than 0.05 were considered significant.

# RESULTS  



# Baseline Patients’ Characteristics  

The mean age for the PPCI arm was $54\pm9.75$ years. The minimum age was 30 years, and the maximum was 78 years. The PI arm mean age was $55\pm10.62$ years with a minimum of 29 years and a maximum of 83 years. Regarding gender, no significant difference was found in both arms (Table  1). PPCI arm had more hypertensive patients than PI arm $(P=0.059)$ ). No statistical significant difference between both arms regarding diabetes and Ischemic heart disease. Smokers were more in the PI arm than PPCI arm $(P{=}0.004)$ . One hundred twenty-nine patients had successful reperfusion in PI arm, and 7 patients had failed PI reperfusion (Table  1; Fig.  1).

# ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY  



# PPCI Arm Versus PI Arm  

There was no statistical significant difference regarding the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), end diastolic diameter (ED), and end systolic diameter (ES) between both arms (PPCI and PI) ${P}{=}0.764$ , $P{=}0.193$ , and $P{=}0.886$ ). Normal ventricular segments wall motion was detected in 80 patients $(30.1\%)$ . No difference was found between both PPCI and PI arm $\scriptstyle(P=0.924.$ ). Number of patients with akinetic and hypokinetic segments was 91 $(34.2\%)$ and 95 $(35.7\%)$ subsequently and did not show a statistical significant difference ${P}{=}0.258$ and $P{=}0.257$ ). Only myocardium wall preservation showed significant difference in PI arm over PPCI arm $\scriptstyleP=0.033,$ ) (Fig. 2A).  



# Subgroups Analysis (Early PPCI, Late PPCI, Early PI, and Late PI)  

Early PPCI subgroup had the highest LVEF values $(56.9\pm7.5)$ than early PI $(53.1\pm11.\Bar{4})$ , late PI $(52.5\pm10.7)$ , and late PPCI $(52.3\pm8.7)$ $\scriptstyle(P=0.042)$ (Fig. 2B). No statistical difference was found regarding the ED results between all subgroups $(P=0.729)$ ). Late PI subgroup showed the lowest ES value between all subgroups $(3.9\pm0.7)\$ $(P=0.002)\$ ). Highest incidence of myocardium wall thinning was detected in late PI cohort $(84.3\%)$ . Myocardial wall preservation was best achieved in early PI cohort $(23.4\%)$ over early PPCI $(9.8\%)$ . Incidence of segmental wall motion abnormality had a significant difference between all subgroups $P{=}0.002$ ). Most akinatic segments were detected in late PPCI $(49.4\%)$ , while the least were in early PPCI $(17.6\%)$ . Early PI had the highest number of patients with hypokinetic segments $(46.8\%)$ followed by late PPCI $(33.3\%)$ (Fig. 2C).  



# THE PROCEDURE DATA AND COMPLICATIONS  



# PPCI Arm Versus PI Arm  

Femoral access in CA was more used in PPCI; 67 patients $(51.5\%)$ , while radial access was more common in the PI arm; 85 patients $(62.5\%)$ $(P{=}0.015)$ (Table  2). Suction devices and IV Eptifibatide were more frequently used in the PPCI arm than PI $\scriptstyle{\calP}=0.006$ and $P{=}0.000$ ) subsequently (Table 2). No statistical significant difference regarding complication incidence in both arms $(P=0.518)$ ).  



# Subgroups Analysis (Early PPCI, Late PPCI, Early PI, and Late PI)  

There was no statistical significant difference between all subgroups regarding the access pathway during the CA procedure $(P=0.131\$ ) (Table 3; Fig. 3). Late PPCI required more use of suction devices $(25.3\%)$ and more IV Eptifibatide administration $(21.5\%)$ while early PI had the least use of both $(4.3\%)$ (Table  3; Fig.  3). There was no statistical significant difference in TIMI flow results between all subgroups $\scriptstyle{P=0.750})$ . G3 TIMI was achieved in 248 $(93.2\%)$ of all patients. Incidence of complications after 30 days between subgroups revealed no statistical significant difference $(P=0.735$ ).  



# 30-DAY MORTALITY  



# Primary End Point  

Two patients died, one in the PPCI arm and the other in the PI, due to persistent ventricular arrhythmia. No reported cases of congestive HF or reinfarction up to 30 days in both arms.

# DISCUSSION  

The proposed approach has dual benefits of both PI strategy (i.e., fibrinolysis followed by CA/PCI) and streptokinase (SK), where it is the most available fibrinolytic drug in Egypt. Recent study suggests that using streptokinase as the thrombolytic agent in PI approach may be reasonable in low- and middle-income countries to overcome financial barriers and extend reperfusion opportunity in STEMI patients.3 Our results revealed efficacy when using SK in PI approach. PI approach achieved comparable results to PPCI arm regarding LVEF, myocardium dimensions, and myocardium wall motion. There was no statistical significant difference in all results between both arms (PI and PPCI) except for myocardium wall preservation. PI approach provided more wall preservation over PPCI did $83.8\%$ vs. $73.1\%$ $\scriptstyleP=0.033)$ ) (Fig. 2A).  

Since timing is the key to successful reperfusion,1 patients in late PI subgroup had better LVEF values than those in late PPCI (Fig. 2B). This could be attributed to the earlier reperfusion of late PI than late PPCI; $5.3{\pm}2.5$ hrs vs. $7.1\pm3.6$ hrs). Also, 6 patients who had failed PI reperfusion then underwent urgent CA were included in our study. This might affect the LVEF results. Within group, variance analysis would give us more illustrative results. This result is in agreement with other previously published data.4,5 This delay was not intended anyway and reflected the real world situation. On the other hand, late PI may not preserve myocardium wall as late PPCI ( $15.7\%$ vs. $34.2\%$ ) (Fig. 2C).

Research has proved that those early PPCI deliver the best outcomes for STEMI patients.6,7 However, it is noticeable from our results that early PI ( $\leq3$ hrs) showed less segmental wall motion abnormality than late PPCI ${>}3$ hrs) (Fig. 2C). Hence, early reperfusion by PI is better than waiting for PPCI.8 Manjunath et al showed that global longitudinal strain GLS estimated by speckle-tracking imaging provide more informative results when used.9 They used SK in their work $(88.5\%)$ and resulted in preserved LVEF $(53.12\pm5.61)\$ after a 6-month follow-up.  

Complication rate is consistent with results from STREAM trial.8 STREAM included STEMI patients who were presented within 3 hours onset of symptoms and could not undergo PPCI within 1 hour after first medical contact. The primary end point (death from any cause, shock, congestive HF, or reinfarction up to $30\mathrm{d}$ ) occurred in $(12.4\%)$ and $(14.3\%)$ in the PI arm and in the PPCI arm ${P=}0.21.$ ). The current study had 5 patients with complications (Table 2). Two patients were complicated with death and nonmajor bleeding in PPCI arm, while 3 patients had death, ischemic stroke, and nonmajor bleeding in PI arm $ \scriptstyle{P=0.518} $ ). These results showed that regardless of the reperfusion method in STEMI patients, reperfusion in the early hours is the key to successful reperfusion with low complication incidence. Late PI $(\geq3\mathrm{~hrs})$ ) had 2 patients complicated with ischemic stroke and nonmajor bleeding. Frequently used radial approach in PI arm than in PPCI could be a leading factor in reducing the bleeding risk in PI arm.10,11 Radial approach performed by experienced operator in our center was a contributor to low bleeding incidence.  

In 1 study, STEMI patients ${\ge}75$ years underwent PI approach (by streptokinase) had higher MACE and mortality compared with patients $<75$ years old. Age was an independent factor.12 This conclusion was confirmed by previously published meta-analysis, which defined old age as ${>}65$ , 70, or 75.13 The main concern of using streptokinase in PI approach is related to intracranial hemorrhagic (ICH) risk. Our study included 5 patients with age ${\ge}75$ years old. None of them reported ICH in follow-up. Vincent Auffret et al discussed efficacy of PI approach in patient with $\geq70$ years old and bleeding risk.14 They found that no difference regarding in-hospital MACE following PI or PPCI in patient $\geq70$ years. Higher rate of ICH detected in patients receiving PI could be attributed to using full-dose lytics. Unlike STREAM trial, patients included in Vincent Auffret et al study received a full-dose of a fibrin-specific agent [Tenecteplase (TNK): $96\%$ , Reteplase: $4\%$ ] regardless of age. On the other hand, recently published study illustrated that using half dose of tenecteplase in patients with age ${\ge}65$ years decreased the risk of bleeding.15  

We reported 10 patients with high-thrombus burden in PI arm in comparison to 30 patients in PPCI arm. Twenty-four patients of PPCI arm were injected by IV Eptifibatide (Table 2). The use of suction device $(P=0.000)\$ ) and IV Eptifibatide $P{=}0.006)$ was significantly higher in PPCI arm compared with PI. Using SK in early PI seemed to be helpful in opening infarcted arteries and dissolving thrombi with higher rate than early PPCI while keeping low incidence of complications. Suction device using rate was higher in late PPCI subgroup comparing to late PI $(P=0.003\$ ), while early PI had less suction devise use than late PI (2 vs. 8 patients). In case of late reperfusion ${\ge}3$ hours of onset of symptom, there is more time for thrombus complexity formation.16 Grade 3 TIMI was reached in many patients who reperfused early by either PPCI or PI ${\itP}=0.75,$ ). These results are in agreement with other published data.8,9,17  

According to guidelines2 and other studies,18 it is better to reperfuse the occluded artery by PI approach than waiting for PPCI. In Beni-Suef city, PPCI is not available in many centers. Our PI results are comparable to PPCI. It seems that no extra risk of complications is expected when using SK in early PI approach if late PPCI is the other option especially when combining it with radial technique.19 Patients who reperfused early $\ensuremath{\mathrm{(\le3~hrs)}}$ ) or late ${>}3$ hrs)  

by streptokinase in PI approach had encouraging results regarding LVEF, myocardium wall motion and preservation, and TIMI flow. Less use of suction device and IV GPI inhibitors administration were observed without increasing the complications risk.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

Utilizing streptokinase in early reperfused patients by PI approach (≤3 hrs) seems safe and efficient when PPCI delay ( ${\>}120\operatorname*{min}$ from symptom onset) is the other option.
