# Single-Dose Intraventricular Nimodipine Microparticles Versus Oral Nimodipine for Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage  

Andrew P. Carlson, MD, MSCR; Daniel Hänggi, MD; George K. Wong, MD; Nima Etminan, MD, PhD; Stephan A. Mayer, MD; François Aldrich, MD; Michael N. Diringer, MD; Erich Schmutzhard, MD; Herbert J. Faleck, DO; David Ng, PhD; Benjamin R. Saville, PhD; Thomas Bleck, MD; Robert Grubb Jr, MD; Michael Miller, PhD; Jose I. Suarez, MD; Howard M. Proskin, PhD; R. Loch Macdonald, MD, PhD; on behalf of the NEWTON Investigators\*  

Background and Purpose—EG-1962 is a sustained release formulation of nimodipine administered via external ventricular drain in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. A randomized, open-label, phase 1/2a, dose-escalation study provided impetus for this study to evaluate efficacy and safety of a single intraventricular $600~\mathrm{mg}$ dose of EG-1962 to patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, compared with standard of care oral nimodipine.  

Methods—Subjects were World Federation of Neurological Surgeons grades 2–4, modified Fisher grades 2–4 and had an external ventricular drain inserted as part of standard of care. The primary end point was the proportion of subjects with favorable outcome at day 90 after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (extended Glasgow outcome scale 6–8). The proportion of subjects with favorable outcome at day 90 on the Montreal cognitive assessment, as well as the incidence of delayed cerebral ischemia and infarction, use of rescue therapy and safety were evaluated.  

Results—The study was halted by the independent data monitoring board after planned interim analysis of 210 subjects (289 randomized) with day 90 outcome found the study was unlikely to achieve its primary end point. After day 90 followup of all subjects, the proportion with favorable outcome on the extended Glasgow outcome scale was $45\%$ (65/144) in the EG-1962 and $42\%$ (62/145) in the placebo group (risk ratio, 1.01 $[95\%$ CI, 0.83–1.22], $P{=}0.95$ ). Consistent with its mechanism of action, EG-1962 significantly reduced vasospasm $50\%$ [69/138] EG-1962 versus $63\%$ [91/144], $P{=}0.025.$ ) and hypotension $7\%$ [9/138] versus $10\%$ [14/144]). Analysis of prespecified subject strata suggested potential efficacy in World Federation of Neurological Surgeons 3–4 subjects $46\%$ [32/69] EG-1962 versus $32\%$ [24/75] placebo, odds ratio, 1.22 $[95\%$ CI, 0.94–1.58], $P{=}0.13$ ). No safety concerns were identified that halted the study or that preclude further development.  

Conclusions—There was no significant increase in favorable outcome for EG-1962 compared with standard of care in the overall study population. The safety profile was acceptable.

Key Words: clinical trial $\sqsubset$ infarction $\sqsubset$ nimodipine $\sqsubset$ standard of care ◼ subarachnoid hemorrhage imodipine is the only drug approved in North America, the European Union, and many other countries to improve outcome of patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). It is an L-type dihydropyridine calcium channel antagonist that is administered orally as well as intravenously in Europe and some other countries. The US Food and Drug Administration approval is based on 4 pivotal trials,1–4 which demonstrated improvement in neurological outcome and reduction of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) after aSAH. Meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials of nimodipine and other calcium channel antagonists5 demonstrated reduction of poor outcome in these patients with a number needed to treat of 19. Various routes of administration and doses have also been studied. Intravenous administration has not independently demonstrated efficacy5 and is not approved in the United States. In the original new drug application, doses ranging from 30 to $120\mathrm{mg}$ orally every 4 hours were studied; however, no dose-dependent efficacy was demonstrated. Higher doses of nimodipine carried an increased risk of hypotension and even of mortality under some conditions.3,6,7 At these doses, plasma, brain, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concentrations of nimodipine are lower than the $\mathrm{ED}_{50}$ and are commonly not measurable in the CSF.8  

We hypothesized that efficacy of nimodipine could be improved and adverse effects minimized by administration of nimodipine into the subarachnoid space. Data supporting local delivery of dihydropyridines to patients with SAH include that of Kasuya et al,9 who developed sustained release nicardipine pellets for placement in the basal cisterns during craniotomy for repair of ruptured aneurysms.10–12 In addition, the bioavailability of oral nimodipine is variable, frequently poor, and compliance with every 4-hour dosing is difficult, particularly later in the 21-day course.13,14 A single dose sustained-release formulation of nimodipine could have benefit in terms of patient compliance and bioavailability.13,15  

EG-1962 was, therefore, developed for direct administration of nimodipine into the subarachnoid space to produce higher CSF concentrations and potentially better efficacy with fewer side effects.10,11 A liquid sustained-release microparticle formulation was synthesized for single intraventricular dose administration so that patients undergoing neurosurgical clipping or endovascular coiling could be treated. This also would avoid repeated intraventricular injections and potentially achieve higher subarachnoid space nimodipine concentrations based on circulation of the microparticles to the basal cisterns and release of nimodipine there as opposed to administration of native nimodipine in the ventricles followed by progressive dilution as it circulated to the subarachnoid space.  

The safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics of EG-1962 were studied in a dose-escalation study (NEWTON [Nimodipine Microparticles to Enhance Recovery While Reducing Toxicity After Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: Phase 1/2a Multicenter, Controlled, Randomized, Open-Label, Dose Escalation, Safety, Tolerability and Pharmacokinetic Study Comparing EG-1962 and Nimodipine in Subjects With Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage]) that included 72 subjects.16 In this study, EG-1962 was safe, well-tolerated, and improved outcomes. This provided impetus for NEWTON-2 which was a randomized, double-blind (double-dummy), placebo-controlled study to evaluate efficacy and safety of a single intraventricular $600~\mathrm{mg}$ dose of EG-1962 to patients with aSAH, compared with standard of care oral nimodipine.17 We report here the primary results of this study.  



# Methods  



# Study Design  

The full protocol is published and is summarized here.17 The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The study was a double-blind, double-dummy, randomized controlled study with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The 2 study arms were a single dose of $600~\mathrm{mg}$ sustained-release intraventricular EG-1962 and an oral placebo or a single intraventricular injection of $\mathrm{NaCl}$ , $0.9\%$ and oral nimodipine. Each arm had a comparable placebo/dummy investigational product (oral placebo for the EG-1962 group and intraventricular NaCl, $0.9\%$ for the oral nimodipine group). The only substantial protocol amendment during the trial was addition of a pharmacokinetic sub-objective. Local institutional review board approval was obtained at each participating site for the protocol and amendments.  

The study was funded by Edge Therapeutics, who managed study operation, drug distribution, site monitoring, and data collection. The authors possess the complete study data set and conducted analysis, wrote the article without restrictions from the sponsor, and had final responsibility for article preparation and submission without influence from Edge Therapeutics or use of medical writers or editors.  



# Participants  

Adult (age 18–75) patients with aSAH secondary to a saccular aneurysm repaired by either surgical clipping or primary coiling were screened for enrollment. Further entry criteria included placement of an external ventricular drain (EVD) for clinical indications, modified Fisher scale ${>}1$ and postrepair World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFNS) grade 2–4.18,19 Elderly (age ${>}75$ ) and poor-grade (WFNS 5) patients were excluded because of the high mortality in these groups.18,20 Further exclusion criteria included major complication during aneurysm repair resulting in stroke or worsened hemorrhage and need for stent or other repair technique, such as trapping and bypass.  

Initial procedures and data were collected in the intensive care unit and hospital ward and clinical and radiographic follow-up was performed in the treating physician’s clinic.  



# Interventions  

After randomization, the single intraventricular injection was performed as soon as feasible within 48 hours of aSAH. The patient received standard of care oral nimodipine up until randomization and then received oral investigational product (nimodipine or identical placebo) for up to 21 days. The $600~\mathrm{mg}$ EG-1962 dose was chosen because it had the most optimal tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy in the phase 1/2 dose-escalation study.16 All interventions occurred during the hospital stay and subjects discharged before day 21 were given oral nimodipine or placebo to continue until day 21 at the treating physician’s discretion.  



# Objectives and End Points  

The study had 2 objectives, the primary being to compare the efficacy of intraventricular EG-1962 to standard of care oral nimodipine. The secondary objective was to determine the safety of intraventricular EG-1962 compared with standard of care oral nimodipine.  

The primary efficacy end point was the proportion of subjects with favorable outcome at day 90 measured by the extended Glasgow outcome scale (GOSE ${\ge}6$ ).21 The secondary efficacy end point was the proportion of subjects with favorable outcome on the Montréal cognitive assessment $(\ge26)$ at day $90.^{22,23}$ These were assessed at each site by trained, independent, and blinded assessors. Safety was assessed by the incidence and severity of adverse events based on the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 4.0) in EG-1962 treated subjects compared with subjects treated with standard of care oral nimodipine and by the proportion of subjects with delayed cerebral infarctions on computed tomography at day 30 that were not present on computed tomography obtained during the prerandomization phase. Hypotension was defined as a decrease in systolic blood pressure ${>}20\mathrm{mmHg}$ or diastolic blood pressure ${>}10\mathrm{mmHg}$ , or a systolic blood pressure ${\leq}90\mathrm{mmHg}$ , confirmed by 3 consecutive readings and requiring medical treatment. Hydrocephalus was defined according to investigator opinion. Bacterial meningitis was defined as a positive bacterial culture of CSF obtained through the EVD or of the EVD tip. Aseptic meningitis was defined as findings consistent with central nervous system inflammation, such as fever higher than $38\mathrm{{^\circC}}$ , new headache, new meningeal or cranial nerve signs, CSF pleocytosis, elevated protein, or decreased glucose in the absence of positive bacterial culture. Diagnosis and therapy were at the discretion of the treating physician.  

Vasospasm was defined as any event, adverse or otherwise, that was called vasospasm by the investigator. DCI was defined by Vergouwen et al,24 for neurologically assessable patients and by radiological evidence and clinical judgment for subjects in whom the neurological scales were not assessable. Rescue therapy was defined as induced hypertension, superselective infusion of vasodilator drugs or balloon angioplasty performed because the investigator believed it was indicated for angiographic vasospasm or DCI.17  

Pharmacokinetic data were to be collected on up to 18 subjects. Health economic information, vasospasm, DCI, and rescue therapy were also collected.24,25  



# Randomization and Blinding  

After obtaining informed consent, randomization was performed centrally using interactive response technology and was stratified by region (United States versus non-United States) and postrepair WFNS (2 versus 3–4). Allocation was communicated only to the designated research pharmacist for preparation of the investigational product. Only the pharmacy personnel were not blinded. The pharmacist prepared the EG-1962 or $0.9\%$ NaCl, both $6\mathrm{mL}$ volume and attached it to a manifold that contained an empty syringe for CSF removal, the investigational product, an empty syringe for remixing investigational product, and a fourth syringe containing $0.9\%$ NaCl used to flush the investigational product through the EVD. All syringes and connections were wrapped in opaque plastic so that the contents could not be visualized. This manifold was then placed in an opaque envelope and brought to the bedside. Each site was required to have a plan for allocation concealment approved by the sponsor. In most cases, the principle investigator alone (with no other people in the patient’s room) performed the injection under aseptic conditions after wrapping opaque plastic on the proximal EVD tubing. First, CSF was withdrawn, the investigational product was remixed and then injected followed by up to $3\mathrm{mL}$ of $0.9\%$ NaCl to ensure complete intraventricular delivery of the investigational product. The manifold and syringes were then replaced in the opaque envelope and discarded.  

Nimodipine is supplied as capsules in the United States and tablets in other regions, necessitating 2 oral placebos, both of which were manufactured to be identical to the respective nimodipine formulations.  



# Sample Size Estimation and Statistics  

The study was powered based on the results of NEWTON-1, which demonstrated a ${>}30\%$ difference in favorable outcome (GOSE ${\ge}6.$ ) between treatment and control groups.16 More conservatively, to detect a $15\%$ difference with $\beta{=}0.15$ and 1-sided $\mathrm{\Delta{\alpha=}0.025}$ and assuming a control favorable response rate of $28\%$ , 374 subjects would be required. A prespecified nonbinding futility analysis was planned after 150 subjects completed day 90 follow-up. An interim analysis, with sample size re-estimation, after 210 subjects completed day 90 follow-up was planned. The primary analysis was considered significant at $\mathrm{{\alpha}}\mathrm{{\alpha}}\mathrm{{\times}}0.0223$ , which controlled the overall $\upalpha$ at 0.025.

The protocol specified analysis was to be conducted on a full analysis set defined as randomized subjects who took at least one dose of investigational product. Data presented here, however, are based on all randomized subjects, which is more akin to an intention to treat set. The primary end point analysis used logistic regression with study arm, region of enrollment, WFNS grade (2 versus $3{+}4$ ), and age $(<60$ versus ${\ge}60$ ). Region of enrollment was included to account for potential pharmacokinetic differences between oral nimodipine formulations, as capsules are used in the United States and tablets are used in most other parts of the world. If the primary outcome was missing, it was imputed by carrying the day 30 value forwards. If there was no day 30 outcome, the day 90 outcome was recorded as unfavorable. A tipping point approach was planned for the primary end point to assess sensitivity to missing data. No imputation was planned for the secondary outcome, although the results presented impute the Montréal cognitive assessment as for the GOSE except that zero was assigned if the Montréal cognitive assessment was missing at days 30 and 90.  



# Results  

The first subject was randomized in July 2016. Sixty-five centers in 11 countries participated. The independent data monitoring committee met in February 2017 for a safety analysis, which did not raise any concerns. The futility analysis was conducted in December 2017, and the sample size assessment in March 2018 after 210 subjects completed day 90 follow-up. At this point, the data monitoring committee recommended that the study be halted due to a low likelihood of reaching the primary end point. No safety concerns were raised.  

Two hundred eighty-nine subjects were randomized up until March 2018 (Table  1; Figure  1). Six subjects in the  

EG-1962 and one in the oral nimodipine group were randomized but not treated (Figure 1). They were not included in the final analysis set but are included here in the intention to treat analysis. An additional 17 subjects in the EG-1962 and 16 in the oral nimodipine group did not complete the study through day 90, most commonly because of death (Figure 1). Day 90 outcome was missing for other reasons for 11 EG-1962 (3 randomized not treated, 6 lost to follow-up, one withdrawal of consent) and 2 oral nimodipine subjects (one randomized not treated, one lost to follow-up). Of subjects with day 90 outcome missing, the last observation (day 30) was carried forward in 6 and death was imputed in 5 in the EG-1962 group. The day 30 observation was carried forward for one subject in the oral nimodipine group. All subjects received the entire $6~\mathrm{mL}$ intraventricular injection and no subjects treated with EG-1962 crossed over to be treated with oral nimodipine. Exposure to oral investigational product was lower in the oral nimodipine group (total dose received based on number of capsules or tablets taken: EG-1962; $6017{\scriptstyle\pm1257~\mathrm{mg}}$ [mean, SD] versus oral nimodipine; $5589{\pm}1785\mathrm{mg}$ ).  

For the primary outcome, $45\%$ of subjects in the EG-1962 and $43\%$ in the oral nimodipine group achieved favorable outcome (GOSE 6–8) at day 90 follow-up (risk ratio $_{\mathrm{\Omega=1.01}}$ $[95\%$ $\mathrm{Cl}{=}0.83{-}1.22]$ , $P{=}0.95$ , adjusted logistic regression, Figure 2; Table 2).

The proportion of subjects with favorable outcome on the Montréal cognitive assessment was $34\%$ in the EG-1962 and $37\%$ in the oral nimodipine group (risk rati $\phantom{-}\phantom{}{=}1.17$ $[95\%$ CI, 0.88–1.55], $P{=}0.66$ , intention to treat logistic regression, Figure 3; Table 3). In the prespecified strata, WFNS grade 2 subjects had more favorable outcomes in the oral nimodipine group, whereas grades 3–4 subjects showed better outcome with EG-1962 (Tables 2 and 3). There also was an imbalance in outcomes between the groups depending on the region of the study (Tables 2 and 3).  

The EG-1962 group had a lower incidence of any vasospasm, angiographic vasospasm, DCI, and rescue therapy (Table I in the Data Supplement). After safety outcomes, fewer subjects in the EG-1962 were reported to have hypotension. Hydrocephalus was more common in the EG-1962 group whereas other adverse events occurred with similar frequency in the groups (Table I in the Data Supplement).  



# Discussion  

This study found that intraventricular administration of sustained-release nimodipine, $600~\mathrm{mg}$ was feasible and demonstrated a favorable safety profile. It did not demonstrate superiority to oral nimodipine in the intent to treat population of all randomized WFNS 2–4 subjects. Pharmacokinetics of EG-1962 showed that the $600~\mathrm{mg}$ dose produced plasma concentrations of 5 to $10\mathrm{ng/mL}$ that are slightly lower than those associated with oral nimodipine. CSF concentrations are orders of magnitude higher than those achieved with oral nimodipine.26,27  

A phase $1/2$ dose-finding, safety, pharmacokinetic, and clinical effects study found 4 of 9 subjects $(44\%)$ receiving EG-1962, $600\mathrm{mg}$ achieved favorable outcome defined the same as in this study and overall 27 of 45 $(60\%)$ receiving EG-1962 had favorable outcome compared to 5 of 18 $(28\%)$ treated with oral nimodipine.16  

Possible explanations for the lack of superior efficacy of EG-1962 include that the dose of EG-1962 may have been too high or too low. A lower dose might be more effective if there were deleterious effects of the high CSF nimodipine concentrations associated with doses of EG- $1962{\scriptstyle\geq}100$ mg or if there were other unidentified adverse effects of EG-1962. The safety profile of EG-1962 does not provide any evidence for this. No directly relatable complications, such as ventriculitis or hydrocephalus, occurred at a higher rate in the EG-1962 group.  

Pharmacokinetics of EG-1962 showed doses of 100 to $800~\mathrm{mg}$ produced similar CSF concentrations, so if a lower dose was used, it would have to be ${<}100~\mathrm{mg}$ (additional data unpublished). Such a dose would produce plasma nimodipine concentrations lower than with oral nimodipine. A higher dose of EG-1962 might be more effective if it achieved higher plasma concentrations. However, no dose-response was observed in the clinical trials of oral nimodipine.5 It also is possible that nimodipine did not permeate into the cisterns most filled with blood and presumably where high concentrations are most important.28,29  

The only changes in this study design compared with the first human study were implementation of blinding, use of a single dose, inclusion of more subjects and a 1:1 randomization ratio.16,17,30 Of these changes, only blinding and increased sample size increase could account for the current findings. Although every attempt was made to blind allocation, it is possible that the study arm could be surmised either at the time of injection or if a hypotensive response to the oral investigational product was observed. Given the large team comprising neurosurgeons, neurointensivists, and others caring for these patients and following patient management guidelines, it is unlikely that such observations introduced systemic bias against subjects in one of the study arms. Nimodipine may not have a favorable benefit: risk ratio in patients with contemporary SAH. The EG-1962 studies cannot provide any information about this because there were no patients that did not receive nimodipine in some form. Finally, EG-1962 may be effective, particularly in poor-grade patients, but detecting efficacy may be hampered by sample size, effect size, benefits of rescue therapy in the control group, insensitive outcome measures or other clinical trial design factors.

This study was designed to evaluate efficacy of EG-1962 compared with oral nimodipine. Although there was lack of superiority of EG-1962, EG-1962 reduced hypotension, vasospasm, and rescue therapy. A question is whether these features support use of EG-1962, and another study evaluating noninferiority of EG-1962 to oral nimodipine should be conducted. Such a noninferiority design, however, would be hampered by lack of confidence that the benefit of oral nimodipine demonstrated decades ago in randomized clinical trials is appropriate to guide design of such a study in the necessarily selected SAH population. In addition, the $95\%$ CI (0.67–1.77) of the adjusted odds ratio of favorable outcome in this study compared to these data from the prior oral nimodipine trials indicate that a much larger sample size would be required to demonstrate noninferiority using a reasonable noninferiority margin.  

The subgroup analysis trending toward efficacy in WFNS $3{+}4$ patients coupled with recent mechanistic preclinical work highlighting the possible effect of nimodipine on spreading depolarization31,32 or spreading ischemia33 suggests there may still be potential efficacy of local sustained release delivery in select high-risk patients. The trend toward efficacy in poor-grade subjects would be consistent with the higher risk of DCI in these subjects. This would have to be viewed in light of the trend towards worse outcome in good grade subjects and the lack of any difference in efficacy between grades in the phase 1/2a study.16 In addition, the proportion of patients with good outcomes in the oral nimodipine group $(43\%)$ was higher than expected $28\%$ in the phase $1/2\mathbf{a}$ study). Remarkably, $21\%$ (31/145) of oral nimodipine subjects who were WFNS grade 2–4 with an EVD had a GOSE of 8, meaning they reported no sequelae whatsoever of their SAH. This cannot be solely explained by inclusion of WFNS grade 1 subjects that were grade 2 after aneurysm repair, of which there were 12 of 145 $(8\%)$ of oral nimodipine subjects.

# Conclusions  

Locally delivered, sustained-release microparticles containing $600~\mathrm{mg}$ nimodipine did not increase favorable outcomes as compared to standard of care oral nimodipine. Favorable outcomes with EG-1962 trended higher in the prespecified WFNS 3–4 subgroup but lower in the WFNS 2 subgroup. The safety profile was acceptable. Future studies could investigate noninferiority of EG-1962 compared to oral nimodipine or evaluate poor-grade subjects.
