Editorial  

# Tranexamic acid: One more step towards its widespread use  

# ARTICLEINFO  

Keywords:   
Tranexamic acid   
Bleeding   
Trauma  

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is an antifibrinolytic agent that blocks the lysine-binding sites of plasminogen, resulting in inhibition of plasminogen activation and fibrin binding to plasminogen. Fibrinolysis is impaired, clot breakdown is inhibited, and bleeding gets reduced. Reliable evidence that TXA reduces blood loss and the need for blood transfusion in surgical patients has been available for over 20 years. TXA is therefore recommended to prevent and treat bleeding during major surgery [1,2]. During the last decade, several large trials also assessed the benefit of TXA in emergent bleeding situations (Fig. 1) [3–7]. The CRASH-3 trial (Clinical Randomisation of an Antifibrinolytic in Significant Head injury) is the most recent one and contributes to highlight both efficacy and safety of TXA [3].  

The CRASH-2 trial (Clinical Randomisation of an Antifibrinolytic in Significant Haemorrhage) was the first large, pragmatic randomised controlled trial from the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine Trials Unit investigating the use of TXA in bleeding patients [4,5]. Trauma patients with, or at risk of significant bleeding were randomly allocated within $^{8\mathrm{~h~}}$ following injury to receive either a loading dose of ${1\mathrm{~g~}}$ of TXA followed by an intravenous infusion of ${1\mathrm{~g~}}$ over $^{8\mathrm{~h~}}$ or matching placebo. The results showed a reduction in all-cause mortality with TXA and a reduction in death due to bleeding. The reduction was largest for those treated soon after injury and TXA treatment within $^{3\textrm{h}}$ of injury reduced the risk of death due to bleeding by nearly $30\%$ [5]. The benefit of TXA also seemed to be larger for patients with severe shock. Guidelines now recommend administering TXA to the trauma patient who is bleeding or at risk of significant haemorrhage as soon as possible and within $^{3\textrm{h}}$ after injury, and propose the administration of the first dose of TXA en route to the hospital [8].  

As many patients with traumatic haemorrhage also have traumatic brain injury (TBI) and intracranial bleeding, TXA is likely to reduce intracranial bleeding and thus mortality. This hypothesis has been evaluated in a randomised controlled trial nested within the CRASH-2 trial [6]. Eligible patients of the CRASH-2 trial that also had TBI were included. The analysis of the 270 participants suggested that TXA was likely to be associated with reduced intracranial haemorrhage growth without increasing the risk of focal ischaemic lesions. There was also a non-statistically significant association with improved mortality rate. However, this exploratory study was conducted among TBI patients with significant extracranial haemorrhage. The effect of TXA might be different in patients with isolated TBI, requiring a specific assessment.  

Consequently, the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine Trials Unit led the CRASH-3 trial to assess the effect of TXA in patients with isolated TBI: 12,737 patients with isolated TBI who were within $^{3\textrm{h}}$ of injury, had a GCS score of 12 or lower or any intracranial bleeding on CT scan, were randomly assigned to receive TXA (loading dose ${1\mathrm{g}}$ over $10\mathrm{min}$ then infusion of $^{1}{\mathrm{~g~}}$ over $^\textrm{\scriptsize8h)}$ or matching placebo [3]. TXA failed to reduce the primary endpoint, i.e. the risk of head injury-related death in hospital within the whole population. However, after exclusion of patients too severe to be saved, namely those with a GCS score of 3 or bilateral unreactive pupils at baseline, TXA reduced the risk of head injury-related death compared to placebo ( $12.5\%$ vs $14.0\%$ ; RR $0.89\left[95\%\mathrm{CI}0.80–1.00\right]$ ). Especially, TXA decreased the risk of head injury-related death in patients with mild-to-moderate head injury (RR 0.78 $\left[95\%\mathrm{CI}0.64–0.95\right]_{\cdot}$ ) but not in patients with severe head injury. As in CRASH-2, early treatment was more effective than later treatment in patients with mild and moderate head injury, with a $10\%$ decrease in treatment effectiveness for every 20-minute delay. Moreover, there was no increase in disability in survivors treated with TXA and the trial found no evidence of adverse effects.  

One may discuss whether we can conclude that TXA offers benefit after acute TBI while the primary endpoint of CRASH-3 is negative. For the most inflexible statisticians, the answer may be no [9]. Nevertheless, the subgroups where TXA improved outcomes were pre-specified, large (5615 patients with mild and moderate head injury) and the results were congruent with previous published data [6,10]. On the one hand, from a physiopathologic point of view, the most severe patients, including those with a GCS score of 3 and those with unilateral or bilateral unreactive pupils, are unlikely to benefit from TXA as they already have extensive intracranial haemorrhage or brain herniation before treatment. As they are hopelessly ill, their inclusion in the analyses systematically modifies difference in events expected by giving TXA. On the other hand, early administration of TXA is likely to improve outcome of patients with less severe injury by reducing haematoma expansion, which occurs within the first hours after injury. As a result, benefit from TXA is seen in patients with bilateral reactive pupils and those with GCS 9-15, with a timedependent effect on mortality. TXA is not the magic bullet but it is the first drug that provides pharmacological neuroprotection after TBI. After years of research and disappointing results, including corticosteroids, erythropoietin, magnesium, citicoline, progesterone and hypothermia, CRASH-3 is the first trial to show that a drug has neuroprotective effects for patients with TBI and can even reduce mortality.

The London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine Trials Unit also assessed the use of TXA for post-partum haemorrhage (PPH), so in the context that led to its discovery\*. The WOMAN trial (World Maternal Antifibrinolytic Trial) included 20,060 women with PPH after a vaginal birth or caesarean section randomly assigned to receive either ${\mathfrak{1}}{\mathfrak{g}}$ intravenous tranexamic acid or matching placebo in addition to usual care [7]. If bleeding persisted after $30\mathrm{min}$ or stopped and restarted within $24\mathrm{h}$ , a second dose of ${1\mathrm{g}}$ of TXA or placebo was given. Death due to bleeding was significantly reduced in women receiving TXA $1.5\%$ vs $1.9\%$ risk ratio [RR] 0.81, $95\%$ CI 0.65–1.00; $P=0.045$ ). However, neither all other causes of death nor hysterectomy differed significantly by group, resulting in persistent discussions between inflexible statisticians and more open-minded physicians [9]. The authors concluded that TXA reduced death due to bleeding in women with PPH with no adverse effects, strengthening European guidelines that already recommended the administration of TXA in PPH at a dose of $1\mathrm{~g~IV~}$ as soon as possible [8]. Interestingly, as in the CRASH-2 trial, this effect was only seen if TXA was given early, within $^{3\mathrm{~h~}}$ of giving birth [5,7].  

The London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine Trials Unit is currently analysing the effects of TXA in acute upper and lower gastrointestinal bleeding: 12,000 patients with acute gastrointestinal bleeding were randomly assigned in the HALT-IT trial to receive either a loading dose of ${1g}$ of TXA followed by an intravenous infusion of 3 g over $24\mathrm{~h~}$ or matching placebo [11]. The trial is measuring the effects of the treatment on hospital mortality but also on re-bleeding, non-fatal vascular events, blood transfusion, surgical intervention and general health status. Results are expected in 2020.  

Surprisingly, TXA safety is still a matter of debates although there is strong evidence demonstrating that TXA is safe. CRASH-2, CRASH3 and WOMAN, including thousands of patients, did not show any difference in the incidence of side effects [3,4,7]. The risk of thromboembolic events, including pulmonary embolism, deep-vein thrombosis, myocardial infarction and stroke did not differ significantly in the TXA versus the placebo group. In CRASH-2, there were even fewer fatal and non-fatal myocardial infarctions with TXA $\mathrm{RR}=0.64$ , $95\%\operatorname{CI}0.42$ to 0.97; $P=0.035$ ) raising the hypothesis that the antifibrinolytic agent may have an antithrombotic effect [4,12]. The potential mechanisms include inhibition of the inflammatory effects of plasmin, effects on platelets and effects on factors $\mathsf{V}$ and VIII. Moreover, the risk of seizure did not differ significantly between groups, even in the CRASH-3 trial [3].  

Of note, the increased use of TXA in operating theatres and labour wards has increased the consequent risks of drug errors, especially wrong-route drug errors. Reported cases of accidental spinal administration of TXA have increased over the last decade, highlighting TXA neurotoxicity [12,13]. TXA spinal administration results in generalised convulsions, hypertension, severe tachycardia and ventricular arrhythmia, with catastrophic outcomes and a mortality rate closed to $50\%$ [13,14]. Indeed, spinal administration of ${1g}$ of TXA is expected to produce a concentration in the cerebrospinal fluid approximately 7000 times higher than after intravenous injection. Management remains symptomatic and supportive, including anticonvulsants, such as thiopental and magnesium sulphate, and cerebrospinal fluid lavage. Strategies for prevention of wrong-route errors should be implemented to favour the widespread adoption of TXA for the management of bleeding patients.  

Now that we have one more demonstration that TXA is an inexpensive, widely available, safe and effective drug that has been shown to save lives, even after acute traumatic brain injury, let’s roll! And remember that every minute counts.  

\*Utako Okamoto (1918–2016) was a Japanese female research scientist who discovered TXA in the 1950s in her quest to find a drug that would treat bleeding during PPH.
