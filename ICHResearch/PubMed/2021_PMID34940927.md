# Lessons Learned from Phase II and Phase III Trials Investigating Therapeutic Agents for Cerebral Ischemia Associated with Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage  

Adnan I. Qureshi1, Iryna Lobanova $1^{*}\oplus$ Wei Huang1, Muhammad F. Ishfaq1, Joseph P. Broderick2, Christy N. Cassarly3, Renee H. Martin3, R. Loch Macdonald4 and Jose I. Suarez5  

2021 Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature and Neurocritical Care Society  



# Abstract  

One of the challenges in bringing new therapeutic agents (since nimodipine) in for the treatment of cerebral ischemia associated with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is the incongruence in therapeutic benefit observed between phase II and subsequent phase III clinical trials. Therefore, identifying areas for improvement in the methodology and interpretation of results is necessary to increase the value of phase II trials. We performed a systematic review of phase II trials that continued into phase III trials, evaluating a therapeutic agent for the treatment of cerebral ischemia associated with aSAH. We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines for systematic reviews, and review was based on a peer-reviewed protocol (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews no. 222965). A total of nine phase III trials involving 7,088 patients were performed based on eight phase II trials involving 1558 patients. The following therapeutic agents were evaluated in the selected phase II and phase III trials: intravenous tirilazad, intravenous nicardipine, intravenous clazosentan, intravenous magnesium, oral statins, and intraventricular nimodipine. Shortcomings in several design elements of the phase II aSAH trials were identified that may explain the incongruence between phase II and phase III trial results. We suggest the consideration of the following strategies to improve phase II design: increased focus on the selection of surrogate markers of efficacy, selection of the optimal dose and timing of intervention, adjustment for exaggerated estimate of treatment effect in sample size calculations, use of prespecified go/no-go criteria using futility design, use of multicenter design, enrichment of the study population, use of concurrent control or placebo group, and use of innovative trial designs such as seamless phase II to III design. Modifying the design of phase II trials on the basis of lessons learned from previous phase II and phase III trial combinations is necessary to plan more effective phase III trials.  

Keywords:  Phase II, Phase III, Clinical trials, Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, Death, Disability, Systematic review  



# Introduction  

Guidelines from the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association [1] emphasize the need for identifying new strategies for reducing death and disability rates associated with cerebral ischemia in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). The guidelines acknowledge that, apart from oral nimodipine, other interventions such as endothelin-1 antagonists [2], intravenous calcium channel antagonists [3], and magnesium sulfate [3] have not shown any benefit in phase III clinical trials despite promising results in phase II trials. The incongruence between results observed in phase II and phase III trials [4] appears to be a major impediment in bringing new therapeutic agents for treatment of cerebral ischemia associated with aSAH. We performed a systematic review of phase II trials that evaluated a therapeutic agent for treatment of cerebral ischemia associated with aSAH and continued forward into phase III trials. Our intent was to identify areas of improvement in the methodology and interpretation of phase II trials in patients with aSAH.  



# Methods  



# Registration  

The protocol is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with identification number 222965 [3, 4]. Three investigators (WH, MFI, and IL) independently abstracted the data to incorporate every published study from January 1st, 1995, to November 5th, 2020.  



# Literature Search  

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses. We performed a computerized literature search of the MEDLINE and Cochrane databases on November 05, 2020, with the following search terms: “clinical trials,” “subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH),” “hemorrhage,” “tirilazad,” “endothelin antagonist,” “randomized trials,” “phase II trial,” “phase III trial,” “stroke,” “aspirin,” “ticlodipine,” “standardized medical management,” “nicardipine,” “magnesium,” “blood in subarachnoid space,” “inflammation,” “free radicals,” “nitric oxide,” “tirilazad,” “endothelin,” “vasospasm,” “smooth muscle spasm,” “nimodipine,” and “cilostazol.”  

The complete search algorithm used in MEDLINE was as follows: SAH OR hemorrhage (Medical Subject Headings Terms) OR brain hemorrhage) OR cerebral hemorrhage) OR cerebrovascular) OR cerebrovascular accident) OR cerebrum Medical Subject Headings Terms) OR brain) AND phase 2) OR phase 3) OR trials) AND inflammation) OR free radicals) OR nitric oxide) OR endothelin) OR vasospasm) OR smooth muscle spasm) OR platelet mediated) OR platelet thrombi) OR proliferation) AND tirilazad,) OR steroid) OR statin) OR endothelin antagonist) OR nimodipine) OR magnesium) OR aspirin) OR cilostazol). An additional manual search included conference abstracts and bibliographies of candidate studies and recent systematic reviews for a comprehensive literature search.  



# Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria  

The following criteria was used to identify relevant studies: (1) original articles with full text available; (2) published in the English language; (3) not an editorial, letter to editor, or case report; (4) evaluated a therapeutic agent for treatment of cerebral ischemia associated with aSAH; (5) phase II trials that continued into phase III trials and results reported; and (6) phases II and III trials designs consistent with Food and Drug Administration (FDA) definitions of various trial phases [5].  



# Data Abstraction  

The data abstraction form identified the name of the study, phase of the study, design (blinded or not), number of centers, number of patients, age of patients, percent of women, percent of poor grade, percent of thick subarachnoid clot or Fisher grade 3, end points/outcomes used in the study, and interventions.  



# Results  

A total of 153 studies were identified, of which 17 met the study criteria (see Fig.  1). The trials evaluated the following therapeutic agents: intravenous tirilazad mesylate, intravenous nicardipine, intravenous clazosentan, intravenous magnesium, oral statins, and intraventricular EG-1962 (sustained release nimodipine) (see Tables  1 and 2). A total of eight phase II trials involving 1,558 patients and nine phase III trials involving 7,088 patients were performed. In two instances, one phase II trial resulted in two phase III trials, whereas in one instance, two phase II trials resulted in one phase III trial.  



# Intravenous Tirilazad Mesylate  

Tirilazad mesylate is a nonglucocorticoid 21-aminosteroid that inhibits lipid peroxidation and reduces cerebral vasospasm and ischemic injury [6].  



# Phase II Trial  

The sample size was chosen to detect a rate $0\mathrm{f}\geq5\%$ in serious and unexpected drug-related adverse events (with emphasis on hepatic and cardiac toxicity) in each tier: 60 patients in each of $0.6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ and $2~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ , and 120 patients in $6~\mathrm{mg/kg/day}$ $80\%$ power). The patients were randomly assigned to one of three daily dosage tirilazad tiers: $0.6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $(n=51)$ , $2~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $(n=42)$ ), or $6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $\displaystyle(n=91,$ ) tirilazad or vehicle $(n=61$ ) intravenously in divided doses within $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ after onset and continued through day 10 following aSAH [7]. All patients also received oral nimodipine.  

No serious adverse events of tirilazad treatment were identified at any of the three doses. Favorable outcome (defined by either good recovery or moderate disability on the Glasgow Outcome Score [GOS]) at 3 months was seen in $82\%$ , $90\%$ , and $71\%$ in $0.6~\mathrm{mg/kg},2.0~\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{kg}.$ and $6.0\mathrm{mg/kg}$ dose of tirilazad-treated group compared with $70\%$ in vehicle-treated group [7, 8]. The rate of symptomatic vasospasm was $31\%$ , $21\%$ , and $40\%$ in $0.6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ , $2.0~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ , and $6.0~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ dose of tirilazadtreated group compared with $41\%$ in vehicle-treated group. Although mortality rate was nonsignificantly higher in $6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tirilazad-treated group, none of the deaths were considered to be related to study drug.

# Phase III Trials  

Two phase III trials were initiated: one in Europe, Australia, and New Zealand and the other in North America initiating tirilazad within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ of onset and continued through 10  days post aSAH. The primary endpoint was symptomatic vasospasm (ischemic symptoms related to delayed cerebral vasospasm). Both the North American and Europe, Australia, and New Zealand trials were originally powered to detect a $33\%$ reduction in rate of symptomatic vasospasm. The primary outcome of North American trial was changed to mortality at day 76 based on results of the Europe, Australia, and New Zealand trial. The sample size of 300 patients in each group was considered adequate to detect a $50\%$ reduction in mortality in either of tirilazad mesylate groups (power $=80\%$ , $\upalpha=0.025$ with Bonferroni adjustment). The Europe, Australia, and New Zealand trial [9] randomly assigned 1,105 patients to placebo containing a citrate vehicle $(n=253.$ ), or $0.6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $\primen=257,$ ), $2~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tirilazad $(n=249)$ ), or $6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $n=256)$ of tirilazad. The North American trial randomly assigned [8] 902 patients (897 received at least one dose) to placebo containing a citrate vehicle $'n=300)$ , $2\mathrm{mg/kg/day}$ tirilazad $(n=298)$ ), or $6\mathrm{mg/kg/day}$ $\left(n=299\right)$ of tirilazad.

In the Europe, Australia, and New Zealand trial, patients randomly assigned to $6~\mathrm{mg/kg/day}$ of tirilazad had lower mortality (absolute reduction of $9\%$ , $p=0.01$ ) and symptomatic vasospasm (absolute reduction of $8\%$ , $p{=}0.048^{\prime}$ ) compared with patients treated with vehicle. The rates of favorable outcome by GOS at 3 months were $67\%$ , $67\%$ , $64\%$ , and $72\%$ in patients randomly assigned to vehicle, 0.6 mg/kg/day, $2~\mathrm{mg/kg/day}$ , and $6~\mathrm{mg/kg/day},$ respectively. The difference was nominally significant only between the $6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ and vehicle groups (absolute reduction of $5\%$ $\cdotp{=}0.01\$ ).  

In the North American trial, there were no significant differences among the $2~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ or $6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tirilazad groups compared with vehicle group with regards to symptomatic vasospasm or mortality rates. Favorable outcome was observed in $73\%$ , $68\%$ , and $72\%$ of patients randomly assigned to vehicle, $2~\mathrm{mg/kg},$ and $6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ of tirilazad, respectively. A post hoc analysis demonstrated that men with admission World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFNS) grades IV and $\mathrm{V}$ had a $33\%$ , $52\%$ , and $5\%$ mortality rate in the vehicle-treated group, $2\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{kg}$ and $6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tirilazad-treated groups $(p=0.03)$ . The mortality reduction was not seen in women suggesting that tirilazad was more effective in men.  



# Summary  

The rate of premature discontinuation was higher in phase III trials $(14-26\%)$ compared with phase II trial $(2\%)$ . Tirilazad was initiated within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ and within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ of the aSAH in phase III trials and phase II trial, respectively. There was no clear dose-dependent effect on outcomes in the phase II trial. However, discordance between phase II and phase III trials was more prominent in the North American trial. The North American trial used oral (not intravenous) nimodipine and had higher use of prophylactic hemodilution, hypertension, and hypervolemia, primary angioplasty; and anticonvulsant medication particularly phenytoin. There was evidence of benefit in men with WFNS grades IV and V in the North American trial (gender effect). Subsequently, careful assessment of pharmacokinetics of tirilazad suggested that phenytoin increased clearance by inducing metabolism, women (particularly young women) had increased clearance, and increased variability in clearance was seen in patients with aSAH [10–12]. The interaction between phenytoin and tirilazad highlighted the importance of identifying drug-drug interactions particularly pharmacokinetic cytochrome P450 enzyme- or transporter-mediated interactions prior to phase III trials. Recently, the FDA has provided guidance for developing a systematic, risk-based approach for evaluating drugdrug interactions either as stand-alone study or as part of phase II or phase III trials and determining essential information to communicate in labeling [13]. The document also provides guidance for the use of index perpetrators (predictably inhibit or induce drug metabolism by a given pathway) and index substrates (well defined increase in systemic concentrations when administered with a strong inhibitor) in such studies.  

Two additional randomized trials compared high daily dose tirilazad of $(15~\mathrm{mg/kg)}$ with controls in women [14, 15]. There were significant reductions in rate of symptomatic vasospasm and cerebral infarction with the $15~\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{kg}$ dose in women in both trials. There was a significant reduction in mortality in one trial and a trend was seen in the other trial with high-dose tirilazad in women with admission WFNS grades IV and V. Mortality at day 76 [8] or at day 91 [14, 15] was used as the primary outcome, which may be neither sensitive nor specific to identifying therapeutic benefit and requires large sample sizes for detection of any difference [16, 17]. Additional outcomes measures used such as GOS may also require further consideration regarding sensitivity for identifying therapeutic effects [18].  



# Intravenous Nicardipine  

Nicardipine is a dihydropyridine calcium channel antagonist with cerebral vasodilatory properties [19].  



# Phase II Trial  

The phase II trial was based on a previous dose-escalation study (dose levels from 0.01 to $0.15~\mathrm{{mg/kg/h})}$ of intravenous nicardipine performed in 67 patients with aSAH who had follow-up angiography performed 7–10  days post SAH [19]. A dose-related trend was noted: $24\%$ of 33 patients treated at the highest dose level and $68\%$ of 34 patients treated at lowest level developed arteriographic vasospasm. Hypotension (defined as a systolic blood pressure of $\leq100\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg})$ was observed at least once in 19 patients, more frequently with the largest dose but without adverse events that required discontinuation of the medication.  

Patients were randomly assigned to high-dose $'n=184$ , $0.15\mathrm{mg/kg/h)}$ or $50\%$ lower dose ( $\scriptstyle{n=181}$ , $0.075\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{{kg/h})}$ nicardipine which was administered by continuous intravenous infusion within 7  days of onset and continued for up to 14 days post aSAH [20]. The trial was prematurely terminated because of problems with stability of stored nicardipine.  

The study drug was initiated at $50\%$ of dose for $^{\mathrm{~8~h~}}$ , increased to $75\%$ for next $^{8\textrm{h}}$ , and increased to full dose if tolerated. If the full dose was not tolerated, the dose was reduced to half dose again and either increased back to full dose or discontinued. Subjects in the highdose group received a significantly smaller proportion of the planned dose than those in the low-dose group $(80\%\pm0.2\%$ vs. $86\%\pm0.2\%.$ , $p<0.05)$ , largely because of premature treatment termination after adverse events. There incidence of angiographic vasospasm was lower in the high-dose group compared with low-dose group $19\%$ vs. $39\%$ , $p{=}0.08,$ ) [20, 21] although follow-up angiography was only performed in $24\%$ and $17\%$ of patients in the high-dose and low-dose nicardipine treated  groups, respectively. Therefore, the comparison was underpowered due to small number of patients who underwent follow-up angiography. Ischemic symptoms attributed to vasospasm were reported in $31.5\%$ and $31.0\%$ of low-dose and high-dose nicardipine-treated patients, respectively.  



# Phase III Trial  

The sample size of 1600 patients (800 in each group) was planned to detect an $8\%$ increase in good recovery rate at 3 months, assuming that $28\%$ of patients would experience delayed ischemic deficits with associated death or disability in $14\%$ of patients (power $=90\%$ power. alpha $=0.05\mathrm{\AA}$ ). The magnitude of benefit was based on the assumption that death and disability due to vasospasm would be reduced by $50\%$ with intravenous nicardipine. A total of 906 patients [21] were randomly assigned to either high-dose nicardipine $\overset{\prime}{n}=449$ , $0.15~\mathrm{{mg/kg/h})}$ or placebo $(n=457)$ ); within 7 days and treatment was continued for up to 14  days following aSAH. The trial was terminated early because oral nimodipine became widely available and continued enrollment in the placebo arm while withholding oral nimodipine was not considered ethical.  

The incidence of symptomatic vasospasm $46\%$ vs. $32\%$ , $p<0.001,$ ), and therapeutic hypervolemia, induced hypertension, and/or hemodilution for symptomatic vasospasm ( $38\%$ vs. $25\%$ , $p<0.001\$ ) was higher in the placebo-treated group than in the nicardipine-treated group. The incidence of moderate to severe angiographic vasospasm was significantly lower in those randomly assigned to nicardipine compared with those randomly assigned to placebo ( $33\%$ vs. $51\%$ , $p<0.01$ ) although angiograms between days 7 and 11 were available for $23\%$ and $26\%$ of patients receiving nicardipine and placebo, respectively [22]. The treatment was discontinued prematurely due to adverse events in $14\%$ and $6\%$ of patients in nicardipine and placebo groups, respectively. Pulmonary edema and azotemia were observed in $6.0\%$ of patients receiving nicardipine compared to $2.4\%$ of patients receiving placebo $(p<0.01)$ . At 3  months, $55\%$ and $56\%$ of nicardipine-treated and placebo-treated patients had a good recovery according to the GOS, respectively, without any difference in mortality ( $17\%$ vs. $18\%$ ).  



# Summary  

None of trials recruited the intended target number of patients. The phase II trial provided evidence of reduction in angiographic (in a small subset) and symptomatic vasospasm with the higher dose compared with lower dose of intravenous nicardipine. The absence of a placebo group did not allow derivation of an estimated event rate difference between treatment and placebo groups for sample size estimates for the phase III trial. The higher dose of nicardipine of $0.15\mathrm{mg/kg/h}$ was used in the phase III trial despite higher rates of premature termination (compared with $0.075~\mathrm{{mg/kg/h})}$ in the phase II trial. The problems with stability of stored nicardipine seen in phase II trial highlighted the importance of compliance with FDA recommendations on Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls and Good Manufacturing Practices to ensure activity and stability of investigational drugs [23].  



# Intravenous Clazosentan  

Clazosentan is a selective endothelin receptor antagonist and ameliorates cerebral vasospasm in patients with aSAH [24] and was evaluated in Clazosentan in Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (CONSCIOUS) trials.  



# Phase II b CONSCIOUS‑1  

Seventy-nine patients per group were planned to detect a relative risk reduction of $50\%$ in moderate and severe angiographic vasospasm against placebo for each dose comparison $\overset{\prime}{\alpha}=0.0166\overset{\cdot}{,}$ assuming an event rate of $60\%$ in the placebo group. The trial randomly assigned 413 patients with aSAH [24] 1:1:1:1 to placebo or clazosentan (1, 5, or $15\mathrm{mg/h},$ ) initiated within $56\mathrm{~h~}$ and continued up to 14 days after initiation of treatment.  

Study treatment was completed by 85 $(89\%)$ , 83 $(77\%)$ , 85 $(77\%)$ , and 77 $(79\%)$ patients randomly assigned to placebo, $1~\mathrm{mg/h}$ , $5~\mathrm{mg/h}$ and $15~\mathrm{mg/h}$ groups, respectively. Moderate or severe angiographic vasospasm (central blinded ascertainment) was reduced in a dose-dependent fashion with clazozetan ( $43\%$ , $39\%$ , and $23\%$ in the 1, 5, and $15~\mathrm{mg/h}$ groups versus $66\%$ in the placebo group, $p<0.02$ for each group compared with placebo). Cerebral infarcts and delayed ischemic neurological deficit due to vasospasm on central review showed a trend toward improvement with clazosentan $(37\%,28\%$ , and $29\%$ in the 1, 5, and $15~\mathrm{mg/h}$ groups versus $39\%$ in the placebo group, nonsignificant). Clazosentan was associated with increased rates of pulmonary complications, hypotension, and anemia.  



# Phase III CONSCIOUS‑2 and CONSCIOUS‑3  

On the basis of CONSCIOUS-1, two phase III studies (CONSCIOUS-2 and CONSCIOUS-3) were initiated to assess the effect of clazosentan (initiated within $56\mathrm{~h~}$ and continued up to 14  days of aSAH) on the incidence of cerebral vasospasm-related morbidity and all-cause mortality, and clinical outcome, in patients with aSAH. Both CONSCIOUS-2 and CONSCIOUS-3 required presence of diffuse clot on the admission computed tomography (CT) scan.  

The effect of various doses of clazosentan in surgicaltreated and endovascular-treated patients were evaluated separately in CONSCIOUS-2 and CONSCIOUS-3, respectively. Two separate trials [25] were indicated due to lower rates of angiographic vasospasm $36\%$ vs. $55\%$ ) and delayed ischemic neurological deficits ( $15\%$ vs. $23\%$ ) in endovascular-treated patients compared with surgically treated patients as well as the $5~\mathrm{mg/h}$ dose was less effective in endovascular-treated patients in the exploratory analysis of CONSCIOUS-1 [24, 25].  

The primary efficacy end point was a composite of cerebral vasospasm-related morbidity and all-cause mortality within 6 weeks post aSAH, including vasospasm-related cerebral infarction or delayed ischemic neurological deficits (where vasospasm was the primary cause or a relevant contributing factor) or the requirement for rescue therapy for vasospasm. Due to discordance between investigators and central review committee in determination of the relationship between cerebral vasospasm and morbidity in CONSCIOUS-1, a blinded, centralized critical events committee was used to determine whether morbidity was related to cerebral vasospasm in CONSCIOUS-2 and CONSCIOUS-3.  

Assuming the rates of the primary endpoint were $40\%$ and $35\%$ in placebo-treated patients in CONSCIOUS-2 and CONSCIOUS-3, respectively (based on CONSCIOUS-1 data), the original sample size calculations for both CONSCIOUS-2 and CONSCIOUS 3 trials provided $90\%$ power to detect a $30\%$ relative risk reduction in the rate of primary endpoint with clazosentan $\langle\alpha=0.025,$ . On the basis of the interim analysis, this estimated rate of primary endpoint in placebo-treated patients was revised to $30\%$ leading to sample size of 1,157 in CONSCIOUS-2 [25]. In CONSCIOUS-3, assuming that $2\%$ of patients would not receive treatment, the sample size needed to detect a relative risk reduction of $30\%$ in patients that received at least one dose of clazosentan (5 or $15~\mathrm{mg/h})$ was 1500 [25].  

In CONSCIOUS-2, 1147 surgically treated patients with aSAH were randomly assigned to intravenous placebo $ \overset{\prime}{n}=383\$ ) or clazosentan $(5{\mathrm{~mg/h}},n{=}764)$ [2, 25]. There was no significant difference in the primary endpoint ( $21\%$ vs. $25\%$ , $p{=}0.10\$ ) or poor functional outcome (defined by Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended [GOSE] score $\leq4_{,}$ ) at 3  months $29\%$ vs. $25\%$ , $p{=}0.10,$ ) among patients treated with clazosentan and patients treated with a placebo. In CONSCIOUS-3, 577 endovascularly treated patients with aSAH were randomly assigned to placebo $'n=189,$ , or clazosentan $(5~\mathrm{mg/h}$ , $n{=}194$ or $15~\mathrm{mg/h}_{\cdot}$ , $n{=}188.$ ). The trial was halted prematurely (October 2010), after $38\%$ of the planned sample size was randomly assigned based on recommendations from the Data and  Safety Monitoring Board after nonsignificant results from CONSCIOUS-2 became available. Planned confirmatory analyses and formal testing of the global null hypothesis were not performed in CONSCIOUS-3. The analysis was underpowered due to a much smaller number of patients available than required as per power calculations used in planning of CONSCIOUS-3. In CONSCIOUS-3, the primary end point occurred in $27\%$ , $24\%$ , and $15\%$ of patients treated with placebo, clazosentan $5~\mathrm{mg/h},$ and clazosentan $15~\mathrm{mg/h}_{\cdot}$ , respectively, $(p=0.007$ for comparison between placebo-treated patients and $15~\mathrm{mg/hr}$ clazosentan-treated patients). Poor functional outcome occurred in $24\%$ , $25\%$ , and $28\%$ of patients treated with placebo, clazosentan $5~\mathrm{mg/h}$ , and clazosentan $15~\mathrm{mg/h}_{\mathrm{:}}$ , respectively (a nonsignificant difference). Pulmonary complications, anemia, and hypotension were more common in patients who received clazosentan than in those who received placebo in both trials.  



# Summary  

Two of three doses ( $\mathrm{\Delta}5~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ and $15~\mathrm{mg/kg)}$ of clazosentan evaluated in phase II trial were evaluated in the phase III trial $\mathrm{1~mg/kg}$ was not included). The analysis of the CONSCIOUS-3 data was underpowered and considered an exploratory analysis. The primary end point of vasospasm-related morbidity and all-cause mortality was significantly reduced with $15~\mathrm{mg/h}$ clazosentan without any difference in rates of functional outcome (defined by GOSE at 3  months). It remains unclear whether the lack of difference in functional outcomes between placebo-treated and clazosentantreated groups despite a lower rate of vasospasmrelated morbidity and all-cause mortality was partly due to higher frequency of pulmonary complications, anemia, and hypotension associated with clazosentan treatment. The combination of pulmonary complications, anemia, and hypotension [26–28] may be exceptionally detrimental (and perhaps an impediment for further evaluation of a therapeutic agent) because each of these three conditions is associated with an increased risk of death or disability in patients with aSAH.  



# Intravenous Magnesium  

Magnesium inhibits the release of excitatory amino acids and blocks the N-methyl-d-aspartate–glutamate receptor resulting in a reduction of ischemic injury in animal models [29, 30], and is a noncompetitive antagonist of voltage-dependent calcium channels. Two separate sets of phase II and phase III trials were initiated: Magnesium and Acetylsalicylic acid in Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (MASH) and Magnesium for Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (MASH-2) trials; and the Intravenous Magnesium Sulfate for Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (IMASH) trial.  



# Intravenous Magnesium Sulfate (Continuous 64 mmol/L/day)  



# Phase II trial MASH  

A sample size of a minimum of 200 patients was planned to detect a $40\%$ reduction in hypodense lesions on CT scans with magnesium ( $50\%$ in placebo and $30\%$ with magnesium treatment, power $=80\%$ , $\upalpha=0.05$ ). A total of 283 patients with aSAH were randomly assigned with a factorial design: magnesium versus placebo and acetylsalicylic acid versus placebo [31]. Intravenous magnesium sulfate $(64~\mathrm{{mmol}/\mathrm{{L}/\mathrm{{day})}}}$ was initiated within 4 days post SAH and continued until 14  days after aneurysm treatment [32]. A fixed intravenous magnesium dosing regimen of $64\mathrm{mmol},$ /day was used due to ability to maintain serum magnesium concentrations between 1.0 and $2.0\mathrm{mmol/L}$ in a previous dose-finding study [32].  

New hypodense lesions $46\%$ and $44\%$ ) and delayed cerebral ischemia (defined as the occurrence of a new hypodense lesion on CT with associated clinical cerebral ischemia, $16\%$ and $24\%$ ) were seen in patients treated with intravenous  magnesium and placebo, respectively. Excellent outcome (defined by modified Rankin scale [mRS] of 0) at 3  months was demonstrated in magnesium-treated patients compared with placebo-treated patients ( $13\%$ and $4\%$ , risk ratio [RR] 3.4; $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 1.3 to 8.9, $p{=}0.008;$ ). Poor outcome (mRS score of 4–5) at 3  months, was lower in magnesiumtreated patients compared with placebo-treated patients $27\%$ vs. $35\%$ , RR 0.77, $95\%$ CI 0.5–1.1, $p<0.0001)$ .  



# Phase III trial MASH‑2  

Poor outcome (mRS score of 4–6) at 3 months post SAH was the primary efficacy endpoint. Although a sample size of 1082 patients was considered adequate to detect an absolute $8\%$ reduction in poor outcome (power $=80\%$ , $\upalpha=0.05$ ) with intravenous magnesium sulfate treatment, a larger sample size of 1200 was eventually chosen to reliably detect a smaller treatment effect. A fixed intravenous magnesium dosing regimen of $64~\mathrm{mmol},$ /day was used due to previously demonstrated safety and ability to maintain serum magnesium concentrations between 1.0 and $2.0\mathrm{mmol/L}$ in a dose-finding study [32]. A total of 1204 patients with aSAH [3] were randomly assigned to receive intravenous magnesium sulfate $(n=606)$ or placebo $(n=597)$ within 4  days post SAH and continued until 14 days after aneurysm treatment. One subject was excluded as randomization code was missing because the medication packaging containing the study number was lost. Poor outcome occurred in $26.2\%$ and $25.3\%$ of patients in the intravenous magnesium group and placebo group, respectively (RR 1.03, $95\%$ CI 0.85–1.25). In a subgroup analysis of 616 patients [33], delayed cerebral ischemia occurred in $24.2\%$ and $26.4\%$ of patients treated with placebo and intravenous magnesium, respectively.  



# Summary  

The treatment effect of magnesium was overestimated based upon the phase II trial results. In MASH and MASH-2, $19\%$ and $11.7\%$ of patients did not receive any treatment for intracranial aneurysm. Poor outcome in those patients was $48\%$ , compared with $23\%$ in those who received treatment for aneurysm.  



# Intravenous Magnesium Sulfate (Using a Prescribed Dose and a Titrated to Targeted Serum Magnesium Concentrations)  



# Phase II Trial  

A total of 60 patients with aSAH [34] were randomly assigned to receive intravenous magnesium sulfate (80  mmol/day, $n=30$ ) or saline infusion $(n=30)$ ) within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ post SAH and continued for 14  days. Patients also received intravenous nimodipine. A lower rate of symptomatic vasospasm ( $23\%$ vs. $43\%$ , $p{=}0.06,$ and higher rate of favorable outcome (good recovery or moderate disability according to the GOS at 6 months, $67\%$ versus $53\%$ , $p{=}0.29^{\cdot}$ ) was seen in patients treated with intravenous magnesium sulfate compared with the placebo group.  



# Phase II Trial  

A total of 40 patients with aSAH [35] were randomly assigned to receive intravenous magnesium (serum $\mathrm{Mg^{++}}$ target 4 to $5.5~\mathrm{mg/dl}$ , $n{=}20$ ) or control solution $(n=20)$ within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ post SAH and continued for 10 days. Symptomatic vasospasm, confirmed by angiography, occurred in $30\%$ and $31\%$ of patients receiving magnesium and placebo, respectively. A higher proportion of patients treated with magnesium had a favorable neurological outcome (GOS 4 to 5 at 3-month post SAH, $65\%$ vs. $50\%$ , $p{=}0.36)$ [35].  



# Phase III Trial IMASH  

A sample size of 348 patients was planned to detect an absolute increase in rate of favorable outcome of $15\%$ assuming a $50\%$ event rate in placebo group [35] $\scriptstyle({\mathrm{power}}=80\%$ , two-sided $\upalpha=0.05$ ). A post hoc recalculation using $63\%$ favorable outcome in the control group yielded $85\%$ and $49\%$ power to detect $15\%$ and $10\%$ absolute increase, respectively. A total of 327 patients with aSAH [36] were randomly assigned to magnesium (serum magnesium target twice of baseline value, $n{=}169.$ ) or saline placebo $(n=158)$ ) within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ post SAH and continued for 10 to 14 days. Favorable outcome at 6 months (GOSE score 5–8) was seen in $64\%$ and $63\%$ of patients in the magnesium group and saline group, respectively (odds ratio [OR], 1.0). The proportions of patients with clinical vasospasm were similar $25\%$ vs. $18\%$ ) in the magnesium group and saline group, respectively.  



# Summary  

An important difference in the trials is the time to initiation and treatment duration as well as dose used were different in the phase II and phase III trials. No sample size calculation was reported for either of the two phase II trials. The pilot study used fixed dose of intravenous magnesium sulfate (80 mmol/day) [34] and IMASH used magnesium infusion titrated to achieve target serum magnesium concentration (serum magnesium target twice of baseline value) [36]. Initial sample size estimate for a phase III trial based on the pilot study [34] was approximately 800 patients which was much larger than then sample size used in IMASH. IMASH was conducted between June 1, 2002, and December 31, 2008, to detect an absolute increase of $15\%$ in favorable outcomes at 6  months with magnesium treatment (compared with placebo). The results of MASH were published in 2005 [31] which suggested that a $7\%$ reduction in poor outcome occurred with intravenous magnesium. Several other studies [31, 34–38] were published between 2002–2010 which demonstrated that absolute difference in good outcome defined as GOS score 4 and 5 varied considerably and was lower than the estimate used in the design of the IMASH trial. These observations highlight the need for adequate preliminary data for estimating treatment effect prior to planning a phase III trial and methods for incorporating new data to modify the design of ongoing trials.  



# Oral Statins  

Statins (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase inhibitors) increase nitric oxide release from endothelium, reduce inflammation, and increase ischemic threshold [39]. A pilot trial was conducted followed by phase III Simvastatin in Aneurysmal  

Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (STASH) trial to evaluate effect of statins in patients with aSAH.  



# Phase II Trial  

A sample size of 80 patients was planned to detect a $50\%$ reduction in transcranial Doppler demonstrated vasospasm duration $\mathrm{(power}=80\%$ , two-sided $\upalpha=0.05$ , assuming $64\%$ event rate in control patients). A total of 80 patients with aSAH [40] were randomly assigned to receive oral pravastatin $(40~\mathrm{mg})$ or placebo daily within $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ and for up to 14 days. Vasospasm occurred in $62.5\%$ and $42.5\%$ of patients treated with placebo and statin, respectively $\left(p=0.006\right)$ . There was a significant reduction in vasospasm-related delayed ischemic deficits, and in hospital mortality in the pravastatin group.  



# Phase III Trial STASH  

A sample size of 800 patients was planned to detect a $7\%$ absolute increase in proportion of patients with good outcome (power $=90\%$ , two-sided $\upalpha=0.05^{\cdot}$ ) using an ordinal analysis of the 6-month mRS. A total of 803 patients with aSAH [41] were randomly assigned to receive either simvastatin $\mathrm{(40~mg}$ , $n=391$ ) or placebo $(n=412)$ daily within $96\mathrm{{h}}$ and continued for 21 days. The study drug was stopped on discharge from the neurosurgical unit in those believed to be at low risk for cerebral vasospasm (prior to 21  days). The trial was initiated on Jan 6, 2007, but there was an 8-month recruitment hold from May 15, 2009, to address regulatory issues regarding trial drug labeling and dispensing. On resolution, recruitment in non–United Kingdom sites was not feasible anymore. The mean time interval between onset and first dose was 2.1 and 2.2  days for simvastatin and placebo treatment, respectively. At 6 months, $72\%$ and $72\%$ of simvastatin-treated and placebo-treated patients had a favorable outcome defined by $\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2$ , respectively. The primary ordinal analysis of the mRS, adjusted for age and WFNS grade on admission, gave a common OR of 0.97 $(95\%\mathrm{CI}0.75–1.25)$ . At 6 months, $10\%$ and $9\%$ of simvastatin treated and placebo-treated patients had died (log-rank $\mathrm{p}{=}0.592.$ ). For the secondary outcomes, clinical delayed ischemic deficits occurred in $16\%$ of simvastatin and placebo groups. Study drug compliance was relatively low, with only 496 $(62\%)$ of 803 patients taking full trial medication according to the protocol. Of the 307 patients, 62 patients were withdrawn from study medication because of subject withdrawing from study $(n=16)$ , use of contraindicated medication $(n=18)$ , abnormal liver function tests $(n=13)$ , missed several doses $(n=6)$ , rash $(n=2)$ , nausea $(n=1)$ , and undetermined cause $(n=6)$ . Distribution of mRS scores did not differ between groups in patients who were recorded as being fully trial drug compliant (OR 1.13, $95\%$ CI 0.82–1.56). A post hoc analysis did not find any difference in proportion of patients with favorable outcome in strata defined by time interval (0–24, 25–48, 49–96, and $>96\mathrm{~h~}$ ) between symptom onset and first dose $\overset{\prime}{\underset{\mathit{P}}{=}}0.96\$ ).  



# Summary  

Some heterogeneity exists between the trials with respect to type of statin (pravastatin and simvastatin), time of drug initiation $^{\prime}72\mathrm{~h~}$ and $96~\mathrm{h}$ ), and length of treatment (14  days and 21  days). There are differences between pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic properties of pravastatin and simvastatin [42] although the relationship with clinical effectiveness in patients with aSAH is unclear. Pravastatin is the active open hydroxy acid form while simvastatin is an inactive prodrug (lactone that requires conversion to simvastatin hydroxy acid) [42]. Simvastatin is highly lipophilic with high affinity for plasma proteins. Pravastatin has much higher water solubility and low affinity for plasma proteins. The lactone form of simvastatin and simvastatin hydroxy acid have higher permeability through blood brain barrier compared with pravastatin.  

The phase III trial was initiated in 2007. After initiation of the STASH study, two other phase II trials were published in 2008 and 2013 [43, 44] which reported nonsignificant lower rates of vasospasm detected by transcranial Doppler [44] and by angiography [43] with high-dose simvastatin $(80\mathrm{mg/day)}$ without any detectable reduction in death or disability. A meta-analysis published in 2010 summarizing the effect of four randomly assigned trials did not identify any reduction in poor outcome (pooled RR 0.92; $95\%$ CI 0.68–1.24) or mortality (pooled RR 0.37; $95\%$ CI 0.13–1.10) [45]. These observations highlight the need for adequate preliminary data for estimating treatment effect prior to planning a phase III trial and methods for incorporating new data to modify the design of ongoing trials.  



# Intraventricular Sustained Release Nimodipine  

EG-1962 consists of nimodipine nanoparticles in a biodegradable polymer suspended in hyaluronic acid administered as one intraventricular injection that releases nimodipine into the subarachnoid space for at least 21  days [46]. Nimodipine Microparticles to Enhance Recovery While Reducing Toxicity After Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (NEWTON) 1 and 2 trials evaluated the therapeutic benefit of EG-1962 in patients with aSAH.  



# Phase II Trial NEWTON  

A total of 72 patients with aSAH were randomly assigned in an open label design to receive a single dose via intraventricular EG-1962, $(n=54)$ or routine enteral nimodipine $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{(}n=18,\ell}\mathrm{)}$ ) within $60\mathrm{~h~}$ post SAH [46]. The trial included patients with WFNS grade 2 to 4 patients with an external ventricular drain inserted as standard of care and limited recruitment to those aged 75  years or less [46]. Stringent inclusion criteria resulted in exclusion of 923 of 998 patients who were screened for enrollment. Cohorts of 12 patients received a single dose of 100 to $1{,}200~\mathrm{mg}$ of EG-1962 in a 3:1 (EG-1962: enteral nimodipine) ratio to determine the maximum tolerated dose. Oral or intravenous nimodipine was permissible prior to randomization. There was a second phase planned to compare the safety and tolerability of the selected dose of EG-1962 based on analysis of 72 patients with aSAH with enteral nimodipine but was not conducted because there were only two dose-limiting toxicities with no relationship between serious adverse events and dose.  

In the NEWTON trial [47, 48], the maximum tolerated dose was ${800}~\mathrm{mg}$ . The maximum tolerated dose was not based on prespecified threshold of $\geq$ three patients having dose-limiting toxicity in any of EG-1962 treatment tiers. No safety concerns limited dose escalation to $1200~\mathrm{mg}$ . However, injection volume for $1200~\mathrm{mg}$ was $12~\mathrm{ml}$ and only three patients received full dose. Favorable outcome at 3 months on the GOSE occurred in $60\%$ and $28\%$ ( $56\%$ with $100~\mathrm{mg}$ , $67\%$ with $200~\mathrm{mg}$ , $78\%$ with $400~\mathrm{mg}$ , $44\%$ with $600~\mathrm{mg}$ , $56\%$ with $800~\mathrm{mg}.$ , and $67\%$ with $1200~\mathrm{mg})$ of EG-1962–treated and enteral nimodipine–treated patients, respectively. The rates of favorable responses did not exhibit any dose response, as the rate was already high at the $100~\mathrm{mg}$ dose. EG-1962 reduced delayed cerebral ischemia ( $31\%$ versus $61\%$ , $p{=}0.03)$ , and the initiation of rescue therapy ( $24\%$ vs. $56\%$ , $p{=}0.02,$ compared with enteral nimodipine. Study medication– related adverse event rate of $22\%$ was similar between the EG-1962 and enteral nimodipine groups.  



# Phase III Trial NEWTON‑2  

Sample size of 374 patients was planned to detect an increase $0\mathrm{f}\geq15\%$ in the proportion of patients with a favorable GOSE at 3 months treated with EG-1962 post aSAH ( $85\%$ power, one-sided $\upalpha=0.025$ ) [47]. The $600~\mathrm{mg}$ EG-1962 dose was chosen because it had the most optimal tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy in the phase II dose-escalation study. A total of 289 patients with aSAH were randomly assigned to receive a single dose intraventricular dose of $600~\mathrm{mg}$ EG-1962/or oral placebo $(n=144)$ or a single intraventricular injection of sodium chloride $\mathrm{(NaCl)/}$ oral nimodipine ( $\overset{\prime}{n}=145)$ within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ post SAH using the same inclusion criteria as the NEWTON trial. Blinding of study treatment was maintained by using a nontransparent injection syringe to prevent identification of intraventricular EG-1962 suspension and using identical appearing oral nimodipine and placebo capsules. The study end points were ascertained by study investigators who blinded to study intervention and care of patients. Enrollment in the trial was halted for futility based on a planned interim analysis after 210 patients completed 90-day follow-up. There was no difference in proportion with favorable outcome on the GOSE in the EG-1962 and placebo group ( $45\%$ vs. $42\%$ , RR 1.01 $[95\%$ CI 0.83–1.22], $p{=}0.68,$ ). A prespecified subgroup analysis suggested potential efficacy in WFNS 3–4 patients $46\%$ EG-1962 versus $32\%$ placebo, $p{=}0.13,$ ). EG-1962 significantly reduced vasospasm $750\%$ vs $63\%$ , $p{=}0.03^{\cdot}$ ) and hypotension $7\%$ vs. $10\%$ , $p{=}0.33.$ ) compared with control group.  



# Summary  

The decision to move forward with the phase III trial was based on reduced rates of cerebral ischemia and rescue therapy and high rates of favorable outcomes at 3 months with EG-1962 in the phase II trial. The analysis of various doses of intraventricular EG-1962 in the phase II trial did not demonstrate a dose-dependent benefit. One of the challenges in the phase II trial was the blinding of treatment since the dose of intraventricular EG-1962 suspension was identifiable by the volume of injection and also smaller dose was tested prior to testing higher dose identifying the dose tier (open label dose-escalation design).  



# Discussion  



# Strategies Identified  

Several strategies were identified in our review of previous phase II trials and their translation into phase III trials, and pertinent literature that could potentially increase the predictive value of phase II trials (see Table 3 and below) in aSAH [4].  

Identify More Appropriate Surrogate End Points of Efficacy Reduction in unvalidated surrogate end points such angiographic and/or symptomatic vasospasm with nicardipine, clazonestan, and EG-1962 was demonstrated in both phases II and III trials, without any reduction in the rate of death or disability in phase III trials [49]. Part of the incongruence is attributable to occurrence of cerebral infarction by vasospasm-independent pathways [50]. Concurrent assessment of cerebral angiography and tissue perfusion with 15O-positron emission tomographic imaging [51] suggests that regional hypoperfusion and oligemia frequently occurs in territories and patients without angiographic vasospasm. Presence of autoregulatory failure also contributes to occurrence of cerebral infarction independent of angiographic vasospasm [52, 53]. Relatively high interobserver variability seen in phase III trial of nicardipine and CONSCIOUS-1 also limits use of surrogate end points such as symptomatic vasospasm and cerebral ischemia [21, 24]. Cerebral ischemia as a surrogate endpoint may not be representative of secondary injuries following aSAH which involves inflammatory response [54, 55] and likely glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic dysfunction [56]. This cascade of events manifests as early and delayed hydrocephalus and diffuse cerebral edema [57, 58]. A perfect surrogate end point in phase II trial should be predictive of both qualitative and quantitative components of treatment effect measured by phase III trial end points [59]. A surrogate endpoint that identifies treatment benefit is acceptable for screening new therapies for phase III trials [60, 61]. There continues to be need for surrogate end points for phase II trials in patients with aSAH that are validated across multiple studies using meta-analytic approaches with quantification of reliability using $R^{2}$ -type measures [62], plots of within-trial treatment arm differences comparing definitive trial and surrogate end points, and/or $\mathbf{Z}$ -statistic for the surrogate end point [59, 62–64].  



# Selection of Optimal Dose  

Dose-finding designs and/or analysis that identify the most promising doses or doses to use in later studies [65, 66] were lacking in phase II trials of intravenous magnesium and oral statins. The dose-finding trials should identify a no-effect dose and the mean effective and maximal effective doses of a therapeutic agent [67]. Poor dose selection is considered one of the main reasons for high failure rates in phase III trials [68]. Parallel dose comparison studies (for tirilazad) or dose-escalation design (for intraventricular EG-1962) have been used but various techniques such as Bayesian methods and modeling, and pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic approaches were not used. The Bayesian methods allow selection of the dose for new patients within a phase II trial using both toxicity and activity data from patients previously treated in the trial using modeling functions [69]. Data from completed trials with fixed dose designs can be reanalyzed as a continuum of dose levels if doses are reconfigured on a $\mathrm{mg/kg}$ basis, or adjusted for renal function, or lean body mass. Mathematical modeling for quantification of a graded dose response relationship [70] may provide more precise dose discrimination. The differences in bioavailability between enteral and intravenous routes for a therapeutic agent should also be considered which may result in preferential use of intravenous route. Enteral administration of nimodipine showed lower bioavailability compared with intravenous administration particularly in patients with poor-grade aSAH [71, 72].  



# Selection of Optimal Timing of Intervention  

The time windows for intervention in almost all trials were selected arbitrarily without analyzing therapeutic response as a function of time interval between aSAH onset and therapeutic agent initiation in contrast to detailed analyses performed in ischemic stroke trials [73]. Initiating the therapeutic intervention was often delayed to 4 (intravenous magnesium) to 7  days (intravenous nicardipine) post aSAH which may have been suboptimal in preventing early cerebral ischemia in patients with aSAH. Early cerebral infarction on CT scan was seen in $21.5\%$ of patients within 3 days of aSAH [74] and in $27\%$ of patients $24{-}48\mathrm{~h~}$ after aneurysm treatment [75] and was associated with higher rates (compared with late infarction) of death or disability at 3- and 6-months post aSAH [74, 75]. Procedure related cerebral infarctions may not be amenable to therapeutic agents targeting cerebral ischemia [76] and may have to excluded from phase II trial analysis. However, NA-1 (Tat-NR2B9c), an inhibitor of postsynaptic density-95 protein initiated at the end of endovascular treatment of ruptured or unruptured intracranial aneurysm resulted in lower rates of infarctions (compared with placebo) on MRI at $12{-}95\mathrm{~h~}$ after infusionin a phase II trial suggesting that early infarctions can also be reduced by appropriately timed administration of $^{a}$ pertinent therapeutic agent [77].

# djustment for Exaggerated Estimates of Treatment Effect  

Using a large treatment effect observed in phase II trials of intravenous magnesium (absolute difference of $15\%$ and intraventricular EG-1962 (absolute difference of $32\%$ ) resulted in sample size selection for phase III trial that was inadequate for detecting smaller magnitude but clinically meaningful differences. The treatment effect is frequently overestimated in phase II trials [2, 3, 7, 8, 20, 21, 24, 31, 34–36, 40, 41, 78] as a result of disproportionate distribution of patients with prognostically important characteristics or ascertainment bias with open label designs or selection of optimized clinical centers. An analysis of 57 pairs of phase II and phase III trials performed in oncology [79] found that the treatment effect was $26\%$ larger in phase II trials compared with phase 3 trials. Subsequently, only 15 $(26.3\%)$ of the 57 phase III trials were positive despite positive phase II trials. Another prominent discrepancy was seen in phase II b trial [80] and phase III trial [81] evaluating the use of recombinant factor VII in patents with intracerebral hemorrhage. The phase II b trial demonstrated a reduction in severe disability or death with recombinant factor VII which was not seen in phase III trial. Several explanations were proposed including practice variations by expansion to more sites (73 sites increased to 122 sites), lower rate of severe disability or death in the placebo group $45\%$ reduced to $24\%$ ), and overall improvement in overall care (2002–2004 and 2005–2007). Exaggerated treatment effect estimates from phase II trials should be anticipated during the design of a phase III trial. A sample size re-estimation can be planned which addresses the potential that the assumptions made during the phase III design were not accurate [82, 83].  



# Prespecification of Go/No‑Go Criteria  

In the phase II trials involving patients with aSAH (see Tables  1 and 2), quantitative thresholds for continuing an intervention into phase III trials were mostly not prespecified and at times based on sponsor’s decision. Interpretation of absolute treatment effect may require a prespecified clinically meaningful futility margin [82, 83] and method of comparison that adjusts for testing of multiple null hypotheses within the same analysis and appropriately reflect the small sample sizes and multistage design of phase II trials [84]. Futility designs with the futility hypothesis based on a direct comparison of the randomly assigned treatment arms have been used in phase II trials involving patients with ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage [83]. Futility is declared if the absolute treatment effect is less than δ, a prespecified clinically meaningful futility margin, in  favor of the experimental treatment. For example, the primary hypothesis in the Intracerebral Hemorrhage Deferoxamine (i-DEF) trial specified that if the difference in good outcome proportions ( $\mathrm{\Delta}_{\mathrm{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\mathrm{mRS}0{-}2$ at day-90) was less than $12\%$ in favor of deferoxamine, then it would be futile to move deferoxamine forward to phase III trial [85].  



# Use of Multicenter Design  

Some of the phase II trials evaluating intravenous magnesium sulfate were single center studies. The ratio of centers between phase III and phase II trials ranged from 1.95 in the nicardipine cooperative aneurysm study trial to 10 in the intravenous magnesium trial (see Table  1) [86]. Multicenter designs that include an adequate number of centers may be required to provide a reliable measure of heterogeneity in overall patient management and protocol compliance. Such heterogeneity was identified in an analysis of 5,972 patients with aSAH enrolled in randomly assigned clinical trials in the Subarachnoid Hemorrhage International Trialists (SAHIT) repository. Single center studies do not identify biases introduced by low enrolling centers seen in phase III trials ( $78\%$ of the participating centers in 51 cerebrovascular diseases trials enrolled $<2\%$ of trial participants) [87], the effect of stratification by center [88], low number of events per center [89], or the exclusion of participants with fixed-effects models or Mantel–Haenszel methods if they belong to a center in which no participants in a certain treatment arm experienced an event.  



# Appropriate sample Size  

To adjust for high variance in patients with aSAH in rates of death or disability between trials (note large $95\%$ CIs in phase II trials of intravenous magnesium trials and intraventricular EG-1962 in Table  2) or in large cohorts of patients with aSAH [90, 91] sample size must be adequate to provide reliable measure of target response rate [92] within prespecified precision. Sample size calculations were not reported for both of the phase II trials that were used to design IMASH. The sample size of the phase II trial of nicardipine was much smaller than intended, confounding any quantitative interpretation.  



# Enrichment Strategies  

Enrichment strategies involve prospective use of any patient characteristic to select a study population in which detection of a  drug  effect is more likely than it would be in an unselected population [93]. There is limited use of enrichment strategies focusing on patient homogeneity, prognostic characteristics ensuring high rates of end points, or the selection of patients who are more likely to respond to the treatment based on existing data. For example, a treatment reducing cerebral ischemia in patients with aSAH is likely to most effective in those at high risk of cerebral ischemia such as patients with thick subarachnoid blood clot (prognostic enrichment) which was not always used in phase II trials (Table  1). Kreiter et  al. [94] estimated that limiting recruitment to patients with thick subarachnoid clot could reduce sample size by $10\%$ . Enrichment should be balanced with overall recruitment and generalizability goals and with potential label restrictions.  



# Use of Control or Placebo Group  

Most of the phase II trials in aSAH included in this review included a control or placebo group except the intravenous nicardipine trial. Temporal changes in outcome associated with improvements in patient management, variations in eligibility criteria, protocol adherence, and the definition of primary outcome measures across clinical trials necessitate inclusion of control patients for estimation of event rates in untreated patients [82, 83].  Due to high variability between cohorts in rates of clinical end points among patients with aSAH (see Table  2), deriving threshold values from historical controls may not be reliable in single arm phase II trials and therefore fail to provide the requisite data necessary for proceeding with a phase III trial [82, 83]. A simulation study [95] suggested that randomly assigned phase II trials with a control group performed better in the presence of a negative historical bias, in the presence of high variability, and were more consistent across variation of historical bias. Single arm phase II trials were highly sensitive to the ratio of patients from treatment centers (high versus low patient volumes), the presence of patient selection effects or temporal drift in response rates, and/ or random small sample variation in historical controls [96] and these biases were not corrected by increasing the sample size [97].  



# Innovative Phase II Trial Designs  

The lack of new treatments in patients with aSAH is similar to the widening gap between scientific discoveries and their translation into innovative medical treatment in other diseases recognized by the FDA in 2004 followed by new emphasis on use of adaptive designs in clinical trials [98]. Adaptive designs that allows modifications to the trial and/or statistical procedures of the trial after its initiation without undermining its validity and integrity to reduce conduct time and numbers of patients [99] have not been widely used in trials involving aSAH patients. Traditional (frequentist) statistical methods were predominantly used which use information from previous studies only at the design stage [100]. In contrast, the Bayesian approach uses Bayes’ Theorem to formally combine data from interim analyses and accommodate change to sample size or randomization scheme, and even some unplanned trial modifications. There have been some interim modifications in trial designs in aSAH trial which may not be consistent with formal adaptive designs. For example, the North American trial of Tirilazad design changed the primary outcome from symptomatic vasospasm to mortality at day 76 based on the results of Europe, Australia, and New Zealand trial. Another adaptive design that has not been used in trials involving patients with aSAH is the combined phase II/III trials [101] where the selection of the most effective experimental treatment in phase II continues in the phase III trial if futility threshold is not met. Another benefit of the seamless phase II/III design is that data from the phase II can be included in the final analysis [102]. Such a design was used in comparing tenecteplase with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) for acute ischemic stroke [103]. In the phase II trial, an early (24-h) outcome of major neurological improvement was to be used to selected one of three tenecteplase doses. Subsequently, randomization between the selected dose and rt-PA continued in the phase III trial with four interim analyses and one final analysis of 1,908 patients.  



# Limitations of Our Analysis  

Our review included only phase II that continued into phase III studies thus biasing analysis to phase II studies that provided promising results and excluded phase II trials that did not provide promising results. New designs of phase II trials, such as the futility or adaptive designs mentioned, have to be validated in patients with aSAH by demonstrating the ability to identify treatments effects that are comparable to those seen in subsequent phase III trials. Certain therapeutic interventions such as intravenous calcium antagonist fasudil hydrochloride (completed before our study period [104, 105]) and intrathecal thrombolytics (nine pilot trials [106, 107] without a phase III trial) did not meet criteria for inclusion. We acknowledge that various aspects of phase II trials such as reliance on surrogate end points, lack of optimal dose or optimal time window, or exaggerated treatment effect, had different effects on incongruence phase II and III trials pertaining to intravenous tirilazad mesylate, intravenous nicardipine, intravenous clazosentan, intravenous magnesium, oral statins, and intraventricular EG-1962. We were unable to consistently differentiate between prespecified secondary analyses and post hoc analyses [108] due to lack of details provided within the statistical analysis plan in several trials. Furthermore, criteria to assess the scientific merit of results derived from such analyses [109] were not applied to reduce the confounding effect of small sample size and chance.  



# Other considerations  



# Establishing Standards for Preclinical Data  

We did not perform an analysis of use (or lack of use) preclinical data for identifying therapeutic effect, dose selection, and time window for maximum benefit. For ischemic stroke trials, Stroke Therapy Academic Industry Roundtable (STAIR) [110, 111] provided recommendations for identifying the appropriate neuroprotective agents for evaluation in phase II and phase III trials such as demonstration of efficacy (short-term and long-term histological and functional outcomes) in at least two species, and in at least two laboratories that use different models, and in both permanent and transient focal ischemia. A consistent minimum neuroprotective concentration across different species allows prediction of starting dose in humans. STAIR [112] also identified sex differences in drug effectiveness as a consideration which may have been relevant to tirilazad. In 2010, FDA also recommended that the starting dose of an investigational agent should have a pharmacologic effect and be reasonably safe to use based on preclinical models [113].  



# Successful Phase III Trials Without Phase II Trials in Patients with aSAH  

There is precedence in proceeding to phase III trials without phase II trials in patients with aSAH although event rates may be overestimated during planning. Oral nimodipine $\mathrm{60~mg}$ every $4\mathrm{~h~}$ ) initiated within $96\mathrm{~h~}$ of aSAH was evaluated in two trials in patients with aSAH without a preceding phase II trial or any dose-finding studies. Allen et al. [114] reported that a deficit from cerebral arterial spasm that persisted and was severe or caused death by the end of the 21-day treatment period occurred in 8 of 60 patients given placebo and in 1 of 56 given nimodipine $(p=0.03)$ . The British aneurysm nimodipine trial [115] randomly assigned 554 patients based on the assumption that cerebral infarction would occur in $15\%$ of placebo-treated patients and $7\%$ $50\%$ relative reduction) in nimodipine treated patients. The incidence of cerebral infarction ( $22\%$ versus $33\%$ ) and poor outcome at 3  months ( $20\%$ versus $33\%$ ) was significantly lower among patients randomly assigned to nimodipine. However, the incidence of cerebral infarction was much higher than anticipated in both groups and the reduction of $34\%$ was less than the predicted $50\%$ reduction. The International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial (ISAT) [116] randomly assigned 2,143 patients (recruitment discontinued after interim analysis) with ruptured intracranial aneurysms to neurosurgical or endovascular treatment to detect a $5\%$ absolute reduction in the proportion of patients dependent or dead at 1 year assuming event rates of $24\%$ and $19\%$ for neurosurgical and endovascular treatment groups, respectively. The proportion of patients who were dependent or dead at 1  year was higher than anticipated in both endovascular and neurosurgical treatment groups $(23.7\%$ versus $30.6\%$ ). Despite the higher event rates, a significant reduction with endovascular treatment was detected due to higher than anticipated risk reduction $(6.9\%$ absolute reduction).  



# Comparison with Phase II Trials in Other Diseases  

The challenge of incongruence between phase II and phase III trials is not unique to aSAH trials. The incongruence between phase II and phase III trials can range between $20-40\%$ in other diseases [117]. A recent study by the Biotechnology Innovation Organization [118] analyzed clinical success rates in advancing drugs to market between 2006 and 2015 found that $15.3\%$ of all drugs entering phase II clinical testing reached the market with somewhat lower rates for neurological $(14.2\%)$ and cardiovascular $(11.2\%)$ agents. A higher failure rates was seen for trials evaluating first-in-class agents compared with those evaluating new indications for already-approved drugs [118]. An FDA review [5] identified 22 instances of drugs, vaccines and medical devices since 1999 for which promising phase II clinical trial results were not confirmed in phase III trials due to lack of efficacy in 14 cases, lack of safety in 1 case, and both lack of safety and efficacy in 7 cases.  



# Conclusions  

Strategies identified in our review (see Table  3) may assist in the design of future phase II trials evaluating new therapeutic strategies for cerebral ischemia associated with aSAH. Creating standard procedures for assessment of preclinical data and surrogate end points, identifying the appropriate time window for various therapeutic interventions, and identifying methodological and statistical elements that could improve phase II trial design is important and may require the creation of a working group of clinical and preclinical investigators similar to the STAIR working group [119] and HEmorrhagic stroke Academia inDuStry (HEADS) Roundtable [120] for ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage, respectively.
