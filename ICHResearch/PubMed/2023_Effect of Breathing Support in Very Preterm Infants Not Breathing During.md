# Effect of Breathing Support in Very Preterm Infants Not Breathing During Deferred Cord Clamping: A Randomized Controlled Trial (The ABC Study)  

Elizabeth Nevill, $\mathsf{MN}^{\top}$ , Lindsay F. J. Mildenhall, $\mathsf{FRACP}^{1,2}$ , and Michael P. Meyer, MD1,2  

Objective To determine if providing respiratory support to very preterm infants who fail to breathe regularly during deferred cord clamping (DCC) decreased red cell transfusion.  

Study design Infants less than 31 weeks of gestation undergoing DCC who were apneic or not breathing regularly at 15 seconds underwent stratified randomization. Pale, limp, and nonresponsive infants were excluded. The standard group received gentle stimulation in a neutral position for 50 seconds; the intervention group received intermittent positive pressure ventilation via face mask and T piece from 20 to 50 seconds of age with a fractional inspired oxygen of 0.3. The primary outcome was the proportion transfused, with a secondary composite outcome of death, severe intraventricular hemorrhage, or chronic lung disease.  

Results Of 311 assessed infants, 113 met the inclusion criteria and were studied; 57 received the intervention and 56 standard treatment. Patient characteristics were similar. Overall, 105 infants $(93\%)$ received the intended 50 seconds DCC (54 in the intervention group and 51 in the standard group). Rates of transfusion were similar $(28\%$ vs $30\%$ in the intervention vs control groups), as were rates of the composite outcome $46\%$ vs $38\%$ in the intervention vs the control arms; $P=.45)$ ).  

Conclusions Providing breathing support during 50 seconds of DCC in this single-center cohort seemed to be safe and feasible, but did not decrease the transfusion rates or improve outcomes. (J Pediatr 2023;253:94-100).

lthough there are differences of opinion in defining optimal timing of cord clamping, improved survival has been noted in infants less than 34 weeks postmenstrual age where the cord was clamped at 30 seconds or later compared with immediate clamping.1,2 Randomized trials and systematic reviews document a decrease in blood transfusion during the neonatal admission.3-5 Deferred cord clamping (DCC) facilitates transfer of blood from the placenta to the infant (placental transfusion) based on direct measurement of blood volume, continuous weighing after delivery, a decrease in residual blood in the placenta, and increased hemoglobin and hematocrit.2,6-8  

Studies reported various influences on the amount of placental transfusion (measured by residual placental volume), such as whether breathing was established before cord clamping.9 In a preterm lamb model, establishing respiration before clamping might be important for stability of the early transitional circulation, especially the cerebral circulation.10 In a singlecenter clinical study by Katheria et al, providing DCC in combination with assisted breathing via positive pressure ventilation or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) did not improve hematocrit or other outcomes vs a group of preterm infants undergoing DCC alone.11 In this study, however, $92\%$ of infants established spontaneous respiration while receiving respiratory support with an intact cord.11 An observational study at our center suggested that very preterm infants who were not breathing had increased transfusion requirements and worse neonatal outcomes than those who established breathing before clamping.12  

Currently, there is no consensus in the literature as to whether very preterm infants who are not breathing but do not otherwise seem to be compromised should be allowed to complete a transition period with the cord intact, receive early resuscitation during DCC, or have the cord clamped immediately.13 We planned a study to facilitate breathing by providing intermittent positive pressure ventilation (IPPV) to very preterm infants not breathing before cord clamping. We hypothesized that this strategy would assist in the establishment of spontaneous breathing and, by improving placental blood transfer, would decrease neonatal transfusions. The intervention could also stabilize the transitional circulation and improve outcomes, such as intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH).

# Methods  

The Assisted Breathing Before Cord Clamping study was a single-center randomized controlled trial with masked outcome assessment carried out in a neonatal intensive care and perinatal center at Middlemore Hospital New Zealand (ACTRN12615001026516). The study protocol has been previously published.14 Briefly, preterm infants less than 31 weeks of gestation undergoing DCC were eligible, provided they were either not breathing or making irregular nonsustained breathing efforts during DCC. Written informed consent was obtained before labor where possible; when presentation was in active labor, ethical approval for randomization followed by deferred consent was granted (Northern A Health and Disability Ethics Committee, New Zealand [15/NTA/146AM01]).  

After delivery, infants were wrapped with sterile plastic wrap and either placed at the foot of the maternal bed for vaginal births or on a sterile warm towel on the maternal thighs at caesarean section. A neonatal attendant skilled in resuscitation placed the infant supine with the head and neck in a neutral position and provided gentle tactile stimulation. Breathing effort was assessed in the first 15 seconds after birth and infants with compromised breathing (as noted elsewhere in this article) were randomized within 20 seconds to 2 groups. The rapid randomization was achieved as described previously, having been tested in simulation studies.14 The intervention group was given positive pressure ventilation using a $\mathrm{T}$ piece resuscitator (Neopuff, Fisher & Paykel) with colorimetric carbon dioxide detection device (Pedicap, Covidien). A peak inspiratory pressure of $20~\mathrm{cm}$ water and positive end-expiratory pressure of $5~\mathrm{cm}$ with $10\mathrm{~L~}$ of gas flow and blended oxygen of $30\%$ was delivered for 30 seconds and the cord clamped at 50 seconds. The comparison group continued to receive gentle stimulation and positioning as described elsewhere in this article, and the cord was also clamped at 50 seconds. Exclusion criteria included severe fetal growth restriction, twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome, maternal compromise, placental abruption, and known severe congenital malformation. Infants who established regular breathing movements or were crying by 15 seconds were ineligible. In addition, infants were excluded if, in addition to being apneic, they were flaccid and pale. A 50-second period of DCC was chosen because this period would allow a 30-second intervention. Our center previously practiced a 40-second delay.12 Stratified randomization according to gestation was carried out with computer-derived sequences and random block sizes (2- 10). Numbered opaque sealed envelopes contained a colorcoded card indicating group assignment, which could not be ascertained without opening the envelope (only carried out once breathing had been assessed). Multiples were randomized individually. After cord clamping, all infants were taken to the radiant warmer and after assessment received CPAP or IPPV with an initial inspired oxygen concentration of $30\%$ . Outcome assessments and data entry were carried out by study team members masked to the intervention received. After delivery, the attending team only noted the time of cord clamping after birth in the medical record and not whether a breathing intervention was carried out. One research assistant (who was not involved in clinical management) entered group assignment into a Research Electronic Data Capture system and this information was password protected. Transfusion decisions were made only by senior specialists who had no knowledge of group assignment and were not present at delivery when the intervention was given.  

The primary outcome was blood transfusion during the neonatal admission. This factor was assessed as the proportion of infants transfused. The number of transfusions and volume given were also recorded. The administration of blood transfusions was in keeping with the study center transfusion guidelines, and transfusion decisions were made by senior clinicians unaware of study group assignments. Our unit transfusion guidelines were unchanged during the study and have been published.14 Records were also kept of infants not transfused who met the guidelines and of any transfusions not mandated by the guideline. It was expected that a small number of transfusions would be required if infants required transfer to a surgical center. These numbers were included in the transfusion outcome. Likewise, if infants died during the neonatal admission, transfusions received before death were included.  

A secondary composite outcome of death, chronic lung disease (CLD) or severe IVH (grade 3 or 4) was determined. Other secondary outcomes included Apgar scores, delivery room resuscitation, the requirement for endotracheal intubation, and core temperature at 5 minutes of age and on admission. Apart from delivery room assessments, all other outcomes were determined without knowledge of group allocation. The duration of respiratory support during neonatal admission was recorded, as was surfactant use. Blood pressure (obtained from central arterial lines if present), acidbase balance, blood lactate, and inotrope use, all within the first 48 hours, were noted. Hemoglobin levels on days 1, 2, 3, and 7 and receipt of phototherapy were recorded. Neonatal outcomes, defined according to local Australia and New Zealand Neonatal Network coding criteria included CLD (defined as requirement for respiratory support or oxygen at 36 weeks postmenstrual age), home discharge with oxygen, IVH rates (grades III and IV and any grade reported after the day 5 and day 28 cranial ultrasound examinations), necrotizing enterocolitis defined by modified Bell stage 2 or higher, retinopathy of prematurity highest stage recorded and requiring laser therapy, early onset sepsis at less than 48 hours of age, late onset sepsis defined by positive blood culture or cerebrospinal fluid after 72 hours, death during neonatal admission, and length of hospital stay in days.15  

# Statistical Analyses  

The sample size was based on an observational study at our site, which indicated that $60\%$ of infants that did not breathe during DCC received a blood transfusion. This finding compared with a $30\%$ transfusion rate in the group that breathed during DCC. A $50\%$ relative decrease in the proportion transfused would require 100 infants whose primary outcome could be assessed. This process would provide $85\%$ power at a 2-sided significance level of .05. For a secondary composite outcome of death, CLD, or severe IVH, the sample size would allow a $36\%$ difference to be detected ( $80\%$ power, significance .05), with historical data indicating the composite outcome in $58\%$ of infants. To allow for study drop-outs, the sample size was inflated to 120 infants (60 per group).  

We used $\chi^{2}$ tests for categorical data and numeric data were analyzed with Mann-Whitney nonparametric statistics or the $t$ test if normally distributed (IBM SPSS Statistics 26, IBM Corporation 2020). Median differences between groups with $95\%$ CIs were used as appropriate. A 2-sided probability ( $\mathrm{\Delta}P$ value) of less than .05 was used throughout. Analyses were carried out on an intention-to-treat basis. Data collection was carried out by research nurses and study authors who were masked to group assignment. Standardized data collection forms were created and data entered into Research Electronic Data Capture (Research Electronic Data Capture system; Vanderbilt University Medical Centre, Institute for Clinical and Translational Research, hosted by University of Auckland) by an administrator.16 A data safety monitoring committee reviewed data in a blinded fashion every 6 months.  

# Results  

Between March 2016 and December 2020, 311 infants less than 31 weeks of gestation were assessed for eligibility (Figure). Excluded infants included 93 $(29\%)$ ) breathing spontaneously by 15 seconds of age and 16 $(5\%)$ requiring immediate resuscitation owing to compromise at birth (apneic, flaccid, and pale). Of the 16 excluded infants, the median Apgar score was 2 (range, 0-4), 10 of the 16 $(63\%)$ were intubated in the delivery room, and 5 of the 16 $(31\%)$ died (4 deaths occurred before 48 hours of age). Of the 120 infants randomized, 7 declined consent for collection of outcome data; therefore, 113 were eligible for analysis; 57 received the intervention and 56 received standard treatment. Maternal and infant characteristics were similar and are summarized in Table I.  

In all cases, the neonatal team member was able to carry out the required procedure by standing adjacent to the surgeon, wrapping and positioning the baby, and providing the required intervention. In no cases was the cord too short. Similarly, the intervention proved feasible for all eligible vaginal births. There was 1 instance of incorrect group allocation when the research assistant announced the incorrect intervention, which was analyzed by intention to treat in the original intended group.

# Discussion  

In this study, we were able to achieve the planned DCC in a high proportion of eligible infants. However, we found no difference between the groups in any of the transfusion measures or measured hemoglobin levels. These findings suggest there was no difference in placental transfusions. Similar findings have been noted in 2 other studies of preterm infants that compared DCC with and without respiratory support. In a study by Katheria et al, the majority of infants were breathing during DCC and one group received CPAP or IPPV. Similar to our reported results, there was no difference in hematocrit.11 In another study, infants less than 32 weeks received 60 seconds of DCC and 1 group was given CPAP. Similar hemoglobin levels were noted in the 2 groups, although an assessment of breathing status during DCC was not carried out.17 Our current study only included infants who were not breathing regularly during DCC. We expected improved placental transfusion in the intervention group related to respiration (and that IPPV would facilitate this). Although the use of a colorimetric $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ detector indicated gas exchange in $70\%$ of cases where it was applied, there was no difference in the time to fully establish regular respiration between the groups or in other early respiratory measures.  

Would a period longer than 50 seconds before cord clamping in our study have altered the results? Evidence from a systematic review indicated that transfusion benefits were seen after 30 seconds of DCC in preterm infants, although term infants subjected to continuous weighing before cord clamping continued to gain weight (presumably owing to ongoing transfusion) up to several minutes after birth.3,5 We did not extend beyond a total of 50 seconds in our study because we were studying a group of preterm infants who were not breathing and could be deemed at an increased risk of adverse outcomes. Nevertheless, with increased monitoring, support, and equipment it is possible to carry out physiological-based cord clamping in extremely preterm infants, only clamping the cord after respiration is established, rather than waiting for a specific length of time.18 A noninferiority feasibility study was carried out in infants less than 32 weeks with a mean clamping time of more than 5 minutes, although measures of placental transfusion were not reported.19 A study examined the effects of PBCC in infants ${>}32$ weeks in need of resuscitation. Infants were randomized to receive early cord clamping vs. lung aeration via IPPV or spontaneous respiration with the cord clamped after calorimetric detection of carbon dioxide. There were no differences in the heart rate (primary outcome) or oxygen saturations and there was no apparent short-term benefit from the PBCC intervention.20

Other factors that could affect placental transfusion include the delivery method and positioning of the infant. There is evidence of a smaller placental transfusion occurring with cesarean deliveries.6 In our study, $69\%$ of deliveries occurred after a caesarean delivery, but hemoglobin levels were comparable with vaginal deliveries (results not shown). Positioning the infant in relation to the placenta was not shown to significantly effect weight gain before cord clamping in term infants held either at the level of maternal thighs or below.21  

Although infants in the intervention group received $30\%$ oxygen before DCC and the control group infants did not, we found no important differences in the 1-minute Apgar scores or first recorded oxygen saturation. After DCC, both groups received initial oxygen concentrations of $30\%$ . Because it took another 2 minutes after DCC to obtain saturation readings for most infants, it may be that any initial differences were no longer apparent by this time. Oxygen requirements in the delivery room were also not significantly different. It proved difficult to monitor heart rate during the 50 seconds of DCC, especially during caesarean section because there was only 1 neonatal team member in sterile attire. Other studies have found that dry gel electrodes can detect a heart rate in less than 15 seconds.22 This method was not available for our study, but could be of great benefit in future studies. There were minor differences in initial lactate and base deficit levels, but these differences were thought not to be significant clinically. There were no differences in inotrope use in the first 48 hours or in the number of infants receiving treatment for patent ductus arteriosus, similar to the study by Katheria et al.11 Based on studies in preterm lambs, stabilization of the transitional circulation by DCC has been thought to be a potential mechanism for reducing IVH by maintaining left atrial filling and systemic circulation, including carotid flow.10 A more recent set of experiments in a similar model, however, did not find the same effects of DCC and ventilation on the transitional circulation and the earlier study may have described the effects after a 2-minute asphyxial insult.23 We did not find any difference in the transitional circulation, IVH of any grade, or the secondary composite outcome of mortality, CLD, and severe IVH.  

After the cord was clamped, the 2 groups had similar transitions in the delivery room. This finding suggests that the control group was not significantly disadvantaged and that the placental circulation was still intact. Although we preselected a group of preterm infants who were not breathing, only a small percentage of infants $(5\%)$ seemed to be more compromised by virtue of poor tone, activity, and color, where the cord was clamped, and resuscitation commenced. Other large randomized studies have noted that more than $20\%$ of eligible preterm infants did not receive more than 30 seconds of DCC owing to concerns over the condition of the infant.24 The 16 infants who were excluded from the study because they were limp and pale with poor tone in the initial seconds after birth had lower Apgar scores, were more likely to be intubated in the delivery room, and had higher mortality $(31\%)$ . It seemed that they constituted a sicker group that required early intervention.  

One of the strengths of the study was that we completed DCC in a high percentage of preterm infants who were not regularly breathing by 15 seconds; considered as a vulnerable group of very preterm infants that may have been excluded from other studies.24 The deferred consent process facilitated infants of mothers in preterm labor or those who required emergency cesarean deliveries for maternal reasons to be assessed for randomization. Our method did not require specialized equipment and, therefore, could be managed at the bedside by a single neonatal attendant. Caregivers were blinded to group allocation, and this factor also applied to outcome assessment. Transfusion criteria were relatively stringent and applied consistently.  

In contrast, weaknesses included a single-center design and a lower than expected transfusion rate in the control group compared with our observational study, possibly reflecting greater illness severity in the observational study patients.14 This difference affected the study power to detect any difference in transfusion rates between the 2 groups. It might be more realistic for future studies to consider a noninferiority outcome because the 2 groups had similar transfusion requirements. In addition, our secondary composite outcome was based on an historic event rate of nearly $60\%$ , but in the current study the overall rate was just more than $40\%$ , rendering the study underpowered for the composite outcome. Other weaknesses were our inability to rapidly measure heart rate, and although we provided other measures such as positioning and gentle stimulation in the control group, we did not evaluate the effect of these factors. We accept that other neonatal outcomes such as severe IVH are relevant, but would require considerably larger sample sizes. As suggested by others, the exact timing of DCC may be less important than allowing infants to establish respiration with an intact cord.18 In this study, we did not prolong this time beyond 50 seconds for practical reasons.  

In conclusion, providing brief, early PPV to very preterm infants who were not breathing during DCC did not significantly improve findings during the early transitional period in the delivery room or in relation to assessed neonatal outcomes. Having a skilled neonatal attendant alongside for the delivery in either the operating room or birthing suite may facilitate achieving the desired timing of cord clamping with more than $90\%$ of infants attaining the 50-second target in our study. n
