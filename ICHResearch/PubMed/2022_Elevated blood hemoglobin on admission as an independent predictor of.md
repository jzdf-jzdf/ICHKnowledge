# Elevated blood hemoglobin on admission as an independent predictor of unfavorable outcomes in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage

# Abstract  

Hemoglobin (HGB), a potent spasmogen, may cause irreversible damage to the brain after aneurysm rupture. However, there is no clinical evidence to reveal the relationship between blood HGB concentrations on admission and the prognosis of patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). We retrospectively reviewed all aSAH patients admitted to our institution between January 2015 and December 2020. Functional outcome was assessed at 90 days after discharge using the modified Rankin scale (mRS). Independent risk factors associated with 90-day unfavorable outcomes were derived from a forward stepwise multivariate analysis. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was conducted to identify the best cutoff value of HGB to discriminate 90-day unfavorable outcomes. Then, patients were divided into two groups according to the cutoff value of HGB, and to account for imbalances in baseline characteristics, propensity score matching (PSM) was carried out to assess the impact of HGB on in-hospital complications. A total of 800 aSAH patients without anemia on admission were retrospectively enrolled in this study. Elevated blood HGB $\mathrm{OR}=1.02\$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}1.00{-}1$ .03, $p{=}0.018\$ on admission was identified as an independent risk factor associated with 90-day unfavorable outcomes, and the cutoff value was $149.5\mathrm{g/L}$ . After PSM, patients with an $\mathrm{HGB}>149.5~\mathrm{g/L}$ had a higher incidence of in-hospital delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) $33.9\%$ vs. $22.0\%$ , $p{=}0.013,$ ) and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) ( $11.9\%$ vs. $4.0\%$ , $p{=}0.006)$ . Patients with a blood $\mathrm{HGB}>149.5~\mathrm{g/L}$ on admission might develop more DCI and DVT during hospitalization, leading to 90-day unfavorable outcomes in aSAH patients. # Introduction  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a lifethreatening neurosurgical emergency with a high mortality rate of $22\%$ to $50\%$ [23, 26]. Currently, researchers generally agree that irreversible acute pathophysiological events such as blood release, acute reactive hyperemia, and transient cerebral ischemia occurring in the brain within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ after ictus are important causes of poor prognosis in aSAH patients [13, 32, 33]. In this dangerous period, due to blood release and erythrocyte lysis, the brain is exposed to high concentrations of hemoglobin (HGB). Notably, previous studies have shown that HGB is a potent spasmogen. When the cerebrovascular reserve is impaired, HGB will induce nitric oxide (NO) deficiency, voltage-dependent potassium current suppression, and an increase in endothelin (ET)-1 production to lead to early arterial constriction and delayed vasospasm after aSAH [15, 16, 25, 31, 35]. Therefore, based on the vasoconstrictor effect of HGB, we hypothesized that elevated blood HGB concentration increases the initial concentration of cerebral HGB after aneurysm rupture and aggravates early brain injury, which leads to certain severe complications and unfavorable outcomes in aSAH patients.  

Most of the current studies on blood HGB in aSAH patients have focused on controlling postoperative anemia rather than investigating the impact of preoperative HGB concentrations on patient prognosis [19, 27–30, 37, 38]. Therefore, the relationship between HGB concentration on admission and the prognosis of patients with aSAH remains elusive. Only one study in 2019 revealed that elevated blood HGB on admission was associated with an increased risk of postoperative cerebral ischemia, pulmonary embolism, lower-limb deep vein thrombosis, and poor prognosis in poor-grade high-altitude aSAH patients after clipping [44]. However, whether this conclusion is generally applicable to all patients with aSAH in plain areas and the best blood HGB cutoff value to discriminate unfavorable outcomes are unclear.  

In the present study, we aimed to (1) identify the relationship between blood HGB on admission and the prognosis of aSAH patients; (2) determine the blood HGB threshold value that leads to unfavorable outcomes; and (3) search for clinical evidence of unfavorable outcomes caused by elevated blood HGB, providing new intervention targets for future preventive treatments.  

# Methods  

# Study design  

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of aSAH patients admitted from 2015 to 2020 in the Beijing Tiantan Hospital. All patients’ data were derived from the Longterm Prognosis of Emergency Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (LongTEAM) Registry study (Registration No. NCT 04,785,976). In this study, all patients had angiographically documented aneurysms with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), which were confirmed by either computed tomography (CT) or lumbar puncture. We set the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) $\mathrm{age}\geq18$ years; (2) emergency admission; (3) less than $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ from the rupture to the admission and less than $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ from the admission to treatment; (4) single aneurysm; and (5) treated with surgical clipping or endovascular coiling. The main exclusion criteria were (1) anemia on admission $(\mathrm{HGB}<120~\mathrm{g/L}$ for men and $<110~\mathrm{g/L}$ for women); (2) previous SAH; (3) history of neurosurgery due to any reasons; (4) physical disability due to any previous disease; and (5) treatment including external ventricular drainage, lumbar puncture, angiography, intubation, and/or mechanical ventilation at other hospitals before presentation to our hospital.  

We have obtained approval from the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY 2021–008- 01). Informed consent for clinical analysis was obtained from all individual participants or their authorized representatives. All the analyses were performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki and the local ethics policies. All patients were managed according to the guidelines [4].  

# Patients’ data  

All patients’ data were from their medical records. We collected (1) demographic information, including age, sex, and medical history; (2) location of aneurysm; (3) World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grade on admission; (4) first CT-related information on admission, including modified Fisher (mFS) grade, Graeb score, Subarachnoid Hemorrhage Early Brain Edema (SEBES) score, and acute hydrocephalus; (5) first laboratory examination on admission, including glucose (GLU, fasting plasma glucose normal range: [3.9–6.1] mmol/L) level, white blood cell (WBC, normal range: $[3.5{-}9.5]\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ count, monocyte (MONO, normal range: $[0.10{-}0.60]\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L})$ count, lymphocyte (LY, normal range: $[1.10{-}3.2]\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ count, neutrophil (NEUT, normal range: $[1.80{-}6.30]\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L})$ count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and hemoglobin (HGB, normal range: [120–160 for male; 110–150 for female] $\mathrm{g/L}$ ) concentration; (6) treatment modality, including surgical clipping and endovascular coiling; (7) in-hospital complications such as delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), seizures, intracranial infection, stress ulcer bleeding, abnormal hepatic function, urinary tract infection, hypoproteinemia, pneumonia, and deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Supplemental Table  1 shows the detailed diagnostic criteria.  

# Outcome measurement  

The primary outcome was the modified Rankin scale (mRS) score at 90 days after discharge (the neurosurgeon followed up with patients via telephone or an outpatient appointment). We defined unfavorable outcomes as an mRS score $\geq3$ . The secondary outcome was the occurrence of in-hospital complications.  

# Statistical analysis  

All statistical analysis were performed using SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) and GraphPad PRISM 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA). $p<0.05$ was considered significantly different.  

Descriptive variables were summarized as the mean $\pm$ standard deviation (SD) or median values with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentage) for categorical variables. Variables were tested by using the chi-square test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney test, or ANOVA test for continuous variables, as appropriate. All risk factors that showed a statistical trend $(p<0.05)$ in univariate analyses were included in a multivariate logistic regression analyses (forward stepwise) to identify risk factors being independently associated with the 90-day unfavorable outcomes. Associations were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the best cutoff value of blood HGB on admission to discriminate 90-day unfavorable outcomes. Further, the area under the curve (AUC) in ROC analysis was used to compare the predictive ability between HGB and other independent risk factors. To investigate the effect of blood HGB on admission on in-hospital complications and prognosis, we divided the patients into two groups according to the best cutoff value of blood HGB on admission, and to minimize bias by confounding, we performed propensity score matching (PSM) with a match tolerance of 0.02 and a ratio of 1:1 to adjust for imbalances of baseline characteristics between the two groups. Finally, in the match pairs, all in-hospital complications and 90-day outcomes were regarded as binary variables and were assessed by the chi-square test.  

# Results  

# Patients’ characteristics  

A total of 800 patients were included in the present study. A total of 658 $(82.2\%)$ patients had 90-day favorable outcomes, and 142 $(17.8\%)$ patients had 90-day unfavorable outcomes. Patients with an unfavorable outcome were older $59.5\pm10.6$ vs. $53.6\pm10.9$ , $p<0.001\$ ), had a higher incidence of hypertension $(100/142~[70.4\%]$ vs. 384/658 $[58.4\%]$ , $p{=}0.008,$ ), were more likely to choose surgical clipping treatment (84/142 $[59.2\%]$ vs. 308/658 $[46.8\%]$ , $p{=}0.008,$ ), and had worse clinical status on admission, including a higher proportion of WFNS grades 4–5 (77/142 $[54.2\%]$ vs. 90/658 $[13.7\%]$ , $p<0.001\$ ), mFS grades 3–4 (135/142 $[95.1\%]$ vs. 493/658 $[74.9\%]$ , $p<0.001,$ , Graeb scores 5–12 (34/142 $[23.9\%]$ vs. 37/658 $[5.6\%]$ , $p<0.001\$ ), and acute hydrocephalus (70/142 $[49.3\%]$ vs. 250/658 $[38.0\%]$ , $p{=}0.013\$ . Further, patients with an unfavorable outcome had a significantly higher GLU level (median [IQR] 8.71 [7.12–10.44] vs. 7.47 [6.60–8.69], $p<0.001,$ ), WBC count (median [IQR] 14.99 [12.03–17.69] vs. 12.00 [8.51–14.87], $p<0.001,$ ), MONO count (median [IQR] 0.51 [0.33–0.73] vs. 0.39 [0.26–0.54], $p<0.001\$ ), NEUT count (median [IQR] 13.29 [10.48–15.78] vs. 10.52 [8.09–13.32], $p<0.001,$ , NLR (median [IQR] 14.41 [9.27–20.27] vs. 10.90 [6.97–15.62], $p<0.001,$ , and HGB concentration (median [IQR] 145 [133–156] vs. 140 [131–150], $p{=}0.015$ on admission (Table 1).

In addition, age, WFNS grades 4–5, mFS grades 3–4, Graeb scores 5–12, WBC count, and surgical clipping treatment were also independently associated with 90-day unfavorable outcomes in aSAH patients (Table 2).

# Ninety‑day unfavorable outcomes and in‑hospital complications after propensity score matching  

Patients with a blood HGB on admission $>149.5~\mathrm{g/L}$ were younger $(51.0\pm10.3\$ vs. $56.1\pm11.0$ , $p<0.001.$ ); had a higher prevalence of current smoking (120/226 $[53.1\%]$ vs. 121/574 $[21.1\%]$ , $p<0.001,$ ) and history of hypertension $(150/226[66.4\%]$ vs. 334/574 $[58.2\%]$ , $p{=}0.033,$ ; had a higher incidence of Graeb scores 5–12 (29/226 $[12.8\%]$ vs. 42/574 $[7.3\%]$ , $p{=}0.014)$ ; had a lower proportion of female (44/226 $[19.5\%]$ vs. 413/574 $[72.0\%]$ , $p<0.001,$ ); had a lower incidence of WFNS grades 4–5 (26/226 $[11.5\%]$ vs. 114/574 $[19.9\%]$ , $p=0.005,$ ); had a higher WBC count (median [IQR] 13.52 [10.79–16.33] vs. 12.03 [9.50–15.09], $p<0.001\$ ), MONO count (median [IQR] 0.45 [0.33–0.66] vs. 0.38 [0.25–0.54], $p<0.001\$ ), LY count (median [IQR] 1.12 [0.76–1.55] vs. 0.94 [0.68–1.32], $p<0.001.$ ), and NEUT count (median [IQR] 11.91 [9.20–14.47] vs. 10.56 [8.16–13.47], $p<0.001,$ ). We performed PSM to minimize the confounding bias due to baseline characteristics between patients with $\mathrm{HGB}>149.5~\mathrm{g/L}$ versus $\leq149.5~\mathrm{g/L}$ , after which two evenly balanced cohorts $(n=177$ in each cohorts) were analyzed for 90-day unfavorable outcomes and in-hospital complications (Table 3).

# Discussion  

Since the concept of “early brain injury (EBI)” was first introduced in 2004, increasing attention is being focused on pathophysiological events occurring in the brain during the early period after ictus [13, 20]. The current study suggested that EBI is a major factor in the poor prognosis of patients with aSAH because some injury-related index changes may lead to serious complications such as vasospasm or DCI, which affects the prognosis of patients [1, 34]. Among these EBI indicators, hemoglobin, as a potent vasospasmogen released following red blood cell lysis, may lead to brain damage [16, 25]. However, there is no clear clinical evidence to confirm this view. Moreover, whether blood HGB concentrations can predict patients prognosis is unclear.  

![](images/fc8d1b29fb086cc3e35530214aa2a4b68d834c1b713e69341a27769ac909110d.jpg)  
Fig. 3   Relationship between blood HGB concentration on admission and incidence of in-hospital DCI and DVT before and after PSM  

Previous studies demonstrated that anemia was associated with worse outcomes in aSAH patients [18, 43]. Therefore, we excluded patients who were anemic on admission to investigate whether blood HGB concentrations in nonanemic patients were associated with outcomes. Our study identified blood HGB concentrations on admission to be independently associated with 90-day unfavorable outcomes in aSAH patients, and an $\mathrm{HGB}>149.5\mathrm{g/L}$ was identified as the best cutoff value for the prediction of an unfavorable 90-day outcome. After PSM, patients with $\mathrm{HGB}>149.5~\mathrm{g/L}$ had an unfavorable 90-day outcome, with a higher incidence of inhospital DCI and DVT, and the incidence increased as HGB concentrations increased, which may be the main reason for poor patient outcomes.  

The development of DCI is a complex process, and once DCI occurs, it will lead to great damage to the patient’s body by leading to physical disability and cognitive impairments [29]. However, current treatments for DCI are very limited, and adequate prevention remains elusive [6, 40, 42]. There are three main mechanisms by which HGB induces the development of DCI. (1) HGB reduces the availability of cerebral NO. Patients with low NO concentrations in the CSF after aSAH are more prone to develop delayed vasospasm [10, 17]. NO is a powerful vasodilator produced from L-arginine via NO synthase (NOS) [11, 14]. After NO is synthesized, it diffuses across endothelial cell membranes, enters vascular smooth muscle cells, and activates soluble guanylate cyclase to mediate vasodilation [41]. Current studies have demonstrated that NO depletion after aSAH may be central to the resulting vascular dysfunction. In this process, as an NO scavenger, HGB released following erythrocyte lysis can reduce NO availability in the surrounding cerebral vasculature, which may mediate vasospasm development [3]. (2) HGB induces the development of spreading depolarizations (SDs). After aSAH, brain energy stores are rapidly depleted, and the decreased activity of $\mathrm{Na}^{+}\mathrm{K}^{+}$ -ATPases leads to rapid alterations in ionic distribution across the cell membrane, resulting in increased intracellular $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ and extracellular ${\mathbf{K}}^{+}$ and promoting the development of SDs, which leads to hypoperfusion in injured brain tissues [7]. Two previous studies demonstrated that the application of ${\mathbf{K}}^{+}$ and HGB either topically or in CSF could induce SDs and cortical spreading of ischemia in rats, providing a novel mechanism for the development of DCI [8, 9]. (3) HGB increases the level of cerebral endothelin-1 (ET-1). Unlike the function of NO in vasodilation, ET-1 is a vasoconstrictor released by HGB stimulation from cerebral endothelial cells by mechanisms that involve transcription and translation. Moreover, HGB abrogates the effect of NO, leading to a secondary increase in ET-1 due to the loss of tonic inhibition of ET-1 production by NO [22]. The above fact is based on the concentration of cerebral HGB released from the blood after aneurysm rupture. However, there is no mechanistic study of blood HGB concentration for DCI occurrence; thus, the relationship between blood HGB concentration and CSF-HGB concentration is also unclear. We hypothesized that patients with high HGB concentrations in the blood also have high initial cerebral HGB when the aneurysm ruptures, activating a relevant mechanism for DCI development.  

DVT is a common in-hospital complication for aSAH patients that is associated with increased death and disability [12, 21]. The mechanism of DVT in patients with aSAH is not clear, and the possible causes are immobility and a prothrombotic state caused by bleeding. High HGB is widely accepted as a risk factor for high blood viscosity, which means that in aSAH patients with high HGB concentrations, a hypercoagulable state and thrombosis may more easily arise in the deep venous systems, especially during prolonged decubitus with low circulatory flow [24].  

Current research on blood HGB concentrations in aSAH patients has mostly focused on preventing and treating postoperative anemia since anemia after aSAH is associated with poor outcomes and death [2, 19, 27]. Previous studies showed that maintaining the blood HGB concentration at $11{-}12~\mathrm{g/dL}$ may reduce vasospasm incidence and improve outcomes [37, 38]. However, no article has revealed an upper limit of safety value for blood HGB concentration in patients with aSAH. It is of great significance to reveal this phenomenon, which could help clinicians intervene in aSAH patients in advance at a very early stage or during hospitalization, reduce related complications, and thus improve the prognosis for aSAH patients.  

Triple-H therapy (3H [hypervolemia, hypertension, and hemodilution]) is a treatment for vasospasm or DCI that became popular after 1970, intending to augment cerebral blood flow (CBF) through and around vasospastic arteries through collaterals [36]. However, with the popularization of clinical applications, the safety and efficacy of 3H in the treatment of vasospasm or DCI are controversial. A metaanalysis demonstrated no sound evidence from controlled studies for a positive effect of 3H or its separate components on CBF in SAH patients [5]. For hemodilution, one previous study indicated that hypervolemic hemodilution is associated with an increased incidence of complications and may reduce HGB excessively to cause anemia-related complications [40]. Another study demonstrated that increased CBF was not associated with hypervolemic hemodilution, whereas treatment with hypertension alone or in combination with hypervolemia increased CBF [39]. Therefore, it seems ambiguous to treat vasospasm or DCI by increasing CBF through hemodilution alone. Furthermore, is there a way to minimize vasospasm or DCI with early interventions? The results of our study provide a new idea to prevent the occurrence of vasospasm or DCI; that is, early hemodilution therapy targeting blood HGB after aneurysm rupture reduces the blood HGB concentration to a safe value in aSAH patients with high blood HGB concentrations (we believe that $149.5~\mathrm{g/L}$ is a relatively safe and easily achievable dilution value) and thereby reduces the incidence of vasospasm and DCI during hospitalization. However, both hypervolemic and isovolemic dilutions may cause potential brain injury. Based on the absence of relevant study conclusions, the safety and efficacy of this intervention need to be confirmed by more relevant prospective clinical trials in the future.  

# Limitations  

There were several limitations in this study that need to be discussed. First, the statistical data were retrospectively collected. Second, the patients were all from the North China Plain in China, and the effect of racial and regional factors on blood HGB concentrations is unknown. Third, the relationship between blood HGB concentration and cerebral HGB concentration after aneurysm rupture is not clear. Fourth, different treatment modalities may have a potential impact on the cutoff value of HGB. Fifth, there are still many puzzles about the mechanism of early brain injury development after aSAH, and the development of vasospasm or DCI is a complex process. It may be one-sided to try to prevent its occurrence by reducing HGB concentration alone.  

# Conclusions  

This is the first study that found that elevated blood HGB concentration at emergency admission was an independent risk factor of poor 90-day prognosis in patients with aSAH, and patients with blood HGB concentration higher than $149.5~\mathrm{g/L}$ had a higher incidence of in-hospital DCI and DVT, and a higher probability of poor 90-day prognosis. We provided a new idea to regard blood HGB concentration as an early intervention target to prevent the occurrence of vasospasm or DCI during hospitalization. However, the safety and efficacy of early hemodilution therapy need to be confirmed by further clinical trials.
