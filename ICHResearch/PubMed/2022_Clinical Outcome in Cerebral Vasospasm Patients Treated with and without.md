# Abstract  

Background Cerebral vasospasm (CV) after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is still a problem. Hypertension, hypervolemia, and hemodilution (triple-H) therapy and oral nimodipine only a modest effect on patients. Intra-arterial treatment, including nimodipine, has been studied, but only as retrospective and single-arm prospective studies. We compared the outcomes between CV patients who received an adjunct intra-arterial nimodipine infusion (IANI) and those who received the standard medical treatment alone in a prospective randomized controlled trial.  

Methods In this study, patients between the age of 18 and 80 years, who underwent angiography within 14 days after aneurysm obliteration, were recruited and randomized to receive adjunct IANI or not, if they were identified with angiographic vasospasm. All the angiographic and neurologic data were recorded and analyzed during their admission, at the discharge date, and during the 6-month follow-up period.  

# Keywords  

Results From June 2016 to December 2018, we enrolled 68 patients who were randomized into two groups, 36 in the intervention group and 32 in the control group. The patients’ characteristics, aneurysm data, and modalities of treatment were similar between the two groups. # Introduction  

A ruptured cerebral aneurysm is one of the severe emergency conditions in neurosurgery, causing both morbidity and mortality. A high rate of rebleeding in the acute phase obliges neurosurgeons to try to secure the aneurysm, by either clipping or coiling embolization. However, even in complete

obliteration of an aneurysm, many patients develop cerebral vasospasm (CV) with impending neurological deficits.  

Hemodynamic augmentation therapy, including induction of hypertension and maintenance of normovolemia, has been used for many years without satisfying results.1 The guidelines for the management of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) by the American Heart Association and American Stroke Association published in 2012 recommend oral nimodipine for the treatment of CV, which has been proven to improve clinical outcomes.2  

Many vascular neurosurgeons have tried to use additional endovascular treatment, such as balloon angioplasty or various intra-arterial vasodilators, but only a few retrospective studies and noncomparative prospective studies have been published so far.3–5  

Biondi et al are believed to be the first to publish a retrospective study on 25 aSAH patients who received adjunct intra-arterial nimodipine, which resulted in an neurologic improvement in 19 of 25 patients $(76\%)$ .6 Since then, intra-arterial nimodipine injection has been used increasingly and many series were published. $7^{-18}$ However, there was no randomized controlled trial (RCT) in the literature.  

Our study aims to compare the clinical outcome between aSAH patients who received an adjunct intra-arterial nimodipine infusion (IANI) in addition to standard medical treatment and those who received standard medical treatment alone in the prospective, single-center RCT.  

# Material and Methods  

This single-center RCT was approved by the human research ethics committee of the university board (MTU-EC-SU-1– 099/59). All patients or their relatives were informed and informed consent was obtained.  

We recruited aSAH patients aged 18 to 80 years, in whom aneurysm obliteration was performed, by either surgical clipping or endovascular therapy in the Division of Neurosurgery from June 2016 to December 2018. The patients’ neurologic functions, including Hunt and Hess scale (H&H), World Federation of Neurological Societies (WFNS) grade, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, and motor strength (grade 0 to 5) of all extremity muscles during the hospital stay were recorded.  

All patients received the hemodynamic augmentation therapy, which included maintaining normovolemia and raising systolic blood pressure ${\sim}20\%$ from baseline by vasopressor or inotropic medication if indicated. The patients were administered with oral nimodipine, $60\mathrm{mg}$ every 4 hours for 21 days.  

In all patients, after aneurysm obliteration, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was routinely performed within 14 days; the exact timing was based on the severity of the bleeding and patient’s general condition. Angiographic vasospasm was defined as any acute tapering or focal narrowing of any vessels compared with prior diagnostic imaging. Patients were enrolled, when DSA revealed angiographic vasospasm. The patients were categorized into the good neurologic baseline group ([WFNS] score grades I to III), and the poor neurologic baseline group (WFNS grades IV to V). We performed block randomization with four patients in each block and assigned randomly into two intervention subjects and two control subjects. The intervention group received IANI in the same diagnostic angiography session. Nimodipine dosage ranged from 1 to $5\mathrm{mg}$ in each vessel, at the rate of 1 mg per  

5 minutes (maximum dosage $\leq10\mathrm{mg}$ directly into the artery. The total dosage of nimodipine depended on significant angiographic improvement or any observed nimodipine-related complications, such as hypotension and bradycardia. The measurement of the vessels was performed before and after infusion. To better compare the efficacy of this treatment, the intervention group received only one session of IANI as a rescue treatment. Every angiography was performed by experienced neurointerventionalists.  

Thereafter, both groups continue with induced hypertension, normovolemia, and hemodilution. Among the enrolled patients, we monitored whether they developed any signs of clinical vasospasm such as alteration of consciousness, new motor deficits, unexplained fever or headache. To exclude other causes, all patients underwent laboratory tests and computed tomography (CT) scans. In this study, we did not routinely perform transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD) to monitor the patients due to the well described investigator dependency of TCD.  

Motor strength improvement was defined as an increase of the grade at least by one. Functional status, including the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS), modified Rankin scale (mRS), and Karnofsky’s performance scale (KPS), was recorded on day of discharge from the hospital and during the follow-up period.  

All neurologic data were examined by four blinded physicians, and only one blinded investigator analyzed all the data using the patient ID. Any inconsistent data were rechecked and concluded by more than one examiner. The CT data (modified Fisher scale, aneurysm number, size, and location), and the angiographic data (complete aneurysm obliteration and caliber of vessels suspected of vasospasm) were recorded.  

# Statistical Analysis  

For categorical data and continuous data, we used the chisquared test, and we used an independent sample-t test and presented their mean and standard deviations (SD). A pvalue of $_{<0.05}$ was considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.  

# Results  

From June 2016 to December 2018, 68 patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in this study. These patients were randomized into two groups, 36 in the intervention group and 32 in the control group (►Fig. 1).  

There were no significant differences in demographics, clinical conditions at admission, and incidence of hydrocephalus requiring external ventricular drainage. The majority of patients had a modified Fisher grades III and IV aSAH. All patients had clinical vasospasm (►Table 1).  

Aneurysm data and modalities of treatment are shown in ►Table 2. Aneurysm location and configuration as well as type of treatment were not different between the groups.

The mean time interval between aneurysm rupture and control DSA was 9.72 $\mathrm{^{\prime}SD}=3.17\$ ) days in the intervention group and 8.69 $\mathrm{\mathop{SD}}=2.87\mathrm{\mathop{.}}$ ) days in the control group. Immediately before DSA, nearly half of the patients in each group had a GCS score of 15. The angiography showed an incomplete obliteration of the aneurysm in two cases from the intervention group. They underwent re-operation with clip adjustment and underwent re-DSA to confirm complete aneurysm obliteration. Angiography complications were not different between the two groups. Three thromboembolic complications occurred (two in the intervention group and one in the control group), which were treated by thrombectomy, resulting in thrombolysis in cerebral infarction grade 3 (TICI 3). One groin hematoma in the control group was managed conservatively. In the treatment group, the mean dosage of nimodipine per patient was $3.30\mathrm{mg}$ $\mathrm{'SD}=1.33\$ ). All patients in the intervention group showed increase of the vessel’s caliber (range: $0.21{-}0.63\mathrm{mm}$ ). The anterior cerebral artery (ACA) was the most responsive vessel (response in 38 vessels), followed by the middle cerebral artery (MCA) (response in25 vessels).  

The degree of caliber change was the highest in the basilar artery, which showed an average of $0.63\mathrm{-mm}$ improvement, followed by the internal carotid artery (ICA) $\left(0.54\:\mathrm{mm}\right)$ and vertebral artery (VA) $(0.47\mathrm{mm})$ . After nimodipine infusion, only three patients had transient hypotension without sequelae.  

Within 24 hours after IANI, the GCS score improved in $33.33\%$ and the motor strength in $38.89\%$ of the patients. In the control group, The GCS score improved in 12.5 and the motor strength in $9.38\%$ of the patients $\mathbf{\zeta}p=0.04$ and 0.005). The mean length of hospital stay was not different. In the intervention group, there was one death due to severe urinary tract infection and septic shock. More than half of the patients of both groups had a GCS score of 15 at discharge. The percentage was slightly higher in the intervention group, but without significance. The improvement of the GCS score during hospital stay was not significantly different between the two groups (47.22 vs. $43.75\%$ ; $p=0.69$ ; ►Table 3).  

However, the intervention group experienced significantly more often motor improvement from their baseline than the control group (58.33 vs. $21.88\%$ ; $p=0.002\$ . This was also significantly different in the subgroup of patients who only had preangiographic motor deficits. In both groups, the mRS at discharge was not different. ►Table 4 shows the follow-up data (follow-up period ranged from 6 to 17 months; mean of 8.6 months). In 23 surviving patients of each group, follow-up data were available. Our goal of follow-up for more than 6 months was achieved in $65.71\%$ of the intervention group and in $71.88\%$ of the control group. About half of the patients were followed up for more than 1 year. During the follow-up period, GCS improvement was not different between the two groups. The significant difference of motor improvement seen between the intervention and control group at discharge was not found during follow-up $(p\mathrm{-value}=0.08)$ ).  

Seven patients from the intervention group and two from the control group died during follow-up; all patients had been in poor clinical condition due to the severity of the aSAH

# Case Illustrations

After placing an external ventricular drainage and craniotomy with aneurysm clipping, she underwent hemodynamic augmentation therapy. On day 8, DSA was performed, but before DSA, she developed left hemiparesis. Angiographically, severe vasospasm at the right M2 segment was found. # Discussion  

CV after aSAH still represents a challenge for neurosurgeons. Hemodynamic augmentation therapy, nimodipine administration (especially orally), and endovascular treatment are considered standard.  

In our single-center randomized study of IANI versus standard medical treatment, there was a significant immediate improvement of motor strength and GCS score after infusion. The positive effect on motor strength was also found at discharge. However, the functional outcome was not different in the long-term follow-up.  

Intra-arterial nimodipine is one of those endovascular CV treatments which gained increasing interest in the last two decades. After publication of the study by Biondi et al, many other studies were published.6–18 Before our study began, there was only one single-arm prospective study (Hänggi et al) that performed intra-arterial nimodipine in 26 patients with medical refractory CV. Hänggi et al. concluded that intra-arterial nimodipine might have benefits in angiographic and clinical outcomes, but only temporarily. However, there was no control group in this study. After the start of enrollment of our study, several studies on intraarterial nimodipine were published. To this day, the study with the highest number of patients is the one by Park et al.16 They analyzed $73\mathsf{CV}$ patients retrospectively and found a positive correlation between the change in GCS score and the improvement of vessel diameter, but the effect on outcome remained unclear. Therefore, they concluded that this procedure is reasonable but does not always prevent delayed cerebral infarction.  

Angiography is an invasive study, which might cause some complications, for example, thromboembolic events and groin hematomas. However, in our study, these complication rates were similar to those in other studies.19,20 Furthermore, in experienced hands, these complications could be managed simultaneously within the angiography session. In this study, these complications had no effect on neurologic outcomes. Nevertheless, the risks and benefits of routine postoperative angiography should be carefully considered.

This study showed that significantly more patients in the intervention group had a motor improvement at discharge. This effect was not found any longer during follow-up, possibly due to the dropout rate $(p=0.08)$ . Furthermore, our results show that IANI improves patients’ consciousness faster than spontaneous recovery.  

The reason why IANI still has an effect, despite the systemic oral administration of nimodipine, is unknown. However, we agree with Seker et al who concluded that intra-arterial administration of nimodipine could reach a maximum drug level at targeted vessels while decreasing the total dosage and minimizing systemic side effects, such as hypotension.21 In our study, we found only three clinically nonsignificant instances of transient hypotension, which did not require treatment. Consequently, intra-arterial nimodipine could be considered a safe procedure.  

ASAH might have general effects on cerebral vessels. Usually, DSA could only detect large to medium vessel caliber narrowing. We believe that it is also possible for vasospasm to occur in small vessels, which is difficult to be detected by DSA. This is the reason why our protocol requires an infusion of nimodipine in the main vessels (ICA, VA). We aim to infuse intra-arterial nimodipine into the whole vessel territory.

However, there are some limitations of our study including the following: the positive effect of a single session of IANI might be reduced by recurrent CV, which should be better treated with multiple infusions. Also, there are patients who were lost to follow-up, which might have influenced the long-term results. Nevertheless, optimistically, we believe that patients will not get worse once the neurologic disease is stable. We did not examine the cognitive function and activities of daily living, which could help demonstrate other potentially beneficial effects of IANI. Another limitation is that we did not perform cerebral perfusion measurements $/$ imaging due to unavailability in our hospital. That should be considered in future studies.  

# Conclusion  

IANI allows effective treatment of CV. To our knowledge, this is the first RCT demonstrating a statistically significant difference of motor strength between the treatment group and the control group within 24 hours after IANI and on the day of discharge. This procedure also has a positive effect on the GCS score within 24 hours in the intervention group compared with the control group. However, there was no significant difference in outcome parameters (GCS, GOS, mRS, KPS) at discharge and during the 6-month follow-up period.
