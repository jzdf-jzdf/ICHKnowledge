# Therapeutic review: The role of tranexamic acid in management of traumatic brain injury, nontraumatic intracranial hemorrhage, and aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage  

Jana Sigmon, PharmD, BCCCP, Harris Health System, Houston, TX, USA  

Kelli L. Crowley, PharmD, BCPS, BCPPS, Department of Pharmacy, UPMC Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA, USA  

Christine M. Groth, PharmD, BCCCP, FCCM, University of Rochester Medical Center, Rochester, NY, USA  

Purpose: To summarize current literature evaluating tranexamic acid in the management of intracranial bleeding associated with traumatic and nontraumatic brain injuries and implications for clinical practice.  

Summary: Intracranial hemorrhage, regardless of etiology, is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Tranexamic acid is an antifibrinolytic with anti-inflammatory properties shown to reduce mortality in trauma patients with extracranial injuries. In traumatic brain injury, a large randomized trial found no difference in outcomes when tranexamic acid was compared to placebo; however, subgroup analyses suggested that it may reduce head injury–related mortality in the context of mild-to-moderate injury if treatment occurs within 1 hour of symptom onset. More recent out-of-hospital data have disputed these findings and even suggested harm in severely injured patients. In spontaneous, nontraumatic intracranial hemorrhage, treatment with tranexamic acid did not result in a difference in functional status; however, rates of hematoma expansion, even though modest, were significantly reduced. In aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, tranexamic acid may prevent rebleeding, but has not led to improved outcomes or reduced mortality, and there is concern for increased incidence of delayed cerebral ischemia. Overall, tranexamic acid has not been shown to result in increased risk of thromboembolic complications across these classes of brain injury.  

Conclusion: Despite its favorable safety profile overall, tranexamic acid does not seem to improve functional outcomes and cannot be routinely recommended. More data are needed to determine which head injury subpopulations are most likely to benefit from tranexamic acid and which patients are at increased risk for harm.  

Keywords: intracranial hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, tranexamic acid, traumatic brain injuries  

Am J Health-Syst Pharm. 2023;80:1213-1222 ntracranial hemorrhage (ICH) occurs by spontaneous rupture or traumatic injury of cerebral blood vessels, leading to blood accumulation within the skull and damage to the surrounding brain tissue.1 For those who survive, permanent brain damage and long-term health complications often adversely affect quality of life.1 Events are classified as either traumatic brain injury (TBI) caused by mechanical damage, such as traumatic subdural hematoma or traumatic subarachnoid hematoma, or by spontaneous intracranial bleeding, such as severe ICH or aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH).1 For both spontaneous and traumatic events, localized bleeding within the brain increases intracranial pressure and constricts perfusion to surrounding tissue. Within hours, edema can develop, triggering an inflammatory response and hematoma expansion (HE), which may lead to herniation and death.1,2 In traumatic injury, both endogenous and exogenous factors can alter coagulation, leading to either hyperfibrinolysis and bleeding or fibrinolytic shutdown, causing fibrin deposits in organ tissues.3-5 Therefore, to optimize brain preservation and neurological function, it is crucial to decelerate bleeding and maintain adequate cerebral blood perfusion while balancing these processes.6 In cases where surgical or interventional procedures to stop bleeding are delayed or not appropriate, medical management with a drug that promotes procoagulant activity at the site of the hematoma, but does not greatly increase the risk of thrombus formation, may be desirable.1  

Parenteral tranexamic acid is an antifibrinolytic agent approved in 1986 by the Food and Drug Administration for short-term use in hemophilia patients for reduction or prevention of hemorrhage with tooth extraction. More recently, it has been used offlabel for a variety of blood loss management indications.7 It is a synthetic lysine derivative that impedes fibrinolysis through 2 complementary mechanisms. The first is inhibition of enzymatic activation of plasminogen to plasmin, thereby reducing fibrin cleavage; the second is occupation of lysine receptor sites on plasminogen, consequently blocking binding to fibrin monomers. These disruptions in the fibrinolytic system result in conservation of the hemostatic fibrin matrix and inhibition of clot dissolution.7,8 Tranexamic acid’s propensity for binding plasminogen gives it a potent antihemorrhagic effect, and, at high doses in animal models, it has been associated with thrombus formation.5,8 Tranexamic acid also exerts an anti-inflammatory effect, possibly by reducing production of complement C5a, as observed by decreased quantities of interleukin-6, creatine kinase, and plasminogen activator inhibitor.5,8  

There is considerable interest in tranexamic acid as a therapeutic option for ICH. It is an attractive option when bleeding sites need to be contained while also maintaining perfusion

# TBI  

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that 223,000 hospitalizations and 60,000 deaths each year are due to TBI.10 The pathophysiology of TBI starts with a focal injury leading to axonal membrane damage and release of excitatory neurotransmitters, resulting in cellular hypoxia and inflammation.11 Approximately $75\%$ of patients with TBI will present with ICH and $50\%$ will continue to have HE during admission.12 Within minutes of traumatic injury, hemorrhagic bleeding and tissue damage can induce an endogenous coagulopathy in approximately $25\%$ of patients that continues for hours.3,5 Hypoperfusion, alterations in coagulation and platelet function, and sympathoadrenal activation lead to hyperfibrinolysis, increasing the risk for ICH.3 This is further exacerbated by exogenous coagulopathic factors from blood transfusions, hypothermia, and acidosis. The resulting effect can be either hyperactivity of fibrinolysis or fibrinolytic shutdown, identifiable by clot lysis at 30 minutes after maximum clot strength (LY30) on thromboelastography (TEG).3 Both states are associated with increased mortality, although in the former this is caused by hemorrhage, while mortality in the latter results from endorgan damage.3 Much interest exists in using tranexamic acid to attenuate acquired hyperfibrinolysis seen in TBI. However, concerns about cerebral ischemia in nontrauma patients and risk for increasing rates of fibrinolytic shutdown warrant caution.3,9,13  

The CRASH-2 trial was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial comparing tranexamic acid $^\textrm{\scriptsize1g}$ intravenous (IV) administered over 10 minutes followed by infusion with $^\textrm{\scriptsize1g}$ over 8 hours vs placebo.14 It included 20,211 trauma patients presenting within 8 hours of injury. The study was not limited to the TBI population, but $53\%$ of participants had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of less than 12, two-thirds had blunt trauma, and the majority presented within 3 hours of injury. In this study, tranexamic acid significantly reduced mortality, if given within 3 hours, without increasing the frequency of occlusive events. It did not reduce transfusion requirements, however, suggesting that benefits from tranexamic acid beyond attenuating HE may exist.14 When a subgroup of 270 patients with isolated TBI were analyzed, there were no differences in outcome between patients receiving tranexamic acid and placebo.12 However, small randomized controlled trials in the TBI population have demonstrated that tranexamic acid reduces hematoma growth and may improve clinical outcomes (Table 1).15,18  

The CRASH-3 trial was a randomized, placebo-controlled trial comparing tranexamic acid $\mathrm{~1~g~IV~}$ administered over 10 minutes followed by a 1-g infusion over 8 hours vs placebo (Table 1).16 It included 12,737 patients presenting within 3 hours of isolated TBI with a GCS score of 12 or less or intracranial bleeding. There were no differences in baseline characteristics between the groups, and $71\%$ of patients had a GCS score of less than 12. In both groups, the mean (SD) time from injury to treatment was 1.9 (0.7) hours and the majority $(72.2\%)$ received treatment within 3 hours. The primary outcome of head injury–related death within 28 days of injury $18.5\%$ vs $19.8\%$ ) was similar between the groups (relative risk [RR], 0.94; $95\%$ confidence interval [CI], 0.86 to 1.02). Head injury– related death within 24 hours significantly improved with tranexamic acid (RR, 0.81; $95\%$ CI, 0.69 to 0.95). A sensitivity analysis removing those with expected poor outcome (GCS score of 3 or bilateral unreactive pupils) had similar results (RR, 0.72; $95\%$ CI, 0.56 to 0.92). Subgroup analyses, determined a priori, revealed that tranexamic acid had benefit in patients with mild-tomoderate head injury (GCS score of ${\tt\geq}9$ ) and those with bilateral reactive pupils; head injury–related death occurred in $5.8\%$ vs $7.5\%$ (RR, 0.78; $95\%$ CI, 0.64 to 0.95) and $11.5\%$ vs $13.2\%$ (RR, 0.87; $95\%$ CI, 0.77 to 0.98) of these patients, respectively. No benefit was found in those with severe injury. In regression analysis, tranexamic acid was most effective in less severely injured patients $\'P=0.007)$ . A multivariable analysis adjusting for GCS score, blood pressure, and age found that tranexamic acid was more effective if it was used early (within 1 hour) in patients with mild-to-moderate injury $\left(P~=~0.005\right)$ ), but time to treatment did not make a difference in severe injury. Overall risks associated with tranexamic acid were low, with no difference between the groups in the frequency of vascular occlusive events, stroke, seizure, or other adverse effects. Limitations to these data included a protocol change after enrollment from including patients within 8 hours of injury to within 3 hours. Additionally, the primary outcome of head injury–related death at 28 days could have missed certain cohorts of patients who died beyond this timeframe, does not have universal endorsement, and was determined by clinicians, which may have influenced how deaths were classified, inducing recall or observer bias. Of note, this trial took place in many low-income areas at resource-limited institutions, possibly with limited availability of neurosurgical interventions. Therefore, generalizability to the US patient population is challenging.16  

A recent meta-analysis published in 2020 sought to determine whether tranexamic acid improves mortality and neurological outcome (severe disability, vegetative state, and death on the Glasgow Outcome Scale) in patients with TBI.18 It included 10,444 patients from 7 randomized trials, comparing tranexamic acid to placebo within 24 hours of injury. Overall, mortality was not different between the tranexamic acid $\left(18\%\right)$ and placebo $(19.3\%)$ groups, (RR, 0.93; $95\%$ CI, 0.86 to 1.01). The rates of poor neurological status (RR, 0.90; $95\%$ CI, 0.61 to 1.33) and both ischemic (RR, 1.33; $95\%$ CI, 0.35 to 5.04) and hemorrhagic (RR, 0.71; $95\%$ CI, 0.37 to 1.35) complications were also similar. These data were limited as not all patients had isolated TBI, the degree of brain injury differed across the studies, and the authors did not account for other therapies that could increase the risk for bleeding. The authors concluded that tranexamic acid may be indicated in initial TBI care, but further randomized trials are needed.18  

Recent out-of-hospital data have challenged the benefits of giving tranexamic acid early. Rowell et al17 sought to explore whether early tranexamic acid within 2 hours of TBI would improve outcomes (Table 1). This was a multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial bolus followed by 1-g infusion over 8 hours, tranexamic acid $2{\mathbf{-}}\mathbf{g}\mathrm{~IV~}$ bolus followed by placebo infusion, and placebo bolus and infusion. It included 966 patients 15 years of age and older with moderate-to-severe TBI (GCS score of ${\le}12$ , at least one reactive pupil, and systolic blood pressure [SBP] of ${\geq}90\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ ). Baseline characteristics were similar across the groups, except that more penetrating injuries were found in the bolus-only group. Across the groups, mean GCS score was 7.6 to 7.8 and median time from injury to study drug administration was 40 to 43 minutes. The primary outcome of a favorable neurological outcome at 6 months, defined as a Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended score of greater than 4, occurred in $65\%$ of pooled tranexamic acid–treated patients vs $62\%$ of patients in the placebo group (difference, $3.5\%$ ; $P=0.16$ ). Similarities in mortality at 28 days $14\%$ vs $17\%$ ; difference, $-2.9\%$ ; $95\%$ CI, $-7.9\%$ to $2.1\%$ ), Disability Rating Scale score at 6 months (6.8 vs 7.6; difference, $-0.9$ ; $95\%$ CI, $-2.5$ to 0.7), and progression of ICH $16\%$ vs $20\%$ ; difference, $-5.4\%$ ; $95\%$ CI, $-12.8\%$ to $2.1\%$ ) were also found. Limitations to these data included that they were obtained from a pooled analysis of all tranexamic acid–treated patients in a study that originally sought to compare different dose regimens, not all patients received an infusion, two-thirds of patients had evidence of fibrinolytic shutdown on TEG, a high percentage of patients were lost to follow-up, and there was a low incidence of ICH $\left(58\%\right)$ .17  

To further explore early tranexamic acid use, the BRAIN-PROTECT study retrospectively analyzed a prospective Dutch cohort of patients with severe TBI.19 A total of 1,827 patients were enrolled, of whom 1,375 had confirmed TBI and 719 had isolated TBI. Tranexamic acid was administered to $38\%$ of patients $\mathrm{^rn}=693.$ ), with the majority receiving $^\textrm{\scriptsize1g}$ . Tranexamic acid– treated patients were older, had higher injury severity scores, had lower GCS scores, and had higher heart rates. The median GCS score was 4 (interquartile range, 3-7), and time from injury to treatment was not reported. The frequency of the primary outcome of 30-day mortality was higher in the entire cohort of patients who received tranexamic acid compared to those who did not $37\%$ $[\mathrm{n}=242]$ vs $30\%$ [n $=322]$ ]; $P=0.005^{'}$ ). This difference remained when the data were analyzed by confirmed TBI and isolated TBI. After adjusting for differences in baseline characteristics, there was no longer any difference in mortality between the groups for those with confirmed TBI. However, in those with isolated TBI, mortality remained higher with tranexamic acid (odds ratio [OR], 4.49; $95\%$ CI, 1.57 to 12.87; $P=0.005$ ). It is unclear why tranexamic acid may have led to worse outcomes in more severe isolated head injury, as the fibrinolytic state of these patients was unknown.19

The benefits of tranexamic acid in treating patients with TBI include modest reductions in ICH growth and early head injury–related mortality in the context of mild to moderate injury. It seems that there is minimal risk with tranexamic acid, as differences from placebo were not evident, but the definitions used for detection of vascular occlusive events were not always specified. There is also a suggestion of harm if tranexamic acid is administered in severe TBI or after fibrinolytic shutdown has begun. Because of these concerns and limitations, tranexamic acid cannot be recommended at this time for isolated TBI without extracranial injuries.  



# Nontraumatic ICH  

Nontraumatic ICH accounts for only $10\%$ of all strokes yet contributes to almost half of the deaths associated with cerebrovascular disease.20 Morbidity is also significant, with $60\%$ of patients remaining functionally dependent 1 year after insult.21 Expansion of the primary insult, defined as a relative increase in volume of more than $33\%$ , occurs in $23\%$ to $38\%$ of patients within the first 24 hours, occurring in $24\%$ of patients within 4 hours of symptom onset.22,23 Established therapies aimed at decreasing ICH growth include blood pressure management and reversal of coagulopathy. Current ICH guidelines recommend lowering SBP to less than $140~\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ to improve functional outcomes. Coagulopathy, due to medications or other underlying medical conditions such as liver disease or malignancy, may increase risk of HE.24 In patients with anticoagulation-associated ICH, reversal of anticoagulation should be performed as soon as possible to improve survival.25 Unfortunately, HE occurs in patients without coagulopathy; thus, hemostatic therapies such as tranexamic acid are attractive for patients with spontaneous ICH.23  

The first study to use tranexamic acid in spontaneous ICH was the TICH-2 trial (Table 2).26 This was a randomized, placebo-controlled study comparing tranexamic acid $1\mathrm{gIV}$ over 10 minutes followed by a 1-g infusion over 8 hours vs placebo. It included 2,325 patients presenting within 8 hours of symptom onset. Baseline characteristics were similar between the groups, with a mean GCS score of 13.5, mean National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 13, and mean hematoma volume of $24\mathrm{mL}$ . Time from stroke onset to randomization was 3.6 hours (range, 2.6 to 5 hours), and time from randomization to treatment was 21 minutes (range, 13 to 33 minutes). Routine care, including blood pressure management, surgical intervention, and venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, was administered in accordance with clinical guidelines. There was no difference in the primary outcome of functional status at 90 days, assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Secondary endpoints were improved in the tranexamic acid group compared to the placebo group, including rate of HE at day 2 $25\%$ vs $29\%$ ; OR, 0.80; $95\%$ CI, 0.66 to 0.98; $P=0.03$ ), mean increase in hematoma volume from baseline (3.72 vs $4.90\mathrm{mL}$ ; mean difference, $-1.37\mathrm{mL}$ ; $95\%$ CI, $-2.71$ to $-0.04~\mathrm{mL}$ ; $P=0.0432$ ), and early mortality (7 days) $(9\%$ vs $11\%$ ; OR, 0.73; $95\%$ CI, 0.53 to 0.99; $P$ $=~0.041\$ ). There were no differences between the groups in the incidence of venous thromboembolism or other serious adverse events. This study had several limitations, including the heterogeneous population and a large proportion of patients with intraventricular hemorrhage $(32\%)$ . Given that recovery from ICH may take several months, the time to evaluation of functional status may need to be expanded. Most patients were enrolled more than 3 hours after hemorrhage onset, potentially outside the window of early HE. Additionally, the delay in administration may have contributed to the lack of benefit seen with tranexamic acid. Overall, despite reduced HE and early death, there was no difference in functional outcome at 90 days in those who received tranexamic acid for ICH.26  

The spot sign, an area of contrast enhancement representing continued extravasation within a hematoma, is an independent predictor of HE, in-hospital mortality, and worse functional outcomes among survivors of ICH.29,30 Patients with a spot sign may be at the highest risk for HE and may benefit the most from early use of hemostatic agents.29 STOP-AUST was a multicenter, double-blind, placebocontrolled trial comparing tranexamic acid $1\mathrm{~g~IV~}$ over 10 minutes followed by a 1-g infusion over 8 hours vs placebo in patients with ICH who had a spot sign (Table 2).27 A total of 100 patients were included within 4.5 hours of symptom onset and within 1 hour of computed tomography angiography (CTA). Patients with a GCS score of less than 8, large volume hemorrhages (greater than $70\mathrm{mL}$ ), and brainstem hemorrhages were excluded due to the likelihood of poor outcomes. Baseline characteristics were similar between the groups, with a median GCS score of 14.5, median NIHSS score of 13, median hematoma volume of $14.6~\mathrm{mL}$ , and median time from last known well to treatment of 150 minutes. Blood pressure was managed according to the standard of care at the time of study enrollment. The primary outcome of HE at 24 hours was similar in the tranexamic acid $(44\%)$ and placebo $\left(52\%\right)$ groups (OR, 0.72; $95\%$ CI, 0.32 to 1.62; $P=0.41$ ). Secondary endpoints including thromboembolic events and mortality at 90 days were also similar. Subgroup analyses did not identify significant benefit from tranexamic acid in those with a GCS score of 12 or less or those treated within 3 hours of onset. Other limitations included the use of advanced imaging before treatment, which may limit real-world applicability.27 Additionally, as with the TICH-2 trial, assessing outcomes at 90 days may not have been appropriate given that functional recovery continues for up to 6 months after ICH.31 Given the demonstrated positive safety profile for tranexamic acid, further study is warranted with a larger population, a pragmatic enrollment protocol, and earlier time to treatment.

More recently, a predefined subgroup analysis of TICH-2 sought to assess the efficacy of tranexamic acid in those with or without a spot sign.32 Of the 2,235 patients enrolled, 254 $\left(10.9\%\right)$ had the appropriate imaging for screening and 64 $\left(25.2\%\right)$ were positive for a spot sign. Baseline characteristics were similar between the groups except that spot sign–positive patients who received tranexamic acid had a larger mean hematoma volume at admission (46 vs $38.4\mathrm{mL}$ ) and longer median time to treatment from symptom onset (210 vs 169 minutes). More patients had intraventricular hemorrhage in the spot sign–positive placebo group compared to the tranexamic acid group $41.2\%$ vs $20\%$ ). There was no difference in in the primary outcome of hematoma volume at day 2 among patients positive or negative for a spot sign allocated to receive tranexamic acid or placebo. No differences were noted in rates of serious adverse events, safety outcomes, or thromboembolic events. Several limitations including a small sample size and overall delay in treatment were noted.32  

There is no evidence that tranexamic acid improves functional outcomes in patients with ICH or decreases HE in patients positive for a spot sign. Given the positive safety profile of tranexamic acid, further studies are warranted to understand the impact of early tranexamic acid administration and the role of the spot sign in prevention of HE. A study investigating the use of tranexamic acid in patients with ICH within 2 hours of onset receiving care in a mobile stroke unit is recruiting (STOPMSU trial).33  



# aSAH  

Patients with aSAH are at high risk of rebleeding, delayed cerebral ischemia, and death.34 Only $25\%$ to $50\%$ of patients with aSAH survive to receive medical attention, and over half of survivors suffer from long-term neurological deficits.35,36 The first 24 hours are crucial, as early identification, blood pressure control, and surgical or endovascular treatment of the aneurysm are necessary to prevent these complications.  

Historically, surgical intervention for aSAH was delayed until at least 14 days after the initial bleed as data suggested that earlier operative intervention increased morbidity and mortality.37 As such, patients were medically optimized with antifibrinolytics to prevent rebleeding. In the 1970s, high doses of aminocaproic acid (24 to $36\mathrm{~g~}$ per day) and tranexamic acid (6 to ${12\mathrm{~g~}}$ per day) for up to 14 to 21 days until surgical intervention were evaluated.38 Antifibrinolytic agents reduced rates of rebleeding from $15\%$ to $20\%$ to $5\%$ to $10\%$ .39 Subsequently, in the early 2000s, a large meta-analysis demonstrated that antifibrinolytics significantly reduced rebleeding, but this benefit was offset by increased incidence of cerebral ischemia and no difference in overall outcomes of death or disability.13 At this time, practice also shifted to focus on early intervention rather than prolonged medical management.  

More recently, in the era of early intervention, the efficacy of shorter courses of antifibrinolytic therapy in patients with aSAH has been assessed in several small studies. A systematic review of 10 randomized controlled trials assessing oral or IV antifibrinolytics in patients with aSAH found that antifibrinolytics reduced rates of rebleeding but had no effect on functional outcomes.40 More concerning was the increased RR for development of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) with antifibrinolytic therapy (RR, 1.41; $95\%$ CI, 1.04 to 1.91). It should be noted that heterogeneity among trials was a major limitation in interpreting these pooled results.40  

The ULTRA study was a recent prospective, multicenter, open-label trial that evaluated the efficacy of early administration of tranexamic acid as a 1-g bolus followed by ${\mathrm{~1~g~}}$ every 8 hours compared to usual care at the time of SAH diagnosis (Table 2).28 The study enrolled 955 patients after confirmation of nontraumatic SAH, and therapy continued until the start of endovascular or surgical treatment or for a maximum of 24 hours (whichever came first). Most patients had a Fisher Grade Score of IV, had an anterior location of the aneurysm, and were not on anticoagulation or antiplatelet agents. The median time from symptom onset to the start of tranexamic acid was 185 minutes (range, 130 to 333 minutes), and the time to treatment of the aneurysm was 14 hours (range, 5 to 20 hours). There was no difference in the primary outcome of functional status assessed by mRS at 6 months. A good neurological outcome (mRS score of 0 to 3) occurred in $60\%$ of patients in the tranexamic acid group and $64\%$ of patients in the control group (OR, 0.87; $95\%$ CI, 0.67 to 1.13). No difference in serious adverse effects, rebleeding before aneurysm treatment, DCI, thromboembolic complications, or allcause mortality at 6 months was demonstrated. These data were limited by delays in obtaining CT imaging (median delay, 93 minutes). The goal of ultra-early treatment with tranexamic acid was therefore not achieved. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been conducted to further explore the role of tranexamic acid compared to usual care or placebo in aSAH.41-44 A review by Feng et al42 included 5 studies and 2,359 patients and found no difference in mortality with tranexamic acid (OR, 0.94; $95\%$ CI, 0.75 to 1.18; $P=0.61$ ), but a reduction in the risk for rebleeding (OR, 0.62; $95\%$ CI, 0.41 to 0.93; $P=0.02$ ). There was low heterogeneity in the studies evaluating mortality $\left(I^{2}~=~22\%\right)$ ), but significant heterogeneity among the studies assessing rebleeding $\left(P\mathrm{~=~}58\%\right)$ . One study published in 1984 contributed to this heterogeneity, and, when this was removed from the analysis, there was no statistically significant difference in rebleeding and no remaining heterogeneity. No differences in hydrocephalus or DCI were identified. Liu et al43 included 7 studies with 2,917 patients and found no difference in mortality (risk difference [RD], 0.01; $95\%$ CI, –0.03 to 0.4; $P=0.63$ ) or functional outcomes (RD, $-0.01$ ; $95\%$ CI, $-0.05$ to 0.02; $P=$ 0.51) with tranexamic acid. However, differences were identified in the rates of rebleeding (RD, $-0.08$ ; $95\%$ CI, $-0.01$ to $-0.05$ ; $P<0.0001$ ), hydrocephalus (RD, 0.04; $95\%$ CI, 0.01 to 0.08; $P=0.02$ ), and seizure (RD, 0.04; $95\%$ CI, 0.00 to 0.08; $P=0.05$ ). The frequency of complications including DCI was not different between the groups (RD, 0.00; $95\%$ CI, $-0.03$ to 0.03; $P=0.96$ ). Ren et al44 included 10 studies with 2,810 patients and found no difference in mortality, functional outcomes, or DCI with tranexamic acid, but did find a significant reduction in the incidence of bleeding and an increase in the incidence of hydrocephalus. A subgroup analysis was performed assessing duration of tranexamic acid treatment and dose, which revealed that the significant reduction in bleeding was driven by studies that included longer durations (greater than 72 hours) and higher doses (greater than 6 g) of tranexamic acid and studies were typically published before the year 2000. An analysis by Bouillon-Minois et al41 demonstrated a significant improvement in mortality with tranexamic acid (RR, 0.78; $95\%$ CI, 0.58 to 0.98; $P<0.001$ ). However, many studies included were published in the 1970s. Of note, this analysis included patients with nonaneurysmal SAH, although the results were largely driven by those with aSAH.41  

It is currently unclear whether patients unable to receive rapid surgery or endovascular treatment, such as those in resource-limited settings, may benefit from a prolonged duration of tranexamic acid before intervention to prevent rebleeding. One study aimed to evaluate the use of tranexamic acid in patients with aSAH for up to 72 hours; however, $70\%$ of patients were clipped or coiled within the first 24 hours of hospital admission, making the results less generalizable for patients in resource-limited settings.45  

Current data suggest that tranexamic acid may prevent rebleeding in patients with aSAH; however, this has not correlated with improvement in clinical outcomes or mortality and may be impacted by increased risk of hydrocephalus and DCI that offsets any benefit in bleeding reduction. Given this potential risk with tranexamic acid in aSAH, further study is warranted to assess whether earlier administration has a role in decreasing rates of rebleeding with improvement in patient outcomes.  



# Clinical practice considerations  

The practicality of tranexamic acid administration is challenging. Many barriers exist due to lack of resources, lack of access for administration, a greater focus on stabilizing injuries and resuscitation (fluid and blood administration), and placement of priority on obtaining prompt surgical consultations. Using the intraosseous route of administration and having emergency medicine pharmacists present to focus on timely and appropriate pharmacotherapy may help mitigate some of these barriers.  

An appropriate dose and administration are critical to ensure safe and effective use. Most studies have used $1\mathrm{~g~IV~}$ administered over 10 minutes followed by a 1-g infusion over 8 hours. To avoid hypotension, the maximum recommended rate is $100~\mathrm{mg/}$ min.46 Recent guidelines have challenged this dose recommendation, suggesting that ${2\mathrm{~g~}}$ could be administered as a single dose over 1 minute in otherwise healthy, nonelderly patients to ease administration.47 However, doses greater than $_{2\textrm{g}}$ have been associated with more adverse effects, such as thrombosis or seizure, compared to placebo.18,48 Other adverse effects of tranexamic acid include headache, dizziness, and visual disturbances.46  

On the basis of the literature reviewed, tranexamic acid offers modest benefit in patients with TBI, with minimal risk and a relatively low cost. A cost-effectiveness analysis found that tranexamic acid is cost-effective in patients with both mild and moderate TBI, especially when given early.49 This has been challenged recently by prehospital data suggesting that tranexamic acid has no benefit and that there is possible harm in fibrinolytic shutdown.19 Clinicians should use TEG evaluation to select patients with hyperfibrinolysis who are most likely to benefit from tranexamic acid (LY30 of greater than $3\%$ ) and avoid use in patients with fibrinolytic shutdown (LY30 of less than $0.8\%$ ).3  

In patients with aSAH, early surgery or embolization is the standard of care to prevent rebleeding, replacing the historical need for prolonged administration of tranexamic acid. However, there may be a role for tranexamic acid to prevent rebleeding in resourcelimited settings where rapid intervention is not possible. The optimal dose and duration of therapy for this indication have not been established.  



# Conclusion  

Benefit from tranexamic acid in TBI has only been shown in specific patient subgroups, and it is unclear which patients are most likely to benefit. Further trials should focus on TEGguided therapy to identify patients with hyperfibrinolysis. Data have not shown improvement in functional outcomes in nontraumatic ICH or aSAH and only small decreases in hematoma volume, which does not warrant tranexamic acid use. Despite the favorable safety profile of tranexamic acid for most of the indications discussed, more data are needed to determine which head injury subpopulations are most likely to benefit from tranexamic acid and which patients are at increased risk for harm.
