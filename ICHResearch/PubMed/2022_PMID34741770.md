# Abstract  

Background: Transdermal glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) has potential beneficial properties in acute stroke including intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and possible clinical benefits suggested in ultra-early stroke (<6 h). Our meta-analysis updated the evidence on its safety and benefits in acute stroke.  

Methods: We searched major electronic databases for randomized trials comparing transdermal GTN versus placebo/control in acute stroke. Primary outcomes were mortality, 9O-day modified Rankin Scale (mRS), and blood pressure (BP) effects. Secondary outcomes included early, late, resource utilization, and surrogate outcomes. Safety outcomes were adverse events. Reviewers identified studies, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias (RoB) using a modified Cochrane RoB instrument and quality of evidence (QoE) using GRADE. We also performed a priori subgroup and trial sequential analyses (TSA) on primary outcomes. These subgroup analyses were ICH versus ischemic stroke, minor $(\mathsf{NIHSS}\leq5\$ ) versus major $(\mathsf{NIHSS}>5\$ ) ischemic stroke, ischemic stroke with versus without thrombolysis, prehospital versus non prehospital settings, time from stroke to randomization $\le6\mathrm{~h~}$ versus ${>}6\mathrm{~h~}$ , and high versus low overall RoB studies.  

Results: Seven eligible primary trials enrolled 5363 patients. GTN reduced BP (mean difference $\left[\mathsf{MD}\right]=-4.74\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ , $95\%$ confidence interval $\left[{\sfCI}\right]=-6.03$ to $-3.45\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}]$ and diastolic BP $(\mathsf{MD}=-2.94\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ , $95\%\mathrm{Cl}=-3.74$ to $-2.13\mathrm{mm}\ H_{8}\right);$ $24\mathsf{h}$ posttreatment but did not affect 4- to 10-day mortality (relative risk [RR] $=1.11$ , $95\%{\sfCl}=0.82$ to 1.49), 90-day mortality $(\mathsf{RR}=0.96\$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{Cl}=0.77$ to 1.19), and 90-day $m\mathsf{RS}>2$ $\left(\mathsf{RR}=0.98\right.$ , $95\%{\mathsf{C}}|=0.93$ to 1.03) compared to control/placebo. The QoE was high for primary outcomes with no subgroup effects detected. GTN did not affect secondary outcomes and increased risk of headache and hypotension. TSA generally supported our conclusions regarding primary outcomes.  

Conclusions: Transdermal GTN reduces BP in acute stroke but does not alter clinical outcomes even in ultra-early stroke $(\leq6~\mathsf{h})$ .

# INTRODUCTION  

High blood pressure (BP) is present in ${>}70\%$ of patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS).1 It is associated with poor outcomes like acute stroke recurrence, death within a few weeks, or combined death and dependency after several months.1-4 High BP is similarly common in acute intracerebral hemorrhage $({\mathsf{ICH}})^{5}$ and may be associated with hematoma expansion and increased mortality.6-8 It is recommended to lower BP in ${\mathsf{ICH}}^{9,10}$ although controversy exists regarding optimal BP target and there is no current literature on the role of prehospital BP reduction.  

The management of high BP in AIS and the decision to treat/ not to treat has been a constant debate since 1985. Clinicians are concerned that BP lowering will compromise cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) when there is dysfunctional cerebral autoregulation. Current available guidelines recommend withholding antihypertensive in the early poststroke period unless there is markedly elevated BP $(>220/120\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg})$ or with $\mathsf{BP}>185/110\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ for patients eligible for thrombolysis or BP ${>}180/105\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ during the $^{24-\mathrm{h}}$ period following reperfusion.11-13  

Nitric oxide (NO) donors are candidate agents to lower BP in acute stroke. NO is a cerebral vasodilator (might preserve CPP), modulates vascular and neuronal function, is neuroprotective, and inhibits apoptosis.14 In addition, vascular NO concentrations are low in acute stroke, which are associated with increased stroke severity, mortality and institutionalization.15 These observations support the fact that NO supplementation might be beneficial.  

Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) is a NO donor. Transdermal GTN offers constant drug release across the skin into the systemic circulation for $24~\mathsf{h}$ to achieve sustained steady-state plasma concentrations.16 It offers a formulation which is easily administered in many settings (prehospital, emergency department [ED], and inpatient) managing acute stroke, which may minimize fluctuations in drug concentrations and hence BP.  

The latest meta-analysis published in $2016^{17}$ and Cochrane review in $2017^{18}$ , using data from five completed transdermal GTN trials $\begin{array}{r}{\langle\boldsymbol{n}=4197,}\end{array}$ ), reported no improvement in outcomes across various domains (death, disability, cognition, mood, and quality of life) with transdermal GTN versus placebo/control. However, in a prespecified subgroup analysis of patients with time from stroke to randomization $\le6\mathrm{~h~}$ versus ${>}6\mathfrak{h}$ $\mathbf{\zeta}n=312$ ), these two reviews reported a favorable functional outcome as measured by modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 90  days with transdermal GTN. These reviews had important limitations. Four out of five selected trials had small sample sizes $(n\leq90)$ . The remaining multicenter ENOS trial $[n=4011;$ )  

dominated the pooled analysis $(95.6\%$ of all patients and $86.9\%$ of those randomised within $^{6\mathfrak{h}}$ of onset). In addition, all included trials were conducted by a single research group and it is important that other research groups study the role of transdermal GTN in acute stroke. Finally, a relatively small number of patients $(n=312$ ) were treated within $^{6\textrm{h}}$ of onset and these patients came from just two of the five trials.  

The recently published multicenter RIGHT-2 trial randomized 1149 participants with acute stroke within $^{4\textrm{h}}$ of onset to receive transdermal GTN versus sham therapy.20 Their data more than tripled the patient number used to examine the role of transdermal GTN in ultra-early stroke (onset $\le6\mathrm{~h~}$ . There is an urgent need to update the evidence behind the efficacy and safety of transdermal GTN in acute stroke especially among those patients with ultra-early (≤6 h) presentation.  

The aim of this meta-analysis was to examine, using recent data, whether transdermal GTN improved important patient-centered outcomes and was safe among patients with acute stroke in prehospital and hospital settings compared to placebo/control by reviewing randomized controlled trials (RCTs).  



# METHODS  

This meta-analysis was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). The reference number was CRD42020173093. We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement for reporting this meta-analysis.21 The study protocol and statistical analysis plan were published previously.22  



# Eligibility criteria  

We included RCTs investigating the efficacy and safety of transdermal GTN versus placebo/control in adults with acute stroke. Patients aged $\geq16$ years presenting with either acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke to the prehospital, ED, and inpatient settings were considered. Acute stroke patients were defined as those with presentation within 5 days of symptom onset. We selected 5 days since symptom onset as the inclusion cutoff criterion because there could be significant delay in presentation especially for less severe ischemic strokes.23 Patients with ischemic stroke were included regardless of whether they received thrombolysis. The comparator arms included transdermal GTN patch, sham patch, and control with existing standard therapy.  

Primary outcomes were BP measurements and important patientcentered outcomes like mortality and late functional status. BP parameters included systolic BP, diastolic BP, and mean arterial pressure measured at intervals stated by the authors. Late functional status was assessed using mRS within 3 months of stroke onset or later, the preferred outcome measurement for acute stroke trials.24 The hierarchical mRS scores range from 0 to 6, with 0 indicating no symptoms, 1 indicating some symptoms but no significant disability, 2-5 indicating increasing levels of disability and dependency, and 6 indicating death.24  

Secondary outcomes were classified as early, late, resource utilization, and surrogate outcomes. Early secondary outcomes included development of ICH, recurrent stroke, and change in calculated National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Late secondary outcomes included assessment of activities of daily living, cognition, quality of life, and mood. Resource utilization secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay (LOHS) and discharge destination. Surrogate secondary outcomes included cerebral hemodynamics and laboratory parameters like platelet aggregation. Safety outcomes included adverse events reported by the authors.  



# Search strategy  

We searched Medline, PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, and Cochrane Library from inception until June 2021 without language restrictions. We also reviewed reference lists for eligible new trials and searched ClinicalTrials.gov for ongoing/unpublished trials and additional data from published trials.  

We searched the following keywords: stroke, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, ICH, intracranial hemorrhage, transdermal GTN, transdermal nitroglycerin, GTN patch, nitroglycerin patch, trinitroglycerin, prehospital, mortality, BP, functional outcome, humans, and randomized clinical trials.  



# Study selection  

Reviewers (LBL, CWL, LWF, and NWM) independently and in duplicate screened titles and abstracts of all identified studies to generate a list of eligible trials from which full texts were obtained. Subsequently, the same reviewers independently assessed eligibility of these full texts to decide on the final included studies. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus or, if needed, by adjudication from an external reviewer and/or contact with primary authors for clarification.  



# Data extraction  

Two pairs of reviewers (LBL and CWL, LWF and NWM) extracted data from included studies both independently and in duplicate. We used a predesigned data extraction form (Data Supplement S1, Appendix S1) adapted from the Cochrane Collaboration.25  

Data extracted included general study information (authors, publication year, and location[s]), study population details (clinical setting-prehospital vs. ED vs. inpatient, sample size, types of strokes-ischemic vs. hemorrhagic; relevant subgroups like those treated with thrombolysis), comparator arms (different doses and duration of GTN patch, sham and control) as well as primary, secondary, and safety outcomes of interest.  

In RCTs that included more than one arm of GTN dosing and duration,we extracted data from that closest to a single dose-regimen in other primary studies that was used for analysis. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus or, if needed, via an external reviewer and/or contact with primary authors for clarification.  



# Risk of bias and quality of evidence assessment  

We assessed risk of bias (RoB) for each outcome of the individual studies using a modified Cochrane RoB instrument.26 It assesses biases in five domains namely selection bias (random-sequence generation and allocation concealment), performance bias (blinding of participants and researchers), detection bias (blinding of outcome assessment), attrition bias (incomplete outcome data), and reporting bias (selective reporting). Within each domain, we classified RoB as high, unclear or low. Reviewers also judged to determine whether any particular domain was challenging to achieve in any primary studies (like blinding in trials comparing GTN patch vs. existing standard therapy) and likely/unlikely to affect the reported effect size of the outcome.  

A primary trial was considered to have an overall high RoB when it had at least one domain rating of “high risk.” This assessment excluded domain(s) that was/were judged to be logistically impossible to achieve and unlikely to affect the reported effect size of the outcome. The overall RoB for each trial was considered low if RoB was judged to be low in all domains.  

We assessed quality of evidence (QoE) for each outcome using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach that classifies evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low quality based on considerations of RoB, consistency, directness, precision, and publication bias.27  

Assessment of individual and overall RoB as well as QoE was performed independently by two pairs of reviewers (LBL and CWL, LWF and NWM) with any discrepancies resolved by discussion and consensus or if necessary, via consultation with an external reviewer.  



# Data analysis  

All analyses were performed using RevMan 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration) software. We used DerSimonian and Laird randomeffects model a priori to conduct the meta-analysis.We chose the random-effects modelbecause it produces moreconservative confidence intervals (Cls)and considers both within-and betweenstudy variability.21  

For continuous outcomes, we calculated mean difference and its $95\%$ CI whenever possible. When primary studies reported continuous outcomes using median, range, and interquartile range (IQR), we converted them into mean and standard deviation (SD). We adopted a modified conversion method that considers the effects of sample size28; an important limitation of the methods of Hozo et al.29 and Bland.30 For dichotomous outcomes, we calculated relative risk (RR) and its $95\%$ CI.  

For ordinal outcomes, the analytic method (dichotomous, continuous or generic inverse variance) used depended on how the primary authors performed their analyses and the limitations imposed by the primary trial data. If primary authors adopted an ordinal approach and reported proportional odds ratios (ORs), we would use generic inverse variance analysis and report RR and its $95\%$ CI.  

We generated forest plots to demonstrate individual and pooled effect sizes for the outcome of interest if there were at least two studies. We assessed for heterogeneity between studies by first visual inspection of forest plots and then using the $I^{2}$ statistic. $I^{2}$ measures the percentage of total variation in estimated effects of the outcome across studies that is due to heterogeneity rather than to chance. $^{31}\mathsf{A}I^{2}$ value of $0\%$ indicates no observed heterogeneity, and larger values show increasing heterogeneity.  

Regardless of observed $I^{2}$ values, we planned to conduct the following a priori subgroup analyses for each primary outcome when each subgroup analysis was represented by at least two studies. These subgroup analyses were ICH versus ischemic stroke, minor (NIHSS ≤5) versus major $(\mathsf{NIHSS}>5$ ) ischemic stroke, ischemic stroke with versus without thrombolysis, prehospital versus nonprehospital (ED and inpatient) settings, time from stroke to randomization $\le6\mathrm{~h~}$ versus ${>}6\mathfrak{h}$ , and high versus low overall RoB studies. We tested for subgroup interaction using chi-square significance test.32  

Meta-analyses may result in Type I errors when sparse data are collected and with repeated significance testing when a cumulative meta-analysis is updated with new trials.3334 We performed trial sequential analysis (TSA) using a random-effects model for the primary outcomes (mortality, BP, and late functional effects), which were reported as dichotomous or continuous variables as permitted by the software. We used $\upalpha=0.05$ and $\upbeta=0.20$ $80\%$ power), estimated effect sizes and control/placebo rates obtained using several means. These estimates could be derived from sample size calculations in the largest primary trial with low overall RoB or the magnitude of effect and control/placebo rate reported from pooled analysis (empirical estimates). If such information was unavailable, we would use the control/placebo rates in pooled analysis and a clinically significant effect size based on our consensus. Our consensus might be arbitrary but represented our expectations of clinically relevant effect sizes. TSA generates the required information size calculated as diversity-adjusted information size $({\sfD}|{\sfS})^{35}$ for the estimated effect size, thereby providing important information on how many more patients need to be included in further trials. Before DIS is reached, TSA also creates adjusted thresholds for statistical significance (monitoring boundaries) and futility boundaries.33,34 The cumulative  

Z-curve representing the selected trials, if it crosses the monitoring boundary, will signify presence of effect by the intervention or, if it crosses the futility boundary, will signify no effect.33,34 If the Z-curve fails to cross the monitoring or futility boundaries, the DlS is not reached and there is insufficient evidence for a conclusion. TSA was performed using Trial Sequential Analysis v.0.9.5.10 beta (Copenhagen Trial Unit, Centre for Clinical Intervention Research, www. ctu.dk/tsa).

# Secondary outcomes  

Early  

Rates of recurrent stroke were reported in two primary trials19,36 and two subgroup analyses42,43 while rates of symptomatic ICH were reported in one primary trial19 and one subgroup analysis43 on Day 7 or discharge. On Day 4 or discharge, one primary trial20 and one subgroup analysis41 reported neurological deterioration $\mathrm{(NIHSS~\geq4}$ points or $\geq2$ -point increase in any domain). Overall, the RIGHT $\cdot2^{20}$ and $\mathsf{ENOS}^{19}$ trials reported no statistically significant differences between GTN versus control/placebo for neurological deterioration (62/292 vs. 59/294, $p=0.73.$ ) and symptomatic ICH (22/2000 vs. 18/2011, $p=0.59$ ) respectively. Pooled analysis for rates of recurrent stroke revealed no statistically significant differences between the groups $\mathrm{\Omega}^{\prime}{\sfRR}=1.30\$ , $95\%$ ${\sfCl}=0.83$ to 2.04, $I^{2}=0\%$ , $p=0.24$ ; Appendix S5).

There werethree primarytrials92036 threesugroup analyses443 and one secondary analysis45 that reported late secondary outcomes of interest. # Surrogate  

Cerebral hemodynamic changes were reported in two primary trials37,38 $(n<20)$ and platelet aggregation in one primary trial. $^{40}\mathsf{At1}\mathsf{h}$ after treatment, Willmot et al.37 reported no differences between groups for global cerebral blood flow (CBF; mean $\left[\pm5\mathsf{D}\right]=37.8\left[\pm9.7\right]$ vs. $32.8\left[\pm7.3\right]\mathsf{mL}/\mathsf{min}/100\mathsf{g},p=0.66),$ CBF in affected hemisphere (mean $\left[\pm5\mathsf{D}\right]=37.2\left[\pm9.8\right]$ vs. $33.4\left[\pm8.3\right]\mathsf{mL}/\mathsf{min}/100\mathsf{g},p=0.65)$ or unaffected hemisphere (mean $\left[\pm5\mathsf{D}\right]=38.4\left[\pm10.0\right]$ vs. $32.2[\pm6.3]$ $\mathsf{mL/min/100g}$ , $p=0.96)$ , and CPP (mean $\left[\pm5\mathsf{D}\right]=50.9$ $\left[\pm15.8\right]$ vs. 59. $1\left[\pm32.4\right]\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ , $p=0.77.$ ) in stroke patients. # DISCUSSION  

In acute stroke patients, there was generally high-quality evidence that 4 to 12 days of $5~\mathsf{mg}$ daily of transdermal GTN reduced mean systolic and diastolic BP at $^{24\textrm{h}}$ posttreatment but did not affect early (up to 10 days) and delayed (90-day) clinical outcomes. TSA supported conclusions of BP reduction at $24~\mathsf{h}$ and no effect on 90- day mortality and $\mathsf{mRS}$ scores by GTN whereas its effect on posttreatment mortality (4-10 days) was inconclusive.  

GTN reduced mean systolic BP by $4.74\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}(6.03\substack{-3.45\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}}$ lower) and diastolic BP by $2.94\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}(3.74\mathrm{-}2.13\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ lower) at $^{24\textrm{h}}$ posttreatment. Limited studies reported that GTN had no effect on CPP, CBF, and platelet aggregation in acute stroke but increased CBF in SAH. We reported no benefits on posttreatment mortality (4-10 days), 90-day mortality, and $\mathsf{mRS}>2$ by GTN in our subgroup analyses of patients with time from stroke to randomization $\le6\mathrm{~h~}$ , contrary to what was previously suggested.17,18 There was moderate-quality evidence that GTN did not affect SAEs on day 7 or 90 but increased risk of headache and hypotension.  

Unlike previous reviews,17,18 we added the results from the large, multicenter RIGHT-2 trial20 $(n~=~1149)$ involving patients with ultra-early stroke $(<4\mathrm{~h~})$ to our main and subgroup analyses.

Another multicenter trial (MR ASAP) in the Netherlands is ongoing and targets to recruit 1400 participants with acute stroke within $^{3\mathfrak{h}}$ of onset to investigate the effects of GTN on important clinical outcomes including 90-day mRS scores.52 However, this trial faces recruitment difficulties related to the coronavirus pandemic.  

There was limited literature on GTN as an adjunct to revascularization therapy, i.e., intravenous (IV) thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy (MT) especially in ultra-early AIS. IV thrombolysis and MT in AIS are time-sensitive and eligible patients should receive them in the fastest achievable onset-to-treatment time.1.12 BP should be carefully lowered to $\leq185/110\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ before initiation of IV thrombolysis and MT and should be maintained at ${<}180/105\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ for at least $24~\mathsf{h}$ afterward.11,12 Transdermal GTN offers an easily administered formulation in prehospital and ED settings, often the first contact points for ultra-early AIS. GTN may serve to better prepare patients for revascularization by lowering BP to recommended levels especially in the prehospital setting, thereby allowing more patients to be treated and earlier. The potential role of transdermal GTN as an adjunct to thrombolysis and MT among ultra-earlyAlS patients is worthy of further research.  



# LIMITATIONS  

First, the majority of data came from the ENOS and RIGHT-2 trials $(96.2\%$ of all patients and $97.2\%$ of those randomized within 6 h of onset), which dominated the analyses. Second, six of seven primary trials came from one research group and it is important for other groups to study how GTN affect acute stroke. Third, three of the seven primary trials36,38,39 had high overall RoB and small sample sizes (17-90). The remaining four primary trials with low overall RoB included the two multicenter studies19,20 with large sample sizes $(>1000)$ ). We tried to mitigate the effects of disparity in overall RoB and sample sizes in the primary trials on the primary outcomes by performing a subgroup analysis of high versus low overall RoB studies and TSA. Fourth, patient characteristics differed between the primary trials and this reflected clinical heterogeneity between study populations and could improve external validity. However, unexpectedly low rates of males $(17\%)$ and hypertension $(25\%)$ were reported in the GTN arm of a small trial $n=18^{\prime}$ ).37 This discrepancy likely resulted from skewed reporting with small sample sizes. Fifth, there are limited data on the effects of GTN together with IV thrombolysis and MT on AIS. Finally, our results and conclusions might not apply to other NO donors.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

Transdermal glyceryl trinitrate reduces blood pressure in acute stroke (ischemic and hemorrhagic) but does not improve clinical outcomes. Our subgroup analysis gathered more evidence that showed no benefits on clinical outcomes in ultra-early stroke $(\leq6~\mathsf{h})$ . The effects of glyceryl trinitrate together with revascularization therapy (intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy) in acute ischemic stroke are unclear and deserve further study especially when glyceryl trinitrate is readily available, inexpensive, and easy to administer in the prehospital setting.
