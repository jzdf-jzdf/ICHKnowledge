# Abstract  

Purpose The spot sign is associated with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) expansion and neurological decline. However, the relationship of the spot sign to secondary intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) has not been well established. The presence of the spot sign in secondary IVH may provide information regarding neurologic outcome in a population with known poor prognosis.  

Methods A subset analysis was performed of patients with IVH from the Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage 2 (ATACH-II) study, a randomized clinical trial examining the effect of intensive blood pressure reduction on hematoma expansion. The presence of the spot sign was determined on CTA and IVH expansion was measured in the first 24 hours. The primary outcome measure was early neurological decline (END), defined as $\geq2$ -point decrease in Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score or $\geq4$ -point increase in NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Secondary outcomes including various radiological and clinical factors were also measured.  

Results Of 57 patients included in this analysis, the spot sign was present in 17 $(29.8\%)$ . The spot sign was a predictor of END in univariate $\scriptstyle{\mathit{\Omega}}^{\prime}P<0.005$ for GCS and $P{=}0.04$ for NIHSS) and multivariate analyses using GCS scores $(\mathrm{OR}{=}7.2\$ , C $\mathrm{~I~}1.6\mathrm{~-~}32.5$ , $P{=}0.01$ ). Median 90 day modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores were significantly different between spot sign-positive and spot sign-negative groups $(P{=}0.02)$ ).  

Conclusions The presence of the spot sign is associated with END in patients with secondary IVH. Identifying this radiologic feature could have important implications for triaging these patients to the appropriate level of care.

# Introduction  

Despite the implementation of multiple diagnostic and therapeutic interventions, the mortality of intracerebral hemorrhage remains high, with rates ranging from $40{-}50\%$ [1–3]. Secondary intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), a common complication of intraparenchymal hemorrhage (IPH), has been previously linked to severe disability at 3 months, long-term functional dependence, and overall increased mortality [3–13]. An IVH is considered primary if the source of the bleed is confined to the ventricular system and secondary if the IVH is a product of extension from an adjacent intraparenchymal hemorrhage [14, 15]. There are four known mechanisms that have been suggested to account for the poor clinical outcomes of patients with IVH: obstructive hydrocephalus, mass effect secondary to intraventricular blood clotting, toxic metabolic intermediaries of blood breakdown, and chronic hydrocephalus [16, 17]. Due to the multifactorial processes underlying IVH, management of IVH poses a dilemma to the interdisciplinary treatment team. Therefore, identifying an independent relevant feature of IVH could aid in the correct selection of patients who could benefit from prompt and appropriate IVH intervention.  

Various imaging features have been identified to evaluate hematoma expansion, including markers on non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) [18, 19] and CT angiography (CTA) [20, 21]. One of the most important radiological features in the evaluation of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is the spot sign, a marker defined as contrast extravasation within a hematoma on CTA. Various single center and multicenter studies have shown that the presence of the spot sign is strongly correlated with ICH expansion and neurological decline [3, 4, 7–9, 13, 20, 22–24]; however, the relationship between the spot sign in patients with secondary IVH and neurological outcome is less well understood. Some studies have found that the spot sign is associated with the presence of IVH [4, 20] and IVH expansion [20], while others have not shown a clear relationship between the two [21, 22, 25, 26]. To our knowledge, there have been no multicenter randomized controlled trials investigating the association of the spot sign with early neurological decline (END) in patients with secondary IVH.  

The objective of this study was to analyze the subset of patients with secondary IVH from the spot sign score in restricting ICH growth (SCORE-IT) cohort of the antihypertensive treatment of acute cerebral hemorrhage (ATACHII) study [27, 28]. The ATACH-II study was a prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trial conducted between 2011 and 2016 which examined the effect of intensive blood pressure control on functional dependence and mortality in patients with ICH [29, 30]. It was hypothesized that the presence of the spot sign in patients with secondary IVH would be associated with expansion and END. The study also assessed other measures, such as IPH expansion, hydrocephalus, external ventricular drain (EVD) placement, and clinical outcomes.

# Material and Methods  



# Participants  

This retrospective analysis included patients with secondary IVH from the SCORE-IT subset of the ATACHII study [27, 29]. In ATACH-II, patients were randomized into two groups, intensive (systolic blood pressure, BP goal ${<}140\mathrm{mmHg},$ and standard (systolic BP goal ${<}180\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg},$ BP treatment within $4.5\mathrm{h}$ after ICH onset. The inclusion and exclusion criteria of these patients were previously described in detail [29]. The SCORE-IT cohort was a subset of patients who had a CTA [27]. Written informed consent was obtained from all ATACH-II patients or family members, and all aspects of the study were approved by each study site’s local institutional review boards.  



# Imaging Analysis  

Baseline admission NCCT within $150\mathrm{min}$ from symptom onset was acquired. In the next $^{241}$ period, a CTA and follow-up NCCT were obtained, and the time elapsed from the admission NCCT to CTA was recorded. The presence of the spot sign on CTA was determined by the two definitions previously described in the SCORE-IT study (Fig. 1; [27]). The first definition was the presence of at least one focus of contrast extravasation in the hematoma, with any size or morphologic feature and density greater than 120 Hounsfield units [27]. The second definition was any intrahematoma contrast extravasation with a minimum diameter of $1.5\mathrm{mm}$ in any dimension, with spot-like or serpiginous shape, without any connection with vessels outside the hematoma, without corresponding hyperdensity at NCCT, and with attenuation at least double that of the background hematoma (in Hounsfield units) [27]. One of the two definitions had to be met to consider the presence of the spot sign, which was confirmed by two readers. Any disagreement was resolved by consensus from a board-certified neuroradiologist.  

Semi-automated volumetric analysis was performed using Mango image processing software (Research Imaging Institute, University Texas Health of Science Center at San Antonio, Texas, USA). The IVH and IPH volumes were segmented on the NCCT at the baseline admission and $^{241}$ time points to determine IVH and IPH expansion. IVH volume was corrected manually based on ventricular anatomical landmarks, likely decreasing the possibility of including the parenchymal component of the intracerebral hemorrhage. IPH expansion was defined as $30\%$ volume increase during the first 24 hours based on previous studies [20].  



# Measurement of Outcomes  

A comparison of the baseline characteristics between spot sign-positive and spot sign-negative patients was conducted as well as a comparison of the subset of patients with the entire ATACH-II cohort. Early neurlogical decline was determined by the change in Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores and the National Institutes of Health stroke scale (NIHSS) scores in the first $^{241}$ . Clinically significant END was specified as either a $\geq2$ -point decrease in GCS score or a $\geq4$ -point increase in NIHSS, as defined in a previous study [31]. These variables were assessed using univariate analyses and multivariable logistic regression. Multivariable logistic regression variables included spot sign, IVH expansion, IPH expansion, and hydrocephalus. These variables were chosen based on univariate results and on variables that have been previously linked with neurological decline [31, 32]. Late neurological outcome was assessed by the modified Rankin scale (mRS, range 0–6) score measured at 90 days. A $\chi^{2}$ -test was used to assess the relationship of the spot sign to functional dependence measured by the mRS score. The GCS, NIHSS, and 90-day mRS median scores were compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test.

The association of the spot sign with the following radiological and clinical variables was examined: IVH and IPH expansion in the first $^{241}$ , hydrocephalus, and EVD placement using independent sample two-tailed T-tests for continuous variables and $\mathsf{X}^{2}$ -tests for categorical variables. The relationship of BP randomization to END and both IVH and IPH expansion was also evaluated. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at $P<0.05$ .  



# Results  



# Patient Selection and Baseline Characteristics  

From January 2011 to January 2016, a total of 1000 patients with ICH were enrolled in ATACH-II. Of those, 166 patients had a CTA available for analysis. From this subset, 57 patients $(34.3\%)$ were diagnosed with secondary IVH, 17 $(29.9\%)$ of which were spot sign-positive and 40 $(70.1\%)$ spot sign-negative (Fig. 2). All spot sign-positive patients met the first definition for spot sign, and 13 of these patients met the second definition. For all patients, the median time elapsed from the initial the NCCT to CTA was $10.5\mathrm{min}$ (interquartile range $4–241.5\mathrm{min}$ ).

# Secondary Outcome Measures  



# Radiologic Outcomes  

Mean baseline and $^{241}$ IVH volumes and IPH expansion are listed in Table 3. A total of 27 out of 57 patients $(47.4\%)$ developed IVH expansion, including 10 out of 17 $(58.8\%)$ spot sign-positive patients and 17 out of 40 $(42.5\%)$ spot sign-negative patients. A total of 16 patients developed IPH expansion, and 5 of these patients $(33.3\%)$ were spot signpositive. No significant difference was found in IVH or IPH expansion between spot sign-positive and spot sign-negative patients $\scriptstyle{\calP}=0.11$ and 0.99, respectively). Lowering BP also did not affect IVH or IPH expansion $\scriptstyle{P=0.2}$ and 0.66, respectively).  



# Clinical Outcomes  

The total number of patients presenting with hydrocephalus was 14 out of 57 $(24.6\%)$ . No significant association was found between spot sign presence and either hydrocephalus $(P=0.58)$ or the use of an EVD $(P=0.44)$ . For late neurological outcome analyses, there was a significant difference between median mRS at 90 days for spot sign-positive patients (5, interquartile range, $\mathrm{IQR}=4-6$ and spot signnegative patients (4, $\mathrm{IQR}=2.25-5$ , $P{=}0.02$ ).  



# Discussion  

The main purpose of this study was to determine the importance of the spot sign in END in patients with secondary IVH. The analysis found that in these patients the presence of a spot sign is an independent predictor of END. Late neurological outcomes, measured by median 90-day mRS scores, were different between spot sign-positive and spot sign-negative patients, which supports studies showing that patients with the spot sign have a worse long term prognosis [4, 8, 9]. No association of the spot sign with IVH expansion, IPH expansion, hydrocephalus, or EVD placement was found  

Previous literature has shown that END is linked with multiple factors in ICH, including the presence of a spot sign [4, 31, 32], presence of IVH [31, 32], IVH expansion [32], and IPH expansion [4, 12, 31–33]. To our knowledge, this is the first time that the relationship between the spot sign and END in patients with secondary IVH extension has been established in a randomized controlled clinical trial cohort. These results may have potential implications in the levels of supervision and prompt interventions in patients with secondary IVH and spot sign. Additionally, this study may provide information for future studies examining factors which contribute to END in patients with ICH.  

Interestingly, IVH expansion and hydrocephalus were not associated with END or spot sign in this study. Previous studies have shown that IVH expansion is an independent predictor of poor clinical outcomes at 90 days and increased mortality [32, 34], and hydrocephalus is an independent predictor of increased mortality in patients with ICH [35]. It is currently unclear how these two factors contribute to neurological decline in the first $^{241}$ in patients with ICH. The evidence concerning the association between IVH expansion and the spot sign is inconclusive, as some studies have found a significant association [20, 36], while others have failed to confirm it [21, 22, 25, 26]. Recent research has shown that the intracranial volume-pressure response curve is a dynamic process in which many parameters such as elastance, compliance, and medications interact simultaneously, and interpretation of hydrocephalus and its clinical outcome should be done on a patient-based level [37–39]. In this study, the relationship between the spot sign and need for an EVD is difficult to interpret since there is heterogeneity of EVD placement guidelines among different hospitals and conflicting evidence about the effect of EVD placement on prognosis in patients with ICH [35].  

Limitations of the study include small sample size, imaging protocol heterogeneity among different hospital sites, and the retrospective nature of the analysis. Additionally, there was unequal distribution in BP randomization when comparing this subset with the ATACH-II cohort, likely due to the small sample size. Loss of participant follow-up information may contribute to an attrition bias. Although the sample size was small, the multicentered nature of this study, which included various hospital settings suggests that the results could be generalizable to a larger population, but further investigation with a larger sample size is warranted.  



# Conclusion  

The presence of the spot sign is associated with END in patients with secondary IVH expansion. Identifying this radiologic feature could have important implications for triaging these patients to appropriate levels of care.
