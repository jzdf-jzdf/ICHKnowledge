Results: Of 4,722 patients screened from January 2016 to February 2018, 361 patients were included. Two hundred eighty-five $(79\%)$ received EVT, of whom $87.0\%$ had favorable CTs, $91\%$ favorable CTPs, $81\%$ both favorable profiles, $16\%$ discordant, and $3\%$ both unfavorable. Favorable profiles on the 2 modalities correlated similarly with 90-day functional independence rates (favorable $CT=56\%$ vs favorable ${\mathsf{CTP}}=57\%.$ adjusted odds ratio $[\mathsf{aOR}]=1.91$ , $95\%$ confidence interval $[\mathsf{Cl}]=0.40{-}9.01$ , $p=0.41$ ). Having a favorable profile on both modalities significantly increased the odds of receiving thrombectomy as compared to discordant profiles $(\mathsf{aOR}=3.97\$ , $95\%$ $\mathsf{Cl}=1.97{-}8.01$ , $p<0.001.$ . Fifty-eight percent of the patients with favorable profiles on both modalities achieved functional independence as compared to $38\%$ in discordant profiles and $0\%$ when both were unfavorable $(p<0.001$ for trend). In favorable CT/unfavorable CTP profiles, EVT was associated with high symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) $(24\%)$ and mortality $(53\%)$ rates.  

Interpretation: Patients with favorable imaging profiles on both modalities had higher odds of receiving EVT and high functional independence rates. Patients with discordant profiles achieved reasonable functional independence rates, but those with an unfavorable CTP had higher adverse outcomes.  

ANN NEUROL 2020;87:419–433  

he efficacy and safety of endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) due to anterior circulation large-vessel occlusion (LVO) is well established in multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs), both in the early (0–6 hours)1–5 and late treatment windows ${>}6$ hours).6,7 Simple imaging with noncontrast computed tomography of the head (CT) was the primary imaging modality used for the majority of early window trials.1,3–5 The late window trials, DEFUSE 3 (Endovascular Therapy Following Imaging Evaluation for Ischemic Stroke $3)^{6}$ and DAWN (DWI or CTP Assessment With Clinical Mismatch in the Triage of Wake-Up and Late Presenting Strokes Undergoing Neurointervention With Trevo), relied on CT perfusion (CTP) or magnetic resonance diffusion and/or perfusion, and utilized automated imaging analysis with Rapid Processing of Perfusion and Diffusion (RAPID; iSchemaView, Menlo Park, CA) software to determine eligibility. Consequently, the recent American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines reflected this time dichotomization and recommended perfusion imaging only in latewindow ${\bf\zeta}_{>}6$ hours) patients prior to thrombectomy.8  

In real-world practice, treating centers utilize different imaging selection modalities prior to thrombectomy based on their preferences and the availability of imaging technologies. Some centers triage patients solely based on CT imaging, whereas others include both CT and perfusion imaging parameters in their decision-making algorithms. In cases where both modalities are available, findings may differ between the 2 imaging studies, which can result in uncertainty regarding whether to proceed with thrombectomy. The rates of concordance and discordance between CT and CTP, the impact of their profiles on the decision to proceed to EVT, and how this may change as time elapses since last known well (LKW) are not well characterized.  

Moreover, it remains unclear if favorable profiles on CT and CTP correlate similarly with thrombectomy clinical outcomes. It is also unknown if a favorable CT profile without CTP would be sufficient to select patients for thrombectomy in the late time window. Also, many patients do not meet the imaging criteria for the RCTs that established thrombectomy efficacy and safety. Thrombectomy clinical outcomes in these patients and their correlation to pretreatment imaging findings have not been systematically evaluated.  

Because the guidelines were driven from RCTs that utilized a specific criteria on either CT or CTP, and given the lack of prospective data that compare these 2 primary imaging modalities prior to thrombectomy, the optimal imaging method for EVT patient selection remains undetermined. Hence, more data are needed to compare the accuracy of these modalities for selecting patients for EVT. Although a randomized trial would provide the ultimate evidence on the optimal imaging selection, randomization to CT only versus CT plus perfusion imaging for thrombectomy selection would be challenging to conduct without more data on the relative predictive value of favorable and unfavorable profiles of these 2 imaging modalities.  

We conducted a phase 2, nonrandomized, prospective cohort study to evaluate the concordance and discordance between CT and CTP imaging profiles and describe the association between their findings with the decision to preform thrombectomy as well as clinical outcomes. These data will be used to help design a randomized controlled trial that assesses the efficacy and safety of thrombectomy in patients with imaging profiles not tested in prior RCTs, and evaluate if there is a heterogeneity in thrombectomy treatment effect related to CT and CTP imaging profiles.  

The aims of the study were as follows  

1. To prospectively assess the concordance and discordance between noncontrast (CT) and perfusion (CTP) imaging modalities in AIS patients with anterior circulation LVO who presented up to 24 hours from LKW   
2. To describe the association between the different profiles on CT and CTP with the treatment decision for thrombectomy versus medical management only.   
3. To evaluate the correlation between the concordant and discordant profiles on CT and CTP with the clinical outcomes following thrombectomy  

4. To assess the effect of treatment time windows, early (0–6 hours) and late ${\bf\zeta}_{>0}$ hours) from LKW to groin puncture on outcomes among different imaging profiles  

# Patients and Methods  

# Study Design and Participants  

SELECT (Optimizing Patient’s Selection for Endovascular Treatment in Acute Ischemic Stroke) was a multicenter, prospective cohort study that enrolled AIS patients with anterior circulation LVO who presented up to 24 hours from LKW at 9 US study sites from January 2016 to February 2018. All patients were adults ${\geq}18$ years old with AIS with National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score $\geq6$ at presentation due to LVO on CT angiography (CTA) in anterior circulation (internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery: M1/M2 segments), that were normal or with minimal deficits at baseline (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] 0–1). Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are included in Table 1. All patients received a unified prethrombectomy imaging protocol with CT, CTA, and CTP with mismatch determination using RAPID software. Favorable imaging profiles were defined a priori and provided to the treating physicians. The decision to proceed with thrombectomy or medical management only was not randomized and left to the discretion of the treating physician. The initial enrollment window was 0 to 8 hours from LKW to groin puncture for EVT patients and LKW to emergency room (ER) arrival for medical management only; this window was extended to up to 24 hours after results of the DAWN7 study were presented in May 2017. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at each participating site prior to the start of the study and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02446587). All study participants or their legally authorized representatives provided informed written consent in accordance with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulations 21 CFR Parts $50.20~-$ 50.27 and the current version of the Declaration of Helsinki prior to data entry into the SELECT database. The subject or their legal representative was provided a copy of the signed informed consent document.

# Imaging Analysis  

An independent neuroimaging core laboratory evaluated all imaging (CT, CTA, CTP, cerebral angiogram, magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) blinded to clinical outcomes and enrollment site. The core laboratory consisted of 2 neuroradiologist readers, and when in disagreement, a consensus was reached by the core laboratory readers. For every enrolled patient, the imaging evaluation included preprocedure CT Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score (ASPECTS), CTA collateral score,9 modified collateral score,10 capillary index score,11 CTP (cerebral blood flow [CBF], cerebral blood volume, time to the peak of the residual function [Tmax]), CTA ASPECTS,12 modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction $\mathrm{(mTICI)}^{13}$ on cerebral angiogram, and postprocedure either CT or MRI up to 7 days. The prespecified favorable profiles were CT: ASPECTS ${\ge}6$ and CTP (1) core volume measured on regional CBF $(<30\%)$ of ${<}70\mathrm{ml}$ and (2) ratio between the critically hypoperfused tissue (Tmax ${>}6$ seconds) and ischemic core volume on CBF $\geq1.2$ with an absolute difference of ${\geq}10\mathrm{ml},$ .2 The definition of favorable profile on CTP was obtained from EXTEND-IA (Extending the Time for Thrombolysis in Emergency Neurological Deficits–Intra-Arterial),2 which represented the highest-level randomized data regarding CTP criteria for EVT at the time of the SELECT study design. A sensitivity analysis using mismatch definitions from DEFUSE $3^{6}$ (mismatch volume $\geq15\mathrm{ml}$ and mismatch ratio $\geq1.8$ ) was also performed. Final infarct volume was measured on postprocedure diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences using manual segmentation of the region of interest. If postprocedure MRI was not available, final infarct volume was measured on noncontrast CT.  

# CTP Acquisition Protocol  

CTP protocols for each site were adjusted to harmonize acquisition parameters. Criteria were brain coverage of at least 8cm z-axis, temporal sampling resolution no more than 1.8 seconds, tube voltage $580\mathrm{kVp}$ , volume CT dose index ${<}360\mathrm{mGy}$ , scan duration between 60 and 90 seconds, high iodine concentration contrast agent (eg, Omnipaque 350 [GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL] or Isovue 370 [Bracco Diagnostics, Milan, Italy]), injection flow rate between 4 and $6\mathrm{ml}/\mathrm{s}$ , and amount of contrast injected between 40 and $50\mathrm{ml}$ , with no scan delay after bolus injection. Allowed scan modes used were: burst mode (GE [Munich, Germany], Toshiba [Tokyo, Japan], Philips [Best, the Netherlands]), jog mode (Philips), or dynamic helical shuttle (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). For CTs with a detector width $<8\mathrm{cm}$ , either $2\mathrm{CT}$ perfusion runs or dynamic helical shuttle mode were required. Iterative reconstruction methods were avoided to reduce variability between vendors.  

# Study Treatments and Procedures  

Although the definitions of favorable imaging profiles were provided to the physicians, the decision to proceed with thrombectomy plus medical management (MM) or MM alone were not mandated by those profiles. Results of all imaging studies were available to the treating physicians. EVT primarily included the use of stent retrievers; adjunctive use of aspiration was allowed. Each site was allowed to employ their thrombectomy procedure practice for femoral access, sedation, and cardiovascular monitoring. Standard AHA guidelines8 directed medical management was provided to all patients.  

# Outcomes  

The primary outcome was a 90-day functional independence rate $(\mathrm{mRS~}=~0{-}2)$ . Secondary outcome measures included infarct volume on follow-up DWI or CT if follow-up MRI was not performed up to 7 days after stroke onset, rates of recanalization (complete and partial)  

using the mTICI system (score $\geq2\mathrm{b}$ defined as favorable reperfusion),13 safety defined as symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) by SITS-MOST (Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke-Monitoring Study) and ECASS II (European Co-operative Acute Stroke StudyII)15 criteria, neurological worsening $(a\geq4$ -point increase on the NIHSS score up to 24 hours due to the stroke), mortality, and procedural complications including groin (or arterial access site) hemorrhage, infections, or vascular injury.  

The determination of mRS scores was performed either in person or by phone by certified raters unaware of the imaging profiles of the individual patient.  

Certified raters who were blinded to the treatment allocation and imaging results obtained the mRS score at discharge and 90 days. The scores were obtained either in person (the preferred method) or over the phone using a structured questionnaire that was provided to the sites and is included in the Supplementary Figure.  

# Statistical Analysis  

We assessed the concordance and discordance (favorable on 1 modality but unfavorable on the other) between CT and CTP imaging profiles as defined by the core laboratory interpretation. Baseline characteristics were described among these 4 groups. Univariate comparisons were made using Pearson $\chi^{2}$ or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, and Student $t$ test or Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables, where appropriate. Interrater and intrarater reliability between the core laboratory readers was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficient.  

Marginal probabilities were calculated at mean age and NIHSS to identify the likelihood of receiving thrombectomy as related to the different imaging profiles. Because we observed variability in treatment patterns between centers based on imaging profiles, mixed-effects logistic regression models were used, considering centers as the random effects. Additionally, the likelihood of receiving thrombectomy in patients with favorable and unfavorable CT and CTP profiles was calculated based on the changes in the ischemic core volume on CTP, CT ASPECTS, and time. Missing 90-day follow-up data were imputed using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method as specified in the statistical analysis protocol. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to compare the functional independence rate between the imaging profiles on CT and CTP after adjusting for predefined variables (age, NIHSS at presentation, serum glucose at presentation, clot location, intravenous tissue plasminogen activator pretreatment, time from LKW to groin puncture, and transfer status). Adjusted odds ratio (aOR) along with its $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) were reported. Due to the limited number of safety events, only NIHSS score at presentation was adjusted for when using logistic regression models to compare safety outcomes in patients with discordant profiles. As 2 prior trials showing thrombectomy efficacy and safety in early5 and late6 time windows used a mismatch ratio of 1.8 and mismatch volume of $15\mathrm{ml}$ as thresholds, whereas another2 implemented a mismatch ratio of 1.2 and mismatch volume of $10\mathrm{ml}$ , similar to our definition in the SELECT analyses, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using these thresholds from the DEFUSE $3^{6}$ to define an unfavorable CTP profile. Outcomes were compared between concordance and discordance groups obtained by using these thresholds.

# Results  

# Patients Cohort Overview  

A total of 4,722 patients were screened between January 2016 and February 2018; 4277 were deemed as ineligible and 445 patients met criteria described in Table 1 and were consented. Of the 445 consented patients, 59 patients were excluded from the EVT group and 25 from the MM group due to missing CTA or CTP imaging, technically inadequate perfusion imaging or CT angiograms, baseline $\mathrm{mRS}>1$ , primary use of aspiration, NIHSS $^{<6}$ at presentation, and incomplete or distal vessel occlusion. Three hundred sixty-one $\mathrm{(EVT}=285\$ and ${\mathrm{MM}}=76) $ ) met the inclusion criteria and entered the analyses. Complete 90-day follow-up was available for 341 $(94.5\%)$ patients. For 20 $(5.5\%)$ patients, analysis was completed using LOCF with discharge mRS as specified in the statistical analysis protocol. Figure 1 shows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram of the screened, enrolled, and analyzed patients.  

# Imaging Profiles on CT and CT Perfusion with Their Concordance and Discordance in Patients Treated with Thrombectomy  

Among EVT patients, 223 $(78\%)$ were treated in the early window (0–6 hours) and 62 $(22\%)$ beyond 6 hours. Median (interquartile range [IQR]) age was 67 years $(57-$ 77 years) and 62.5 years (50–72 years) for early and late treatment windows $\left(\phi=0.08\right)$ , respectively. Forty-six percent of early-window patients and $58\%$ of late-window patients were females $(\rho=0.10)$ . Stroke severity as measured by NIHSS score at presentation was similar: median $(\mathrm{IQR})\:=\:17\$ (12–21) and 15.5 (10–20), $p=0.12$ for earlyand late-window patients, respectively. Last seen well to procedure times were median (IQR) 199 (139–260), range $=69$ to 359 and 429 (380-502), range $=361$ to 1,056 minutes, early and late time windows, respectively $(\rho<0.001)$ .  

Core laboratory analysis revealed that $87\%$ of the thrombectomy patients had a favorable CT (early: $88\%$ and late: $82\%$ ) as compared to $91\%$ with a favorable CTP $(91\%$ and $92\%$ , early and late windows, respectively).  

In $81\%$ $\mathbf{\delta}(\mathbf{n}~=~231$ ) of the thrombectomy patients, there was an agreement between CT and CTP profiles with both having a favorable profile, $3\%$ $(\mathtt{n}=9)$ ) had unfavorable profiles on both modalities, and $16\%$ $(\mathrm{n~=~45})$ ) had a discordant profile (Fig 2). In patients treated with MM alone, 22 $(29\%)$ had a discordant profile.  

Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of the core volumes in each ASPECTS category showing a higher degree of discordance with lower ASPECTS and larger perfusion core volumes.

Supplementary Table 2 details the baseline characteristics of the 4 different imaging profiles based on concordance and discordance. Besides the expected differences in CT ASPECTS and CTP volume, unfavorable CT patients had longer times from LKW to ER arrival, whereas NIHSS scores were higher in those with unfavorable CTP.  

Sensitivity analysis using a mismatch ratio of $<1.8$ and mismatch volume of $<15\mathrm{ml}$ to define an unfavorable CTP resulted in 3 additional patients being classified as unfavorable, 2 of those had a favorable CT and 1 had an unfavorable CT as well. # Discussion  

In the SELECT patients who received thrombectomy, there was a high rate of favorable imaging profiles on both CT and CTP. This was true in both the early and late time windows. Favorable imaging on both modalities likely influenced the decision to proceed with thrombectomy, and these patients had a high rate of favorable outcomes.  

In spite of the high concordance rates, there was, however, almost $20\%$ of the patients where CT and CTP had discordant profiles. Our data suggested that the imaging results of both modalities may have affected thrombectomy treatment decisions. Although multiple additional clinical variables may contribute to the treatment decision whether to proceed with thrombectomy, our results showed that having an unfavorable profile on either modality significantly reduced the likelihood of performing thrombectomy. The likelihood of receiving thrombectomy in patients with a favorable CT profile were 6 times higher if CTP was favorable compared to unfavorable CTP. Similarly, in patients with a favorable CTP profile, having a favorable CT profile increased the likelihood of thrombectomy by 4-fold. The impact of perfusion imaging findings on thrombectomy decisions were more apparent in the late window, which could be a due to the current available evidence of thrombectomy efficacy beyond 6 hours being based on trials that relied on perfusion imaging.6,7  

Prior attempts to assess the correlation between thrombectomy outcomes and the imaging modalities utilized for triage were limited to single-center retrospective analyses16–18 and had conflicting findings. Some studies demonstrated better thrombectomy outcomes with CTPbased selection,16,17 whereas others showed similar outcomes and a delay in treatment with perfusion imaging acquisition.18 These results are limited by the retrospective design with baseline imbalances, selection and stratification biases, and the lack of prespecified imaging protocols. These studies included only EVT patients and did not assess the difference in treatment decisions based on imaging findings. Thus, a systematic and prospective evaluation of imaging modalities and the association of their profiles with thrombectomy treatment decisions and outcomes has not been performed before.  

In regard to clinical outcomes, favorable profiles on CT and CTP correlated similarly with functional independence rates following EVT. We examined if these findings would differ between those who were treated early versus late. Similar outcomes were seen between favorable CT and favorable CTP when the results were dichotomized by time window. The high overlap between the favorable profiles on CT and CTP probably explains the similar outcomes when the 2 modalities are compared. Because both imaging studies were available to the physicians, it is possible that findings on both modalities may have affected treatment decisions.  

We observed high rates of functional independence in patients with favorable profiles on both CT and CTP in SELECT, which is consistent with results from RCTs that utilized perfusion imaging–based selection in early2,5 and late6,7 windows. Although favorable profiles on both CT and CTP correlated with the highest odds of achieving functional independence, EVT outcomes in patients with discordant profiles were still reasonable, as $38\%$ of these patients achieved functional independence with thrombectomy. These rates are comparable to data from prior trials.1,3,4 Hence, our data suggest that relying on either imaging alone could lead to excluding some patients who might benefit from EVT. Among patients who had an unfavorable CT and might have not been treated with EVT, a favorable CTP profile may have led physicians to make the decision to treat these patients. Ten percent of our patients were in this category, and the rate of functional independence in this group approached $50\%$ after EVT, which is comparable to the rate seen in patients with favorable profiles on both CT and CTP. Basing the decision solely on CTP, however, could result in missing patients who may benefit from EVT. Six percent of the study’s cohort might have been excluded from EVT based on their unfavorable CTP profile but were likely treated in SELECT because of their favorable CT profile. Twenty-four percent of these achieved good outcome with thrombectomy as compared to none who had similar imaging profiles and received only MM. Furthermore, successful reperfusion in those with discordant profiles was associated with higher rates of functional independence, but this did not reach statistical significance. These findings raise the possibility that the number of successfully treated patients might be maximized if both imaging modalities are used and patients are treated if either modality shows a favorable profile, and highlight the need for randomized data in patients with disagreement between the 2 modalities to evaluate thrombectomy safety and efficacy.  

Among the discordant patients, a favorable versus unfavorable CTP demonstrated a stronger association with good versus poor outcomes after EVT than a favorable versus unfavorable CT. However, because these patients were not randomized to a control group, the actual benefit and risks of EVT cannot be determined. Also, it is notable that patients with an unfavorable CTP profile and a favorable CT profile who were treated with EVT had high rates of mortality, sICH, and neurological worsening. Prior studies also reported similar findings with reperfusion in this population.19,20 This emphasizes that the benefit versus risk of thrombectomy in this population needs to be tested by further prospective randomized data comparing EVT to MM. A limited number of patients with an unfavorable profile on both CT and CTP received EVT in the study cohort. There are no data regarding the efficacy and safety of thrombectomy in these patients, and thrombectomy is not recommended for these patients in current guidelines. The ongoing SELECT $2^{21}$ trial will assess the efficacy and safety of endovascular thrombectomy in this patient cohort.  

Our data are reflective of real-world practice. Some practices utilize restrictive inclusion criteria for EVT to exclude patients at higher risk of reperfusion injury, whereas others triage patients with more inclusive criteria allowing intervention on more patients while acknowledging the risk of potential complications. Both of these practices are well represented in our cohort by the concordant and discordant subpopulations, respectively. There were differences in the imaging profiles of patients treated with EVT. The proportion of EVT patients having both a favorable CT and a favorable CTP profile ranged from $58\%$ to $100\%$ , demonstrating heterogeneity in treatment patterns between the sites. This reflects real-life clinical practice and the pragmatic design of the study, which provided the definition of the favorable and unfavorable profiles without mandating the treatment decision. We accounted for this heterogeneity in treatment patterns by utilizing mixed-effects models to assess the relationship between imaging profiles and treatment decisions.  

We observed high rates of concordance between favorable CT and CTP profiles in patients treated with thrombectomy. Although patients with discordant imaging profiles accounted for $16\%$ of thrombectomy-treated patients, the discordance rate was higher $(29\%)$ in the group that received MM only. This observation is likely a result of individualized patient selection for thrombectomy, as there are minimal data supporting EVT efficacy and safety in patients with discordant profiles. The rate of discordance encountered in clinical practice is likely higher, and the ongoing SELECT $2^{21}$ trial is capturing these patients and will assess the efficacy and safety of EVT in this population.  

The 2 modalities measure different physiological parameters. CT measures tissue hypodensities,22 whereas perfusion imaging measures the CBF parameters.23 Although we found a high level of agreement on favorable profiles, the core volumes varied within the same ASPECTS with higher discordance rates seen with lower ASPECTS and larger perfusion core volumes. ASPECTS represent the so-called large-core subpopulation. These patients were largely excluded from prior trials in both early and late windows. We previously reported that EVT may result in better functional independence in these patients with decreasing likelihood of benefit as stroke size increases and time progresses. In a recent HERMES (Highly Effective Reperfusion evaluated in Multiple Endovascular Stroke Trials) meta-analysis assessing functional and safety outcomes based on baseline CT and MRI assessment, a significant improvement in functional outcomes was shown in patients with ASPECTS 3 to 5; however, an increase in symptomatic ICH was also found.25 Another HERMES meta-analysis demonstrated improvement in outcomes with thrombectomy in patients with an ischemic core volume $>70\mathrm{ml}$ .26 However, there were imbalances in baseline characteristics that could not be adjusted for due to the limited sample size. Therefore, randomized data from large core patients are also necessary to clarify the benefit and risks of EVT.  

In spite of the significant recent advances in the evidence supporting EVT for acute ischemic strokes, there are still important remaining questions. Whether there is a significant difference in EVT outcomes and treatment effect based on the imaging profiles on CT versus perfusion images, and if EVT is safe and efficacious in large core infarcts on either modality are yet to be determined. We have used the results from SELECT24 to inform the design of SELECT $2^{21}$ ; a phase 3, multicenter, prospective, randomized, open-label, blinded, endpoint assessment clinical trial assessing EVT efficacy and safety in patients with unfavorable profiles on CT and/or CTP images and assess if there was a heterogeneity of EVT treatment effect in patients with different imaging profiles.  

The study is limited by the lack of randomization in the selection paradigm by CT versus CTP. Although we adjusted for study site, there are likely biases in favor of 1 modality versus another between individual physician or study sites. Furthermore, the assignment of EVT versus MM was not randomized, and it is quite possible that some of the patients who were not treated with EVT had unfavorable clinical characteristics that contributed to the decision not to proceed with thrombectomy given the unfavorable prognosis. We focused our assessment on CT and CTP, whereas some centers utilize MRI-based thrombectomy patient selection.2 7 We expanded our inclusion criteria from a 0- to 8-hour treatment time window to 0 to 24 hours after DAWN results were published,7 which resulted in more early- than latewindow patients. We also acknowledge that intraobserver and interobserver agreement with ASPECT scores are variable.28,29 Although we used mismatch criteria from EXTEND-IA2 trial, SWIFT-PRIME (Solitaire with the  

Intention for Thrombectomy as Primary Endovascular Treatment)5 in the early window, and DEFUSE $3^{7}$ in the late time window used cutoffs of $15\mathrm{ml}$ for mismatch volume and 1.8 for mismatch ratio to define a favorable profile. However, changing these criteria did not alter the patient selection in the study cohort significantly, as shown by the sensitivity analysis. The interrater agreement was moderate in our core laboratory, and the intrarater agreement was good. A very limited number of the thrombectomy patients had unfavorable CTP in our latewindow cohort, suggesting physicians were reluctant to treat these patients with EVT. The high concordance rate between a favorable CT and favorable CTP is likely due to patient selection for thrombectomy; this concordance rate is likely lower in an unselected population. In addition, some of the subgroups evaluated, especially patients with an unfavorable CT and an unfavorable CTP profile, have a small sample sizes; therefore, estimates of event rates may be imprecise, and firm conclusions cannot be drawn from these findings.  

In the SELECT patients who received thrombectomy, there was a high rate of favorable imaging profiles on both CT and CTP. Favorable imaging on both modalities likely influenced the decision to proceed with thrombectomy, and these patients had a high rate of favorable outcomes. In cases with unfavorable profile on either modality, having a favorable profile on the other significantly increases the probability of a treatment decision to proceed with thrombectomy. Patients with discordant imaging had lower rates of functional independence following EVT; however, these rates are potentially more favorable than patients who are treated with MM alone. In the discordant profile patients, those with an unfavorable CTP had high adverse-outcomes incidences following thrombectomy. As these discordant patients typically had large ischemic core lesions, RCTs are necessary to assess the safety and efficacy of EVT in patients with large baseline ischemic core lesions on either CT or CTP.
