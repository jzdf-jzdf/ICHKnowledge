# Tirofiban vs. aspirin in patients with acute ischemic stroke: A meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials

# ABSTRACT  

Keywords: Stroke Tirofiban Aspirin  

Background and objectives: Antiplatelet therapy is recommended as the standard treatment for patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) who, for several reasons, did not receive thrombolysis or thrombectomy. However, whether tirofiban or aspirin provides greater benefits for these patients remains unclear. Therefore, we aimed to perform a meta-analysis comparing the functional outcomes and hemorrhagic risks associated with tirofiban and aspirin in the management of AIS.  

Methods: We searched PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library for studies comparing tirofiban to aspirin in patients with AIS who did not receive thrombolysis or thrombectomy until September 2024. Out­ comes were modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and mortality at 90 days, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, and any bleeding events. Statistical analysis was performed using the R Studio (version $2024.04.1\substack{+748)}$ .  

Results: We included 3 randomized controlled trials with a total of 1959 patients, of whom 996 $(50.8\%)$ were in the tirofiban group. Excellent $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}1)$ ) functional outcome (RR 1.25, $95~\%$ CI: 1.05–1.49; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=70\%)$ and favorable $(\mathrm{mRS}0{-}2)$ functional outcome at 90 days (RR 1.09, $95~\%$ CI: 1.01–1.16; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=35\%_{-}$ were significantly higher in tirofiban compared to aspirin. Furthermore, tirofiban showed no difference in mortality (RR 0.77, $95~\%$ CI: 0.24–2.53; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=56\%^{\cdot}$ ), or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (RR 3.42, $95~\%$ CI: $0.27\substack{-43.30}$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=38\%_{\cdot}$ . However, any bleeding event (RR 1.75, $95~\%$ CI: 1.25–2.45; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ) was more common in the tirofiban group. Lastly, the meta-regression analysis showed that the outcomes were not influenced by the initial NIHSS of the included studies $(p>0.05)$ .  

Conclusion: Tirofiban is associated with better functional outcomes at 90 days, with no difference in mortality. Additionally, despite being associated with higher bleeding events, there is no difference in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. Therefore, our results suggest that tirofiban is a promising alternative to aspirin.  

# 1. Introduction  

Globally, stroke is the second leading cause of mortality, and the third cause of combined death and disability [1]. In 2019, acute ischemic stroke (AIS) accounted for $62.4~\%$ of all stroke cases [2], with around 3.15 million deaths annually, and 62.53 million disability-adjusted life years reported in 2020 [3]. Despite the severity of the condition, many patients arrive late after symptom onset, missing the window for thrombolysis, and mechanical thrombectomy is often unavailable [4].  

For those patients, antiplatelet drugs can be used to prevent further neurological damage, effectively lowering the risk of subsequent unfa­ vorable functional outcomes and long-term mortality [5,6]. Both tir­ ofiban and aspirin (ASA) are safe and effective for improving long-term outcomes after a stroke [6,7], reducing the risk of new blood clots that could obstruct blood flow to the brain, and potentially preventing more severe stroke-related damage.  

While several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared the efficacy of tirofiban and ASA in stroke management and had results favoring tirofiban, a comprehensive meta-analysis synthesizing the available evidence has not yet been conducted. Therefore, we aimed to perform a meta-analysis of RCTs to evaluate the long-term outcomes of patients treated with tirofiban versus aspirin following AIS.  

# 2. Methods  

This meta-analysis was conducted following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [8] guide­ lines and the recommended protocols by the Cochrane Collaboration [9].  

# 2.1. Search strategy  

Our investigation involved a search of the PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases until September 2024. The search strategy was created with the terms ‘tirofiban’, ‘glycoprotein IIb/ IIIa’, ‘aspirin’, ‘acetylsalicylic acid’, and ‘stroke’. A prospective regis­ tration was not performed for this systematic review.  

# 2.2. Outcomes definitions  

Our primary outcomes assessed functional recovery at 90 days, categorized as either excellent or favorable. An excellent outcome was defined by a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0–1, reflecting full proficiency in carrying out routine tasks and responsibilities at 90 days. A favorable outcome was defined by an mRS score of 0–2, indicating self-sufficiency and independence in daily activities.  

Additionally, our safety outcomes included symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (SIH), evaluated using the ECASS III study criteria or the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification, any bleeding event, and 90-day mortality.  

# 2.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria  

The inclusion criteria for this systematic review were as follows: (1) randomized clinical trials; (2) comparing tirofiban to aspirin in patients with ischemic stroke who did not receive either thrombolysis or thrombectomy; (3) sample size of at least 50 individuals in each group; and (4) primary outcome reported for both groups.  

Conversely, the subsequent exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cohorts, reviews, letters, meta-analyses, case reports, conference ab­ stracts, or comments; (2) full-text not available; and (3) lack of primary outcome information or unclear outcome descriptions.  

# 2.4. Data extraction  

Following de-duplication, the studies were uploaded to the Rayyan systematic review platform [10]. Two authors (M.P.R.O. and D.C.R.S.) independently screened the titles and abstracts from the search results to identify potentially relevant studies. This initial screening was followed by a full-text review of the potentially eligible studies. Any disagree­ ments were resolved through discussions with a third author (P.H.F.S.), ensuring consensus.  

Data extraction was performed by a team of two authors (M.P.R.O. and G.T.O.P.), which included information as follows: (1) publication details (e.g. first author, publication year); (2) country; (3) sample size; (4) baseline characteristics of the patients (e.g. age, gender); (5) etiol­ ogy; and (6) outcomes.  

# 2.5. Statistical analysis  

The statistical analysis was conducted in R Studio (version $2024.04.1\substack{+748)}$ . We utilized risk ratio (RR), mean difference (MD), and $95~\%$ confidence intervals (CI) to compare treatment effects. Heteroge­ neity was assessed using the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ statistics, with an $\mathrm{I}^{2}>50\%$ indicative of high heterogeneity. A random effects model, according to the Der Simonian-Laird method, was applied to estimate the variability among studies. Publication bias was analyzed using the Egger’s test alongside a funnel plot analysis (Supplementary Content 1). Lastly, meta-regression was used to investigate the relationship between the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and statistically significant outcomes (Supplementary Content 2).  

# 2.6. Risk of bias  

The risk of bias was evaluated using the Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2). Two authors (P.H.F.S. and G.T.O.P.) assessed the quality of the studies. Any discrepancies in their evaluations were resolved through mutual agreement.  

# 2.7. Certainty of evidence  

We utilized the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Devel­ opment and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to evaluate the certainty of evidence for each outcome in this meta-analysis. GRADE provides a systematic framework to assess the quality of evidence based on key domains such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Each domain contributes to the overall confidence in the effect estimates, categorizing the certainty as high, moderate, low, or very low. Since all the studies included in our analysis were ran­ domized trials, we initially rated the certainty as high and adjusted it accordingly, based on identified issues or strengths within these do­ mains (Supplemental Digital Content 3).  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Study selection  

Initially, 812 articles were identified: 170 from PubMed, 325 from EMBASE, 227 from Web of Science, and 90 from Cochrane Library. After de-duplication, 535 unique articles remained for preliminary screening. Of these, 527 were excluded based on title or abstract assessment, leaving 8 articles for full-text review. Ultimately, 3 randomized controlled trials involving 1959 patients were included in the qualitative and quantitative analysis. The reasons for excluding the remaining ar­ ticles are detailed in Fig. 1.  

# 3.2. Baseline characteristics of included studies  

A total of 1959 patients were included, with 996 $(50.8\%)$ in the tirofiban group and 963 $(49.2\%)$ in the aspirin group. The most com­ mon stroke etiologies were small vessel occlusion $(56.6~\%)$ and large artery atherosclerosis $(20.5\%)$ . Additional details about the baseline characteristics of each study are summarized in Table 1. At admission, hypertension and diabetes were the most frequent comorbidities, ac­ counting for $64.9\%$ (1272/1959) and $29.2\%$ (572/1959) of patients, respectively. Further information on the clinical characteristics of each study is summarized in Table 2.  

# 3.3. Excellent functional outcome  

Information over excellent functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}0{-}1)$ at 90 days was obtainable from 919 patients receiving tirofiban and 886 patients receiving aspirin. After analysis, tirofiban showed significantly higher excellent functional outcome than aspirin (RR 1.25, $95~\%$ CI: 1.05–1.49; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=70\%)$ , as illustrated in Fig. 2.  

The certainty of evidence was downgraded to moderate, mainly due to high heterogeneity (inconsistency). Moreover, meta-regression revealed a weak positive trend favoring tirofiban with increasing NIHSS $(\upbeta=0.0193)$ , but this association was not statistically significant $\left(\mathbf{p}=0.8037\right)$ .

# 3.4. Favorable functional outcome  

Data regarding favorable functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}0{-}2)$ at 90 days was available from 993 patients receiving tirofiban and 959 patients receiving aspirin. After analysis, tirofiban showed significantly higher favorable functional outcome than aspirin (RR 1.09, $95~\%$ CI: 1.01–1.16; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=35\%)$ ), as illustrated in Fig. 3.  

The certainty of evidence was downgraded to moderate, primarily due to publication bias. Furthermore, meta-regression indicated a weak positive trend favoring tirofiban with increasing NIHSS $(\upbeta=0.0068)$ , although the association did not reach statistical significance $\mathbf{\rho}(\mathbf{p_{\rho}}=$ 0.8316).  

# 3.5. Mortality  

Information about mortality was obtainable from 1007 patients receiving tirofiban and 945 patients receiving aspirin. However, no significant difference was found between groups (RR 0.77, $95~\%$ CI: 0.24–2.53; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=56\mathrm{~\%~}$ ), as illustrated in Fig. 4.  

The certainty of evidence was downgraded to moderate, essentially due to high heterogeneity and a large $95~\%$ confidence interval (imprecision).  

# 3.6. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage  

Data regarding symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was available from 993 patients receiving tirofiban and 959 patients receiving aspirin. However, no significant difference was found between groups (RR 3.42, $95~\%$ CI: 0.27–43.30; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=38\%^{\cdot}$ ), as illustrated in Fig. 5.  

The certainty of evidence was downgraded to moderate, mostly due to a very large $95~\%$ confidence interval.

# 4. Discussion  

As summarized in Table 3, our findings demonstrated that the use of tirofiban is associated with a higher excellent $(\mathrm{mRS}0{-}1)$ and favorable $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2)$ functional outcomes when compared to aspirin. However, our meta-analysis also suggests that tirofiban is associated with higher occurrence rates of any type bleeding event, with no difference regarding symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. Similarly, we found no difference in mortality rates across groups.  

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to directly compare the effectiveness of tirofiban and aspirin in the treatment of acute stroke in patients ineligible for intravenous throm­ bolysis or endovascular therapy. While prior meta-analyses have eval­ uated the effectiveness of tirofiban across various scenarios, some suggesting potential benefits for functional outcomes without significant bleeding risks, these analyses were limited. Many included observa­ tional studies failed to control key covariables, and incorporated het­ erogeneous study designs, reducing their conclusions’ strength and generalizability [14,15].

The first randomized controlled trial to evaluate the use of tirofiban versus aspirin in patients ineligible for other reperfusion therapies was the ESCAPIST Trial (Efficacy and Safety of tirofiban in Clinical Patients With Acute Ischemic Stroke). The ESCAPIST trial demonstrated that tirofiban was superior to aspirin in achieving excellent functional out­ comes and reducing mortality rates while showing no significant dif­ ference in bleeding rates.  

The treatment of AIS has seen significant advancements, both in the development of fibrinolytic therapies and the applicability of endovas­ cular treatments, fundamentally altering the prognosis for many stroke patients [16]. However, these therapies are only suitable for some in­ dividuals affected by the condition, leaving some patients without ac­ cess to these interventions. In this context, for patients who are not eligible for any acute reperfusion therapy, the use of the antiplatelet drug is preconized both as single or dual antiplatelet agents (DAPT).  

The beneficial effect of tirofiban on functional outcomes was observed in two of the included studies, RESCUE BT2 and ESCAPIST. However, the TREND study did not demonstrate a significant difference between the aspirin and tirofiban groups in terms of functional out­ comes. The primary objective assessed in the TREND study was neuro­ logical deterioration, in which the tirofiban group showed an advantage. The absence of statistical significance in functional outcomes in TREND could be due to low statistical power, as the sample size calculation was based on neurological deterioration rather than mRS, unlike RESCUE BT2 and ESCAPIST. The heterogeneity in the assessed functional out­ comes can be partly attributed to variations in inclusion criteria across the studies, such as differences in the minimum and maximum NIHSS scores at admission. For instance, the RESCUE-BT trial does not impose an upper NIHSS limit, leading to the inclusion of more severe cases in the analysis. Additionally, variations in baseline characteristics may contribute to the observed heterogeneity, as the prevalence of small vessel etiology is notably higher in RESCUE-BT compared to the other studies.  

Although the overall rate of bleeding events (regardless of clinical significance) was higher in the tirofiban group, no significant difference was observed in SIH. There are limitations in assessing bleeding out­ comes, as some intracranial bleeds may be asymptomatic, and the absence of control tomography could have led to an underestimation of the actual bleeding rate. Nevertheless, clinicians should be aware that tirofiban use is associated with higher rates of bleeding. However, the clinical impact of these bleeding events is often minimal, and the ben­ efits of tirofiban on functional outcomes may outweigh the risks. Clinical decisions should be individualized, carefully weighing the benefit-risk profile, particularly in patients with a high risk of bleeding, as the elevated bleeding risk can lead to significant or potentially lifethreatening complications. The absence of differences in SIH – including zero events in both groups in one study (TREND) – may serve as a positive indicator of the safety of tirofiban therapy during the acute phase of stroke. Similarly to our findings, Liu et al. [14] reported no difference in intracranial bleeding between groups in patients who un­ derwent thrombolysis, with or without subsequent endovascular treatment.  

Furthermore, in the meta-regression analysis, using NIHSS at admission as an independent factor to assess its impact on functional outcomes, we found no statistically significant association. This result offers an important perspective, indicating that regardless of NIHSS at admission, tirofiban remained significant in promoting functional re­ covery in patients receiving this treatment. Nonetheless, the primary limitation of this analysis is the characteristics of the studies pop­ ulations, which did not include more severe strokes.  

The cost of tirofiban treatment is often a significant concern, as the price of aspirin is considerably lower, making tirofiban potentially a more expensive option for stroke management. However, a recent study utilizing a Markov modeling analysis, primarily based on the results from the RESCUE BT2 trial, demonstrated that tirofiban therapy is costeffective, hence tirofiban was found to be superior to aspirin in terms of long-term costs, underscoring its potential economic viability despite the higher initial expense [17].  

# 4.1. Limitations  

Our study has some limitations. First, the time periods of inclusion in the RCTs analyzed vary $(12–24\mathrm{h})$ , which may affect the interpretation of our results. However, the authors believe that the time windows defined in these studies are sufficiently similar to offer a meaningful understanding of tirofiban’s use in the acute stroke setting. Secondly, the geographical limitation of the included studies, all of which were conducted in China, affects the generalizability of the findings. The population characteristics may not represent those of other regions, and the higher prevalence of atherosclerosis as a leading cause of stroke in China may influence the interpretation of outcomes, potentially limiting their applicability to stroke cases with different etiologies in other populations. Additionally, the TREND study excluded patients with cardioembolic stroke, which also limits the generalizability of these findings to all stroke etiologies. Moreover, no publication bias was identified in most outcomes, except for the 90-day favorable mRS, which should be interpreted with caution. Given the small number of studies in this analysis and the fact that similar outcomes did not exhibit the same pattern, the authors believe this may represent a spurious association, acknowledging the limitations of Egger’s test regression.  

# 5. Conclusion  

Our meta-analysis showed higher excellent and favorable functional outcomes associated with the administration of tirofiban in acute ischemic stroke patients when compared to the well-established tradi­ tional treatment with aspirin. As for the risks revolving the therapies, although tirofiban demonstrated a higher risk of bleeding of any type, its risk of SIH is no different from aspirin, as well as the mortality rate. These findings may lead to new transforming possibilities around the management of stroke episodes, especially as an outcome-modifying treatment for contexts in which known reperfusion therapies are limited. Therefore, further controlled randomized trials in other populations with different particularities are necessary to confirm our results and more clearly define the treatment use.
