# Optimum Baseline Clinical Severity Scale Cut Points for Prognosticating Intracerebral Hemorrhage: INTERACT Studies  

Shoujiang You , MD, PhD\*; Danni Zheng, BPharm, PhD\*; Sohei Yoshimura, MD, PhD; Menglu Ouyang, MPH, PhD; Qiao Han, MD; Xia Wang, PhD; Yongjun Cao, MD, PhD; Candice Delcourt, MD, PhD; Lili Song, MD, PhD; Hisatomi Arima, MD, PhD; Xiaoying Chen, PhD; Chun-Feng Liu, MD, PhD; Richard I. Lindley, MD; Thompson Robinson, MD; Craig S. Anderson, MD, PhD; John Chalmers, MD, PhD; for the INTERACT Investigators  

BACKGROUND: The optimal cut point of baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and Glasgow Coma Scale scores for prognosticating acute intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is unknown.  

METHODS: Secondary analyses of participant data are from the INTERACT (Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage Trials) 1 and 2 studies. Receiver operating characteristic analyses were used to compare the predictive performance of baseline NIHSS and Glasgow Coma Scale scores, ICH score, and max-ICH score. Optimal cut points for predicting 90-day clinical outcomes (death or major disability [defined as modified Rankin Scale scores 3–6], major disability [defined as modified Rankin Scale scores 3–5], and death alone) were determined using the Youden index. Logistic regression models were adjusted for age, sex, hematoma volume, and other known risk factors for poor prognosis. We validated our findings in the INTERACT1 database.  

RESULTS: There were 2829 INTERACT2 patients (age, $63.5{\pm}12.9\$ years; male, $62.9\%$ ; ICH volume, 10.96 [5.77–19.49] mL) included in the main analyses. The baseline NIHSS score (area under the curve, 0.796) had better prognostic utility for predicting death or major disability than the Glasgow Coma Scale score (area under the curve, 0.650) and ICH score (area under the curve, 0.674) and was comparable to max-ICH score (area under the curve, 0.789). Similar findings were observed when assessing the outcome of major disability. A cut point of 10 on baseline NIHSS optimally (sensitivity, $77.5\%$ specificity, $69.2\%$ ) predicted death or major disability (adjusted odds ratio, 4.50 $[95\%$ CI, $3.60\substack{-5.63}$ ). The baseline NIHSS cut points that optimally predicted major disability and death alone were 10 and 12, respectively. The predictive effect of ${\mathsf{NIHSS}}\geq10$ for poor functional outcomes was consistent in all subgroups including age and baseline hematoma volume. Results were consistent when analyzed in the independent INTERACT1 validation database.  

CONCLUSIONS: In patients with mild-to-moderate ICH, a baseline NIHSS score o $\geq10$ was optimal for predicting poor outcomes at 90 days. Prediction based on baseline NIHSS is better than baseline Glasgow Coma Scale score.

cute spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is the most serious form of stroke,1 where reliable outcome prediction assists in management, counseling, and decisions over treatment. Although various composite prognostic scales have been developed for stroke, scores on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) measured upon presentation (at baseline) remain the most widely used for evaluating the pattern and severity of neurological deficits after stroke,2–9 including ${1\mathrm{CH.^{7-9}}}$ Defining the optimal prognostic cut points on the NIHSS and GCS could further assist in clinical decision-making. Herein, we present our study where we aimed to determine the optimal cut point for scores on the NIHSS and GCS at presentation for predicting adverse 90-day clinical outcomes in participants of the main phase of the INTERACT (Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage Trial) 2. The findings were externally validated using data from the pilot phase of the INTERACT1. We also evaluated the predictive capability of baseline NIHSS and GCS scores across important subgroups of baseline hematoma volume, early hematoma expansion, age, and time from onset to randomization.  



# METHODS  

Individual deidentified participant data used in these analyses will be shared by request from any qualified investigator following approval of a protocol and signed data access agreement via the Research Office of The George Institute for Global Health, Australia.  

The INTERACT studies were international, multicenter, open, blinded end point assessment, and randomized controlled trials, as described elsewhere.10,11 In brief, a total of 3233 participants (INTERACT1, 404 and INTERACT2, 2829) with spontaneous ICH $(<6$ hours of onset) and elevated systolic blood pressure (BP; $150{-}220\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ ) without a clear indication or contraindication to randomized treatment and no massive ICH or requirement for early decompressive surgery were randomly assigned to intensive (systolic BP target, $<140$ mm Hg within 1 hour) or guideline-recommended (systolic BP target, $<180\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ ) BP management. The study protocol was approved by the appropriate ethics committee at each participating site, and written informed consent was obtained directly from the patient or an appropriate surrogate. This article follows the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guideline.12  

Demographic characteristics and clinical variables were recorded at the time of presentation, with neurological severity measured on the NIHSS and GCS scores at baseline, 24 hours, and on day 7 (or earlier on discharge from the hospital). These assessments were undertaken by investigators at each center who were not involved in the clinical management of patients and were blinded to the randomized treatment allocation. The primary outcome was death or major disability (modified Rankin Scale scores, 3–6). Secondary outcomes were major disability (modified Rankin Scale scores, 3–5) and all-cause death alone at 90 days.  

Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and number $(\%)$ for categorical variables; betweengroup comparisons were made using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or $\chi^{2}$ test. Receiver operating characteristic analyses were used to assess the predictive performance of baseline NIHSS and GCS scores, and the area under the curve analyses were compared using the Delong test.13 In addition, we further evaluated and compared the predictive ability of baseline NIHSS and GCS scores with existing prediction scores—the ICH score14 and max-ICH15—by receiver operating characteristic analyses. Optimal prognostic cut points on the NIHSS and GCS scores at baseline for predicting 90-day outcomes (death or major disability, major disability, and death) were developed and validated using Youden index16 in the INTERACT2 and INTERACT1 data sets, respectively. Crude and multivariable logistic regression models were developed with known confounders, including age, sex, recruitment from China, baseline hematoma volume (logtransformed), systolic BP, time from ICH onset to randomization, antithrombotic use, BP-lowering treatment, intraventricular hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, hematoma location, and baseline GCS score, for optimal NIHSS and 90-day outcomes or NIHSS score for optimal GCS and 90-day outcomes. To assess the robustness of prognosis for the optimal cutoff point of baseline NIHSS score on 90-day outcomes, important subgroup analyses were conducted in multivariate-adjusted models stratified by age $(\ge65$ versus ${<}65$ years), median time from ICH onset to randomization $(\ge3.7$ versus ${<}3.7$ hours), median baseline hematoma volume $(\geq11$ versus $<11\mathsf{mL}$ , and hematoma expansion at 24 hours $(\ge6$ versus $<6\mathsf{mL}$ ).17 All analyses were performed with SAS software, version 9.3.  



# RESULTS  

A total of 2829 patients with ICH in INTERACT2 (mean [SD] age, 63.5 [12.9] years; 1780 $\left[62.9\%\right]$ male) and 404 patients with ICH in INTERACT1 (mean [SD] age, 62.3 [12.5] years; 262 $\left[64.9\%\right]$ male) were included. Median NIHSS and GCS scores were 11.0 (IQR, $6.0-$ 16.0) and 14.0 (IQR, 12.0–15.0), respectively, in the INTERACT2 population. Table  1 compares baseline characteristics between INTERACT2 and INTERACT1, where, compared with the latter, patients with ICH in INTERACT2 were more often recruited outside of China and had lower baseline diastolic BP, greater use of antithrombotic agents but less frequency of history of ICH, greater baseline hematoma volume, more severe ICH (higher NIHSS score), and more subarachnoid hemorrhage extension. The baseline ICH and max-ICH scores were higher in INTERACT2 than INTERACT1 participants although the ICH and max-ICH scores were relatively low in both cohorts.

After excluding 15 patients without baseline NIHSS or GCS scores and 46 patients without 90-day followup data, 2779 patients with ICH in INTERACT2 and 393 patients in INTERACT1 were included in the present analyses (patient flowchart in Figure S1). Receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve $(95\%$ CI) showed the predictive ability of baseline NIHSS was better than baseline GCS score for death or major disability (NIHSS, 0.797 [0.780–0.814] versus GCS, 0.654 [0.634–0.675]; $P{<}0.001$ ; Fig ure 1A), major disability alone (NIHSS, 0.690 [0.671– 0.710] versus GCS, 0.576 [0.554–0.597]; $P{<}0.001$ ; Figure  1B), and death alone (NIHSS, 0.760 [0.734– 0.785] versus GCS, 0.689 [0.657–0.720]; $P{<}0.001$ ; Figure  1C) at 90 days. Moreover, we found that the area under the curve of baseline NIHSS score was the largest for predicting death or major disability and major disability alone compared with max-ICH and ICH scores. Compared with the max-ICH score, the baseline NIHSS score was better for predicting major disability alone $\mathrm{\Delta}P=0.036\$ ) and comparable for predicting death or major disability $ \mathrm{\Delta}P=0.383\$ ). Compared with the ICH score, the baseline GCS score showed similar performance for predicting major disability alone $:P{=}0.858;$ but less effective for predicting death or major disability $\mathrm{\Delta}P{=}0.024$ ; Figure 2A and 2B; Table S1). For predicting death alone, the area under the curve of max-ICH score was the largest. The max-ICH score was similar compared with the baseline NIHSS score for predicting death $(P{=}0.051$ ), and the ICH score was better than the baseline GCS score ( $\mathrm{\Delta}P{=}0.0003$ ; Figure  2C; Table S1).  

Table 2 shows that a baseline NIHSS cut point of 10 was optimal for predicting 90-day death or major disability (sensitivity, $77.5\%$ ; specificity, $69.2\%$ ; positive predictive value, $74.6\%$ ; negative predictive value, $72.4\%$ ; Youden index, $0.467$ ; and odds ratio, 4.50 $[95\%$ CI, $3.60\substack{-5.63})$ ) and major disability alone (sensitivity, $74.9\%$ ; specificity, $57.6\%$ ; positive predictive value, $56.0\%$ ; negative predictive value, $76.1\%$ ; Youden index, 0.325; and odds ratio, 3.59 $[95\%$ CI, 2.90–4.42]). A baseline NIHSS cut point of 12 optimally predicted death at 90 days (sensitivity, $82.1\%$ ; specificity, $60.0\%$ ; positive predictive value, $21.9\%$ ; negative predictive value, $96.1\%$ ; Youden index, 0.421; and odds ratio, 2.64 $[95\%$ CI, 1.82–3.83]). A baseline GCS cut point of 13 best predicted 90-day death or major disability (Youden index, 0.242) and major disability alone (Youden index, 0.286), and a baseline GCS cut point of 12 best predicted 90-day death (Youden index, 0.128).  

In the INTERACT1 validation analyses, receiver operating characteristic curves showed NIHSS score similarly better predicted adverse 90-day outcomes over GCS (Figure S2) and the predictive ability of baseline NIHSS and GCS scores was not inferior to max-ICH and ICH scores (Figure S3; Table S2) although the cut points varied (8 for death or major disability, 9 for major disability alone, and 12 for death alone, respectively; Table 2).

# DISCUSSION  

In the present analyses of over 3000 well-characterized patients with ICH of mild-to-moderate severity from the INTERACT studies, we have confirmed that the NIHSS score provides a better prognostic utility over GCS and ICH scores and was similar to the max-ICH score, with an optimal prognostic threshold cutoff point being defined. In particular, we found that the predictive strength of ${\mathsf{NIHSS}}{\geq}10$ for poor functional outcomes was consistent across a wide range of baseline hematoma volumes, early hematoma expansion volume, age, and time from ICH onset to randomization.

NIHSS and GCS scores are both simple and widely used measures for grading the severity of the neurological deficit from acute stroke and are strong predictors of functional outcomes in patients with either ischemic stroke or ICH.2–9,18 In patients with ICH, some retrospective single-center studies report significant correlations between baseline NIHSS scores and hematoma volume and in the prediction of both short- and long-term mortality and unfavorable outcomes.7–9 Several compound scales combine baseline NIHSS or GCS scores with other clinical and neuroimaging markers, including age, ICH volume, and ICH location, to predict functional recovery, such as ICH functional outcome score,19 ICH score,14 FUNC,20 and max-ICH score.15 However, some studies indicate that these do not improve upon the predictive ability of baseline NIHSS or GCS scores alone.21,22 For example, a single-center retrospective study of 882 patients with ICH compared 19 prognostic scores to show that the baseline NIHSS score was not significantly inferior to other scores,21 while another study showed that the GCS score was comparable to 3 other grading scores for predicting 30-day mortality.22 Thus, the existing data suggest that baseline NIHSS and GCS scores are both useful tools for predicting functional outcomes after ICH. Our present analyses using the large INTERACT2 database extend this work to show that the NIHSS is superior to GCS in predicting functional outcomes after ICH, with a cut point of $\geq10$ for optimal prediction. The optimal cutoff of baseline NIHSS is higher for the prediction of death than that for major disability. Moreover, in the present analyses, we also found that the predictive performance of baseline NIHSS scores on 90-day outcomes was similar to the max-ICH score and better than the ICH score, which is in line with previous findings in real-world patients with ICH.21 These results were confirmed in the INTERACT1 validation data set. The clear cutoff point identified in the present study is simple and convenient for clinical practice.

It is not entirely surprising that the NIHSS score is better than the GCS score for predicting poor functional outcomes after ICH at 90 days. The NIHSS score is a comprehensive scale that measures various key components of neurological deficit, including the level of consciousness, extraocular movements, sensory function, coordination, language, and speech, with many of these factors being associated with functional recovery within 90 days. In contrast, the GCS score primarily focuses on the level of consciousness.  

Baseline hematoma volume, time from ICH onset to hospitalization, age, and early hematoma expansion are all well-characterized variables associated with 90-day functional recovery after ICH.17,23–25 We were able to show that the baseline NIHSS score of $\geq10$ was consistent in being optimal for the prediction of poor outcomes in our subgroup analyses, and although the strength of the association varied to some extent, it was still robust enough to apply widely across different types of patients with ICH.  

A strength of our study was access to a large and wellcharacterized clinical trial population who were managed across a broad range of health care settings and assessed with standardized outcome measures. However, patients with large hematomas who required early neurosurgery were purposely excluded, as were those who were ineligible through having normal (or low) systolic BP levels at presentation. Another issue is that the Youden index was relatively low for most of the cut points, indicating only a moderate relevance to the prognostic variables. Moreover, we lacked data on prestroke modified Rankin Scale score, which is an important factor associated with baseline NIHSS score and 90-day outcomes. Furthermore, a recent study indicated that some ICH survivors with poor outcomes in the early phase recovered to a good functional outcome at 1 year, suggesting variations in functional recovery over the short and long terms among patients with ICH.26 Unfortunately, we could not assess the predictive effect of baseline NIHSS and GCS scores on long-term outcomes as we lacked long-term followup data. Additionally, patients in the INTERACT cohort had mild-to-moderate ICH with relatively smaller hematoma volume; therefore, the present findings may not be generalizable to all patients with ICH. Although we had conducted robust analyses in clinically important patient subgroups, the optimal NIHSS cut point may still need validation and refinement in real-world patients and different populations. Also, we would like to advise that the use of this baseline NIHSS cutoff with care, especially for critical decisions such as the early withdrawal of care, may lead to a self-fulfilling prophecy in patients with ICH. Finally, there were minor inconsistencies in the optimal cut points for clinical outcomes between the derivation and validation data sets.  

In summary, we derived and validated an optimum baseline NIHSS score cut point for poor functional outcomes in mild-to-moderate patients with ICH: a baseline threshold NIHSS score of 10 was defined as being superior to baseline GCS for predicting poor outcome, including major disability or death and an increased likelihood of major disability alone. Our findings could be used for early identification of patients at higher risk and help to assess the benefit of early intervention in clinical trials.
