# GET-UP Trial 1-year results: long-term impact of an early mobilization protocol on functional performance after surgery for chronic subdural hematoma

OBJECTIVE  Evidence on timing for mobilization after chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) surgery is heterogeneous, and practices differ considerably among neurosurgical centers. The Impact of an Early Out-of-Bed Paradigm in Postoperative Outcomes of Chronic Subdural Hematomas: GET-UP Randomized Prospective Trial (GET-UP Trial) is a randomized clinical trial comparing a postoperative early mobilization protocol to bed rest. Previously reported results at clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery indicated a decreased risk of medical complications in the early mobilization group. Herein, the authors report outcomes at the 1-year follow-up.  

METHODS  The GET-UP Trial is a prospective, randomized, unicentric, open-label study with an intention-to-treat primary analysis designed to evaluate the impact of an early mobilization protocol after burr hole craniostomy for cSDH on the occurrence of medical complications and functional outcomes. Between January 2019 and August 2021, a total of 208 patients were recruited and randomized to either an early mobilization group, in which patients began elevation of the head of the bed within the first 12 hours after surgery, or to a bed rest group, in which patients remained recumbent for 48 hours. Outcomes assessed at the 1-year follow-up included functional status as measured by the Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOSE) and repeat surgery for hematoma recurrence (surgical recurrence).  

RESULTS  A total of 203 patients completed 1 year of follow-up: 101 in the bed rest group and 102 in the early mobilization group. No significant baseline pre-randomization clinical differences were observed between the two management groups. At 1 year after surgery, a favorable functional outcome, defined as a GOSE score $\ge5$ , was observed in 59 patients $(58.4\%)$ in the bed rest group and 78 $(76.5\%)$ in the early mobilization group $(\mathsf{p}=0.006)$ . Death occurred in 25 patients $(24.8\%)$ in the bed rest group and 16 $(15.7\%)$ in the early mobilization group $(\mathsf{p}=\mathsf{0}.108)$ . Surgical recurrence was noted in 6 patients $(5.9\%)$ in the bed rest group and 7 $(6.9\%)$ in the early mobilization group $\mathrm{\Delta}(\mathrm{p}=0.788)$ . Multivariate analysis showed an independent association between early mobilization and an increase in favorable functional outcomes (OR 2.006, $95\%$ CI 1.076–3.739, $p=0.028^{\prime}$ .  

CONCLUSIONS  The GET-UP Trial is the first randomized clinical trial assessing the impact of mobilization strategies on medical complications after burr hole craniostomy for cSDH. Regarding functional results 1 year after surgery, early mobilization was associated with an improvement in functional outcomes without an increase in surgical recurrence. These findings support the preference for an early mobilization protocol in cSDH patients over mandatory bed rest strategies.

KEYWORDS  chronic subdural hematoma; early mobilization; functional outcome; recurrence; traumatic brain injury; trauma  

hronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) is a complex pathology frequently encountered by the neurosurgeon, with an overall incidence of 1.7–20.6 cases per 100,000 persons per year.1 Although surgical results are generally considered satisfactory, a 3-month recurrence rate of approximately $12\%$ has been reported, and up to $26\%$ of patients fail to attain a favorable outcome within this time frame.2,3  

Postoperative care is crucial to optimize patient outcomes. Despite ample consensus on this idea, there is a dearth of good-quality evidence-based management guidelines.4 Recently published and ongoing trials are working toward creating a high-value body of knowledge to address this paucity of guidelines.5–9 Ideally, this research would support the creation of specific guidelines or a set of recommendations regarding cSDH surgical treatment and postoperative care.  

The timing of patient mobilization and its impact on functional outcomes, medical complications, and hematoma recurrence are specific questions for which conflicting answers abound in the literature.10–12 Our group recently published our initial results of the Impact of an Early Outof-Bed Paradigm in Postoperative Outcomes of Chronic Subdural Hematomas: GET-UP Randomized Prospective Trial (GET-UP Trial), a randomized clinical trial assessing the impact of postoperative early mobilization on the occurrence of medical complications in cSDH patients.7 Mobilization within 12 hours after surgery was associated with a statistically significant reduction in medical complications at clinical discharge as compared with a 48- hour bed rest protocol. No difference in repeat surgery for hematoma recurrence (surgical recurrence) between the two management groups was observed. Additionally, secondary outcomes included functional results as measured by the Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOSE) score. At 1 month after surgery, a favorable functional outcome, defined as a GOSE score $\geq5$ , was observed in $72.1\%$ of patients in the bed rest group and $81.7\%$ of patients in the early mobilization group, a difference that did not reach statistical significance.  

The GET-UP Trial protocol planned for a 1-year follow-up, which has been completed. In this report, we present the GET-UP Trial outcomes 1 year after surgery.  

# Methods  

The study protocol was described in detail in the first GET-UP Trial report7 and is summarized below.  

# Patient Population  

As previously reported,7 between January 2019 and August 2021, 224 adult patients were screened for study inclusion. Patients who were 18 years of age or older and considered for burr hole craniostomy drainage of cSDH met the study’s inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria were a previous intracranial procedure, ad initium drainage with craniotomy or intraoperative conversion to craniotomy, concomitant extracranial surgery, need for postoperative sedation exceeding 6 hours, and a previous condition precluding any mobilization. After these criteria were applied and written informed consent was obtained from the patient or a legal representative, the precalculated total of 208 patients was included (Fig. 1). This study was registered with the ClinicalTrials.gov database (http://clinicaltrials. gov), and its registration no. is NCT03788005.  

# Surgical Procedure and Randomization  

Surgical drainage by trepanation followed institutional protocol, as previously described.7 In summary, it consisted of trepanation, flush with body-temperature saline, opening of the neovascularization membranes, and subdural silicon drain placement if technically possible. None of the study participants were submitted to pre- or postoperative middle meningeal artery embolization. Patients were allocated to one of the two study groups in a 1:1 ratio according to computer-generated blocks of 4 randomization: in total, 104 patients were assigned to the 48 hours postsurgery bed rest group according to our institutional protocol (described below), and 104 patients were assigned to the early mobilization protocol.  

# Postoperative Management Protocols  

In patients in the early mobilization group, head of the bed (HOB) elevation over $30^{\circ}$ was initiated as soon as possible within the first 12 hours after surgery. They then progressed to sitting (defined as sitting on a chair or similar, not on a bed), standing (defined as maintaining an upright position, with both feet on the ground and bearing body weight, with or without support from a healthcare provider), and walking as promptly as clinically tolerated. During mobilization, subdural drains remained closed. A minimum period of 8 hours of drainage per day (not necessarily a continuous period and attained preferentially during sleeping hours) was mandatory. Beyond this period, patient mobilization progressed as clinically tolerated. Patients in the bed rest group remained recumbent with the HOB at less than $30^{\circ}$ with drains open for 48 hours. In both groups, subdural drains were removed 48 hours after surgery. Of note, the bed rest group represented the standard-of-care postoperative protocol in our institution at the time of study conduction.  

# Outcome Assessment  

The primary outcome of the GET-UP Trial consisted of medical complications between the immediate postoperative period and clinical discharge and was defined as any infection resulting in antibiotic treatment, any epileptic seizure resulting in antiepileptic drug initiation or increased dosage, and/or any thrombotic event requiring antithrombotic medication. Secondary outcomes included surgical recurrence and GOSE assessment score at clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery. Results of the GOSE assessments were dichotomized into a favorable (GOSE score $\geq5$ ) and an unfavorable (GOSE score $<5$ ) outcome group. An additional analysis dichotomized patients into a good recovery group (GOSE score 7 or 8) and a less-than-good recovery group (GOSE score $<7$ ). Both the primary outcome and the secondary outcomes at 1 month after surgery were reported previously.7  

One year after surgery, the GOSE score was assessed by telephone interview with the patients and/or caretakers.

Results were dichotomized as described above. Interviewers were blinded to randomized group allocation. Additionally, surgical recurrence at this time point was evaluated. A subgroup analysis was performed by selecting patients with a good preinjury functional status, as defined by a preinjury modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score $\leq3$ .  

# Institutional and Ethics Approval  

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Teaching, Formation and Research Department and the Ethics Commission of Centro Hospitalar Universitário de Santo António.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Statistical analysis was performed utilizing the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 26.0 (IBM Corp.). Pearson’s chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, including dichotomized GOSE assessment scores, between study groups (Fisher’s exact test was used when more than $25\%$ of crosstab analysis cells had an expected count value $<5$ ). Numerical variables were compared by applying the Student t-test for a parametric distribution and the Mann-Whitney U-test for a nonparametric distribution. A p value $<0.05$ was considered statistically significant. Groups were compared in an intention-to-treat fashion. Logistic regression was performed to control for confounding variables when considering categorical variables. The results are presented as odds ratios with $95\%$ confidence intervals.  

# Results  

# Patient Recruitment and Baseline Characteristics  

A total of 224 patients were screened for study inclusion, with 16 not meeting the full criteria, as detailed in our initial report.7 The preplanned total of 208 patients was randomized between the study groups, with 104 allocated to each group. The two groups were well-matched at randomization, with no statistically significant demographic, clinical, radiological, or surgical differences observed (Supplementary Table 1). Of note, there was a nonsignificant excess of patients on anticoagulation therapy in the bed rest group compared to those in the early mobilization group (20 $[\hat{1}9.2\%]$ vs 11 $[10.6\%]$ , respectively, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}=0.080)$ and a nonsignificant excess of patients without subdural drain placement in the bed rest group versus the early mobilization group (3 $[2.9\%]$ vs 0 $[0.0\%]$ , respectively, $\mathrm{p}=\mathrm{0}.081$ ).

# Intervention and Follow-Up  

All 104 patients in the early mobilization group followed the early mobilization protocol as detailed in Methods. Protocol nonadherence occurred in 7 patients in the bed rest group and in none of the patients in the early mobilization group, as previously detailed.7 The mean time from surgery to initiation of mobilization was 42.4 hours in the bed rest group and 5.5 hours in the early mobilization group $(\mathbf{p}<0.001)$ ). Predefined outcomes were evaluated at clinical discharge, 1 month after surgery, and 1 year after surgery.  

At 1 year after surgery, a total of 203 patients had completed the study’s clinical evaluation protocol. This represents a total loss to follow-up of 5 patients $(2.5\%)$ , namely 3 $(2.9\%)$ in the bed rest group and 2 $(1.9\%)$ in the early mobilization group (Fig. 1). No statistically significant demographic, clinical, radiological, or surgical differences were observed between the two groups 1 year after surgery (Table 1).  

# Outcomes  

The GET-UP Trial’s primary outcome (assessed at clinical discharge) and the subset of secondary outcomes assessed at clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery have been reported elsewhere.7 Secondary outcomes pertaining to the 1-year follow-up evaluation are summarized in Table 2 and Fig. 2. One year after surgery, a favorable functional outcome was observed in 59 patients $(58.4\%)$ in the bed rest group and 78 $(76.5\%)$ in the early mobilization group $\left(\mathrm{p}=\bar{0}.00\dot{6}\right)$ ). A good recovery was observed in 50 $(49.5\%)$ and 72 $(70.6\%)$ patients $(\mathtt{p}=0.002)$ , respectively. Surgical recurrence at the 1-year follow-up was noted in a total of 13 patients $(6.4\%)$ : 6 $(5.9\%)$ in the bed rest group and 7 $(6.9\%)$ in the early mobilization group $(\mathrm{p}=0.788)$ ). A total of 41 patients $(20.2\%)$ were dead: 25 $(24.8\%)$ in the bed rest group and 16 $(15.7\%)$ in the early mobilization group $(\mathtt{p}=0.1\dot{0}8)$ .

To control for the effect of the occurrence of medical complications, logistic regression analysis was performed (Table 3). # Discussion  

The GET-UP Trial is, to the best of our knowledge, the largest randomized clinical trial to examine the effect of early mobilization on the postoperative outcomes of cSDH patients. As previously reported,7 we found a reduction in the occurrence of medical complications until clinical discharge in the patients who followed a mobilization protocol in the first 12 hours after surgery, as compared with patients who remained recumbent for 48 hours $19.2\%$ vs $34.6\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=0.012)$ . At discharge, functional outcomes, as measured using the GOSE, were similar between the study groups; however, 1 month after surgery, the early mobilization group appeared to perform better, with a higher proportion of favorable functional outcomes $(81.7\%$ vs $72.1\%$ , $\bar{\mathrm{p}}=0.100)$ and a statistically significant association with a good recovery ( $75.0\%$ vs $60.6\%$ , $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}=0.026,$ ). No significant increase in surgical recurrence was observed.  

The GET-UP Trial protocol included an evaluation of functional outcomes 1 year after surgery. At this time point, we observed a statistically significant increase in favorable outcomes in the early mobilization group, as compared with those in the bed rest group $76.5\%$ vs $58.4\%$ , respectively, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}=0.006)$ . Likewise, a good recovery was significantly more frequent in the early mobilization group ( $70.6\%$ vs $49.5\%$ , respectively, $\mathbf{p}=0.002,$ . The proportion of patients who had died 1 year after surgery was also lower in the early mobilization group, but this difference did not reach statistical significance $15.7\%$ vs $24.8\%$ , respectively, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}=0.108)$ ).  

These results reinforce the original findings of the GET-UP Trial, suggesting that early mobilization not only decreases medical complications but also increases patient performance in the long term. A literature review highlighted a relative scarcity of functional outcome reports, as noted by Nouri et al.,13 especially at longer follow-ups. As apparent in the review by Feghali et al.,1 numerous studies have focused instead on radiological outcomes or surgical recurrence rates. Most authors who have reported functional outcomes have concentrated on the results 3 or 6 months after surgery.2,14–19 At 6 months, a favorable outcome was reported in $69.5\%$ to $91.6\%$ of patients; of note, the scores utilized and the definition of favorable outcome varied between these studies.2,14,18,19 Fewer studies have examined the results at 12 months.20–22 Soleman et al.22 reported functional outcomes at 12 months utilizing several scores, including the GOS score. In their study, a favorable outcome, defined as GOS score 4 or 5, was attained in $83\%$ and $86\%$ of patients in each study arm. Our functional results are inferior, although we note that the scores utilized are not the same, even if they are related. We emphasize that Soleman and colleagues consider their longterm follow-up as a strength of their protocol. We agree and furthermore posit that this may be especially pertinent in elderly populations such as cSDH patients, with possibly slower, more gradual recovery times that make functional outcome differences less evident early on. This hypothesis may explain why we observed a smaller, nonsignificant difference between study groups at discharge that increased at 1 month, remaining nonsignificant and becoming notable and statistically significant 1 year after surgery. With this assumption, early mobilization could act by promoting an incremental gain in recovery over time. Concurrently, early mobilization could be contributing by dampening progressive functional deterioration in these elderly patients, something that could only be measured long term and seems supported by our results: $81.7\%$ versus $72.1\%$ of patients with favorable outcomes at 1 month in the early mobilization and bed rest groups, respectively, and $76.5\%$ versus $58.4\%$ at 1 year.

While not often contemplated in the brain surgery field, strong evidence for the benefits of early mobilization already exists in other areas, as stated by the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) literature.23–25 As reported by Tazreean et al.,25 early mobilization reduces postoperative complications, accelerates recovery of functional walking capacity, reduces hospital length of stay, and improves patient-reported outcomes, thereby mitigating the adverse physiological impact of surgical stress and immobilization. In our study, this benefit seems to be driven by its effect on the subgroup of patients with a better premorbid functional status, as it had little to no impact on patients with a poor premorbid status (Supplementary Table 4). This may signal an increased capacity to withstand a more intensive rehabilitation program that may contribute to future enhanced functional outcomes. Furthermore, the association we observed between early mobilization and better functional outcomes seems to be independent of a medical complications reduction. These observations suggest a more wide-ranging mechanism of action compatible with the aforementioned ERAS protocol benefits.  

Importantly, we observed an increase in mortality at 1 year after surgery in the bed rest group ( $15.7\%$ vs $24.8\%$ in the early mobilization and bed rest groups, respectively, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}=0.108)$ . This difference was more pronounced in the preinjury good-functional-status subgroup $9.7\%$ vs $18.7\%$ , respectively, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}=0.080)$ . Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, we consider this disparity relevant and a valid additional argument against the adoption of postoperative mandatory bed rest strategies. This finding is especially pertinent as the historical rationalization against early mobilization was the risk of increased surgical recurrence, which we did not observe in our study’s complete follow-up.  

This study has various limitations. Firstly, its singlecenter design limits the generalizability of its conclusions. cSDH treatment practices are highly heterogeneous among neurosurgical centers,26,27 and our results may not apply equally to different surgical or postoperative procedures. However, our institution’s protocol for cSDH management is one of the most adopted strategies worldwide,1,2 which limits the negative impact on our results’ generalizability. Moreover, the GET-UP Trial’s mobilization protocol was designed to be easily implemented and replicated by other centers: other than the mandatory commencement of mobilization within the first 12 hours after surgery and a minimum daily 8-hour period of recumbency with opened drains, the mobilization strategy progressed as quickly and as intensively as possible, limited only by the patient’s clinical condition and tolerance. Additionally, the intervention was performed entirely by our usual team of healthcare providers, without the need for supplementary staff or equipment.

Additionally, we experienced study participant loss to follow-up. However, this represented only $2.4\%$ of the total patients randomized $2.0\%$ in the early mobilization group and $3.0\%$ in the bed rest group), which we believe does not carry a relevant risk of bias to our conclusions.  

Another topic we find pertinent to discuss is the difficulty in applying the GOSE, as some tiers have subjectivity inherent to their definition. Although we recognize this, we note that its application criteria were uniform among all study patients and blind to the randomization group. Also, the dichotomization chosen distinctly limits this concern, as the utilized cutoffs are objective and less fallible to the subjectivity of study investigators. Moreover, the difference in mortality between groups, although nonsignificant, is an objective finding, further diluting this issue.  

Finally, as previously discussed,7 the heterogeneous nature of the study intervention may introduce some complexity in the analysis. We believe this reflects the reality of the future application of any early mobilization protocol in clinical practice and is in accordance with the intentionto-treat study design.  

# Conclusions  

The GET-UP Trial is a randomized clinical trial comparing two mobilization strategies following cSDH surgery and focusing on medical complications and functional outcomes. We report the trial’s complete follow-up results. One year after surgery, early mobilization was associated with improved functional outcomes without an increase in surgical recurrence.  

The GET-UP Trial aimed to contribute to the enhancement of cSDH treatment. Our results support the adoption of postoperative early mobilization strategies over mandatory bed rest. Added studies, preferably with a multicentric design, are required to confirm our findings and to further advance neurosurgical care.
