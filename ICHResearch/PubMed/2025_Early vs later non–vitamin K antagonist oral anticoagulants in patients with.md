# Early vs later non–vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants in patients with acute ischemic stroke and atrial fibrillation: A meta-analysis and systematic review of randomized trials $\circledcirc$  

Linghua Fu, MD, PhD,1 Jinzhu Hu, MD, PhD,1 Pingping Yang, MD, PhD,2 Qi Chen, MD, PhD1  

# ABSTRACT  

BACKGROUND There are conflicting published data on the optimal timing of non–vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) in atrial fibrillation (AF) patients with acute ischemic stroke.  

OBJECTIVE To compare the efficacy and safety of early initiation of NOACs with later initiation of NOACs in those patients, we conducted a meta-analysis of phase 3 or phase 4 randomized controlled trials.  

METHODS We systematically searched the Cochrane Library, PubMed, and Embase databases. A random effects model wa selected to pool the effect measurement estimates (risk ratios [RRs] and $95\%$ confidence intervals [CIs]).  

RESULTS Three randomized controlled trials with 6442 enrolled patients with AF and acute ischemic stroke were included. Compared with later NOAC therapy, early NOAC therapy was associated with nonsignificant reductions in the risk of primary outcomes (RR, 0.82; $95\%$ CI, 0.65–1.05). Subgroup analysis found that in reducing risk of primary outcomes, early anticoagulation may benefit female patients more than male patients (0.54 [0.35–0.83] vs 0.97 [0.63–1.50]; $P$ for interaction, .06). Numerically lower rates of recurrent ischemic stroke (RR, 0.80; $95\%$ CI, 0.56–1.15), death (RR, 0.96; $95\%$ CI, 0.81–1.15), and systemic embolism (RR, 0.43; $95\%$ CI, 0.16–1.11) were observed in early initiation of NOACs in comparison with later initiation of NOACs. There was no difference in major bleeding (RR, 0.98; $95\%$ CI, 0.55–1.74), symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (RR, 0.93; $95\%$ CI, 0.43– 1.96), or major extracranial bleeding (RR, 0.73; $95\%$ CI, 0.28–1.95) between groups.  

CONCLUSION In AF patients with acute ischemic stroke, early initiation of NOACs is not inferior to later initiation of NOACs in reducing composite events, without increased hazard of bleeding, especially in female patients.

# Introduction  

Non–vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs), including dabigatran, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, and apixaban, have recently become the mainstream choice for anticoagulation in atrial fibrillation $\left(\mathsf{AF}\right)^{\top}$ because of their lower rates of stroke, intracranial hemorrhage risk, and mortality.2–5 Patients with acute ischemic stroke related to AF have high risk of recurrent stroke, which is $3\%$ to $5\%$ in the first 2 weeks,6,7 and hence lifelong anticoagulation therapy is recommended by most guidelines.1,8,9 However, patients with stroke are also vulnerable to secondary hemorrhagic transformation. The concerns about the risk of recurrent stroke in this group must be balanced against the perceived competing risk of secondary hemorrhagic transformation. The optimal timing for initiation of NOACs after acute ischemic stroke in AF patients has not been fully elucidated. The previous guideline8 recommended initiation of anticoagulation based on stroke severity (1-3-6-12–day rule). However, the latest 2 guidelines1,9 did not clearly stipulate the timing of initiation of anticoagulation after acute stroke due to AF. More recently, several randomized controlled trials (RCTs)10–12 have focused on the optimal timing for initiation of NOACs in this population but yielded conflicting results. The TIMING (Timing of Oral Anticoagulant Therapy in Acute Ischemic Stroke With Atrial Fibrillation) and OPTIMAS (Optimal timing of anticoagulation after acute ischemic stroke with atrial fibrillation) trials suggested that early initiation is noninferior to delayed initiation of NOACs in patients with acute ischemic stroke.10,12 In contrast, the ELAN (Early Versus Late Initiation of Direct Oral Anticoagulants in Post-ischemic Stroke Patients With Atrial Fibrillation) trial has reported that early initiation of NOACs in patients reduces the incidence of primary outcome compared with later initiation of NOACs at 90 days.11 Considering the significant but conflicting evidence, we performed a meta-analysis and systematic review to evaluate the optimal time of NOACs in AF patients after acute ischemic stroke.  

# Methods  

In this meta-analysis, the presentations were based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses of studies that evaluate health care interventions.13  

# Literature search  

Using electronic retrieval methods, we systematically searched the Cochrane Library, PubMed, and Embase databases for all the studies reporting use of NOACs in AF patients with acute ischemic stroke that were published before November 2, 2024. No language restrictions were imposed during the searches. To identify studies involving relevant participants, we performed the search with keywords including the following terms: stroke and atrial fibrillation. To identify studies involving NOACs, we performed the search with keywords including the following terms: dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, non–vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants, novel oral anticoagulants, direct oral anticoagulants, and oral anticoagulants. These 2 categories of keywords were combined with the Boolean operator and. The electronic search strategies are provided in Supplemental Table 1. We also reviewed reference lists, journals, and conference abstracts to identify relevant studies. In addition, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov (https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/) to obtain the information on studies that were terminated without being published.  

# Abbreviations  

# Definitions of end points  

AF: atrial fibrillation CI: confidence interval NIHSS: NIH Stroke Scale NOAC: non–vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant RCT: randomized controlled trial  

RR: risk ratio  

The definitions of primary outcomes were adopted by the original studies (Supplemental Table 2). The secondary outcomes included recurrent ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, death, major bleeding, major extracranial bleeding, and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage.  

# Inclusion and exclusion criteria  

Studies satisfying the following criteria were included: studies that compared early use of NOACs with later use of NOACs in AF patients with acute ischemic stroke, studies designed as RCTs, and studies that reported the end points mentioned before. Exclusion criteria were as follows: studies designed as observational studies; certain publications (eg, reviews, editorials, letters, and animal studies) and duplicate data; and phase 2 clinical trials because of their small sample size, short follow-up period, and overestimated bleeding risks.  

# Risk of bias assessment  

All of the studies were evaluated for risk of bias by the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool by 2 reviewers (F.-L.H. and H.-J.Z.) independently. This risk of bias in the RCTs was assessed on the basis of the following 7 domains: random sequence generation (selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome assessors (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), selective outcome reporting (reporting bias), and other bias.  

# Data extraction  

All of the studies retrieved by the search strategy were screened by 2 reviewers (F.-L.H. and H.-J.Z.) independently. The first phase of screening was performed by reading titles or abstracts, and the second phase of screening was to review the full text. Ultimately, articles meeting the eligibility criteria were included. Disagreements were settled by discussion with a third author (Y.-P.P.).  

For each study, the extracted information included the following characteristics: study name, age, proportion of female patients, total number of patients, duration of followup, and end points and their definitions.  

# Patient involvement  

No patients were involved in setting the research question, the outcome measures, the design, or the implementation of the study. No patients were asked to advise on the interpretation or writing of the results. There are no plans to involve patients in the dissemination of the manuscript.  

# Statistical analysis  

Statistical analysis was performed with Review Manager version 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark). For each study, the effect measurement estimates chosen were the risk ratios (RRs) and corresponding $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs). In view of the different definitions of primary outcome, early anticoagulation, and later anticoagulation across the included studies, we selected a random effects model to evaluate the treatment outcomes, which accounts for variability both within studies and between studies. The heterogeneity across the included studies was measured with an $I^{2}$ statistical test, with values of $25\%$ , $50\%$ and $75\%$ representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. The statistical significance threshold was set at $P<.05$ .  

To explore the sources of heterogeneity and to identify the optimal time of initiation of anticoagulation for different clinical statuses, we performed subgroup analysis based on the following parameters: sex (male vs female), clinical stroke severity (with an NIH Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score of 0–10 points at randomization vs with an NIHSS score of 0–10 points at randomization), reperfusion therapy (yes vs no), anticoagulation before the qualifying ischemic stroke (yes vs no), and different time windows for early initiation (days $=2$ vs days $=4$ vs days $=7.$ ). The test for subgroup differences was calculated by $\chi^{2}$ statistics. $P<.05$ for the interaction indicated that the effect of treatment differed between the tested subgroups.  

# Results  

# Search selection  

The search steps are illustrated in Figure 1. A total of 25,095 potential articles (9062 through PubMed, 16,103 through Embase, and 20 through the Cochrane Library) were identified through the electronic retrieval strategies, and no additional studies were identified through manual searches. After duplicates were removed, 23,083 records remained. Based on the titles or abstracts, 1313 records were excluded by screenings, and 14 articles remained for full-text review. A total of 11 articles were subsequently excluded: 1 article14 was excluded because this was a phase 2 clinical trial; 2 articles were excluded for duplicate data; and 8 articles were excluded because they were observational studies. Finally, 3 studies10–12 enrolling AF patients with recent ischemic stroke were included.  

# Characteristics of included studies  

The baseline characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1. Three multicenter, blinded end point RCTs included 6442 AF patients (3232 patients with early use of NOACs and 3120 patients with later use of NOACs) with 9 months of follow-up. Some variations existed between the 3 included studies in trial design, including the different definitions of early and later NOAC therapy and the use (or absence) of image-based criteria for anticoagulation timing. The TIMING trial10 and ELAN trial11 were designed as phase 3 RCTs, whereas the OPTIMAS trial12 was a phase 4 RCT. In the ELAN trial,11 patients with minor or moderate infarct were randomly assigned to early initiation (ie, within 48 hours after stroke onset) or later initiation (ie, at day 3 or 4 after stroke onset); patients with major infarct on imaging were randomly assigned to early NOACs (ie, on day 6 or 7) or later NOACs (ie, day 12, 13, or 14). In the TIMING trial,10 patients were randomly assigned to early (ie, ${\le}4$ days after stroke onset) or later (ie, 5–10 days after stroke onset) initiation of NOACs, irrespective of stroke severity and the size of the infarct on brain imaging. In the OPTIMAS trial,12 participants were randomly assigned to early (ie, $\leq4$ days from stroke symptom onset) or later (ie, 7–14 days) initiation of NOACs, irrespective of stroke severity and the size of the infarct on brain imaging. Most of the participants were male $(54.5\%)$ . The mean age was 78.2 years. Most patients $(61.2\%)$ were taking apixaban as an anticoagulant after recent ischemic stroke.  

# Study quality assessment  

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed. In general, a low risk of bias was identified. Outcome assessment was not reported to be blinded in the 1 study10 that did not report randomization processes.  

# Primary outcomes for early vs later initiation of NOACs in AF patients with acute ischemic stroke  

There were 126 primary outcomes in 3232 patients $(3.9\%)$ in the early NOACs group and 151 primary outcomes in 3120 patients $(4.8\%)$ in the later NOACs group (Figure 2). Compared with later anticoagulation, receiving early anticoagulation was associated with nonsignificant reductions in the risk of primary outcome (RR, 0.82; $95\%$ CI, 0.65–1.05; $P=$ .12), although a numerical trend was evident, with low heterogeneity across the included studies $(I^{2}=8\%)$ .

# Subgroup analyses for primary outcomes in AF patients with acute ischemic stroke  

We based the subgroup analysis on several key clinical parameters (Figure 3). The similar result of the 2 treatment strategies in primary outcome was generally consistent across the subgroups—clinical stroke severity (Figure 3a), anticoagulation before the qualifying ischemic stroke (Figure 3c), reperfusion therapy (Figure 3d), and different time windows for early initiation (Figure 3e)—with the exception of a marginally significant interaction for sex $P$ for interaction, .06). As shown in Figure 3b, compared with later use of NOACs, early use of NOACs significantly reduced the risk of primary outcome by $46\%$ (RR, 0.54; $95\%$ CI, 0.35–0.83; $P=.005^{\circ}\/$ ) in female patients with a stroke but yielded conflicting results in male patients (RR, 0.97; $95\%$ CI, 0.63-1.50; $P=.89)$ .

# Secondary outcomes for early vs later initiation of NOACs in AF patients with acute ischemic stroke  

As shown in Figure 4, receiving early NOACs was also associated with nonsignificant reductions in recurrent ischemic stroke (RR, 0.80; $95\%$ CI, 0.56–1.15; $P=.23$ ; $1^{2}=26\%;$ Supplemental Figure 1a), systemic embolism (RR, 0.43; $95\%$ CI, 0.16–1.11; P $=.08$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ ; Supplemental Figure 1b), and death (RR, 0.96; $95\%$ CI, 0.81–1.15; $P=$ .66; $P=0\%;$ Supplemental Figure 1c) in comparison with later use of NOACs.  

Compared with later NOACs, early NOACs were not found to significantly increase the risk of major bleeding (RR, 0.98; $95\%$ CI, 0.55–1.74; $\begin{array}{rl}{P}&{{}=}\end{array}$ .95; $\begin{array}{rlr}{I^{2}}&{{}=}&{22\%.}\end{array}$ Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 2a), major extracranial bleeding (RR, 0.73; $95\%$ CI, 0.28–1.95; $P=$ .54; $1^{2}=51\%$ ; Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 2b), and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (RR, 0.93; $95\%$ CI, 0.43–1.96; $P=.84$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ ; Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 2c).  

# Discussion  

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis based on RCTs exploring the optimal time of initiation of anticoagulation in patients with AF after acute ischemic stroke. Overall, in those patients, early NOAC therapy is not inferior to later NOAC therapy in reducing composite events, without increased risk of major bleeding. The large sample size from phase 3 or phase 4 RCTs, high-quality design, and subgroup analysis provided reliable conclusions for optimal time of initiation of NOACs in AF patients after acute ischemic stroke.  

Approximately $10\%{-}30\%$ of acute ischemic strokes are linked to AF,15 and ischemic strokes related to AF are more disabling than other ischemic stroke subtypes.16 Ischemic stroke may occur in AF patients either as the initial presenting manifestation of AF or despite appropriate anticoagulation therapy.10,12,17 Given that AF patients who had experienced acute ischemic stroke (within the first few weeks) were excluded from hallmark $\mathsf{RCTs}^{2-5}$ comparing the outcomes of NOACs with warfarin, the safety and efficacy outcomes of NOACs vs warfarin in these patients are less well established, and they are not recommended in AF guidelines for patients after acute ischemic stroke because of the limited evidence. Early anticoagulation may increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage, whereas later anticoagulation may increase the risk of ischemic stroke recurrence. Balancing the risks of ischemic stroke recurrence and intracranial hemorrhage with bleeding risks is a key consideration in evaluating the benefit-risk profile of anticoagulants. The decision about the appropriateness and timing for initiation of NOACs in AF patients with ischemic stroke is a common, long-standing, and challenging clinical scenario. Our meta-analysis suggested that early initiation of NOACs is safe for AF patients with acute ischemic stroke, without increased risk of intracranial hemorrhage, especially in female patients, and can reduce occurrence of the primary outcome.  

Female sex is a risk factor for ischemic stroke in patients with AF.18 As presented in our results, the incidence rate of recurrent ischemic stroke, symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage, or all-cause death in women $(8.4\%)$ is higher than that in men $(5.1\%)$ for AF patients receiving later initiation of NOACs. Compared with male patients, female patients with AF have higher risk of ischemic stroke and hospitalization, yet lower risk of intracranial bleeding.19 The higher rate of ischemic stroke in female patients may explain the benefit of early use of NOACs in this population.  

Intracranial hemorrhage transformation, an important complication in patients with acute ischemic stroke, is associated with worse functional outcomes and higher mortality.20 Hemorrhagic transformation is most common within large infarcts.20 The potential mechanisms of hemorrhagic transformation remain unclear. Anticoagulation may increase this risk of hemorrhagic transformation by promoting extravasation of blood and preventing clotting. However, as observed in our results, the incidence rates of major bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage were low in both the early and late anticoagulation groups, and there was no statistical difference between the groups in this population. The post hoc analysis21 of ELAN suggested that the effect of early initiation of NOACs did not differ for patients with minor, moderate, or major stroke assessed by brain imaging. In addition, early NOAC treatment was not associated with a higher rate of intracranial hemorrhage.21 Our subgroup analysis also suggested that early initiation of NOACs did not increase the primary end point for AF patients with NIHSS score ${>}10$ .  

The previous 4 meta-analyses22–25 based primarily on observational studies were performed to investigate the timing of initiation of anticoagulation in patients with AF after acute ischemic stroke. Those studies reported the early use of NOACs to be associated with reductions in the risks of composite ischemic events, without increasing bleeding events compared with later use of NOACs. These metaanalyses22–25 included more observational studies, but the quality of included studies was low, with moderate to high heterogeneity. In comparison, our results, primarily based on large phase 3 or phase 4 clinical trials, with low heterogeneity, suggested that early NOAC therapy is not inferior to later NOAC therapy, without a significant effect on major bleeding or intracranial hemorrhage. The meta-analyses of observational studies with increased heterogeneity observed between studies need more careful consideration in comparison to meta-analyses of RCTs.26 Synthesis of RCTs is generally considered the highest level of clinical evidence.26 Moreover, many patients in the previous meta-analyses22–25 used warfarin as an anticoagulant after acute ischemic stroke. Previous studies27,28 have shown that NOACs are better than warfarin in patients with AF and acute ischemic stroke, and most guidelines1,8 recommend that NOACs be the first choice for nonvalvular AF, whereas this study included only patients with the use of NOACs. Furthermore, the results in subgroups are important to offer clinicians a more comprehensive picture of early initiation of NOACs as a therapeutic option in AF patients with recent stroke. However, the subgroup analysis was not performed in the 4 meta-analyses.22–25 In comparison, our results were tested in subgroup analysis based on several important clinical parameters. Moreover, we found that female patients were more likely to benefit from early initiation of NOACs in comparison with male patients through subgroup analysis.

# Implications and further research  

There are no evidence-based recommendations on the optimal time to initiate NOACs for AF patients with acute ischemic stroke, and most guidelines8,29 recommend later NOAC therapy in those patients on the basis of experience. The European Heart Rhythm Association guideline recommended initiation of anticoagulation based on stroke severity with the NIHSS score (1-3-6-12–day rule, giving anticoagulants 1 day after onset of transient ischemic attack, 3 days after minor stroke, 6 days after mild stroke, and 12 days after severe stroke).8 The American Heart Association/American Stroke Association recommended starting oral anticoagulation within 4– 14 days after ischemic stroke onset.29 In our findings, early use of NOACs is noninferior to later use of NOACs in AF patients after recent ischemic stroke, especially in female patients, which indicates that early initiation of NOACs should be considered in this population.  

Accumulated evidence30,31 has shown that different types of NOACs have different effects on reducing the risk of new stroke after ischemic stroke, of which apixaban had better long-term effects. Although apixaban was used most in our study, none of the included studies provided subgroup analysis of different types of NOACs in the primary outcome. Factor XIa inhibitors are considered potential hemostasis-sparing anticoagulants for patients at risk of thromboembolic events that reduce bleeding events.32 However, a recent phase $3~{\mathsf{RCT}}^{33}$ that compared the efficacy and safety of asundexian (an oral activated factor XI) with apixaban in AF patients with high risk of stroke reported that asundexian confers protection against major bleeding events, but at the cost of an increased hazard of thrombosis-related complications. The efficacy and safety of different NOACs and factor XIa inhibitors in patients with AF with recent ischemic stroke need further study.  

# Strengths and limitations of this review  

The strengths of this study include the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, large sample size, and high-quality design. In addition, we included only phase 3 or phase 4 RCTs. Furthermore, subgroup analysis was performed to investigate the potential sources of heterogeneity and to identify the optimal timing for initiation of NOACs after acute ischemic stroke in AF patients in different clinical statuses.  

However, several limitations may influence the validity of this meta-analysis. First, the definition of the primary outcome, early anticoagulation, and later anticoagulation differed across the included trials and potentially affected the findings. Therefore, we used the random effects models that account for between-study heterogeneity. In addition, we also performed subgroup analysis based on the time of early anticoagulation initiation and found no differences between subgroups. Second, most patients were from Europe in our analysis, limiting the wide application of the results to all patients. Third, other factors, like infarct size and imaging features that predispose patients to hemorrhagic transformation, are important to evaluate the risk of hemorrhagic transformation. However, only the ELAN study provided data for subgroup analysis based on infarction area. Thus, it seems impossible to perform the subgroup analysis based on these factors. Nevertheless, the NIHSS score is also an important parameter for assessing the severity of ischemic stroke. Our subgroup analysis did not find any difference in NIHSS scores ${>}10$ points and 0–10 points between the early NOACs group and the later NOACs group. Further subpopulation analyses are necessary to enhance the quality of this work. Fourth, although we first found that women are more likely than men to benefit from early NOAC treatment through subgroup analysis of the primary end point, none of the 3 included studies has provided subgroup analysis of secondary end points on sex, so whether this benefit comes from reducing the risk of recurrent ischemic stroke or intracerebral hemorrhage requires further data support. Finally, given the limited number of studies included in the analysis, our findings should be confirmed with future research. The ongoing START (Optimal Delay Time to Initiate Anticoagulation After Ischemic Stroke in Atrial Fibrillation; NCT03021928) trial34 may help guide clinical decisions in optimal timing for initiation of NOACs after recent ischemic stroke in AF.  

# Conclusion  

In AF patients with acute ischemic stroke, early initiation of NOACs is not inferior to later initiation of NOACs in reducing  

composite events, without increased hazard of bleeding, especially in female patients.
