# Tenecteplase versus alteplase for the management of acute ischaemic stroke in Norway (NOR-TEST 2, part A): a phase 3, randomised, open-label, blinded endpoint, non-inferiority trial  

Christopher Elnan Kvistad, Halvor Næss, Bernt H Helleberg, Titto Idicula, Guri Hagberg, Linn Marie Nordby, Kristian N Jenssen, Håkon Tobro, Dag M Rörholt, Kamaljit Kaur, Agnethe Eltoft, Kristin Evensen, Judit Haasz, Guruparan Singaravel, Annette Fromm, Lars Thomassen  

# Summary  

Background Tenecteplase is a modified tissue plasminogen activator with pharmacological and practical advantages over alteplase—which is currently the only approved thrombolytic drug for ischaemic stroke. The NOR-TEST trial showed that ${\bf0.4mg/kg}$ tenecteplase had an efficacy and safety profile similar to that of a standard dose $(0\cdot9\mathbf{mg}/\mathbf{kg})$ of alteplase, albeit in a patient population with a high prevalence of minor stroke. The aim of NOR-TEST 2 was to establish the non-inferiority of tenecteplase ${\bf0.4mg/kg}$ to alteplase ${\bf0.9mg/kg}$ for patients with moderate or severe ischaemic stroke.  

Methods This phase 3, randomised, open-label, blinded endpoint, non-inferiority trial was performed at 11 hospitals with stroke units in Norway. Patients with suspected acute ischaemic stroke with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 6 or more who were eligible for thrombolysis and admitted within $\mathbf{4\cdot56}$ of symptom onset were consecutively included. Random assignment, done by a computer with a block size of 4 and with allocations placed into opaque envelopes to be opened consecutively, was 1:1 between intravenous tenecteplase $(0\cdot4\:\mathrm{mg/kg})$ or standard dose alteplase $(0\cdot9~\mathrm{mg/kg})$ . Doctors and nurses providing acute care were not masked to treatment, but primary outcome assessment at 3 months was masked. The primary outcome was favourable functional outcome defined as a modified Rankin Scale score of 0–1 at 3 months, assessed in the modified intention-to-treat analysis (excluding patients who did not qualify for thrombolysis after randomisation or who withdrew informed consent). The noninferiority margin was $3\%$ . This trial (NOR-TEST 2) is registered with EudraCT (number 2018–003090–95) and ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03854500). The trial was stopped early for safety reasons and is designated part A for analysis. Part B is ongoing with a lower dose of tenecteplase $(0\cdot25\mathbf{mg}/\mathbf{kg})$ .  

Findings Between Oct 28, 2019, and Sept 26, 2021, 216 patients were enrolled. Patient enrolment was stopped after a per-protocol safety review showed an imbalance in the rates of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage between the treatment groups, which surpassed the prespecified criteria for stopping the trial. Of 204 patients entering the modified intention-to-treat analysis, 100 were randomly allocated tenecteplase and 104 were allocated alteplase. All patients were followed up within 14 days of the end of the 3-months’ follow-up period. A favourable functional outcome was reported less frequently in patients receiving tenecteplase (31 $[32\%]$ of 96 patients) compared with alteplase (52 $[51\%]$ of 101 patients; unadjusted OR 0·45 $[95\%$ CI $0\cdot25\mathrm{-}0\cdot80\mathrm{];}$ $\mathbf{p}{=}0\cdot0064)$ ). Any intracranial haemorrhage was significantly more frequent with tenecteplase (21 $[21\%]$ of 100 patients) than with alteplase (seven $[7\%]$ of 104 patients; unadjusted OR 3·68 $95\%$ CI 1·49–9·11]; $\mathbf{p}{=}0\cdot0031)$ . Mortality at 3 months was also significantly higher with tenecteplase (15 $[16\%]$ of 96 patients) than with alteplase (five $[5\%]$ of 101 patients; unadjusted OR 3·56 $95\%$ CI 1·24–10·21]; $\mathbf{p}{=}0\cdot013)$ . Numerically more cases of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage were reported with tenecteplase (six $[6\%]$ of 100 patients) than with alteplase (one $[1\%]$ of 104 patients; unadjusted OR 6·57 $[95\%$ CI 0·78–55·62]; $\mathbf{p}{=}0\cdot061)$ ).  

Interpretation In this prematurely terminated study (terminated to fulfil the prespecified safety criteria), tenecteplase at a dose of ${\bf0.4mg/kg}$ yielded worse safety and functional outcomes compared with alteplase. Our study consequently could not show that ${\bf0.4mg/kg}$ tenecteplase is non-inferior to alteplase in moderate and severe ischaemic stroke. Future stroke trials should assess a lower dose of tenecteplase versus alteplase in patients with moderate or severe stroke.

# Research in context  

# Evidence before this study  

We searched MEDLINE for articles published in English between Jan 1, 2010, and Jan 31, 2022, with the terms “tenecteplase”, “ischemic stroke”, and “clinical trial or study”, to identify articles reporting the use of tenecteplase for the treatment of acute ischaemic stroke. In 2017, the first phase 3, randomised controlled trial (NOR-TEST) investigating the safety and efficacy of intravenous tenecteplase in patients with acute ischaemic stroke showed that $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase was not superior to $0{\cdot}9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ alteplase, but had a similar safety profile to alteplase. Together with four earlier phase 2 studies with randomised comparisons between tenecteplase and alteplase, a total of 1585 patients (828 tenecteplase and 757 alteplase) were included in a meta-analysis. The alteplase dose was $0{\cdot}9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ in all patients, whereas the tenecteplase doses were $0{\cdot}1\mathrm{mg/kg}$ in $7\%$ of patients, $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ in $25\%$ of patients, and $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ in $69\%$ of patients. Although no single trial showed the superiority or non-inferiority of tenecteplase on clinical outcome, the meta-analysis indicated that tenecteplase could be superior in the recanalisation of large vessel occlusions and non-inferior in disability-free 3-month outcome, with no increase in symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage or mortality. The evidence was, however,  

strongly dependent on NOR-TEST, which had a study population with a high prevalence of mild stroke. Further trials were needed to define the optimal dose and characterise the relative risks and benefits of tenecteplase in the treatment of ischaemic stroke.  

# Added value of this study  

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first phase 3, randomised controlled trial investigating the safety and efficacy of $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase in patients with moderate to severe stroke. The trial was prematurely terminated because of an increased bleeding rate in the tenecteplase group, and our findings indicate that a dose of $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ is too high in patients with moderate to severe stroke.  

# Implications of all the available evidence  

The dose of $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase appears not to be safe for patients with moderate and severe ischaemic stroke compared with alteplase. The dose of $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase has been tested in a small series of patients with stroke and in patients who have undergone a thrombectomy, but the safety and efficacy of $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase, or even lower doses, need to be tested against alteplase in phase 3 studies with a general stroke population.  

In myocardial infarction, $0{\cdot}5~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase is used for thrombolysis after a phase 3 trial showed lower rates of non-cerebral bleeding complications compared with alteplase.5 In ischaemic stroke, several phase 2 trials have tested the safety and efficacy of $0{\cdot}1~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ and $0{\cdot}25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase.6–12 One dose-escalation trial in 50 patients with minor stroke presenting within $12\mathrm{h}$ after onset showed that $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase had in­ creased recanalisation rates compared with $0{\cdot}1~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase, with a similar risk of cerebral haemorrhage.9 Another phase 2 trial included 104 stroke patients with a perfusion mismatch on cerebral imaging and did not find any differences in clinical outcome between $0{\cdot}25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase and standard-dose $(0\cdot9\mathrm{mg/kg})$ alteplase.10 In patients receiving thrombectomy, an Australian phase 2 trial showed higher reperfusion rates and more favourable outcomes in patients receiving $0{\cdot}25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase compared with alteplase.11 A second trial from the same group did not find any differences in clinical outcomes between $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ and $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase, applying similar inclusion criteria.12  

The only phase 3 trial assessing tenecteplase in ischaemic stroke (NOR-TEST) showed similar efficacy and safety outcomes with $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase compared with alteplase in 1100 patients.13 However, most patients had either mild stroke or stroke mimics, both of which are associated with a natural good prognosis, thus making it difficult to prove a significant difference in effect. Consequently, the optimal dose of tenecteplase in ischaemic stroke has not yet been established, and international guidelines recommend different doses of tenecteplase for different stroke populations.2 So far, no phase 3 studies have shown the superiority or non-inferiority of tenecteplase to alteplase in ischaemic stroke. In NOR-TEST 2, we aimed to establish the noninferiority of $0{\cdot}4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase compared with a standard dose of $0{\cdot}9~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ alteplase in patients with moderate or severe ischaemic stroke eligible for intravenous thrombolysis.  

# Methods  

# Study design and participants  

NOR-TEST 2 is a multicentre, randomised, open-label, blinded endpoint, phase 3 study at 11 hospitals with stroke units in Norway. The inclusion criteria comprised clinically suspected acute ischaemic stroke in patients aged 18 years or older, living independently before their stroke, admitted within $4{\cdot}5\mathrm{~h~}$ of stroke onset with an National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 6 or more, and eligible for thrombolytic treatment according to Norwegian guidelines, which are similar to the European guidelines.3 Patients with signs or symptoms on awakening or an unknown onset of stroke signs or symptoms were included if an MRI showed a mismatch between diffusion-weighted imaging and fluid attenuated inversion recovery. Patients undergoing a thrombectomy were included if they received thrombolysis as a bridging therapy. A screening log of eligible patients who were not included in NOR-TEST 2 was not required, based on experience in NOR-TEST, for which this log yielded insufficient data quality. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the appendix (pp 1–2).  

Written informed consent was obtained from the participant or a legal representative. The trial was done in accordance with the guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki, and was monitored by external controllers from Western Norway Health Trust Research & Development. The study was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics and the Norwegian Medicines Agency. For the protocol see the appendix (pp 11–39).  

# Randomisation and masking  

Patients were included after an assessment of eligibility by emergency room doctors at the hospital. The treating doctor in the emergency room gave the patient, or their proxy or family, brief verbal information about the study and asked for verbal consent to participate in the study. If the patient was not capable of understanding the information, and if a proxy was not present, obtaining consent was deferred and the patient was enrolled and randomly assigned in the study. The patient or their proxy, or both, received (as soon as possible after random assignment) further verbal and written information about the study and were asked to sign a written consent form. Included patients were randomly assigned (1:1) via block randomisation (block size 4) to receive either tenecteplase $(0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ up to a maximum of $40~\mathrm{mg}$ as a single bolus intravenously) or alteplase $(0{\cdot}9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ up to a maximum of $90\mathrm{mg}$ , with $10\%$ of the dose as initial bolus, followed by $90\%$ in a $1\mathrm{h}$ intravenous infusion). Block randomisation was done by a computer program at the coordinating centre. Participating study centres were not informed about the randomisation sequence or block size. Random assignment was done by drawing a sealed envelope at each centre; these envelopes were opaque, sequentially numbered, and allowed to be opened only after a definite confirmation of the participant’s study eligibility. Doctors and nurses in the emergency room were not masked to the treatment allocation because the administration of allocated drugs differed and could not be concealed, whereas doctors and nurses in the stroke unit treating the patients during the post-thrombolysis hospital stay during the follow-up period were masked to treatment group. Patients were not told which drug they had received. The doctor on call enrolled patients in the trial, and the thrombolysis nurse on call opened the random allocation envelope and gave the treatment; neither of whom were further involved in the trial.  

# Procedures  

NIHSS scores were obtained at baseline before random assignment and at 2 h, $24-48\mathrm{h}$ , and 7 days (or at discharge, if discharged earlier) after treatment. The NIHSS score at baseline was assessed by the doctor on call in the emergency room, and NIHSS scores at the other time­ points were assessed by a stroke nurse or doctor in the stroke unit. Patients’ pre-stroke Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores were roughly estimated at enrolment and then refined after obtaining information from family members or carers. We assessed mRS at 7 days (or at discharge, if discharged earlier) post-treatment, and at 3 months via a telephone or an outpatient consultation. mRS scores at 7 days or discharge were obtained by a stroke nurse or, in some cases, by the treating doctor in the stroke unit. At 3 months, mRS scores were obtained by dedicated stroke nurses and were registered on a separate case report form to keep observers masked to the treatment allocation. Personnel assessing NIHSS and mRS scores were trained in the assessment of patients with stroke. Certification in NIHSS and mRS was recommended, and local investigators were responsible for the training of observers at each participating study centre.  

Before the assessment of thrombolytic treatment and study inclusion, all patients had a CT scan with CT angiography in the emergency room, or an MRI scan in the radiology department. Additional CT studies were done at the discretion of the stroke unit doctors. MRI was preferred and CT was optional for cerebral imaging 24–48 h after treatment. Intracranial haemorrhage was defined as either any haemorrhagic infarction, paren­ chymal haemorrhage, or intraventricular haemorrhage, using a modified version of The European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study 1 criteria.14 The same criteria were applied for the morphological description, dividing intracranial haemorrhage into haemorrhagic infarction type 1 and 2, parenchymal haemorrhage type 1 and 2, remote parenchymal haemorrhage, and intraventricular haemorrhage. Symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage was defined by the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study 3 criteria as any intracranial haemorrhage causally related to an increase of 4 points or more in NIHSS score, or death.15 A radiologist at each study centre, who was masked to the treatment allocation, analysed the images as part of routine tests. All images of intracranial haemorrhages were additionally assessed at the coordinating centre at Haukeland University Hospital, Bergen by experienced stroke radiologists who were also masked to the allocated treatment. The causes of stroke were defined according to the Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment classification.  

# Outcomes  

The primary study endpoint was a favourable functional outcome at 3 months, defined as an mRS score of 0–1 or a return to baseline if the pre-stroke mRS score was 2. Secondary endpoints consisted of any intracranial haemorrhage occurring within 24–48 h of treatment, symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage occurring within $24-48\mathrm{~h~}$ , major neurological improvement at $24\textrm{h}$ , an ordinal shift analysis of mRS at 3 months, poor functional outcome defined as an mRS score of 5 or 6 at 3 months, and death within 3 months. Major neurological improve­ ment was defined as a reduction in NIHSS score of at least 4 points at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ compared with baseline. Owing to the low number of patients, we were unable to do meaningful sub-group safety analyses, but did all of the other presepecified analyses.  

# Statistical analysis  

Because of the practical advantages of a single dose of tenecteplase versus a 1-h infusion of alteplase, a noninferiority analysis was selected for the primary outcome in NOR-TEST 2. For the power analysis, we first estimated the non-inferiority margin. In NOR-TEST, approximately $75\%$ of the patients received intravenous thrombolysis within $^\textrm{\scriptsize3h}$ after symptom onset.13 On the basis of this finding, and a meta-analysis comparing the efficacy of alteplase versus a placebo,16 an absolute difference in effect (ie, mRS score of 0–1) between alteplase and placebo for the study population was estimated to be $8\cdot7\%$ ( $95\%$ CI 6·0–11·4). A non-inferiority margin of $3\%$ was chosen on the basis of clinical considerations and because this number preserves at least half of the conservatively estimated treatment effect of alteplase compared with placebo (with a lower $95\%$ CI limit of $6\%$ ).  

The NOR-TEST dataset, which only included patients with an NIHSS score of 6 or more at baseline $(\mathrm{n}{=}379)$ , showed an absolute difference in favourable functional outcome (ie, mRS score 0–1) of $7.6\%$ between tenecteplase $(48\cdot6\%)$ and alteplase $(41\cdot0\%$ ; unpublished data). For the power analysis, a more conservative estimate of $7\cdot0\%$ was chosen as the absolute difference in proportions in primary outcomes between tenecteplase and alteplase. With a power of $90\%$ a non-inferiority margin of $3\%$ , and using a one-sided Farrington-Manning test for non-inferiority at a significance level of 0·025, the total sample size was estimated to be 1036 patients using a 1:1 random assignment. Tenecteplase was considered non-inferior to alteplase if the lower bound of the two-sided $95\%$ CI in the absolute difference of proportions (tenecteplase to alte­ plase) of patients with favourable outcomes in primary endpoint was more than than $-3\cdot0\%$ . If non-inferiority was established, superiority was declared if the lower bound of the two-sided $95\%$ CI was more than zero.  

Safety reviews by an independent data monitoring committee (appendix pp 5–9) were performed cumu­ latively after each 200 patients were enrolled, and after each 20 patients with an NIHSS score of 15 or more were enrolled. The reason to conduct safety reviews after enrolment of each 20 patients with an NIHSS score of 15 or more was based on a post-hoc analysis from NOR-TEST, which showed increased mortality in patients with an NIHSS score of 15 or more receiving tenecteplase.17 If the safety review after each 200 patients showed a difference in symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage frequency of $5\%$ or more between the treatment groups, study inclusion would be put on hold, and random assignment would be unmasked after the 3-month follow-up period of the last included patient. In the event of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage rates being higher in the tenecteplase group than the alteplase group, an attempt would be made to identify a subpopulation within the tenecteplase group with a marked increased bleeding risk. In this event, the trial would continue with a reduced tenecteplase dose for this subpopulation. Available scientific data on different doses indicate that $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase would be the next most suitable dose.8,11,12 If a specific subgroup could not be found, the first part of the trial would be ended as a part A and the trial continued as a part B with the reduced tenecteplase dose for the entire tenecteplase group $(0{\cdot}25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase vs $0{\cdot}9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ alteplase). Part A would, in this case, be considered a separate trial for analysis and reporting purposes. The data safety monitoring committee charter is provided in the appendix $(\mathrm{pp}5-9)$ .

The primary analysis of outcomes was done as a modified intention-to-treat analysis without stratification. The population for the primary analysis comprised randomly assigned patients who received the study medication and did not withdraw their informed consent. Missing data for $\mathrm{mRS}$ scores at 3 months and NIHSS scores at $24\mathrm{h}$ were not replaced. The per-protocol analysis excluded patients found to have disorders other than stroke (stroke mimics), patients with a pre-stroke mRS score of more than 2, patients with an NIHSS score on admission less than 6, and patients with a missing $\mathrm{mRS}$ score at 3 months. The patients included in the safety analysis were the modified intention-to-treat population.  

Descriptive methods were used to characterise the study population. Primary and secondary outcomes were tested with the $\chi^{2}$ test, Fisher’s exact test, or ordered logistic regression (mRS score shift analysis). The $\chi^{2}$ test was used for categorical variables, and Fisher’s exact test was used in the case of a small sample size. Ordinal logistic regression was performed in consistency with the NOR-TEST study.13 The assumptions of the ordinal logistic regression were assessed, and no violation was found. The significance level was set at $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}05$ . Logistic regression analysis was performed to compute un­ adjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs). ORs were adjusted for baseline variables that were significantly different between treatment groups in the univariate analyses of clinical characteristics, as specified in the statistical analysis plan. We used the normal approxi­ mation (Wald formula) to derive $95\%$ CIs around absolute risk differences. All analyses were done with STATA special edition 14.0. This trial is registered with EudraCT (number 2018–003090–95) and in ClinicalTrials. gov (number NCT03854500) on Feb 26, 2019.

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.  

# Results  

Study enrolment began on Oct 28, 2019. Enrolment was stopped on Sept 26, 2021, after a safety review of the first 200 patients showed an imbalance in the rates of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage between the treatment groups, which surpassed the prespecified criteria for unmasking treatment allocation. The masked safety analysis was performed per protocol by the independent data safety monitoring committee. Only the independent data safety monitoring committee members, and members of the executive working group (CEK and LT), had access to the masked results of the safety analysis at this time.  

The dataset was unmasked on Dec 26, 2021, after the last patient to be enrolled had been followed up for 3 months. All patients were followed up within 14 days of the end of the 3-months follow-up period. The inde­ pendent data safety monitoring committee, the executive working group, and the study steering committee had access to the unmasked dataset at this time. After analysis, the independent data safety monitoring committee could not identify specific subgroups with a marked increased bleeding risk within the study population. Therefore, the first part of the trial was ended as part A, for analysis and reporting purposes, and is reported here. Part B of the trial is continuing per protocol, with a reduced tenecteplase dose for the entire tenecteplase group $(0\cdot25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase vs $0{\cdot}9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ alteplase).

Baseline characteristics of the modified intention-totreat population are shown in table 1. On univariate analysis, patients in the tenecteplase group were noted to be older than patients in the alteplase group, and less frequently had an mRS score of 0 on admission. # Discussion  

Our study did not show non-inferiority of $0{\cdot}4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase to a standard dose of alteplase in moderate or severe ischaemic stroke. In the modified intention-to-treat population, favourable functional outcome at 3 months occurred less frequently in patients allocated tenecteplase compared with those allocated alteplase. Additionally, the rates of intracranial haemorrhage, poor functional out­ come, and mortality were higher in the tenecteplase group. In the per-protocol analysis, fewer patients administered tenecteplase had a favourable outcome and major neurological improvement, and the prevalence of intracranial haemorrhage was higher, compared with those who received alteplase.  

These findings contrast with some previous studies. In NOR-TEST, in which $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase was compared with $0{\cdot}9~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ alteplase, outcomes were similar between groups and no differences were found concerning intracranial haemorrhage, mortality, or other adverse events.13 Additionally, the frequencies of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage did not differ in 379 patients with an NIHSS score of 6 or more on admission (ie, patients similar to those included in NOR-TEST 2; unpublished data: nine $[5\%]$ in the tenecteplase group vs six $[3\%]$ in the alteplase group, $\mathrm{p}{=}0\cdot43)$ . Instead, the crude rates of favourable outcome were $7\cdot6\%$ higher in the tenecteplase group, which formed the basis of the power analysis for NOR-TEST 2.  

The unfavourable clinical effect of the $0{\cdot}4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ dose of tenecteplase is also in contrast to a pooled analysis of the EXTEND-IA TNK trials.18 In these two trials, patients eligible for thrombolysis as a bridging therapy before thrombectomy were included. The pooled analysis did not show any differences in $\mathrm{mRS}$ score at 3 months between $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase and standard dose alteplase, neither in patients younger nor older than 80 years. Regarding the safety outcomes, however, the pooled analysis showed a higher rate of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage in the $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase group than in the alteplase group, and $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase was also associated with an increased risk of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage and mortality compared with $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase. These results are consistent with our findings.  

The high rates of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage in patients administered tenecteplase in NOR-TEST 2 accord with findings of a small dose-escalation study that compared the safety and efficacy of $0.1\mathrm{mg/kg},0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ and $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase in patients with acute ischaemic stroke.7 Before the premature termination of that study (because of slow recruitment), the $0{\cdot}4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ group was discarded as inferior after three symptomatic intracranial haemorrhages had occurred in 19 treated patients $(16\%)$ compared with one $(3\%)$ of 31 patients treated with $0{\cdot}9~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ alteplase. No differences were noted in the frequency of asymptomatic intracranial haemorrhage between $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase and standard dose alteplase (two of 19 patients $[11\%]$ in the tenecteplase group and four of 31 patients $[13\%]$ in the alteplase group).  

The results of NOR-TEST 2 are unexpected when compared with the findings of NOR-TEST. One possible explanation for this discrepancy might be related to the imbalance in age between the treatment groups. Patients randomly assigned to tenecteplase were on average almost 5 years older than were patients receiving alteplase, and they more often had a disability (mRS score ${\geq}1$ ) before treatment. Also, more patients administered tenecteplase ended up with a final diagnosis of ischaemic stroke, whereas the alteplase group included more stroke mimics, which are associated with a natural good prognosis.19 Nevertheless, it is unlikely that these disparities account for all differences in outcome variables between the treatment groups, because the adjusted analysis performed per protocol still showed worse functional and safety outcomes for patients receiving tenecteplase. Another factor to be considered is that the prevalence of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage in the alteplase group might have been lower than expected. A metaanalysis done in 2016 showed a prevalence of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage of $3\cdot9\%$ in 3046 patients who had a stroke treated with alteplase with an NIHSS score of 5 or more.20 However, our study applied a slightly different definition for symptomatic intracranial haemor­ rhage, which required a causal relation between the haemorrhage and clinical deterioration. Such minor differences might affect the frequency of bleeding rates across different studies.  

A limitation of NOR-TEST 2 is its premature termination, albeit per protocol, because of a possible increased risk of harm. The inclusion of fewer patients than the power analysis calculated increases the risk of falsely rejecting the hypothesis of $0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase being non-inferior to alteplase. The results of NOR-TEST 2 part A should therefore be interpreted with caution.  

Another limitation of NOR-TEST 2 is that some variables were skewed between the treatment groups at baseline. It is unlikely that this was caused by irregu­ larities in the random allocation of treatment. Neither local study nurses nor investigators had any knowledge of the randomisation sequence or block size, and the envelopes were not opened unless there was an eligible patient. All exceptions in the randomisation process were registered, as noted for the eight patients who were excluded because a contraindication to thrombolysis was registered after opening of the randomisation envelope. We believe that the skewed variables occurred by chance. Also, the envelope method of randomisation applied in NOR-TEST 2 was identical to the method used in NOR-TEST, in which groups of approximately 500 patients were balanced concerning the same baseline variables. Random assignment with envelopes might, however, be a limitation in studies with a planned low number of patients, since electronic internet random assignment might provide a minimal sufficient balance approach.  

A modified intention-to-treat population was defined, whereby patients who did not receive thrombolysis or who withdrew their informed consent were excluded from the analysis. The rationale for this definition was that inclusion of any patient who did not receive thrombolytic treatment was likely to cause substantial bias to the results. The use of statistical data for patients who have withdrawn their informed consent is not allowed, nor ethically justifiable.  

Tenecteplase has benefits over alteplase in that it is more fibrin-specific, more resistant to plasminogen activator inhibitor, and has a longer half-life, which enables administration of the drug as a single intravenous bolus.21 The rapid administration method of tenecteplase is a considerable advantage in the setting of acute ischaemic stroke compared with the 1-h infusion of alteplase, which might be prone to interruptions and complicate the logistics for patients who need transportation. In our opinion, and similar to its use in treatment of myocardial infarction, we believe that tenecteplase will at some point replace alteplase as the thrombolytic drug of choice in ischaemic stroke. However, the optimal dose of tenecteplase has been uncertain. Several phase 2 trials have shown safety and indicated efficacy using $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase in highly specific stroke populations, but few large trials are using clinical endpoints and broad inclusion criteria.8–11 Up to now, the data for use of tenecteplase in ischaemic stroke have been summarised in a meta-analysis, which showed non-inferiority of tenecteplase to alteplase.22 However, $69\%$ of the patient population in that meta-analysis came from the NORTEST trial $(0{\cdot}4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase) that mostly included patients who had a minor stroke, which has a natural beneficial prognosis. The findings of NOR-TEST 2 part A $(0{\cdot}4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase) and those of EXTEND-IA TNK part 2 $0{\cdot}25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ vs $0{\cdot}4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase) suggest that $0{\cdot}25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ could be the dose of choice for tenecteplase in ischaemic stroke. However, evidence is needed from phase 3 trials assessing efficacy and safety of $0{\cdot}25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ tenecteplase versus alteplase. Future stroke trials should assess a lower dose of tenecteplase versus alteplase in patients with moderate or severe stroke.
