# ARTICLE   HISTORY  

Abstract: Introduction: Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a type of non-traumatic SAH that can have detrimental effects on the central nervous system, resulting in severe disability or death.  

Methods: Early nimodipine is currently the only strongly recommended pharmacological treatment that has shown efficacy in improving neurological/functional outcomes in aSAH patients. Whether statin treatment is of benefit to aSAH patients is an issue that has generated considerable interest and debate. In the present scoping review, we mapped and analyzed the available literature on metaanalyses of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) examining the effect of statins on aSAH. Seventeen meta-analyses of RCTs, published between 2008 and 2023, were identified.  

Results: Treatments in included meta-analyses were based on various regimens of simvastatin, pravastatin, pitavastatin or atorvastatin for up to 21 days. Eleven of the included reports indicated some beneficial effect of statin treatment, reducing rates of at least one of the following: cerebral vasospasm, delayed cerebral ischemia/delayed ischemic neurologic deficit, mortality or functional/ neurological outcome. In contrast, six meta-analyses, showed no such effects.  

Conclusion: The limitations reported by several meta-analyses, included low patient numbers or disproportionate representation of patients from certain RCTs, differences in drug treatment, patient diagnostic criteria and outcome evaluation between RCTs, as well as poor data quality or lack of RCTs data. Knowledge of the reported limitations may aid the design of future clinical trials and/or their meta-analyses.  

Keywords: Statins, aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, aSAH, cerebral vasospasm, delayed cerebral ischemia, delayed ischemic neurologic deficits.  

# 1. INTRODUCTION  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a type of non-traumatic SAH, occurring in approximately $85\%$ cases of aneurysmal rupture [1, 2]. It mainly affects patients over 50 years of age and can lead to detrimental effects on the central nervous system (CNS) [3, 4]. Of note, approximately $50\%$ of patients that survive SAH are thought to have long-term cognitive impairments [3, 5]. After aSAH, aneurysmal re-rupture can be successfully managed by surgical clipping or endovascular coiling [6-8].  

While numerous therapies have been proposed [9, 10], early nimodipine is the only strongly recommended pharmacological therapy for the prevention of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) and improvement of functional outcomes in aSAH patients [11]. DCI represents one of the most feared complications of aSAH, may be present in $30\%$ of patients and has a typical onset between 4 and 10-days posthemorrhage [12]. It has been previously proposed that clinical studies aimed at assessing DCI prevention strategies should incorporate both cerebral infarction identified on CT or MRI and functional outcome [11].  

Clinical deterioration caused by DCI, often termed delayed ischemic neurologic deficit (DIND) is characterized by focal (e.g. hemiparesis, aphasia, hemianopia, neglect) or global neurological impairment, the latter indicated by a decrease of 2 points in the Glasgow Coma Score [12, 13]. DIND and cerebral vasospasm (CVS), another common complication of aSAH that is typically diagnosed angiographically, are two terms that should not be used interchangeably [12], more so since CVS does not always translate to neurological deficits [13].  

Statins, exert pleiotropic effects [14-17], independently of their cholesterol-lowering abilities [3, 18]. They are 3- hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) inhibitors which are capable of upregulating endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), stabilizing endothelial cells, inhibiting AQP4 and ET-1 expression and thus protecting cerebral vessel autoregulation [19] demonstrating fibrinolytic, neuroand angiogenetic [20], anti-oxidant, as well as antiinflammatory effects [9, 10, 15, 21]. Based on these properties, numerous clinical studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), have previously assessed the potential of statin treatment in preventing CVS and DIND and/or in improving neurological/functional outcome in aSAH patients, albeit with contradictory findings [6, 19, 22-43].  

In the present scoping review, we aimed at identifying and analyzing meta-analyses of RCTs reporting the effects of statin treatment in aSAH thus providing the current landscape of the pertinent literature. To the best of our knowledge this is the first scoping review on this topic. Meta-analytical studies typically assess the quality of identified studies as well as sources of variability between them, including parameters contributing to clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Knowledge of these factors can thus aid the design of future pertinent RCTs and/or their metaanalyses.  

# 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS  

The present review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses (PRISMA) extension for scoping reviews [44]. The PubMed database was searched using the keywords “statin” AND “subarachnoid” AND “meta\*” in advanced search builder. The Scopus database was searched using the same strategy for Abstract, Title and Keyword. Retrieved results up until 1/8/2023 were analyzed further.  

Peer-reviewed meta-analyses of RCTs, examining the effect of statins in aSAH patients in the English or German language were included. Non-peer reviewed studies, observational studies, single center studies, case series, case reports, narrative or systematic reviews without meta-analysis, letters to the editor, short communications, animal studies and gray literature, as well as studies published in languages other than the aforementioned were excluded. Original studies were initially identified and screened based on the grounds of the above criteria.  

The retrieved results were screened accordingly based on the abovementioned criteria. This process was conducted independently by two authors (PS, MM) through manual abstract and title screening of the publications and a third author (AK) participated in the screening process in cases of disagreement. Selected articles were subsequently examined in full text for eligibility. Data was extracted from the included studies using a standardized form and the following information was recorded: design and aim, population, number of participants, detailed description of the interventions used and outcomes. The data was summarized in Table form.  

# 3. RESULTS  

Our search strategy identified a total of 162 studies (80 results from PubMed and 82 results from Scopus) that included 48 duplicates. An additional report was identified through full-text reading. Based on our inclusion criteria and following full-text reading of twenty-one reports, seventeen studies were finally included in the present scoping review. A flow diagram showing the study selection process is provided in Fig. (1).  

Eleven of the seventeen included reports indicated some beneficial effect of stating treatment (Table 1). In the earliest meta-analysis identified, Sillberg et al. [45] included 3 double-blind RCTs, incorporating 158 patients. Two of the trials included 39 participants and one trial included 80 participants. In addition, two of them used $80~\mathrm{mg}$ of simvastatin daily, whereas the third used $40~\mathrm{mg}$ of pravastatin daily. The authors of this study concluded that the use of statin therapy decreased the rates of vasospasm, delayed ischemic deficits and mortality after aneurysmal SAH [45]. The metaanalysis of Kramer and Fletcher [46], incorporated 309 patients from 4 RCTs and from 2 additional studies published only in abstract form and designated as “pseudo-RCT” due to reasons related to their randomization. Of these four RCTs, simvastatin at $80~\mathrm{mg}$ daily for a maximum of 14 days was used by three with one study utilizing pravastatin (40 $\mathrm{mg/d}$ for 14 days), while the remaining two studies used either pravastatin ( $\mathrm{40~mg}$ per day until discharge) or simvastatin ( $\mathrm{80~mg}$ per day for up to 21 days). The results of this study indicated a significant effect of statin treatment in RCT on DIND [46]. Reduced mortality rates reached significance only when the two RCTs published in abstract form were excluded from the analysis [46]. An additional report by Tseng and collaborators [47] published in the following year, carried out a meta-analysis on the same RCTs as Kramer and Fletcher [46] and confirmed their findings. The subsequent meta-analysis of 6 RCTs by Su SH et al. on 249 patients who received $40~\mathrm{mg}$ of Pravastatin or $80~\mathrm{mg}$ of Simvastatin within 48-92 hours for 14 or 21 days, showed that statin treatment in patients with aSAH decreased mortality and DIND, whilst the incidence of vasospasm and poor neurological outcome remained unaffected [48]. The authors indicated that while statin treatment should not yet be considered the standard of care for patients with aSAH [48], statins display the potential to reduce mortality in these patients. In 2015, Zhang et al. conducted another metaanalysis of RCTs that included 347 patients with aSAH [49]. Patients in the statin group received $40~\mathrm{mg}$ of Pravastatin or $80~\mathrm{mg}$ of Simvastatin for 14 or 21 days and the study results indicated that acute administration of statins in patients with aSAH decreases DIND and mortality rates, but not the incidence of CVS on transcranial Doppler or the risk of poor outcomes [49]. The meta-analysis of Zhu et al. [28] included data from 9 RCTs/“pseudo-RCT”, treating patients with simvastatin (40 or $80~\mathrm{mg}$ ) and pravastatin $(80~\mathrm{mg})$ for up to 21 days. Their sub-group analysis for RCT-based data on simvastatin indicated that the incidence of vasospasm was the only parameter reduced by the statin treatment [28].

Akhigbe et al. examined 1214 patients, 587 of which received statin therapy ( $\mathrm{40~mg}$ of Pravastatin for 14 days or $40/80~\mathrm{mg}$ of Simvastatin for 14/15/21 days) [50]. The results of this study showed no significant reduction in mortality and no statistically significant difference in the neurological outcome (as assessed with the Modified Rankin Scale for Neurologic Disability -mRS-and Glasgow Outcome Score -  

GOS) between the two groups [50]. Nevertheless, the authors of this study indicated that the use of statins can decrease the occurrence of cerebral vasospasm and DIND in patients with aSAH [50]. The meta-analysis of Choi et al. [32] included data from 8 RCTs/pseudo-RCT for a total of 1150 patients. Their findings on RCT indicated that the subgroup of patients receiving higher statin doses ( $\mathrm{80~mg}$ simvastatin daily for up to 21 days) displayed lower rates of DIND and mortality [32].

In the meta-analysis of Shen et al. on 1597 patients, 700 patients received $40/80~\mathrm{mg}$ of Simvastatin for 14-21 days or $40\mathrm{mg}$ of Pravastatin daily for 14 days, while the rest received a placebo. No significant difference was reported in the disability (evaluated using the mRS score or GOS), or neurological prognosis between the two groups, however, Pravastatin and Simvastatin appeared to reduce the incidence of CVS, DCI, DIND, and mortality [3]. Liu et al. conducted a meta-analysis of nine RCTs, including the STASH trial, with a total of 1464 patients, receiving Simvastatin, Pravastatin, Pitavastatin or Atorvastatin for 14- 21 days. Statins were associated with reduced CVS, whereas a short treatment statin course over 2 weeks also appeared to improve neurological prognosis [51]. The most recent report identified was by Zhong et al. [52], which indicated that the analysis of six RCTs significantly improved the functional prognosis of aSAH patients. Interestingly, this study highlighted pravastatin as the most effective option in reducing ischemic cerebrovascular events, which was superior to simvastatin [52]. While an effect of statin treatment on DCI/ DIND was the most commonly reported positive finding, it is noteworthy that improved patient neurological/functional outcome was the least frequently reported positive result (Table 1).  

In contrast to the aforementioned studies, 6 metaanalyses showed no beneficial effects of statin use. Thus, the earliest meta-analysis by Vergouwen et al., on 190 patients, 94 of whom received statin treatment $\mathrm{80~mg}$ of Simvastatin, or $40\mathrm{mg}$ of Pravastatin daily for 14-21 days) and 96 placebo therapy, showed no statistically significant effect on TCD established vasospasm, DCI, neurological outcomes or mortality rates [53]. Subsequently, Liu and Chen conducted a meta-analysis that incorporated data from 6 RCTs on 1031 patients [54]. There was no observed effect of statin treatment (simvastatin at doses of 40 or $80\mathrm{mg/day}$ for up to 21 days, or pravastatin at a dose of $40~\mathrm{mg/day}$ for up to 14 days) on CVS, DCI, or functional outcome [54]. Similarly, the meta-analysis by Liu H et al. including 1053 patients, receiving $80~\mathrm{mg}$ or $40~\mathrm{mg}$ of Simvastatin daily for 14/15/21 days or 3 weeks showed that statins have no impact on DIND, delayed cerebral infarction, neurologic outcomes, vasospasm, ICU or hospital stay and mortality [55]. Furthermore, Lin et al. published a meta-analysis on a total of 951 patients (mean age 53 years) who were administered either $40~\mathrm{mg}$ or $80~\mathrm{mg}$ of Simvastatin for 14 or 21 days, or a placebo treatment [56]. No beneficial effect of statins was reported and the authors concluded that patients should not be treated routinely with simvastatin during the acute stages of aSAH [56]. In their 2021 meta-analysis on 1068 patients from six RCTs including the STASH trial, Yu et al. [57] showed no effect of three statins (pravastatin, simvastatin, pitavastatin) on clinical outcome based on GOS, Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOSE) or mRS scores.  

Lastly, Bohara et al. studied 1622 patients, with 791 receiving either $40~\mathrm{mg}$ of Pravastatin for 14 days, $40/80~\mathrm{mg}$ of Simvastatin for 14-21 days, $4~\mathrm{mg}$ of Pitavastatin for 14 days and $20~\mathrm{mg}$ of Atorvastatin for 14 days [6]. The results showed that the use of statins in patients with aSAH results in beneficial effects on functional outcomes and mortality, which nevertheless did not reach statistical significance [6].  

# 4. DISCUSSION  

In the present scoping review, we mapped the available literature on meta-analyses of RCTs assessing statin treatment in aSAH patients. Of the seventeen meta-analyses identified, eleven indicated some beneficial effect of statin treatment while six found no such effect. Of note, while the effect of statin treatment on DCI/DIND was the most commonly reported positive finding, improved patient neurological/functional outcome was the least frequently reported positive result.  

The reported limitations of the included meta-analyses are worth pointing out. Thus, three of the identified metaanalyses, two showing a beneficial effect [45, 48] and one showing no such effect [53], reported a small sample size as one of their main limitations. These three earlier metaanalyses had patient samples of less than 250 and did not incorporate data from two large multicenter RCTs on simvastatin, namely the Simvastatin in Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (STASH), which showed no shortor long-term benefit [37] or the high dose of Simvastatin for aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (HDS-SAH), which showed no effect of the higher statin dose [20]. The limitations of the early study of Sillberg et al. [45], such as its underpowered nature and poor data quality have been commented upon by several authors [58, 59]. Disproportionate representation of patients from the STASH trial was on the other hand reported by two subsequent meta-analyses [50, 56], and two additional studies highlighted the inclusion of small RCT cohorts [51, 55] that were hypothesized to be more prone to an overestimation of treatment effects [51].  

Two of the aforementioned meta-analyses [51, 56] further indicated variable severity of recruited patients between RCTs. In this context, of note is that post hoc analysis of the STASH trial had previously indicated that unlike treatment modality (clipping or surgery), poor admission World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade (WFNS), Fisher grade on the initial computed tomography scan or the development of DCI were the risk factors associated with poor outcome at six months [60]. Clinical scales such as the WFNS and Hunt and Hess are currently strongly recommended to determine initial clinical severity and predict outcomes [11].  

Differences in diagnostic criteria for CVS and DIND between RCTs were reported as a limitation by several studies [28, 45, 46, 54]. Of note, thus far, the extent to which imaging modalities used for the diagnosis of vasospasm or treatments against vasospasm have an impact on patient outcomes in SAH remains unknown and warrants investigation in future RCT studies [61].  

Several meta-analyses indicated differences in drug treatment as a significant unavoidable limitation resulting in heterogeneity [6, 28, 48, 49, 51, 55] with one study raising the issue of lack of pharmacokinetic or pharmacokinetic interaction data for simvastatin [55]. In this context, it is also noteworthy that due to their differential pharmacokinetics distinct statins may interact differently with various other drugs and that simvastatin and lovastatin are two statins frequently displaying drug interactions [62].  

Differences in outcome evaluation that stem e.g. from the use of different outcome scales or the assessment of patients at different time points were noted as a limitation by several meta-analyses [6, 48, 49, 51]. Current guidelines recognize this variability in global outcome scale use (e.g. GOS and mRS), as well as the timing of administration and stress the pressing need for an aSAH-specific outcome scale to understand the true disease impact [11].  

Of note, only one meta-analysis assessed statin intervention in ICU and hospital stay [55]. While this meta-analysis indicated no effect of simvastatin on either parameter [55], some evidence for a positive effect of statin intervention on the need for rescue therapy and on inpatient stay in sepsis complicated aSAH has been presented [63].  

The limitations of the present review need to be acknowledged: first and foremost, the evaluation (methodological or otherwise) of the findings in the included metaanalyses was beyond the scope of the present review. We rather indicated reported limitations that may provide guidelines for future RCTs and or meta-analytical designs. In addition, the present findings are limited in scope by our searches that were based on two databases.  

Finally, it is important to point out that the recent (2023) guidelines of the Neurocritical Care Society recommend against the initiation of statin treatment to reduce DCI and improve functional outcomes in aSAH [64]. This recommendation is based on evidence from eight RCTs [64], with the STASH-trial playing the biggest role among the evidence taken into consideration. Although well-designed and adequately powered, the STASH trial was criticized because of its half-dose of simvastatin $(40\mathrm{mg/day)}$ compared to the other RCTs $(80\mathrm{mg/day)}$ , and this difference was thought to influence the efficacy of simvastatin [56]. However, a recent trial also showed no superiority of high-dose $(80\mathrm{mg/day)}$ compared to lower-dose $\mathrm{40~mg/day)}$ simvastatin treatment for patients with aSAH [20]. Until more well-designed RTCs are available, the STASH-trial, although not lacking criticism, will continue to be the hallmark in formulating recommendations and also influence the results of relevant meta-analyses.

# CONCLUSION  

The present findings indicate that the meta-analyses showing any beneficial effect of statins in aSAH outnumber the ones showing no effect (Fig. 2). Moreover, the metaanalyses demonstrating beneficial effects indicated that statin treatment was associated with lower rates for at least one of the following: CVS, DCI/DIND, mortality or functional/neurological outcome. Of note, the limitations reported by several meta-analyses included low patient numbers or disproportionate representation of patients from certain RCTs, differences in drug treatment, patient diagnostic criteria, severity at presentation and outcome evaluation between RCTs, as well as poor data quality or lack of RCT data (e.g. incorporated studies in abstract form). The reported limitations could be taken into consideration during the design of future RCT studies e.g. on specific categories of aSAH [33] and/or their meta-analyses.
