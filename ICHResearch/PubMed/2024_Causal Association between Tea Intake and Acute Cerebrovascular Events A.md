# Causal Association between Tea Intake and Acute Cerebrovascular Events: A Multivariate Mendelian Randomized Study in European Populations

# ABSTRACT  

Background: Numerous research works have investigated the association between tea consumption and the risk of acute cerebrovascular events; however, the results are inconsistent.  

Objectives: We used Mendelian randomization (MR) to evaluate the causal association between tea intake and several acute cerebrovascular events, including any ischemic stroke, large atherosclerotic stroke (LAS), cardiogenic embolic stroke (CES), small vessel stroke (SVS), intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH).  

Methods: We obtained summary genome-wide association study (GWAS) data on tea intake and acute cerebrovascular events in populations of European ancestry. The GWAS on tea intake is derived from the UK Biobank, where we have chosen single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) closely associated with it as instrumental variables. We also obtained summary data on ischemic stroke from a GWAS meta-analysis, as well as summary data on ICH and SAH from the FinnGen study. We first explored the causal association between tea intake and several acute cerebrovascular events using univariate Mendelian randomization (UVMR), and then further assessed the causal association between tea intake and SVS using multivariate Mendelian randomization (MVMR) corrected for multiple confounders.  

Results: In UVMR, genetically predicted increases in tea intake were linked to a lower risk of SVS (OR: 0.58; $95\%$ CI: 0.39, 0.86). There was no causal association between tea intake and the risk of other acute cerebrovascular events. In the MVMR, our results show that there was still a significant causal association between drinking tea and SVS, after adjusting body mass index, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, and alcohol consumption.  

Conclusion: This MR study provides new genetic evidence that increased tea intake reduces the risk of SVS in the European population. However, possibly because of limited statistical power, the study did not find that tea consumption reduced the risk of several other acute cerebrovascular events.  

Keywords: tea, acute cerebrovascular events, stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, Mendelian randomization  

# Introduction  

Acute cerebrovascular events, defined as sudden and severe impairment of cerebral perfusion or vascularity, include both ischemic and hemorrhagic categories, and are one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide [1]. Ischemic cerebrovascular events, including ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack, are a series of symptoms caused by ischemia and hypoxia in brain tissue because of narrowing or blockage of cerebral arteries [2]. Hemorrhagic cerebrovascular events, which encompass intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), are a series of symptoms caused by intracranial or subarachnoid bleeding resulting from the rupture or leakage of cerebral arteries [2]. According to studies, acute cerebrovascular events have higher medical costs than other vascular diseases and the total medical costs will triple if effective interventions are not taken promptly [3]. Because acute cerebrovascular events impose a significant economic burden on society and families, early intervention and prevention are critical [4,5]. The connection between diet and acute cerebrovascular events has slowly gained attention in recent years. Several studies have demonstrated that a high-quality diet is essential for the successful prevention of acute cerebrovascular events [6 8].  

Tea is one of the most widely consumed beverages in the world [9]. Tea contains various bioactive components, such as polyphenols and flavonoids, which have beneficial effects on vascular health through multiple mechanisms. These mechanisms include relaxing smooth muscle contraction, enhancing endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity, reducing vascular inflammation, inhibiting renin activity, combating vascular oxidative stress, improving endothelial function, inhibiting cell proliferation, suppressing angiogenesis, antiatherosclerosis, antithrombosis, and cardioprotection [10 12]. Drinking 2 3 cups of tea per day significantly lowered the risk of stroke compared with persons who did not consume tea or coffee, according to a prospective cohort study by the UK Biobank [13]. A prospective cohort study involving 487,377 participants from the Kadoorie Biobank in China showed that the risk of ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes decreased with increasing daily tea consumption [14]. All of these observational studies, however, have a risk of being affected by confounders and reverse causation. As a result, it is unclear whether tea consumption has a causal relationship with acute cerebrovascular events. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a research method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to estimate the potential causal association between exposure and outcome [15,16]. Because genetic variants are randomly assigned to offspring during meiosis, which is independent of traditional confounding factors as well as reverse causality, MR can achieve the effect of simulating a randomized controlled trial [17]. Therefore, in this study, we used a 2-sample MR analysis to assess the causal association between tea intake and acute cerebrovascular events.

# MR assumptions  

MR study needs to meet the following 3 assumptions (Figure 1): 1) IVs were strongly correlated with exposure; 2) IVs were not correlated with any known confounders; and 3) IVs were not directly associated with outcome and affected outcome only through exposure [17].

# Outcome data sources  

We extracted information on SNPs for ischemic stroke and its subtypes from a GWAS analysis in the MEGASTROKE consortium [19]. This GWAS summary data involved 67,162 cases and 454, 450 controls. To reduce bias caused by population stratification, we selected data from the European ancestry sample only, including any ischemic stroke (AIS; $n=34{,}217.$ ), large atherosclerotic stroke (LAS; $n=4373.$ ), cardiogenic embolic stroke (CES; $n=7193.$ ), and small vessel stroke (SVS; $n=5386.$ ). The GWAS summary data of ICH and SAH was obtained from the FinnGen study, involving 2794 cases of ICH and 1338 cases of SAH. GWAS summary data for ICH and SAH are available in the MRC IEU Open GWAS project database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac. uk/) (ICH: finn-b-I9_INTRACRA and SAH: finn-b-I9_SAH).

# Selection of IVs  

We selected SNPs significantly associated with tea intake genome-wide $(P<5\mathrm{E}{-8})$ as IVs and clumped at $\mathrm{R}2<0.1$ and windows ${>}10{,}000\mathrm{kb}$ to eliminate linkage disequilibrium (LD). SNPs that were significantly associated with the outcome $(P<$ 5E-8) or were not available in the outcome GWAS were removed. In addition, we eliminated ambiguous palindromic SNPs. Hypertension, high cholesterol, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, high homocysteine (HCY), and atrial fibrillation are considered risk factors for ischemic acute cerebrovascular events [20], whereas hypertension, diabetes, alcohol consumption, and being underweight are considered risk factors for hemorrhagic acute cerebrovascular events [21]. To avoid potential confounding, we retrieved each SNP in the PhenoScanner database with settings of $P<5\mathrm{E}{-8}$ , $\mathrm{R}2>0.8$ (http:// www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) and removed SNPs associated with the above risk factors. Finally, we calculated the $R^{2}$ and F statistics for each SNP. $R^{2}$ indicates the proportion of variance in exposure factors explained by IVs. The F statistic represents the strength of the association between IVs and exposure, with an F statistic ${>}10$ indicating a low risk of bias in weak IVs [22]. Finally, the data on tea intake and acute cerebrovascular events obtained from different studies, indicate that the results are unlikely to be influenced by sample overlap.  

However, this study also has certain limitations. First, it has been shown that the incidence of different stroke subtypes varies by race [45 47]; to avoid population stratification, only GWAS data of European ancestry were used, which may make it difficult to generalize our conclusions to other populations. Furthermore, because of the limited information provided by GWAS data, we were unable to further narrow down the racial/ethnic/ancestral/heritage of the study population. Second, although we avoided known confounding factors as much as possible, our findings may still be affected by some unobserved confounding factors. Third, for the several MR analyses with negative results, we may not have sufficient statistical power to detect an association between tea intake and these several acute cerebrovascular events, and therefore these results should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, because appropriate and complete GWAS summary data for HCY were not obtained, we were unable to estimate the causal effect of tea intake on SVS after adjusting for HCY. Further studies are needed to explore the association between tea intake, HCY, and SVS. Fifth, because of the linear nature of our conducted MR study, we were unable to determine the optimal range of tea intake. We look forward to future follow-up nonlinear MR studies to provide a more definitive understanding in this regard. Finally, different types of tea (such as black and green tea) are consumed differently in different regions and contain different levels of chemical compounds, but because of the lack of detailed information on tea types, we were unable to assess whether the effects of different tea types on acute cerebrovascular events unanimous.  

In conclusion, this MR study suggests that genetically predicted tea intake is a causal protective factor for SVS in a European population. However, possibly because of limited statistical power, the study did not find that tea consumption reduced the risk of several other acute cerebrovascular events.
