# Abstract  

Background: Multiple interventions, including catheter‐directed therapy (CDT), systemic thrombolysis (ST), surgical embolectomy (SE), and therapeutic anticoagulation (AC) have been used to treat intermediate to high‐risk pulmonary embolism (PE), but the most effective and safest treatment remains unclear. Our study aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety outcomes of each intervention.  

Methods: We queried PubMed and EMBASE in January 2023 and performed a network meta‐analysis of observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCT), including high or intermediate‐risk PE patients, and comparing AC, CDT, SE, and ST. The primary outcomes were in‐hospital mortality and major bleeding. The secondary outcomes included long‐term mortality ( $\mathtt{\backslash}{\mathtt{\partial}}\mathtt{\partial}$ months), recurrent PE, minor bleeding, and intracranial hemorrhage.  

Results: We identified 11 RCTs and 42 observational studies involving 157,454 patients. CDT was associated with lower in‐hospital mortality than ST (odds ratio [OR] $195\%$ confidence interval (CI)]: 0.41 [0.31−0.55]), AC (OR $195\%$ CI]: 0.33 $\left[0.20\substack{-0.53}\right])$ , and SE (OR $195\%$ CI]: 0.61 [0.39−0.96]). Recurrent PE in CDT was lower than ST (OR $195\%$ CI]: 0.66 [0.50−0.87]), AC (OR $195\%$ CI]: 0.36 [0.20−0.66]), and trended lower than SE (OR $195\%$ CI]: 0.71 [0.40−1.26]). Notably, ST had higher major bleeding risks than CDT (OR $195\%$ CI]: 1.51 [1.19−1.91]) and AC (OR $195\%$ CI]: 2.21 [1.53−3.19]). By rankogram analysis, CDT presented the highest $p$ ‐score in in‐ hospital mortality, long‐term mortality, and recurrent PE.  

Conclusion: In this network meta‐analysis of observational studies and RCTs involving patients with intermediate to high‐risk PE, CDT was associated with improved mortality outcomes compared to other therapies, without significant additional bleeding risk.

# 1 | INTRODUCTION  

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE) is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. PE can be classified as high‐risk (massive), intermediate‐risk (sub‐massive), and low‐risk, depending on hemodynamic stability and right ventricular strain.1 Since intermediate to high‐risk PE is a leading cause of in‐hospital death, numerous studies have sought to improve its outcomes  

Various interventions for acute PE have been evaluated and continue to be an area of active investigation. The 2019 European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommends systemic thrombolysis (ST) as a preferred treatment option for acute high‐risk PE or rescue therapy in deteriorating intermediate‐risk PE unless there is a contraindication.1 However, its high bleeding risk is of concern.2 For this reason, the PE Response Team (PERT) consortium does not recommend ST as first‐line therapy for intermediate‐risk PE. Surgical embolectomy (SE) is recommended for those with contraindications to fibrinolytic or after an unsuccessful attempt of thrombolysis, especially those with high‐risk PE, as it may provide improved favorable efficacy and safety outcomes.3,4 Meanwhile, catheter‐ directed therapies (CDT), which are alternative treatments for intermediate to high‐risk PE, have been attracting attention because of their safety, efficacy, and accessibility.5 The multidisciplinary PERT concept also recommends using CDT for intermediate‐high risk PE or high‐risk PE with high bleeding risk.3 However, there is a paucity of data regarding CDT with limited prospective trials. Thus, there has been difficulty in comparing the efficacy and safety of this method with other advanced therapies.  

Despite the accumulating evidence regarding the treatment options for acute PE, direct comparisons among these interventions have yet to be extensively performed. Herein, we performed a network meta‐analysis to compare ST, CDT, AC, and SE and aimed to elucidate the safest and most effective treatment for intermediate to high‐risk acute PE.  



# 2 | METHODS  

This study was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines6 and enrolled in the PROSPERO (ID: CRD42022352591). An institutional review board/ethics exemption was granted for the innocuousness of the study.  



# 2.1 | Data sources and search strategy  

A comprehensive literature search of PubMed and EMBASE was conducted in 1/2023 individually by two authors (YI, AW). All randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and observational studies comparing at least two methods of treatment/intervention among patients with intermediate or high‐risk PE were included. The following search terms were applied; (high OR intermediate‐high risk OR [intermediate low risk] OR [massive] OR [submassive]) AND (PE) AND ([oral anticoagulation (AC)] OR [heparin] OR [enoxaparin] OR [ST] OR [tissue plasminogen activator] OR [catheter‐directed thrombolysis] OR [mechanical thrombectomy] OR [embolectomy]). We then manually searched secondary sources, such as references from initially identified studies, to collect relevant articles comprehensively. No restrictions on language and publication date were applied.  



# 2.2 | Eligibility criteria  

The eligibility criteria were as follows: a study was published in a peer‐reviewed journal; the design was either a retrospective/ prospective cohort study or RCT that compared at least two different treatment strategies for intermediate or high‐risk PE, including AC ST, CDT, and SE; a study that reported outcomes of interest listed below. In this study, we defined CDT as either catheter‐directed thrombectomy or thrombolysis, with or without ultrasound guidance.  



# 2.3 | Data extraction  

The following details from each study were extracted and tabulated: study characteristics (publication year, study design, comparison intervention, cohort size, the median reported follow‐up period), baseline patient characteristics (age, sex, concomitant diseases or illnesses, underlying conditions, PE risk factors) and outcome measures.  

The risk of bias for non‐randomized studies was assessed using a tool for evaluating the risk of bias for non‐randomized studies of interventions,7 while the Cochrane Collaboration's tool was used for randomized studies. Two investigators (Y. I. and A. W.) independently examined selection, comparability, and outcomes. Disagreements were resolved through consensus.  



# 2.4 | Outcomes  

Our primary efficacy outcome was in‐hospital mortality. When in‐ hospital mortality was unavailable, 5‐day through 30‐day mortality were alternatively collected. Our secondary efficacy outcomes were long‐term mortality (≥6 months) and recurrent PE. We used the results of recurrent PE at the longest follow‐up in each study for recurrent PE cases. The primary safety outcome was major bleeding, and the secondary safety outcomes were minor bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage.  



# 2.5 | Data synthesis and statistical analysis  

We extracted unadjusted and adjusted (whenever available) odds ratios (ORs) for each study. When studies reported propensity score matching or inverse probability treatment of weighting, we used these values for our meta‐analyses. The ORs with a $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) for each outcome were synthesized using R 4.0.3 with the “netmeta” 4.0.3 package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Random effects models were used for all analyses. We calculated $I^{2}$ as a measure of variation across the studies and interpreted them as low when $<25\%$ , moderate when $25\%-50\%$ , and high when $>50\%$ . We also assessed the inconsistency, representing the variability of treatment effects between direct and indirect comparisons at the meta‐analytical level. A two‐tailed $p$ Value of less than $5\%$ was considered statistically significant. In addition, the procedures were ranked using the $p$ ‐scores of $0\mathfrak{-1}$ , which was considered 1 when a procedure was certain to be the best and 0 when a procedure was certain to be the worst.8 Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger's test.9  

For the primary safety and efficacy outcomes, we performed a subgroup analysis stratified by the severity of PE. Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding observational studies not reporting adjusted ORs.

# 3.1 | In‐hospital mortality  

The network diagram of the primary outcome is shown in Figure 2. Data that compared in‐hospital mortality among patients with intermediate or high‐risk PE were available in 54 studies. There was significantly lower in‐hospital mortality among patients who underwent CDT compared with AC (OR 0.33; $95\%$ CI $\left[0.20\substack{-0.53}\right];$ , SE (OR 0.61; $95\%$ CI [0.39−0]), and ST (OR 0.41; $95\%$ CI [0.31−0.55]) (Figure 3). There was no significant difference in in‐hospital mortality with SE compared with ST and AC groups. In addition, no significant difference in in‐hospital mortality was observed between ST and AC (Figure 3). There was significant heterogeneity $(I^{2}=68\%)$ , as well as inconsistency $(p<0.0001)$ .  

We stratified the outcome based on PE severity. 14 studies compared in‐hospital mortality in high‐risk PE patients. All comparisons demonstrated no significant difference in in‐hospital mortality between treatment modalities (Supporting Information: Figure 5). Twenty‐ seven studies compared in‐hospital mortality in intermediate‐risk PE patients (Supporting Information: Figure 6). CDT was associated with a lower risk of in‐hospital mortality than AC (OR 0.46; $95\%$ CI [0.27−0.79]). Otherwise, there was no statistically significant difference between groups regarding mortality.

# 3.3 | Recurrent PE  

Seventeen studies reported recurrent PE. When compared to AC only, CDT (OR 0.36; $95\%$ CI [0.20−0.66]) and ST (OR 0.55; $95\%$ CI [0.32−0.94]) were associated with a lower risk of recurrent PE (Figure 5). CDT had less recurrent PE than ST (OR 0.66; $95\%$ CI [0.50–0.87]). There was low heterogeneity $(I^{2}=24.2)$ , with no significant inconsistency $(p=0.18)$ .  



# 3.4 | Major bleeding outcomes  

Data that compared major bleeding outcomes among patients with intermediate to high‐risk PE were available in 43 studies. AC only was associated with a lower risk of major bleeding than ST (OR 0.45; $95\%$ CI [0.31−0.65]) (Figure 6). In addition, CDT was associated with a lower risk of major bleeding than ST (OR 0.66; $95\%$ CI [0.52−0.84]). There was heterogeneity $(I^{2}=53.6\%)$ with inconsistency $(p<0.0001\$ .  

When stratified by PE risk, ST was associated with significantly higher major bleeding risk than SE in high‐risk PE (OR 4.89; $95\%$ CI [1.07−22.4] (Supporting Information: Figure 7). In intermediate‐risk PE, no significant difference in major bleeding risk was noted among all groups (Supporting Information: Figure 8).

# 3.5 | Minor bleeding outcomes  

20 studies showed that AC had a significantly lower risk of minor bleeding than CDT (OR 0.50; $95\%$ CI [0.29−0.87]) and ST (OR 0.25; $95\%$ CI [0.18−0.35]) (Supporting Information: Figure 9). CDT was associated with a significantly lower risk of minor bleeding than ST (OR 0.50; $95\%$ CI [0.29−0.85]). There was low heterogeneity $(I^{2}=2.7\%)$ with no significant inconsistency $(p=0.42)$ .  



# 3.6 | Intracranial hemorrhage  

Nineteen studies compared intracranial hemorrhage in intermediate to high‐risk PE. Compared to the ST group, AC only (OR 0.33; $95\%$ CI [0.14−0.76]), SE (OR 0.47; $95\%$ CI [0.24−0.92]), and CDT (OR 0.53; $95\%$ CI [0.37−0.75]) had significantly lower intracranial hemorrhage (Supporting Information: Figure 10). There was moderate heterogeneity $(I^{2}=45.3\%)$ with significant inconsistency $(p=0.01)$ .

# 3.8 | Sensitivity analysis  

When excluding observational studies not reporting adjusted ORs, there remained significantly lower in‐hospital mortality in CDT compared to ST (OR 0.52; $95\%$ CI [0.37−0.73]) and AC (OR 0.44; $95\%$ CI [0.24−0.81]) (Supporting Information: Figure 11). Although no significance was found, CDT had lower trends in mortality compared to AC and ST in both in‐hospital intermediate‐risk PE and long‐term outcomes (Supporting Information: Figures 12, 13). Recurrent PE was significantly lower in CDT compared to ST (OR 0.57; $95\%$ CI [0.33−0.96]) and AC (OR 0.33; $95\%$ CI [0.13−0.85]), and a lower trend of recurrent PE was noted compared to SE (OR 0.66; $95\%$ CI [0.32−1.35]) (Supporting Information: Figure 14). Regarding major bleeding, no significant difference was noted between each group, which was also pointed out in the intermediate‐risk PE subgroup (Supporting Information: Figures 15, 16). The only significant finding in minor bleeding was AC having a lower risk than ST (Supporting Information: Figure 17). ICH did not show any difference between groups (Supporting Information: Figure 18).

# 4 | DISCUSSION  

Our meta‐analysis reports comparative outcomes between multiple interventions for PE, which included the following findings. First, CDT was associated with lower risks of in‐hospital mortality compared with AC, SE, and ST in both intermediate and high‐risk PE. CDT also showed improved outcomes in long‐term mortality and recurrent PE, suggesting the efficacy of this method. Second, the risk of major bleeding was higher in ST than AC and CDT. CDT showed significantly lower rates of minor bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage than ST.  

![](images/4c390256cb5ba06d6212191e7a6d34e7e23f058abc39a7c9a4fc02f62f33f609.jpg)  
FIGURE 6 Network meta‐analysis for major bleeding in intermediate to intermediate to high‐risk pulmonary embolism: (A) versus anticoagulation only group (B) versus catheter directed therapy (C) versus surgical embolectomy (D) versus systemic thrombolysis.  

Several strategies have been developed to treat acute PE. The efficacy of fibrinolytic compared to AC‐only has been shown in intermediate to high‐risk PE.11,12 However, due to the substantial bleeding risk of ST, safer and more effective treatments are needed to improve outcomes. One of the essential factors in increasing systemic perfusion and survival lies in facilitating right ventricular recovery. SE can be a salvage therapy for those with contraindications to fibrinolytic or after an unsuccessful attempt of thrombolysis.4 Our study showed a trend toward lower mortality for SE compared to ST. The limitation of SE is that it is only available in large centers where cardiopulmonary bypass is available. CDT showed a significant benefit in terms of in‐hospital mortality in our study. CDT can achieve immediate reperfusion without causing hemodynamic decompensation and is an accessible option.13  

Although ST can reduce mortality in patients with intermediate to high‐risk PE,11,12 it carries a significant risk of bleeding outcomes compared to AC.14 It is also suggested to have higher bleeding risks compared to CDT and SE. In terms of CDT, it is shown to have a comparable safety profile even when compared to AC.5,15–17 Several observational studies investigating intermediate to high‐risk PE reported that CDT was not associated with higher bleeding risks.18,19 This trend was further corroborated by RCTs as well.5,17 Moreover, previous studies have described a lower bleeding risk of CDT than ST, possibly because of lower or no thrombolytic therapy.20 However, pulmonary hemorrhage and intracranial hemorrhage can be a complication of CDT and although there are lower risks this must be considered in applying to individual cases.13 Similar findings were noted in studies comparing SE to ST.21,22 Our study showed potential trend in benefits of SE over CDT in major bleeding in high‐risk PE. However, despite lower bleeding risks in SE, there is a concern for high mortality, especially in the setting of high‐risk PE; thus guideline recommendation is still ST which is more accessible in emergent situations.22  

In our network meta‐analysis, we were able to compare the mortality and bleeding risks following the treatments as specified in our study for patients with intermediate to high‐risk PE. The major strength of our study is that this is the first network meta‐analysis to our knowledge that investigated various interventional strategies to assess mortality and bleeding risks in patients with intermediate to high‐risk PE. Previous retrospective studies and RCTs that we included in this analysis focused on comparing two intervention strategies but did not evaluate risks among the four mentioned strategies, including AC, CDT, SE, and ST. Even in comparison of the two groups, there is a paucity in the data comparing SE versus CDT and ST versus CDT outcomes. Meta‐analyses reporting outcomes of CDT have reported only single‐arm outcomes23 or compared with another method,24,25 and did not describe outcomes between the four interventions we investigated. Our results may provide further insight into the utility and benefits of CDT, as well as enabling to have an overview of the outcomes of each intervention.  

There are limitations to this study. First, due to the nature of meta‐analysis, we were unable to consider the individual indications or clinical circumstances. Treatment options depend on a patient's hemodynamic instability, the need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, or thrombi location.3 However, we included studies with a range of treatments. Given the lack of direct comparisons in clinical trials between some of the interventions, our study can aid in identifying differences in outcomes that have yet to be reported. Second, there was a disparity in the baseline characteristics between the patients receiving different treatments. Since some studies reported only unadjusted OR, which could suffer from selection bias and confounding, our findings should be interpreted cautiously. However, our sensitivity analysis, excluding the observational studies not reporting adjusted ORs, resulted in similar trends, raising the robustness of our results. Third, we included many observational studies, and only 11 were RCTs. Only three RCTs were investigating CDT in our study.5,17,2 6 These studies have a small population; hence the results are difficult to generalize. Observational studies of CDT could suffer from selection bias when choosing which patients should proceed with intervention. Thus, we must consider the risk of overestimating the effect of CDT. Additionally, we could not entirely separate catheter‐directed thrombectomy from catheter‐ directed thrombolysis since some of the studies combined both treatment strategies, hence we could not compare the actual effect of each mode of CDT. Fourth, there was a discrepancy in the definition of major and minor bleeding. Prospective studies with prespecified definitions will better clarify the safety profile of each treatment strategy.  



# 5 | CONCLUSIONS  

CDT was associated with lower in‐hospital mortality in high‐risk or intermediate‐risk PE patients compared to SE, AC, and ST. CDT was associated with a lower major bleeding risk compared to ST. The major limitation of this study is that it is based primarily on an observational study‐based analysis without a homogenous population. Further investigations, especially RCTs, could clarify the utility of the different interventions for managing intermediate to high‐risk PE.
