# Restart TICrH: An Adaptive Randomized Trial of Time Intervals to Restart Direct Oral Anticoagulants after Traumatic Intracranial Hemorrhage  

Truman J. Milling, Jr,1 Steven Warach,1 S. Claiborne Johnston,2 Byron Gajewski,3 Todd Costantini,4 Michelle Price,5 Jo Wick,3 Simin Roward,6 Dinesh Mudaranthakam,3 Adrienne N. Dula,1 Ben King,7 Alexander Muddiman,1 and Gregory Y.H. Lip8  

# Abstract  

Anticoagulants prevent thrombosis and death in patients with atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism (VTE) but also increase bleeding risk. The benefit/risk ratio favors anticoagulation in most of these patients. However, some will have a bleeding complication, such as the common trip-and-fall brain injury in elderly patients that results in traumatic intracranial hemorrhage. Clinicians must then make the difficult decision about when to restart the anticoagulant. Restarting too early risks making the bleeding worse. Restarting too late risks thrombotic events such as ischemic stroke and VTE, the indications for anticoagulation in the first place. There are more data on restarting patients with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage, which is very different than traumatic intracranial hemorrhage. Spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage increases the risk of rebleeding because intrinsic vascular changes are widespread and irreversible. In contrast, traumatic cases are caused by a blow to the head, usually an isolated event portending less future risk. Clinicians generally agree that anticoagulation should be restarted but disagree about when. This uncertainty leads to long restart delays causing a large, potentially preventable burden of strokes and VTE, which has been unaddressed because of the absence of high quality evidence. Restart Traumatic Intracranial Hemorrhage (the ‘‘r’’ distinguished intracranial from intracerebral) (TICrH) is a prospective randomized open label blinded end-point response-adaptive clinical trial that will evaluate the impact of delays to restarting direct oral anticoagulation (1, 2, or 4 weeks) on the composite of thrombotic events and bleeding in patients presenting after traumatic intracranial hemorrhage.  

Keywords: adult brain injury; clinical management of central nervous system injury; clinical trial; head trauma; traumatic brain injury

After these events, clinicians and patients must make the agonizing decision about when to restart anticoagulation. Restarting too early risks making the intracranial bleeding worse. Restarting too late risks all manner of thrombotic events, such as stroke and VTE; that is, the reason they were taking the anticoagulant in the first place. There are few data to guide them. There are more data on restarting patients with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage, but this is a very different disease than traumatic intracranial hemorrhage.4–6 Spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage increases the risk of a future hemorrhage. The intrinsic vascular changes that cause bleeding with anticoagulation, such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy or microvascular injury from hypertension, are widespread and irreversible.7 This means that the first intracranial hemorrhage portends another. In contrast, traumatic intracranial hemorrhage is caused by a blow to the head. The shockwave overcomes vascular resistance at a given point or shears the vessels.5 It is, in most cases, an isolated event portending less future risk. Clinicians who care for trauma patients and experts agree that anticoagulation should be restarted, but they disagree about when.8.9 This uncertainty leads to a responsibility transfer, a ‘‘kick-the-can-down-the-road’’ phenomenon, that in turn leads to long delays in restarting. These delays cause a large burden of strokes and VTE in these vulnerable, elderly patients. Most clinicians wait ${}^{21}$ month to restart even though as many as 1 in 10 patients will have a thrombotic event during that month.9,10 This death and disability may be preventable.  

Recent follow-up studies indicate that risk of rebleeding in traumatic cases becomes very low beyond the 1st week after injury, even if anticoagulation is restarted.10–14 On the other hand, the risk of thrombotic events (especially stroke and VTE) is very high at the 1st week because of a prothrombotic state induced by trauma, bleeding, and hospitalization.15 This risk remains high indefinitely until the anticoagulant is restarted.4,6  

Early restarting of anticoagulation should reduce thrombotic events with only a very small increase in major bleeding.6 However, that conclusion is based on observational studies, and there are no randomized trial data. Guidelines are cautious, recommending decisions on a case-by-case basis.16 This lack of empirical evidence leaves clinicians prone to their well-described conservative attitudes about avoidance of bleeding in anticoagulation decisions .17,18 Highquality evidence is direly needed to deal with these dilemmas.  

Restart Traumatic Intracranial Hemorrhage (the ‘‘r’’ distinguished intracranial from intracerebral) (TICrH) a prospective randomized open label blinded end-point (PROBE) response adaptive clinical trial of restarting anticoagulation in traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, will be conducted through the Coalition for National Trauma Research (CNTR). CNTR coordinates the development, funding, and conduct of multi-site trauma research at medical schools and trauma centers across the United States. This trial will be conducted at 40 level 1 and level 2 trauma center sites (See Supplementary Text 1 for network size and enrollment rationale), which see $>2{,}700$ anticoagulant-associated traumatic intracranial hemorrhages per year.3 CNTR is directly linked to the American College of Surgeons – Committee on Trauma (ACS-COT), a founding member of CNTR and the verifying body of United States trauma centers. # Patient population  

Patients evaluated for acute traumatic intracranial hemorrhage at research sites will be screened for eligibility after clinical stabilization. Patients who meet criteria (Table 1) or their legally authorized representatives will be asked to consent to enrollment. Site trauma registry data indicate that these patients are elderly (median age 80; range 59–99) and that almost all have fall-from-standing isolated brain injuries.3  

# Inclusion/exclusion criteria justification  

Fall risk. We did not exclude patients based on fall risk. One analysis showed that a patient would need to fall almost daily for a year to negate the benefit of stroke prevention from anticoagulation therapy.19 In this model, fall risk was not a relevant factor in the clinical decision to start treatment.19 Fall risk is a common reason that clinicians withhold anticoagulation,20–22 but there is little evidence that it should preclude treatment.19,22 (See Supplementary Text 2 for full discussion.)  

DOACs. Restarting is limited to DOACs (although anticoagulant at index bleeding is not) as opposed to Vitamin K antagonists (VKA), such as warfarin. VKA delayed onset would confound a timing trial, and heparin bridging is not standard in atrial fibrillation, which we expect to be the majority indication. DOACs are associated with lower intracranial hemorrhage risk and lower overall bleeding risk, which would be preferred in patients who have just had life-threatening bleeding.23 It is also likely clinicians would not be compelled by the results on VKA, especially if bleeding is great. They could reasonably contend that the result would have been different with only DOACs, which are rapidly replacing warfarin (Fig. 2).  

# Equipoise  

Clinical equipoise regarding restarting anticoagulation is different for spontaneous versus traumatic intracranial hemorrhage. For spontaneous hemorrhage, there is uncertainty about whether patients should ever be restarted. For traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, the main question is when to restart; not whether. In a global survey of 228 neurologists, neurosurgeons, and thrombosis experts, $98\%$ stated that traumatic patients should be restarted, but they demonstrated wide variability about when.8 Most preferred to restart by 1 month (Fig. 3). The survey authors noted that ‘‘randomized trials are direly needed in this population.’’ Another neurosurgical survey also found most clinicians preferred restarting within a month.9 This equipoise informed our selection of time intervals.  

# Methods  

# Objective  

Restart TICrH aims to show that early anticoagulation restart after traumatic intracranial hemorrhage reduces major thrombotic events at only a small cost in recurrent major bleeding.  

# Treatment  

DOAC is at label dose with label adjustments for creatinine clearance, and at continuation dose, i.e., not initial therapy high doses in the setting of VTE.

# Primary end-point  

A 60-day composite endpoint that includes the following clinical events: new or expansion of intracranial hemorrhage, other $\mathrm{BARC}_{3\mathrm{a}}$ (the widely accepted Bleeding Academic Research Consortium scale which defines major bleeding) or above major hemorrhage,24 stroke, systemic embolism, myocardial infarction (MI), proximal lower extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism, and cardiovascular death (full definitions in Supplementary Text 3 and at www.RestartTICrH.org).

The algorithm decreases the probability of randomization to underperforming arms; that is, those with higher event rates. A second adaptative mechanism for the inclusion criteria is driven by total end-point event rate in all arms (and therefore does not require unblinding) (5). If total event rates are low, the trial will have difficulty detecting a difference between arms; therefore, the algorithm increases the subject end-point risk by increasing the CHA2DS2-VASc score inclusion criteria, enriching the sample for events. This makes detecting a difference between groups easier and prevents the trial from stopping for futility. The PROBE component of the trial is achieved with central blinded assessment of subjects for potential end-point events (by videoconference) with a blinded expert study end-point adjudication board determination of whether a valid end-point event has occurred.  

Composite justification. Composite end-points are standard in secondary prevention trials because they increase study power. Some recent trials of anticoagulation have separated the thrombotic and bleeding end-points. However, in the setting of anticoagulation restart decisions, a composite end-point that includes the relevant thrombotic and bleeding events closely approximates the actual clinical decision: a trade-off between thrombotic and bleeding risk.  

End-point timing justification. As all groups are anticoagulated, we do not expect separation of event curves past 60 days, which is 1 month past the last restart interval (Fig. 4). Any differences in events after 60 days would more likely represent random fluctuations or statistical noise rather than true signal from the difference in the timing of restart. We did not set the end-point prior to 60 days because this allows for the known time, 1 month, that it takes most clots to be replaced with connective tissue and resorbed into the vessel wall and the wall to re-endothelialize.25 Thrombi that developed prior to anticoagulation are at risk of embolization during this period.25 term mortality after DVT is ${<}5\%$ , its long-term mortality and morbidity are quite high.26 DVT patients have a fourfold risk of death versus controls, and an elevated risk for 8 years after the index thrombosis.26 Also, DVT patients rate their quality of life below patients with diabetes or chronic lung disease.27 As many as half of DVT patients develop post-thrombotic syndrome and chronic swollen legs.27 All of this supports including DVT in the primary composite end-point.  

Weighting. Exploratory analysis will use 60-day modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and Disability Rating Scale (DRS), two validated and widely used death and disability functional assessments,28–30 performed in follow-up on all patients. We will examine the disability of the end-point event beyond that of the index bleed disability to see if the opposing bleeding and thrombotic composite components are unequal. There are no published data on this incremental disability. A randomized trial is the only way to level the disability from the index event evenly across groups to enable the additional disability from the secondary event to be seen. We expect half our end-point patients to have ischemic strokes and half to have VTE, with a few having MIs. All will have some degree of brain injury from their index bleeding. DRS and mRS will be used to calculate an appropriate utility-weighting approach to account for the relative severity of bleeding and thrombotic events, as opposed to contributing 1:1 as they will during the trial (see Fig. 6).

# Power and sample size  

Using our Bayesian response adaptive randomization design with potential to stop early for success or futility, we plan to enroll a maximum of 1100 subjects (expected $10\%$ lost to follow-up, leaving 990 analyzable). The study has $87\%$ power (based on expected thrombotic event rates of 0.5, 3.5, and $6.25\%$ and bleeding rates of 1, 0.67, and $0.33\%$ in 1, 2, and 4 weeks respectively. See Supplementary Text 4 for full discussion). This is based on the expectation that the algorithm will allocate $50\%$ of the subjects to the treatment arm with the lowest event rate (which we suspect will be 1 week) and $21\%$ to the arm with the highest event rate (suspected to be 4 weeks). An independent data safety and monitoring committee will surveil for unanticipated effects.  

# Analysis of primary end-point  

Primary analysis plan. All interim and final analyses will be conducted on the primary end-point, the 60-day composite of thrombotic events and rebleeding. Both end-points will be modeled as a function of the three treatment arms in an (arm restart) time/response (events composite) model. Two Bernoulli models will be used to estimate thrombosis and rebleeding. Each of the rates for the Bernoulli values by arm are $\theta_{d}$ and $\delta_{d}$ for thrombosis and rebleeding, respectively. These rates are combined to provide a utility function $U_{d}\bar{=}-\theta_{d}-\delta_{d},$ so that higher values of $U_{d}$ correspond to the better arms.

# Closed loop translation  

CNTR is the largest trauma research network in the country. Each site is led by a trauma surgeon, who with neurosurgeons, most often make restart decisions after traumatic intracranial hemorrhage. CNTR investigators are in the nation’s largest level 1 and level 2 trauma centers with a high volume of traumatic intracranial hemorrhage cases. CNTR is directly linked to the ACS-COT, the governing body for United States trauma center verification, which manages the Trauma Quality Improvement Program (TQIP) with site visits to assess compliance with quality metrics. Using this network for performing a randomized clinical trial leverages an existing group of highly motivated and research-oriented physicianscientists to study a problem that is of importance to their daily clinical practice. Further, the trial findings will be rapidly implemented at trauma centers because of the linkages with ACS-COT and TQIP, an enforcement mechanism already in place (Fig. 7).

# Discussion  

When patients – usually the elderly who fall and hit their head – requiring anticoagulation because of high risk of stroke experience a traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, it is a major medical decision to determine when to restart anticoagulation. This time of anticoagulation restart has grave consequences for patients who must navigate between the high risk of stroke from anticoagulation withdrawal and risk of bleeding from restarting anticoagulation. There are no high quality data to guide decision making, despite the fact that ${>}18{,}000$ patients in United States trauma centers face this problem every year.3 In the 850 United States trauma centers alone, delay in restart of anticoagulation after traumatic intracranial hemorrhage contributes to an enormous burden of thrombotic events, roughly 800 ischemic strokes, 300 pulmonary emboli, and $500\mathrm{DVTs}$ annually.3 Most of these thrombotic events should be preventable with early restart of anticoagulation.  

A $6\%$ annual absolute reduction (estimated difference between 1 and 4 weeks restart) in thrombotic events in 18,055 anticoagulantassociated tICrH (in 2018) patients at 850 United States trauma centers in $\mathrm{TQIP}^{1}$ would eliminate ${>}1000$ stroke and VTE events. Based on existing literature, the risk of rebleeding is ${<}1\%$ by 1 week after traumatic intracranial hemorrhage.12–14 This would lead to a theoretical net benefit of five events prevented per 100 patients or a number needed to treat of 20. The reason that physicians eschew this likely net benefit is a lack of reliable prospective randomized trial data. A rigorous PROBE design – which would alleviate the cumbersome and expensive task of blinding while producing results comparable those of to double-blind studies32 – will produce the best evidence to help realize that benefit. Half the DOAC studies that led to initial United States Food and Drug Administration approval were PROBE. Post-hoc analysis found no difference in the validity of the PROBE studies versus the double blind studies. 32  

The trial design was informed by the published natural history of hematoma expansion, which is common in the first $^{241}$ but rare by day 3.33–36 Intracranial hemorrhage progression is almost never reported 1 week from injury, with the rare exception usually being large subdural hematomas .33–36 We excluded subdural hematomas ‡ 8 mm for this reason (see Supplementary Text 5 for full discussion).  

Response adaptive randomization allows for consideration of more than just ‘‘early’’ versus ‘‘late’’ restarting, facilitating a middle option (2 weeks) with little cost in terms of sample size. A two-arm trial could easily fail if 1 week is too early; that is, if there is too much bleeding, or 4 weeks is too late; that is, there is too much thrombosis. The ideal time interval at 2 weeks would be missed and would require another trial. An adaptive trial is an efficient way to consider more than two plausible scenarios in one trial without assigning too many patients to underperforming arms. The adaptive algorithm sees the underperformers and reduces the probability of randomizing to that arm, quickly to very close to zero, if the underperformance continues.

Adapting inclusion criteria automatically based on pre-defined decision rules is a useful strategy, but it is not entirely novel. Two acute ischemic stroke thrombectomy trials, DAWN37 and DEFUSE 3,38 had design features that would adapt stroke lesion size inclusion criteria if interim analyses indicated that differences between subgroups were small enough to threaten futility. However, these trials showed benefit in the total population, and, therefore, the features were never activated. Using $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -VASc to narrow the study population to higher risk patients who have more end-point events is a strategy to maximize the opportunity to show a difference between groups to prevent trial stopping for futility (Fig. 1). It is a variation on these two trials’ novel features. It will only be novel if it is actually used.  

Closed loop translation also represents a best practice model for high-level evidence. In chest pain centers, ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) door to needle times are a strictly enforced quality metric. It is the same for stroke with regard to administration of thrombolytic therapy. This trial potentially offers a similar degree of rigor for a quality metric in trauma: a door to restart of anticoagulation time. Rapid translation can be driven by the existing and highly respected oversight mechanism of the ACS-COT.  

# Conclusion  

Clinicians may be waiting too long to restart oral anticoagulation after traumatic intracranial hemorrhage. These elderly fall-fromstanding brain injury patients may be experiencing unnecessary thrombotic events as a consequence. A rigorous randomized blinded end-point clinical trial may provide the rationale for clinicians to abandon a conservative bias in anticoagulation decisions. A positive trial finding could prevent an enormous thrombotic disease burden harming vulnerable patients.
