# Abstract  

Objective  To investigate the safety and efficacy of intensive statin in the acute phase of ischemic stroke after intravenous thrombolysis therapy.  

Methods  A total of 310 stroke patients treated with rt-PA were randomly scheduled into the intensive statin group (rosuvastatin $20~\mathrm{mg}$ daily $\times14$ days) and the control group (rosuvastatin $5~\mathrm{mg}$ daily $\times14$ days). The primary clinical endpoint was excellent functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq1\$ ) at 3 months, and the primary safety endpoint was symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) in 90 days.  

Results  The intensive statin users did not achieve a favorable outcome in excellent functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq1)$ ) at 3 months compared with controls $70.3\%$ vs. $66.5\%$ , $p{=}0.464,$ . Intensive statin also not significantly improved the overall distribution of scores on the modified Rankin scale, as compared with controls $\overset{\_}{p}=0.82$ by the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test). The incidence of primary safety endpoint events (sICH) in 90 days did not significantly differ between the intensive statin group and control group ( $0.6\%$ vs. $1.3\%$ , $p{>}0.999,$ ).  

Conclusion  The INSPIRE study indicated that intensive statin therapy may not improve clinical outcomes compared with the low dose of statin therapy in AIS patients undergoing intravenous thrombolysis, and the two groups had similar safety profile. # Introduction  

Stroke is one of the leading causes of disability and death in humans, and acute ischemic stroke (AIS) accounts for approximately $80\%$ of total strokes [1]. The key of AIS treatment is to open blocked blood vessels as soon as possible to save the ischemic penumbra [2]. Recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) is the only thrombolytic drug used to treat AIS that is approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [3, 4]. A recently published meta-analysis of nine randomized controlled studies on thrombolysis confirmed the benefits of intravenous rt-PA thrombolysis within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ after stroke onset [5]. However, rt-PA carries an inherent risk of adverse cerebrovascular events associated with poor clinical outcomes, including symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), which occurs in $1.7{-}6.4\%$ of treated patients [6, 7]. The researchers are looking for a combination treatment to reduce the risk of adverse events and improve clinical prognosis for AIS patients received rt-PA.  

Statins have favorable effects in the treatment and prevention of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular diseases [8, 9], and the Stroke Prevention by Aggressive Reduction in Cholesterol Levels (SPARCL) trial demonstrated that a high dose of statin improved clinical outcomes for AIS patients [10]. However, whether statin therapy benefits AIS patients treated with rt-PA remains controversial. Multiple studies focused on the impact of prior statin use in AIS patients after intravenous (IV) thrombolysis. Some retrospective studies concluded that statin pretreatment was an independent predictor of favorable prognosis at 3 months in AIS patients receiving rt-PA [11, 12], but this beneficial effect was not replicated in later studies [13, 14]. Other retrospective studies concluded that the prior use of statins posed an excessive risk of intracranial hemorrhage to AIS patients treated with rt-PA, which raised new concerns about statin therapy [15, 16]. Few studies investigated the influence of statin use in the acute phase of stroke after IV thrombolysis and they indicated that statin therapy can improve the clinical outcome of AIS patients [17–19]. The choice of statin dose is also inconclusive. A retrospective study conducted by Scheitz et al. [11] found that pretreatment of low doses of statin produced favorable clinical outcomes in AIS patients who received rt-PA and did not increase the risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), but high and medium doses of statins led to an increased risk of sICH. However, another retrospective study suggested that the medium–high doses of statin used in the acute phase after rt-PA treatment produced more favorable clinical outcome in AIS patients [18]. A Chinese retrospective study also showed that higher doses of statins were associated with better clinical outcomes in patients and the early use of statins within $^{24\textrm{h}}$ after thrombolysis in ischemic stroke was safe [19]. However, there are no randomized controlled intervention studies that assessed the efficacy and safety of intensive statin treatment in the acute phase of stroke after IV thrombolysis.  

Therefore, the Intensive Statin Plus Intravenous rt-PA In Acute Ischemic Stroke (INSPIRE) study designed a prospective randomized controlled intervention to observe the safety and efficacy of different doses of statins in the acute phase of stroke after IV thrombolysis.  

# Methodology  

# Trial design and participants  

The INSPIRE trial is a multicenter, randomized, double-blind clinical trial performed in 16 Chinese institutions. Patients in this study were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the intensive statin group and control group. All patients were followed up five times within 3 months. Following-ups were performed on the day of randomization, $24-36\mathrm{h}$ after thrombolysis, 7 days, 14 days or the day of discharge, and 90 days. The study design of the INSPIRE trial is shown in Fig. 1 in Online Resource 2. This clinical trial is registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration number: ChiCTR-IPR-16008642). The institutional research ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University approved the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each subject. The full trial protocol is available in Online Resource 1.  

Patients were eligible for inclusion in the trial if they were (1) over the age of 18 years old, (2) had received rt-PA within $4.5\mathrm{h}$ after symptom onset and (3) signed informed consent by patients or their immediate family members. The following patients were excluded from the trial: (1) patients who were treated with endovascular thrombectomy; (2) had contraindications of intravenous thrombolysis; (3) had mRS score ${>}1$ before the onset of the current stroke; (4) had statin allergy history; (5) had previous active liver disease history; (6) had myopathy history; (7) had intestinal diseases that may affect drug absorption; (8) had serious heart, lung or kidney damage; (9) were pregnant or lactating women; (10) had malignant tumors; (11) participated in clinical trials of other drugs within previous 3 months; or (12) were taking statins before the onset of this stroke.  

# Randomization and masking  

This trial used a randomized block design. The randomization sequence was created by a statistician with no involvement in this trial, and this trial was stratified by the clinical center with a 1:1 allocation with a random block size of 4. The following blind evaluation methods were used: to preserve the masking of the patients and physicians involved in this trial, the details of the assigned group of the patient were given on cards which were contained in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by the statistician. A third party who did not participate in the study and did not know the group of the patients completed the follow-up data.  

# Intervention  

Patients were divided into the intensive statin group and the control group (low dose of statin group) in a 1:1 ratio. The following specific drug regimens were used. Patients in the intensive statin group were administered $20~\mathrm{mg}$ rosuvastatin every night for 14 days. Patients in the control group received $5~\mathrm{mg}$ rosuvastatin every night for 14 days. The interval between the first statin treatment and intravenous thrombolysis was not less than $^{24\textrm{h}}$ . The statin was taken every night before sleeping beginning on the second day. The patients received standard therapy on day 15 and thereafter according to the guidelines, in which a low dose of rosuvastatin therapy was recommended.

# Main outcome measures  

The primary clinical endpoint of the INSPIRE trial was excellent functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq1)$ at 3  months.  

Secondary clinical endpoints included favorable functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq2)$ at 3 months, neurological improvement $\mathrm{(NIHSS{\leq}4}$ from baseline) at 7 days, major neurological improvement $\mathrm{(NIHSS{\leq}8}$ points from baseline) at 7 days, and all-cause death within 3 months. The time was measured from the date of randomization.  

The primary safety endpoint of this trial was symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) using the following specific definition according to ECASS trial[20]: the patient suffered a brain parenchyma hemorrhage in the infarction area or parenchymal hematoma type 2 (PH-2), which were confirmed on computerized tomography (CT) within 7 days after thrombolysis treatment, and an increase of ${\mathrm{NIHSS}}\geq4$ points or death occurred in patients within $^{24\mathrm{h}}$ after thrombolysis therapy. Secondary safety endpoints were all adverse and severe adverse events within 3 months, skeletal muscular adverse events (including myalgia, myopathy, and rhabdomyolysis) within 3 months, $\mathrm{ALT}/\mathrm{AST}>3\times\mathrm{ULN}$ or $\mathrm{CK}{>}5\times\mathrm{ULN}$ at discharge, and deterioration of neurological function $(\mathrm{NIHSS}\geq4$ points from baseline or death) within 7 days.  

# Sample size estimation  

The study sample size was determined based on previous studies [17, 18] that demonstrated that the occurrence rate of the primary endpoint was approximately $39.8\%$ in the control group and $57.2\%$ in the intensive group, and 130 cases were needed per group ( $\upalpha{=}0.05$ , power $=0.8$ , twotailed test). Considering there would be approximately $20\%$ missing data in follow-ups, we set the preliminary estimated sample size at 320.  

# Statistical methods  

A full analysis set and per protocol set were used for the analyses of clinical endpoints and safety endpoints. We used independent sample $t$ -tests to compare between groups of continuous variables with a normal distribution, otherwise the Mann–Whitney $U$ test was used. Statistics of categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test. An ordinal shift analysis of the distribution of 90-day mRS score were performed by using Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test. Logistic regression analysis was used for the primary endpoint event $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq1$ at 3 months), and these variables were described using odds ratios (ORs) and $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs). We performed subgroup analyses for the primary clinical endpoint according to gender, age, smoking, alcohol abuse, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, previous TIA, previous ischemic stroke, diabetes, $\mathrm{NIHSS}\le3$ and The Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification. All of the statistics were two-tailed, and a $p$ value $<0.05$ was defined as a significant difference.  

# Results  

# Patients’ characteristics  

From June 2016 to February 2019, a total of 320 patients with AIS who signed informed consent were initially enrolled at 16 Chinese clinical institutions. Seven participants were excluded based on the time these patients received rt- $\mathrm{PA}>4.5\mathrm{h}$ , and three participants were excluded for prior use of statin. Therefore, a total of 310 patients were enrolled in the full analysis set. Among these patients, 52 patients in the per-protocol set were excluded. The reasons for exclusion were did not receive the allocated intervention (5 patients), lost to follow-up (24 patients), and medication compliance $<80\%$ (23 patients). The patient flow of the study is shown in Fig. 1. Due to the lack of vital differences in baseline characteristics, clinical endpoints and safety endpoints, only the results of the full analysis set was demonstrated in this article, and the results of per-protocol analysis are shown in the Online Resource 2 (Table 3, Table 4, Table 5 in Online Resource 2). Baseline data of the two groups is shown in Table 1, and all variables were balanced between the two groups. The total cholesterol and LDLcholesterol at 14 days were reduced more significantly in the intensive group compared to the controls (Table 2 in Online Resource 2).  

# Primary and secondary clinical endpoints  

The results showed no significant difference between the intensive statin group and the control group in the excellent functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq1$ at 3 months) $70.3\%$ vs. $66.5\%$ , $p=0.464)$ (Table 2). Logistic regression analysis showed that history of TIA $(\mathrm{OR}=9.86\$ , $95\%$ CI 1.46–66.56, $p=0.019.$ ) and atrial fibrillation $(\mathrm{OR}=2.16\$ , $95\%$ CI 1.05–4.45, $p{=}0.037.$ ) were risk factors of less than excellent functional outcome at 3 months (Table 1 in Online Resource2), and the results of peripheral vascular outcomes were not considered meaningful due to the low occurrence rate. In subgroup analysis, the incidence of primary endpoint did not differ in most subgroups between two groups, except that the control group have better primary clinical endpoints in the subgroup of hypertension and small artery occlusion (Fig. 2). Among patients who received intensive statin, the distribution of scores on the modified Rankin scale at 90 days was not improved, as compared with the control group $(p=0.82$ by the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test) (Fig. 3).  

The following results of secondary clinical endpoint analysis (Table 2) were found. The favorable functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq2)$ at 3 months showed no significant difference ( $81.9\%$ vs. $79.4\%$ , $p=0.565^{}$ ) between the intensive group and the control group. The rate of neurological improvement at 7 days $\mathrm{(NIHSS{\leq}4}$ from baseline) $(27.7\%$ vs. $44.2\%$ , $p{=}0.002)$ and major neurological improvement $\mathrm{(NIHSS}\leq8$ points from baseline) at 7 days ( $4.5\%$ vs. $9.0\%$ , $p=0.025,$ were lower in the intensive statin group compared to the controls. There was no statistically significant difference in the all-cause mortality within 3 months between the two groups ( $3.9\%$ vs. $1.3\%$ , $p{=}0.283,$ ).  

# Primary and secondary safety endpoints  

There was no significant difference between the two groups in the primary safety endpoint (symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage) $0.6\%$ vs. $1.3\%$ , $p>0.999.$ ) (Table  3).

# Discussion  

The INSPIRE study focused on the safety and efficacy of different doses of statins in the acute phase of stroke after IV thrombolysis and the acute phase of stroke is $^{24\mathrm{h}}$ to 1 week according to MR Imaging [21], so we set the duration of intensive statin therapy at 14 days which can cover the acute phase of stroke. Another reason for not extending intensive statin use is that long-term high-dose statin use may pose a higher risk of adverse effect to participants, including myopathy, kidney or liver damage and memory loss or confusion [22]. The results of our study showed that intensive statin may not improve the clinical prognosis in patients compared with the low dose of statin, and the two groups had similar safety profile.

Recent studies showed that statins reduced cholesterol and LDL and exerted neuroprotective effects, including endothelial protection, improvement of cerebral blood flow, and anti-inflammatory effects [23, 24]. A meta-analysis published in 2013 demonstrated that statin therapy at the onset of stroke improved the prognosis of 90-day neurological function and reduced the rate of death, disability and stroke recurrence [25]. Based on this evidence, the guideline recommends that patients who are taking statin before the onset of AIS should continue the statin therapy, and patients with AIS who qualified for statin treatment should receive statin therapy as soon as possible [26]. Although multiple evidence indicates that statin may have a favorable effect in AIS patients, the conclusion of current clinical studies on whether statin therapy benefits AIS patients treated with intravenous thrombolysis is inconsistent [11–14, 27]. Besides, most studies focused on the impact of prior statin use in AIS patients after intravenous (IV) thrombolysis, and only a minority of studies investigated the effect of statin use in the acute phase of stroke. Two retrospective studies by Cappellari et al. [17, 18] demonstrated that statin treatment in the acute phase of stroke after rt-PA treatment benefited patients in short- and long-term clinical outcome, but this favorable effect was not replicated in a small sample study [19]. The results of most studies only compared statin users with non-statin users and the dose of statins that provide the best clinical prognosis for AIS patients remains controversial [11, 18]. Only two studies compared high dose of statin and low dose of statin in the acute phase of stroke after IV thrombolysis, and their results demonstrated that the low and high dose of statin had similar clinical outcomes, which is consistent with our study [18, 19]. Based on these evidences, we conclude that statin use in the acute phase of stroke after IV thrombolysis may have a positive influence on patients, but intensive statin may not produce better results compared with a low dose of statin.  

As for safety, most previous studies suggested that the use of statins posed an excessive risk of intracranial hemorrhage to AIS patients treated with rt-PA. A metaanalysis of nine studies including 10,419 patients demonstrated that statin pretreatment increased the risk of symptomatic hemorrhagic transformation in AIS patients treated with rt-PA [28]. Scheitz et al. [11]suggested that the incidence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage after intravenous thrombolysis positively correlated with the dose of pretreatment statins. These findings may be due to the pleiotropic effects of statin in the promotion of thrombolytic efficacy, anticoagulation and antiplatelet effects [29–31]. Many experts believe that the effects of statins improve the clinical outcome of AIS patients via the promotion of recanalization, but it may also increase the risk of sICH [11, 28]. However, studies that focused on the impact of statin treatment in the acute phase of stroke after IV thrombolysis did not find an association between statin use and sICH [17–19]. The different results may be explained by the fact that patients who received statin therapy before stroke are more prone to have a history of vascular risk factors, anticoagulant therapy and antiplatelet therapy, which are known as risk factors for bleeding after thrombolysis. Furthermore, only one of these studies compared the high dose with the low dose of statin, and their results are consistent with our study that intensive statin did not pose a higher risk of sICH to patients [19]. Although there was no significant difference in primary and secondary safety endpoints during our clinical trial between two groups, the adverse events were higher in the high dose group, which indicate that the high dose statin may pose a higher risk of adverse effect to patients. However, the sample size of our study was small, and the results should be interpreted with caution.  

There are many differences between the INSPIRE study and previous studies that investigated the influence of statins in AIS patients treated with rt-PA. First, most previous studies were retrospective studies, and they investigate the impact of statin pretreatment in AIS patients treated with rt-PA. In these studies, whether patients who received statin treatment before stroke onset continued their use of statins in the acute phase of stroke was ambiguous. Therefore, these studies cannot assess the possible effect of statins in the acute phase of ischemic stroke after IV thrombolysis. Besides, patients who received statin therapy before stroke also had more vascular risk factors compared to controls, which reduces the credibility of these studies. Due to that the inclusion of patients with a long history of statin use would increase the heterogeneity of the study, we only included statin-naïve patients. A minority of retrospective studies investigated the impact of statin treatment that started in the early stage of stroke after IV thrombolysis, but several baseline characteristics were unbalanced between the two groups, which may have influenced the outcome of these studies. Whereas, the baseline characteristics were balanced in our study. Second, the controls in most previous studies were patients who did not receive statins or take placebo, but the controls in our study received a low dose of statin. Third, the treatment group in previous studies mostly initiated statin therapy within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ after IV thrombolysis or before the onset of stroke, and we started statin therapy within $^{24\textrm{h}}$ after rt-PA treatment because the subgroup analysis of THRaST study demonstrates that statin treatment started within $^{24\textrm{h}}$ can bring better outcome to patients [18].  

The present study has some limitations. First, there was no randomized controlled trial before our research, so we did our sample size estimation based on limited evidences; this study only included 310 patients, which may have led to insignificant differences between the two groups. Second, the guideline recommends that AIS patients qualified for statin treatment should receive statin therapy as soon as possible [2], so the ethics committee did not approve the no statin group on account of the principles of nonmaleficence and beneficence. Therefore, we cannot compare the impact of different doses of statin with a no statin group. Third, AIS patients on account of atrial fibrillation (AF) are less prone to benefit from statins. Although there were no significant differences between the two groups in AF incidence rate through the statistical analysis, the low-dose statin group has an absolute higher AF incidence rate, which may lead to a bias in the results. Fourth, the results of INSPIRE study were limited in Chinese patients, and it is hard to say that if the results can be generalized among other races.  

# Conclusion  

The INSPIRE study indicated that intensive statin therapy may not improve clinical outcomes compared with the low dose of statin therapy in AIS patients undergoing intravenous thrombolysis, and the two groups had similar safety profile.
