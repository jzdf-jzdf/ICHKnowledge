# Reactive Oxygen Species Scavenger in Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage Patients  

A Multicenter, Randomized Controlled Trial  

Moinay Kim, MD, PhD; Joonho Byun , MD; Yeongu Chung , MD; Si Un Lee, MD; Ji Eun Park , MD, PhD; Wonhyoung Park , MD, PhD; Jung Cheol Park , MD, PhD; Jae Sung Ahn, MD, PhD; Seungjoo Lee , MD, PhD  

BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) have oxidative stress. Oxidative stress contributes to the development and progression of perihematomal edema (PHE) in brain hemorrhage patients. We hypothesized that reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavengers might have a neuroprotective role in the acute period of patients with ICH.  

METHODS: This prospective, multicenter, single-blind, randomized study was conducted between June 2017 and October 2019. Intracranial bleeding, including spontaneous ICH, secondary ICH due to vascular anomalies, venous thrombosis, neoplasms, or hemorrhagic infarction, were included in our study. These ROS scavengers were given for 14 days with a dose of N-acetylcysteine 2000 mg/d and selenium $1600\upmu{\mathrm{g/d}}$ intravenously. Other patients received a placebo. The primary outcome was hemorrhage and PHE volume changes in 2-week follow-up computed tomography between ROS scavenger versus placebo groups.  

RESULTS: In total, 448 patients were enrolled with 123 patients remaining after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the ROS scavenger $\mathsf{\Gamma}(\mathsf{n}{=}57\$ ) and placebo $\scriptstyle(\ncap=66,$ groups. No significant differences in baseline hematoma and PHE volumes were observed but 2 weeks follow-up computed tomography showed significant differences in PHE volume $(21.90\pm17.63$ versus $30.66{\pm}32.35$ , $\widetilde{P\mathrm{<0.01}}$ ) and PHE ratio ( $1.19{\pm}0.73$ versus $2.05{\pm}1.27,$ $P\mathrm{<}0.01$ ). Among clinical factors, time to reach target Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (5.98 hours $[95\%$ CI, 4.82–7.241 versus 8.42 hours], $[95\%$ CI, 6.57–10.77], $P\mathrm{<}0.01$ ) and the length of intensive care unit stays (6.46 days $[95\%$ CI, 2.38–10.55 versus 12.66 days], $[95\%$ CI, 8.47–16.85], $\widetilde{P}\widetilde{<}0.01$ ) were significantly shortened among patients who received ROS scavengers than among patients who did not receive ROS scavenger.  

CONCLUSIONS: ROS scavenger showed a significantly reduced PHE volume, time to reach target Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale, and shortened length of intensive care unit stay in patients with acute ICH. Early and high doses of ROS scavengers in a combination regimen may have played a key role in obtaining a favorable outcome in our study.

Critically ill patients have oxidative stress. Expanding evidence showed a role for systemic oxidative stress in the development and impact of critical illness.3 In the human body, there is an endogenous scavenger system to protect and fight against reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species induced injury.4 Oxidative stress develops when the level of toxic ROS and reactive nitrogen species surmount the host endogenous antioxidant defense system.3 The most critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICU) have incremented mediators of oxidant stress than less critically ill patients.5 These results provide a rationale to administer exogenous ROS scavenger in critical illness situations.  

In the human body, reduction in antioxidants is observed immediately after an acute stroke, possibly due to increased oxidative stress which is more dominant in patients with more severe brain injury.6 In addition to the initial mechanical tissue insult caused by the stroke, secondary brain injury develop a crucial role in further damage.7 Astrocytes, white matter fibers, and the blood-brain barrier are destructed by the inflammatory response, resulting in the ROS balance breakdown.2 ROS induce direct cellular injury via oxidative stress to nucleic acids, cellular proteins and by activating lipid peroxidation, which destroys the cell membrane.3 The schematic representation for ROS scavengers’ role in hemorrhagic stroke is shown in Figure I in the Data Supplement.  

ROS scavengers targeted at preventing or minimizing oxidative stress have contributed new insights into patients with ICH. Previous studies validated ROS scavengers’ effect by exhibiting minimal injury to the membranes of neuronal cells, preventing neuronal death and brain edema by scavenging free radicals, such as hydroxyl radicals and lipid peroxidation inhibition.8,9 However, these were limited as preclinical studies. There are few clinically effective treatment strategies for antioxidants, mainly because of the uncertainties in translating preclinical experiments into clinical trials which have yet to be resolved. Nevertheless, the beneficial effect of ROS scavenger in critically ill conditions like systemic inflammatory response syndrome, sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and shock have been well established.3,5,10–14  

In the acute phase of ICH, edema develops and surrounds the hematoma.15 These perihematomal edema (PHE) develops via the oxidation process by toxic factors like thrombin and iron released from the hemorrhagic clot. Hence, we speculated that ROS scavenger might be the critical factor to reduce PHE in patients with ICH. However, to the best of our knowledge, no report has focused on ROS scavenger’s therapeutic effects in hematoma, PHE, and its subsequent clinical outcome in patients with ICH.  

Our study’s objective was to evaluate the effect of ROS scavenger (N-acetylcysteine and selenium) in the early phase of spontaneous and secondary ICH patients. Our a priori hypothesis is that ROS scavengers may reduce the PTE in ICH patients thereby clinical outcomes will be improved.  



# METHODS  

Appropriate institutional review board approval and patient consent were obtained for this study. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.  



# Study Design and Participants  

This is a prospective, multicenter, single-blind, randomized study conducted in different Korean neuro-ICUs between June 2017 and October 2019. The selection of participating ICUs has been initiated within the Korean Society of Intensive Care Medicine’s neuro-ICU section, with the identification of investigators who have thereafter selected any potential ICUs with neurosurgical facilities and an adequate number of patients an acute brain injury admitted per year. Informed consent was obtained from all patients, families, or their legal representatives before the study enrollment.  

The following are criteria for inclusion of patients in our study: (1) diagnosis of ICH based on computed tomography (CT); (2) age $\geq18$ or $\le80$ years; and (3) expectation of staying in the ICU ${\ge}48$ hours. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ICH that was secondary to hematologic malignancy; (2) patient on medication of antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel, or cilostazol) or anticoagulation (heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin, vitamin $\mathsf{K}$ antagonist [warfarin], or direct oral anticoagulants); (3) brain death or imminent death (not expected to be in ICU for ${>}48$ hours); (4) pregnancy; (5) liver cirrhosis—Child’s class C liver disease; (6) seizure disorder requiring anticonvulsant medication; (7) postcardiac arrest with suspected significant anoxic brain injury; and (8) do not resuscitate ordered patients.  

The modified intention to treat was defined as all subjects randomized after giving consent to participation in the study. However, the subjects who have never taken the investigational products (N-acetylcysteine and selenium) or had no efficacy end points measured in the intention to treat set even after taking the investigational products were excluded from the analysis.  

Unlike localized hematoma, the volume measurement of bleeding is difficult in diffuse hemorrhage cases like epidural hematoma, subdural hematoma, and subarachnoid hemorrhage. Hence, the present study involved only ICH cases where hematoma and PHE are feasible to be measured on CT. These are spontaneous ICH (hypertensive arteriopathy and cerebral amyloid angiopathy) and secondary ICH due to vascular anomalies, venous thrombosis, neoplasm, or hemorrhagic infarction.  

The primary outcome was hemorrhage and PHE volume changes in 2 weeks follow-up CT between ROS scavenger versus placebo groups. Secondary outcomes were the time to reach target sedation level (using the Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale [RASS], target goal range between $^{-2}$ to 0), the length of ICU (until patients were examined medically fit for discharge) and hospital stay with neurological outcome assessed on 30-day using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).  



# Randomization and Study Intervention  

Patients were randomized to receive 2 different ROS scavengers together or matching placebo solutions, in a 1:1 ratio, without stratification and using a block size of 4, by means of a computerized central internet-based allocation service based on computer-generated random numbers. Patients were blinded to the application of ROS scavengers, but the investigators were aware of the randomization results. The randomization was to receive ROS scavengers, N-acetylcysteine (Muteran, Hanhwa Pharma, Seoul, Republic of Korea, 2000 mg [1000 mg BID] IV per day) and selenium (Selenase Tpro, BiosynKorea, Kwangju, Republic of Korea, 1600 µg [800 µg BID] IV per day) simultaneously. The control group received a placebo intravenously. The administration of ROS scavengers or placebo was initiated within 24 hours of admission. These intravenous solutions were administered additionally from the standard nutrition. ROS scavenger was given for 14 days. To maintain a relatively constant serum level of selenium and avoid toxicity, a blood test was performed 2 times a week. When the serum selenium level was higher than the upper limit, selenium was not given. A blood test was repeated on the next day to check the serum selenium level and decide on administration. There is no available blood test for serum acetylcysteine.  



# Data Collection and Definitions  

The related data were obtained from the medical records by a physician and trained research nurse. In this study, the following candidate factors were analyzed: (1) age; (2) sex; (3) comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, ischemic heart disease, and chronic kidney injury; (4) admission category (medical or surgical management); (5) last-known well to ICU admission (hours); (6) causes of hemorrhage; (7) location of hemorrhage; (8) admission scores (the worst of the first 24 hours) including Glasgow Coma Scale, mRS, sequential organ failure assessment, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation 2; and (9) laboratory test results on admission. The sedation level was systematically assessed using the RASS, with the target goal range between $^{-2}$ and 0. Serious adverse events (SAEs) related to drug administration are defined and shown in the Data Supplement.  



# Radiological Evaluation  

The diagnosis of ICH was assessed based on CT scans. All patients underwent a 2-week follow-up brain CT from the admission. To evaluate whether ROS scavengers have improved cerebral metabolism in the acute period of patients with ICH, we measured the volume of hematoma and PHE between initial and 2-week follow-up CT. PHE ratio was defined as the differential value of PHE between admission and 2-week interval CT. Hemorrhage and PHE were volumetrically measured with CT using $1.5\AA^{-}$ to 5-mm thick slices; the slices were processed using software developed in house (AsanJ, based on a plugin package ImageJ [https://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij]). To minimize measurement errors, the hemorrhage and PTE measurements were performed by 2 board-certified neurosurgeons and one board-certified neuroradiologist who were blinded to treatment assignment and the mean value was documented.

# Assessment and Follow-Up  

Neurological assessment was performed using mRS.16 Subjects will be followed until death or hospital discharge. If alive but not in the hospital after 30 days since injury, the neurological status will be evaluated by a visit from the trained nurse, provided they are aware of randomization to one of the 2 arms of therapy. If not possible, the mRS will be completed after the telephone interview performed by a nurse or doctor not involved in the study and patient’s management.  



# Statistical Analysis  

No previous studies focused on the same end points as our present study using the same ROS scavengers (N-acetylcysteine and selenium). Hence, we estimated the sample size from former studies that have involved other ROS scavengers targeting patients with ICH. Based on ROS scavenger’s assumed effect size in patients with ICH (0.6), assuming $10\%$ lost to follow-up, 120 subjects need to be randomized 1:1 ROS scavenger versus placebo afford $90\%$ power to demonstrate with a 2-sided $\upalpha$ of 0.05. The assumed effect size of 0.6 was based on the study results focused on the statin (ROS scavenger) targeting patients with ICH.17 This effect size was derived from Cohen D effect size calculation.  

Qualitative factors were analyzed using a standard $\chi^{2}$ test or Fisher exact test and quantitative factors for unpaired Student $t$ test or Mann-Whitney $U$ test. All values are presented as mean and SD unless indicated otherwise. Univariate and multivariate logistic regressions were conducted to evaluate the potential risk factors associated with unfavorable clinical outcome. The backward elimination $(95\%\mathrm{Cl})$ ) method was used to determine the final model, in which all remaining factors had a $P{<}0.15$ . Statistical analysis results were obtained using SPSS (version 21; SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL), SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and R software (version 3.6.0; www.r-project.org) in association with packages rms and pROC. $P$ value ${<}0.05$ was designed to suggest the statistical significance. The precision of the estimates was assessed with $95\%$ CI.  



# RESULTS  



# Characteristics of ROS Scavenger and Placebo Cohorts  

We prospectively screened 448 patients with hemorrhagic stroke who were admitted to ICU between June 2017 and October 2019. Finally, 123 patients were enrolled in our study after inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure  1). Among these patients, 57 patients were randomized to the ROS scavenger group, whereas the other 66 patients were denoted as non-ROS scavenger (placebo) group. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the ROS scavenger and placebo groups (Table  1). The mean age for ROS scavenger and placebo groups were $54.44\pm16.22$ and $56.11\pm14.29$ years, respectively. Both groups showed male predominance $(64.9\%$ versus $53\%$ , $\scriptstyle{\underline{{\mathsf{P}\mathrm{=}}}}0.2,$ . The most common comorbidities were hypertension $(47.4\%$ versus $47\%$ ) and diabetes ( $17.5\%$ versus $21.2\%$ ). Hypertensive ICH ( $.43.9\%$ versus $40.9\%$ ) was the most common cause of ICH, followed by cerebrovascular disease ${\bf\'29.8\%}$ versus $33.3\%$ ). Administration of osmotic agents showed no significant differences between the 2 groups $\widetilde{P=}0.11$ ). The results are summarized in Table 1.

# Primary Outcome  

There were no significant differences between ROS scavenger and placebo groups on the initial hemorrhage $(34.78{\pm}25.54\$ versus $36.71\pm27.34$ , $\widetilde{P=}0.91$ ) and PHE $\:18.47\pm11.02\:$ versus $19.09{\pm}13.06\$ , $P{=}0.85$ ) volumes. However, 2-week follow-up CT showed significant differences in PHE volume $21.90{\pm}17.63$ versus $30.66{\pm}32.35$ , $P\mathrm{<}0.01$ ) and PHE ratio $1.19{\pm}0.73$ versus $2.05{\pm}1.27,$ $P\mathrm{{<}0.01}$ ), whereas no difference was observed in hemorrhage volume $(20.90\pm19.21$ versus $18.70{\pm}19.74$ , $P{=}0.74$ ; Figure 2 and Table 2).  



# Secondary Outcomes  

In both univariate and multivariate analysis, time to reach target RASS (5.98 hours $[95\%$ CI, $4.82\AA-$ 7.241 versus 8.42 hours], $[95\%$ CI, 6.57–10.77], $P\mathrm{<}0.01$ ) and the length of ICU stays (6.46 days $[95\%$ CI, 2.38–10.55 versus 12.66 days], $[95\%$ CI, $8.47\substack{-16.85}]$ , $P\mathrm{{<}0.01}$ ) were significantly shortened among patients who received ROS scavenger than among patients who did not receive ROS scavenger. However, no significant differences were observed between 30-day mRS and the length of total hospital stay. The results are summarized in Table  3. The full distribution showing percentages of each 30-day mRS outcome is shown in Figure II in the Data Supplement.  



# Serious Adverse Events  

A total of 29 SAEs were reported in 19 patients, and none of them were considered to be potentially related to our study supplements. There were no significant differences in SAE rates between the 2 groups (Table I in the Data Supplement).  



# Laboratory Outcomes  

The mean baseline serum selenium levels were within normal limits for 2 groups (ROS scavenger: $7.8{\pm}2.4~\upmu\mathrm{g}/$ dL versus non-ROS scavenger: $8.4{\pm}3.5\upmu{\mathrm{g/dL}}$ , $P\mathrm{=}0.25$ ). ROS scavenger group showed a significant increase from baseline in serum selenium levels on days 7 and 14 $(P<0.01$ for both comparisons) after selenium administration (Table 4).  



# DISCUSSION  

In this prospective, randomized and multicenter trial involving spontaneous and secondary ICH patients, N-acetylcysteine and selenium administration showed a significant PHE volume reduction measured on 2-week follow-up CT, shortened time to reach target RASS, and the length of ICU stays. No further beneficial effect of these antioxidants was observed on any other outcome. There were no significant SAE occurred within a month of ROS scavenger administration. The neuroprotective potential of N-acetylcysteine and selenium observed in the present study suggest that these are powerful antioxidant with properties that alleviate PHE and functional outcome in patients with ICH. To our knowledge, among various ROS scavengers, the effect of N-acetylcysteine and selenium have not been studied in patients with ICH to date.

We exclusively measured hemorrhage volume, PHE, and its comparable ratio to examine whether N-acetylcysteine and selenium have the potential to improve cerebral metabolism in the acute period of patients with ICH. Twoweek follow-up CT revealed a significantly reduced PHE and time to reach RASS and ICU stay in the ROS scavenger group. We do not know how the length of ICU stay was significantly shortened in the ROS scavenger group.  

Possibly, the neuroprotective potential of ROS scavengers reduced PHE and thereby the patient’s functional outcome has been improved. These results propose that our ROS scavengers are potent antioxidants with properties that mitigate PHE and functional outcomes in the acute period of patients with ICH.  

Although ROS scavenger’s mechanism in patients with stroke is well established, clinical trials have shown positive, negative, marginal, or conflicting results.4,18 However, considerable factors may account for the discrepancy between the clinical outcome of present and past studies. First, prior indications of ROS scavenger have much emerged from smaller and less methodologically robust trials limited to experimental studies.19,20 Second, in contrast to patients in previous studies, patients in our trial received the highest dose of selenium (ie, 1600 µg per day of selenium, which is much more than the maximal dose used in other studies11,21). Third, our study specifically focused on patients with ICH, whereas most previous studies typically excluded such patients and generally enrolled critically ill conditions like systemic inflammatory response syndrome, sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and shock. Finally, multiple ROS scavengers were administered within 24 hours of ICU admission in our study, whereas other clinical studies initiated later in the course of critical illness using monotherapy.22–24

Selenium is one of the trace elements that are essential cofactor for the glutathione peroxidase function to improve antioxidant capacity.13 Meta-analysis of 11 studies targeted critically ill patients with administration of selenium alone or mixed with other trace elements showed a significant reduction in mortality rates.11 The beneficial effect of selenium was also observed in severe sepsis patients. In a prospective randomized placebo-controlled multicenter trial, administration of selenium for 14 days targeted to severe 249 sepsis patients with acute physiology and chronic health evaluation III score ${>}70$ showed a significant reduction in 30-day mortality rate.21  

N-acetylcysteine has various antioxidant effects by reducing NF-κB (nuclear factor-κB) activation and cytokines production and as a precursor of glutathione to remove oxidative stress.4 These agents are the most generally adopted antioxidants in preclinical and clinical models.3 Besides its antioxidant effect, N-acetylcysteine has been safely used as the mucolytic drug for lung disease and acetaminophen overdose treatment.25 Due to their favoring effects in the ICU, it has been widely studied in critically ill patients with septic shock, organ failure, and even with acute respiratory distress syndrome.26,27 A meta-analysis focused on the effect of administrating selenium in ICUs and concluded that none of the trials reported harmful effects of selenium hence it can be safely recommended to expect possible beneficial outcome.11  

Among various available ROS scavenger drugs, we have chosen N-acetylcysteine and selenium for several reasons. First, they are the most widely used antioxidant drugs in preclinical and clinical studies3; hence, much information is given about their safeties and adverse effects. Second, these are generally available in many hospitals and are relatively inexpensive compare to other ROS scavengers like edaravone and glutathione. Also, these drugs have many advantages in the ICU setting. For example, N-acetylcysteine’s mucolytic effect is beneficial in lung disease like pneumonia, which is very common in ICU.28 Systematic review included various antioxidant nutrients and concluded that selenium either alone or in combination with other ROS scavengers were significantly associated with reduced mortality in ICU patients.11 Also, the rate of infectious complications can be reduced by administration of selenium as it improves phagocytosis and immunoglobulin production.4  

Besides N-acetylcysteine and selenium, some studies focus on the potential ROS scavengers that target oxidative stress in patients with ICH. Edaravone (3-methyl1-phenyl-2-pyrazoline-5-one) is one of the most studied free radical scavengers in patients with stroke. Clinical outcomes and the incidence of vasospasm were significantly improved in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage patients after administration of edaravone.18 However, edaravone alleviated neurological outcome in patients with acute ICH but failed to show longer-term benefit and hence warranted large scale randomization clinical trial.29 Statin is another potent oxidative stress remover. Statin blocks the conversion of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A to mevalonate, the rate-limiting step of cholesterol biosynthesis with some anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effect.30 However, statin failed to show its beneficial effect in clinical studies targeted for patients with ICH.22

To enhance ROS scavengers’ effect in patients with brain hemorrhage, we recommend the following rules: (1) ROS scavengers must be administrated as early as possible after admission to the ICU. We think that conflicting results from previous studies were most likely due to less optimal administration time or ineffective dosing schedule. A study involved ischemic stroke patients with administration of ebselen showed corroborate results.31 In their study, the improvement of outcome was only observed in patients who started ebselen within 24 hours of stroke onset. This beneficial effect was not observed in the group that started treatment after 24 hours. We think that the irreversible inflammatory and brain injury cascade proceeds with time, and it is mandatory to define the appropriate time of administration; (2) high doses are likely to be helpful, rather than insufficient doses. A randomized prospective controlled study targeted systemic inflammatory response syndrome patients showed that low serum selenium levels were observed in all patients on admission to ICU.32 Accordingly, we speculated that a higher dose of selenium might be needed in the acute period of critically ill patients. According to the 2015 American Society of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition guideline, selenium dose 1000 to 16 000 µg per day (up to 14 days) should be given to the acute critically ill patient to expect effects like pro-oxidant, cytotoxic, and anti-inflammation.33 Hence, we decided to give the same dose of selenium as recommend from the American Society of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition guideline; (3) rather than single ROS scavenger agent administration, consider combination or cocktail treatment. The complex oxidative stress cascade network would be inhibited much more extensively via various ROS scavengers simultaneously at different steps. Also, metabolic pathways of ROS scavenger results in optimal function when other cofactors are also available in combination.12 Consequently, administration of a single ROS scavenger may result in less or no benefits if others are deficient, which is why we have selected 2 antioxidants in our study.  

Referral bias cannot be excluded as all participating centers in our study were large university-affiliated tertiary care institutions. Heterogeneity in the disease population including the various cause of ICH limits the generalizability of our study results. Consequently, our results may not represent the general population. Different institutions may have different protocols for intracranial pressure management which may have played a significant role too. Patients with ICH are heterogeneous regarding their diagnoses, comorbidities, critical illness, and administration of multiple drugs. These variable considerations may lead to an imprecise interpretation of our clinical outcome. Physician bias such as differential use in ICH management’s concomitant treatment due to a single-blind design of the current study is another limitation. Another limitation includes a short duration of follow-up (30 days) to determine differences in functional outcome more precisely. Regardless of these limitations, our study’s strengths include a comparatively large sample size that was prospectively, randomly designed, and performed in multi centers. Also, unlike previous trials where ROS scavengers’ effect is focused explicitly in a neurocritical disease like ICH or subarachnoid hemorrhage, we have defined and included various types of ICH that are much more generalized and likely to interact in neuro-ICU settings.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

In the present study, combination therapy of N-acetylcysteine and selenium showed a significantly reduced PHE, time to reach target RASS, and the length of ICU stay in patients with acute ICH. The neurological outcome, however, was not significantly changed. The concept of an early and high dose of ROS scavengers in a combination regimen may have played a crucial role in our study.
