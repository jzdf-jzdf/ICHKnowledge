# Optimal drainage time after evacuation of chronic subdural haematoma (DRAIN TIME 2): a multicentre, randomised, multiarm and multistage non-inferiority trial in Denmark  

Mads Hjortdal Grønhøj, Thorbjørn Søren Rønn Jensen, Rares Miscov, Ann Kathrine Sindby, Birgit Debrabant, Torben Hundsholt, Carsten Reidies Bjarkam, Bo Bergholt, Kåre Fugleholm, Frantz Rom Poulsen, on behalf of the DACSUHS group\*  



# Summary  

Background Postoperative drainage after surgical evacuation of chronic subdural haematoma reduces the risk of recurrence, but the optimum drainage time is uncertain. We aimed to investigate the shortest possible drainage time without increasing the haematoma recurrence rate.  

Methods We conducted a randomised, multi-arm and multistage non-inferiority trial at four neurosurgical centres in Denmark. We enrolled adult patients (aged ${\ge}18$ years) with symptomatic chronic subdural haematoma. All patients were treated according to the national standard practice with a burr hole above the maximum width of the haematoma. Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio via a centralised web server to receive $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ , $^{12\mathrm{h}}$ , or $24\mathbf{h}$ of postoperative passive subdural drainage. Randomisation was done by an independent on-call neurosurgeon and was masked until $\mathbf{61}$ after surgery. The primary outcome was symptomatic haematoma recurrence at 3 months after surgery; the rate of recurrence was assessed in a regression model for non-inferiority testing, with no missing data. Personnel assessing the primary outcome were masked to group allocation. Non-inferiority was assessed with a prespecified margin of $7\%$ , in a modified intention-to-treat population—defined as patients with randomly assigned treatment excluding those withdrawing from study participation after randomisation, or experiencing acute rebleedings or accidental drain removal. This trial is registered with ISRCTN (number 15186366); the trial was stopped after the first interim analysis on the advice of an independent safety advisory committee.  

Findings Between March 1, 2021, and June 30, 2022, 347 patients were enrolled and 331 were followed up to 3 months, 105 were assigned to 6 h of drainage, 111 to 12 h of drainage, and 115 to $^{241}$ of drainage. At admission, 83 $(25\%)$ participants were women and 248 $(75\%)$ were men, mean age was 75·7 years $\mathbf{(SD10\cdot5)}$ , median modified Rankin Scale score was 4 (IQR 3–5), and median Glasgow Coma Scale score was 15 (IQR 14–15). At 3 months after surgery, haematoma recurrence was reported in 28 $(27\%)$ of 105 patients who were assigned to ${\mathfrak{6}}\mathbf{h}$ drainage (predicted haematoma recurrence rate $27.0\%$ , $95\%$ CI 18∙5 to 35∙4), 22 $(20\%)$ of 111 assigned to $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ drainage $(19\cdot5\%$ , ${\bf\Omega}^{12\cdot{\bf0}}$ to $27\cdot0!$ ), and 12 $(10\%)$ of 115 assigned to $24\mathbf{h}$ drainage $(10\cdot4\%,4\cdot8$ to 16∙0). The risk of haematoma recurrence was increased by 16∙5 percentage points $95\%$ CI 6∙5 to $26\cdot6)$ in patients drained for $\mathbf{61}$ compared with $24\mathbf{h}$ , and by 9∙1 percentage points (–0∙4 to $18\cdot5\vert$ in patients drained for $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ compared with $24\textbf{h}$ . Therefore, non-inferiority of $\textbf{6h}$ and $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ of drainage to $24\textbf{h}$ of drainage was not established. 20 patients had died by 3 months, seven in the $\mathbf{61}$ group, eight in the $\boldsymbol{12}\mathbf{h}$ group, and five in the $24\textbf{h}$ group. The most frequent known causes of death were haematoma recurrence (three in $\textsl{12}\mathbf{h}$ group), comorbidity (three in $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ group), and pneumonia (one each in $\mathbf{6}\mathbf{h}$ and $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ groups, two in $24\mathrm{~h~}$ group). The most frequent complication was postoperative infection, reported in 20 $(20\%)$ patients in the $^{61}$ group, 25 $(23\%)$ in the $\boldsymbol{12}\mathbf{h}$ group, and 19 $(17\%)$ in the $24\mathbf{h}$ group. The most common infection source was the urinary tract.  

Interpretation Patients surgically treated for symptomatic chronic subdural haematoma and postoperatively drained for $\mathbf{61}$ or $^{12\mathbf{h}}$ had higher rates of haematoma recurrence than did patients drained for $24\mathrm{~h~}$ . The findings from this non-inferiority trial provide evidence to support $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of postoperative drainage as the standard drain time when a fixed drain time approach is used. To provide solid evidence of generalisability of the results to countries other than Denmark, a multinational randomised controlled trial will be needed.

# Introduction  

The incidence of chronic subdural haematoma is rising worldwide, an increase that has been attributed to demographic changes1,2 and use of antithrombotic medication.3 The most frequent precipitating event of chronic subdural haematoma is a minor head trauma that causes blood and blood-breakdown products to collect in the subdural space. # Research in context  



# Evidence before this study  

We searched PubMed from database inception to Dec 1, 2022, for randomised controlled trials on postoperative draining after surgical evacuation of chronic subdural haematoma, with no language restrictions. Key search terms were: “chronic subdural haematoma”, “draining”, “drain time” “complications”, “recurrence”, “subgaleal” and “subdural”. Only two trials met our criteria. One trial from France, published in 2010, reported that $48\mathsf{h}$ drainage was superior to $96\mathsf{h}$ owing to fewer drainrelated complications, but no difference in recurrence rate. The Danish Chronic Subdural Hematoma Study group (DACSUHS) published a trial in 2021 and reported no significant difference in recurrence rate when drained for $24\mathsf{h}$ versus $48\mathsf{h}$ .  



# Added value of this study  

To our knowledge, this is the first  randomised controlled trial to investigate short drainage times of 6 h and 12 h versus $24\mathsf{h}$ , with the aim not to increase the haematoma recurrence rate. We used a non-inferiority design because, at the time of the study planning, we were convinced that shorter drainage times would not be superior with respect to recurrence of haematomas, but we expected that any potential minor disadvantage would be outweighed by other benefits.  

Compared with previous trials, in the present trial, all patients (aged $\geq18$ years) with chronic subdural haematoma were included despite clinical status and demographics. All Danish neurosurgical centres took part, and we included patients regardless if they were judged to be incapacitated at admission, favouring generalisability of the results.  



# Implications of all the available evidence  

The DRAIN TIME 2 trial showed that patients surgically treated for symptomatic chronic subdural haematoma and postoperatively drained for 6 h or $12\mathsf{h}$ experienced a substantially higher haematoma recurrence rate than did patients who were drained for $24\mathsf{h}$ Not surprisingly, we observed that patients drained for 6 h had a significantly shorter hospital stay. There was no difference between groups regarding death, self-reported quality of life, and modified Rankin Scale score at 3 months. Also, there was no difference in thromboembolic complications related to immobilisation nor discomfort due to the drain. Previous trials have shown that draining for longer than $24\mathsf{h}$ has no additional effect on recurrence rate. Our trial provides evidence to support $24\mathsf{h}$ of postoperative subdural drainage as the standard drain time when a fixed drain time approach is used.

Despite uncertainties and differences in the management of chronic subdural haematoma across countries, evidence suggests that postoperative subdural drainage is safe and reduces symptomatic recurrence.11,12 From both the patient and health economic perspective, identifying the shortest possible drainage time without increasing the recurrence rate could reduce drain-related complications, adverse events due to immobilisation, and the length of hospital stay.13 In a randomised controlled trial from Denmark, drainage for $48\mathrm{~h~}$ compared with $24\mathrm{h}$ had no beneficial effect on recurrence rate and mortality at 3 months after surgery, but $24\textrm{h}$ drainage reduced the length of stay at the neurosurgical department.8 Moreover, no differences were seen between groups regarding drainrelated complications and thromboembolic events. Up to now, no study has been done to investigate the benefits of a postoperative drainage time shorter than $24\mathrm{h}$ . Therefore, we conducted a randomised controlled trial (DRAIN TIME 2) to assess whether a postoperative drainage period of 6 h or $12\mathrm{h}$ would be non-inferior to $24\mathrm{h}$ with respect to the haematoma recurrence rate in patients with newly operated chronic subdural haematoma.  



# Methods  



# Study design  

DRAIN TIME 2 was a multicentre, randomised, multiarm and multistage non-inferiority trial at four neurosurgical departments located at hospitals in Denmark: Aalborg University Hospital (Aalborg), Aarhus University Hospital (Aarhus), Rigshospitalet (Copenhagen), and Odense University Hospital (Odense). The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan have been published previously and are in the appendix (pp 26–64).13 The trial protocol was designed by the Danish Chronic Subdural Haematoma Study (DACSUHS) group, which is a national steering committee with participants from all neurosurgical departments in Denmark. The DACSUHS group coordinates the management of and research into chronic subdural haematoma in Denmark. Ethics approval for the study protocol was obtained from the Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark (number S-20200044). This trial was registered in the International Standard Randomized Controlled Trial Register (ISRCTN 15186366).  



# Participants  

Screening of patients to determine eligibility for participation in the trial was performed by the neurosurgical team on admission to one of the four neurosurgical departments. Inclusion criteria were: age 18 years or older; a minimum of 2 weeks since known head trauma; symptoms of chronic subdural haematoma (eg, headache, gait disturbance, confusion or cognitive decline, limb weakness, speech disturbance, drowsiness or decreased consciousness, and seizures), with confirmation on CT or MRI; and a single burr-hole evacuation and placement of a passive subdural drain. A chronic subdural haematoma was defined, on imaging, as a predominantly hypodense or isodense collection on the surface of the brain beneath its outer membrane (dura mater). We included patients with both unilateral and bilateral haematomas.  

We excluded patients if they had any known abnormalities in their CSF (eg, high protein concentrations which can be associated with multiple sclerosis, tumour, nerve inflammation, infection, etc), changes or abnormalities in normal CSF dynamics (eg, obstructive hydrocephalus, normal pressure hydrocephalus, intra­ cranial hypotension, or ventricular peritoneal shunt), or additional or previous intracranial pathology requiring neurosurgical treatment. Patients with recurrent chronic subdural haematoma, or who had undergone previous craniotomy or other transcranial surgery for any reason, were also excluded.  

Written informed consent was obtained from patients at study enrolment. Patients who did not have capacity to provide consent due to severe symptoms could still be enrolled in the trial if oral and written consent was obtained both from the patient’s legal representative and from a health-care professional (medical doctor) who was independent of the interests of the people responsible for the trial and of the interests of the project in general. We considered patients to be incapacitated if they were unable to participate in the medical examination, if they were not aware of time, place, and personal information, or if they were somnolent or comatose.  



# Randomisation and masking  

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to postoperative drainage for either 6 h or $12\mathrm{~h~}$ (experimental arms) or $24\mathrm{~h~}$ (active control arm). Online randomisation was performed at each participating hospital using the web-based Research Electronic Data Capture system (REDCap) randomise module. Randomisation was done by an independent on-call neurosurgeon. Allocation concealment was ensured because the REDCap system only releases the randomisation code when the patient has been recruited into the trial and the signed consent form has been uploaded to the database. Randomisation took place after a medical history was taken and clinical examination performed. Patients and treating physicians were masked to group allocation until $^\mathrm{~6~}\mathrm{~h~}$ after surgery (here, unmasking was required to determine whether the drain should be removed at that time or after $12\textrm{h}$ or $24\textrm{h}$ ). Personnel assessing the primary outcome were masked to group allocation.  



# Procedures  

Patients were treated according to standard practice in Denmark, as described by DACSUHS.8,13 In brief, a single $13\mathrm{mm}$ burr-hole was placed above the maximum width of the haematoma, and irrigation was performed with a syringe containing isotonic saline at a temperature of $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . A multichannel $3\cdot2\mathrm{mm}$ soft silicone drain (Spiral Drain, Redax, Poggio Rusco, Italy) was inserted into the subdural space using an atraumatic technique,14 and the drain was connected to a closed passive system. Patients with bilateral haematomas had two burr holes and drains placed (one on each side). All patients were allowed immediate mobilisation after surgery, with the open drain and soft collection bag fixed above the collar bone. Preoperative management of anticoagulant therapy and resumption after surgery were handled identically at all departments according to the study protocol.13 Radiological subtyping of haematomas into four groups was done, as previously described by the DACSUHS group.15  



# Outcomes  

The primary outcome was symptomatic haematoma recurrence leading to hospitalisation and confirmed on cranial imaging (eg, CT or MRI) and requiring reoperation of a previously evacuated chronic subdural haematoma within 3 months from surgery. The on-call neurosurgical consultant evaluated patients who were readmitted and decided whether re-operation was needed or not. At 3-month follow-up, patients were registered as having had symptomatic recurrence by a DACSUHS member.  

Secondary outcomes were assessed by DACSUHS members and comprised: mortality at 3 months (causes of death were recorded); modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at admission and 3 months postoperatively;16 scores on the short form-36 health survey (SF-36) at admission and 3 months postoperatively for patients not deemed to be incapacitated;17,18 length of stay in the neurosurgical unit; thromboembolic complications within the draining period (eg, deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or brain infarction); and complications and discomfort related to the drain (eg, bleeding from the skin, pain, or infection).  



# Statistical analysis  

With the multiarm and multistage trial design, we needed to choose a number of stages and stage-specific decreasing significance levels, together with stagespecific power levels. We organised the trial into five stages with a cumulative sample size. The overall power was approximately 0·8, and the family-wise error rate was roughly 0·028. The five stage-specific significance thresholds (for a group to continue to the subsequent stage) were $0\cdot5,0\cdot3,0\cdot2,0\cdot1_{\cdot}$ , and 0·025. Assuming a recurrence rate of $16\%$ (based on findings of previous DACSUHS studies)19 in all treatment groups (both experimental $[6\mathrm{~h~}$ and $12\mathrm{~h~}]$ and control $[24\mathrm{~h}])$ ), equal allocation to all treatment groups, and a non-inferiority margin of $7\%$ , we calculated a sample size of 91 per treatment group for the first stage (an overall cumulative sample size of 273); all five stage-specific sample size calculations are provided in the trial protocol.13 An independent safety advisory committee was established to oversee the interim analysis after each stage, to test non-inferiority of both groups at the chosen significance level.  

At the first interim analysis (at the end of the first stage), an independent statistician performed a regression analysis for the primary outcome (haematoma recurrence) to test the non-inferiority of the $6\mathrm{~h~}$ and $12\mathrm{h}$ treatment groups compared with the $24\mathrm{h}$ group at a noninferiority margin of $7\%$ . The observed $\mathrm{p}$ values for the one-sided non-inferiority hypotheses were $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}97$ for (6 h vs $24\mathrm{~h~}$ ) and $\scriptstyle\operatorname{p=0}\cdot67$ ( $^{12\textrm{h}}$ vs $24\mathrm{~h~}$ ). As both p values exceeded the significance cut-off of $0\cdot5$ for the first stage, none of the groups automatically continued to the next stage. The trial was then terminated because the independent safety advisory committee recommended to close both groups due to the seeming inferiority of the absolute risk differences relative to the $24\mathrm{~h~}$ group, and because of corresponding safety concerns.

We present baseline clinical and demographic characteristics in a table, with means and SDs for continuous numerical outcomes, medians and IQRs for discrete outcomes, and percentages for categorical outcomes per treatment group. We calculated unadjusted p values for continuous, discrete, and categorical variables using the ANOVA F-test, Kruskal–Wallis test, and $\chi^{2}$ test (or Fisher’s exact test), respectively. Standard residual plots were prepared for continuous variables (ANOVA), and histograms were compared for discrete outcomes (Kruskal–Wallis). Modification of the statistical models underlying these tests were considered for severe deviations from standard model assumptions. Resulting p values are taken to be exploratory and descriptive in nature and are not reported but were used to identify chance imbalances between the randomized treatment groups.  

The primary outcome of haematoma recurrence was analysed using a regression model, which estimated the absolute difference in haematoma recurrence risk between treatment groups. We used a generalised linear binomial model with an identity link. Treatment assignment was a categorical predictor (6 h vs 12 h vs $24\mathrm{{h}}$ drainage) and estimates were adjusted for trial centre. Using this regression model, we compared the experimental arms (6 h and $12\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage) with $24\textrm{h}$ drainage (active control) to investigate the one-sided noninferiority hypothesis that the absolute risk difference would not exceed $7\%$ .  

For all secondary outcomes, we investigated the superiority of each of the two experimental groups (6 h and $12\mathrm{h})$ to the active control group (24 h). We used two-sided tests with a significance threshold of 0∙05. For mortality, we used the same regression model and predictors as for the primary outcome. SF-36 scores were analysed by linear regression and included baseline values as a predictor in addition to treatment group and study centre. mRS scores were analysed by ordinal logistic regression, including baseline mRS score as an additional predictor. For mRS, effects are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with $95\%$ CIs. Length of stay in the neurosurgical unit was analysed by robust linear regression due to the presence of very outlying observations.  

The occurrence of different types of complications in the three study groups was described in a table, and the occurrence of any complication was analysed similar to mortality. Occurrence of postoperative infections was analysed using logistic regression and the effects reported as ORs and $95\%$ CIs due to lack of convergence and poor model fit of the additive risk regression model. As specified in the protocol,13 additional descriptive summary tables were created for subgroups of patients with and without chronic subdural haematoma recurrence at 90 days. We did a post hoc analysis of drain production between the different treatment groups, with a Kruskal–Wallis test.  

Data were analysed according to a modified intention to treat (ITT) approach. This analysis population included patients who received their randomly assigned treatment and excluded those withdrawing from study participation after randomisation, or experiencing acute rebleedings or accidental drain  removal. Per-protocol or as-treated analyses were not considered since the assigned treatment coincided with the received treatment for every patient. Altogether, the statistical analysis as described here was carried out as specified in the trial protocol, with a few minor adaptations. No outcome data were excluded from the analysis. The frequency of missing data was reported, and the corresponding mechanisms for missingness were reviewed. If missingness was rare, we used complete case analysis—ie, we used the modified ITT population with available outcome measurements. Otherwise, we considered measures to address missing data such as multiple imputation as well as sensitivity analyses.  

All statistical analyses were conducted by an independent statistician, who was masked to treatment assignments, from the Department of Epidemiology, Biostatistics, and Biodemography at the University of Southern Denmark (Odense, Denmark). Analyses were done in STATA (version 17.0 and 18.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

An improvement in $\mathrm{mRS}$ score at 3 months was reported in all three trial groups, with no differences between groups (6 h vs $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , OR 0·8 $[95\%$ CI 0·5 to 1·4]; $12\mathrm{h}$ vs $24\mathrm{h}$ , 1·4 [0∙8 to 2∙3]). The median mRS at 3 months in all three groups was 2 (IQR 1–3); the median change in $\mathrm{mRS}$ between baseline and 3 months was $^{-1}$ (IQR $-2-$ to $^{-1}$ ) in the $6\mathrm{~h~}$ group, $^{-1}$ $-2$ to 0) in the $12\mathrm{~h~}$ group, and $^{-1}$ ( $^{\prime-3}$ to 0) in the $24\mathrm{~h~}$ group.  

Completion of the SF-36 questionnaire was voluntary, and 282 patients who were considered not to be mentally incapacitated at admission were asked to participate. Data for 83 $(29\%)$ patients were not reported due to administrative errors (incomplete data entry) and missingness was considered to be completely at random. 12 patients who had completed the SF-36 at admission were lost to follow-up at 3 months.

Therefore, 187 $(66\%)$ patients completed the SF36 health status assessment. On all SF-36 domains, the median score improved from baseline to follow-up (figure 2). No significant differences in SF-36 domain scores at follow-up were noted between treatment groups, adjusting for study centre and baseline SF-36 score (appendix pp 13–15).  

Five patients had a symptomatic postoperative thromboembolic event related to immobilisation, which were stroke $(\mathrm{n}{=}3)$ and pulmonary embolism $(\mathrm{n}{=}2;$ table 2). Occurrence of any type of thromboembolic complication resulted in adjusted estimated risk difference of –4∙2 percentage points $(95\%\mathrm{CI}-1\cdot8\$ to 3∙5) for $6\mathrm{~h~}$ versus $24\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage and $-3\cdot8$ percentage points $95\%$ CI –10∙7 to 3∙1) for $12\mathrm{~h~}$ vs $24\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage. Three patients in each trial group had empyema. No patients complained of, or experienced, discomfort related to the drain or suffered from other drain-related complications.  

Patients allocated to 6 h or 12 h drainage had a significantly shorter stay at the department of neurosurgery, with adjusted difference in mean length of stay of $-15\cdot1$ days ( $95\%$ CI –23∙2 to $-7{\cdot}1)$ for 6 h vs $24\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage and $-9\cdot0$ days $(-16\cdot9$ to $-1\cdot1)$ for $12\mathrm{~h~}$ vs 24 h drainage. The discharge destination was not recorded consistently and could not be assessed.  

Age and occurrence of bilateral haematomas were identified as chance baseline imbalances between groups, and a post-hoc sensitivity analysis was done for the primary and secondary outcomes, adjusting for these variables as additional covariates (appendix pp 9–12, 16–21). Age accounted for some variation in outcomes (ie, mortality, risk of complications, mRS score, and length of stay). However, the adjustments did not change the estimated differences between treatment groups. In the post-hoc analysis of drain production, differences between treatment groups were noted for unilateral haematomas (nominal $\mathtt{p}{=}0{\cdot}0002,$ ) and for bilateral haematomas (nominal $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}0029)$ . Drain production was lowest for the 6 h group.  

A descriptive subgroup analysis of patients with recurrence versus no recurrence is presented in the appendix (pp 22–23). Independent of drain group, patients with recurrence were more often male and more prone to have a bilateral haematoma than were those without recurrence.  



# Discussion  

In this trial involving patients with chronic subdural haematoma treated with a burr hole, we found that patients with passive subdural drainage of $\mathrm{6h}$ or $12\mathrm{h}$ had a higher risk of haematoma recurrence within 3 months after surgery compared with patients drained for $24\textrm{h}$ . The predicted marginal risk for recurrence within 3 months, as an average over the different study centres, was $27\%$ for $6\mathrm{h}$ drainage, $20\%$ for $12\mathrm{h}$ drainage, and $10\%$ for $24\textrm{h}$ drainage. We hypothesised that $6\mathrm{~h~}$ and $12\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage would be non-inferior to $24\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage, but the first interim analysis showed that the predefined noninferior margin of $7\%$ was exceeded, and thus the trial was terminated early on the recommendation of the independent safety advisory committee.

The study was designed as a non-inferiority trial for haematoma recurrence, which is why the non-inferiority hypothesis is at the core of our interpretation of results. However, the confidence interval for the risk difference between the $12\mathrm{~h~}$ group and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ group crossed 0, suggesting that post-hoc testing for equal risk at a significance level of $5\%$ would not have identified a significant difference between these two groups. This interpretation might seem to contradict the fact that we did not find statistical evidence for non-inferiority (ie, a risk difference of less than $7\%$ ). However, the paradigm of statistical testing contains two types of errors (type 1 and type 2) and both statements (no evidence for noninferiority, and no evidence for a difference) might be due to potential type 2 errors, of which error rate remains uncontrolled by the statistical testing procedure and might be arbitrarily large (unlike in the case of rejections, for which the testing procedure assures a low error probability). Altogether, the seemingly equal recurrence risk between the $12\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage group and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage group should not be understood as opposed to the seeming inferiority of the $12\mathrm{h}$ group.  

We had further hypothesised that drainage of 6 h would be superior to that of $12\mathrm{~h~}$ and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ regarding length of stay in the neurosurgical department, thromboembolic complications related to immobilisation, and discomfort due to the drain. Only five patients in total had a thromboembolic event, with no differences between the three groups, and no patients expressed discomfort or experienced complications due to the drain. Patients drained for 6 h had a much shorter stay at the neurosurgical department compared with those drained for $24\textrm{h}$ . However, we believe this difference in most neurosurgical centres is probably clinically and practically irrelevant and presumably also of no major importance to the patients. Despite no significant difference in the risk of death between the three groups, the direction of effect suggests a clinically relevant increased mortality for shorter drainage times. There was no difference between groups regarding mRS score at 3 months, suggesting no clinically relevant difference between $24\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage and $6\mathrm{~h~}$ and $12\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage. There was no difference between groups regarding self-reported quality of life at 3 months’ follow-up.  

We believe that the cohort of patients included in the present study is representative for generalisability of the findings, because we included all patients with chronic subdural haematoma who were older than 18 years regardless of whether they were judged to be incapacitated at admission, and we recruited patients from across Denmark over a period of 16 months.  

A previous randomised controlled trial reported no differences in haematoma recurrence rate at 3 months after surgery when comparing $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and $^{48\mathrm{~h~}}$ of passive subdural drainage.8 Another randomised controlled trial reported comparable recurrence rates for drainage times of $48\mathrm{~h~}$ and $96\mathrm{~h~}$ , but 96 h drainage was associated with more drain-related complications.20 Results from a prospective observational study on hourly monitored subdural draining during the first $24\mathrm{h}$ after evacuation of chronic subdural haematoma reported that early ( ${\mathrm{\;}}<8{\mathrm{\:\:\cdot\:}}$ h post-surgery) spontaneous cessation and low drain output seemed to be associated with a higher recurrence rate.21 In the appendix (p 8), we reported a significantly lower drain output in patients drained for $6\mathrm{~h~}$ compared with patients drained for $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ and $24\textrm{h}$ . These observations complement the results from the prospective observational study,21 and indicate the amount of fluid drained to be an influencing factor on recurrence.  

The type of drain used has been investigated in studies, but it does not appear to have a significant effect on haematoma recurrence. A non-inferiority randomised trial of subdural and subperiosteal drains after burr-hole surgery for chronic subdural haematoma reported lower 12-month recurrence rates in the subdural drainage group $8\%$ vs $12\%$ , but predefined non-inferiority criteria were not met.22 Subgroup analyses showed that patients treated with platelet inhibitors or anticoagulants had similar recurrence, mortality, and long-term outcomes when comparing subdural and subperiosteal drains.23 Post-hoc analysis suggested that using a passive drain rather than an active drain might reduce the recurrence rate.24 Subgroup analyses in a multicentre, prospective cohort study of chronic subdural haematoma and dexamethasone treatment reported comparable recurrence rates between subdural $(8\%)$ and subgaleal $(9\%)$ drains.25 Although the recurrence rates are comparable between the different type of drains, the present study does not provide evidence for the optimal drain time when using a subgaleal or subperiosteal drain or an active drain. Based on existing evidence, extrapolation of results from passive subdural to passive subgaleal or subperiosteal drains and active drains seems reasonable, but highlights the need for a separate randomised trial.  

Our study has some limitations. First, the early trial termination meant that relatively few patients were available for the analysis of secondary outcomes. There is some evidence that early termination of studies tends to overestimate the effect size; hence, we believe that the estimated risk differences for recurrence between the experimental $6\mathrm{~h~}$ and $12\mathrm{~h~}$ groups and the active control $24\mathrm{~h~}$ group could be biased upwards.26 Second, follow-up imaging at 3 months to assess intracranial status (eg, complete or partial resorption) was not done.  

However, evidence suggests that routine follow-up postoperative intracranial imaging is unnecessary and that clinical evaluation is superior.27 Third, a higher proportion of bilateral haematomas was reported in the 6 h group compared with the $24\mathrm{~h~}$ group, which could have affected the results because bilateral haematomas have been reported to be a predictor of recurrence,28 although other studies have found the opposite.29 The unbalanced randomisation with respect to bilateral haematomas is probably because of the early trial stopping. Fourth, patients drained for $6\mathrm{~h~}$ were older than those who had $12\mathrm{~h~}$ and $24\textrm{h}$ of drainage. This imbalance could affect the results also, because some studies have reported age as a predictor of recurrence.30 Due to the observed chance imbalances between groups, we did a post-hoc sensitivity analysis, in which we included age and bilateral chronic subdural haematoma as additional covariates into our analyses of the primary and secondary outcomes. We found that age had an effect on mortality and risk of complications, and on change in mRS score and length of stay in the neurosurgical department; we also noted that unilateral haematomas reduced the risk for infections. However, the estimated differences between the $6\mathrm{~h~}$ and $12\mathrm{h}$ groups and the $24\mathrm{~h~}$ group did not change significantly, and the shorter drainage times still seemed inferior to $24\mathrm{{h}}$ drainage with respect to risk of haematoma recurrence. Fifth, we chose a relatively short observation period of 3 months, which was presumed to be optimal for evaluating recurrence,13 and this short time could be the reason why the wide differences in recurrence rates did not translate into significant differences in mortality. Sixth, all patients could be mobilised immediately after surgery, with an open drain. Because this approach was standard practice in national guidelines in Denmark, registration was not consistent regarding whether patients were mobilised or not during the drainage period. Absence of these data makes it impossible to evaluate the risk of thromboembolic complications due to immobilisation in the three trial groups. However, the occurrence of thromboembolic events was low across the three trial groups. Finally, we did not monitor drain production hourly, which could have provided us with data about why and when the $24\mathrm{~h~}$ drainage exerts its effect.  

In conclusion, in the present trial involving patients surgically treated for chronic subdural haematoma, postoperative passive subdural drainage for $6\mathrm{~h~}$ or $12\mathrm{~h~}$ resulted in higher haematoma recurrence rates at 3-month follow-up compared with $24\textrm{h}$ drainage. Since the risk differences were substantial, $24\mathrm{h}$ of postoperative subdural drainage is recommended over $6\mathrm{h}$ and $12\mathrm{h}$ of drainage.
