Meta-analysis Background: Alteplase is the standard medical therapy for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients who present within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ of symptom onset. Tenecteplase is a modified alteplase variant with pharmacological and practical advantages over alteplase. Many trials have investigated the efficacy and safety of tenecteplase against alteplase. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of tenecteplase to alteplase across randomized controlled trials.   
Method: Medline, Embase, and Cochrane CENTRAL were used to search the related articles until February 20, 2023. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that compared the effectiveness and safety of tenecteplase against alteplase for AIS patients were included. Screening, risk of bias assessment, and data extraction were performed following PRISMA guidelines. Data were pooled using a random-effect model.   
Results: Ten RCTs were included, with a total of 5123 patients. There was no significant difference between the two interventions in modified rankin scale 0–1 $\mathbf{\dot{m}R}S0{-}1\$ $\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{RR}=1.04\$ , $95\%$ CI [0.99–1.10], $\mathrm{P}=0.11$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%\cdot$ and early neurological improvement $(\mathrm{RR}=1.06\$ , $95\%$ CI $\left[0.97–1.15\right]$ , $\mathrm{P}=0.21$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=35\$ ). There was no difference in the rates of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage $(\mathrm{RR}=1.18\$ , $95\%$ CI [0.84–1.65], $\mathrm{~P~}=0.35$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%)$ . Ten­ ecteplase was associated with significantly higher complete recanalization rate compared to alteplase $(\mathrm{RR}{=}1.17\$ , $95\%$ CI [1.00–1.36], $\mathbf{P}=0.05$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%\mathrm{)}$ . For large vessel occlusion (LVO) patients assigned to tenecteplase, there was a significant improvement in $\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}1$ ( $\mathrm{RR}{}=1.28$ , $95\%$ CI [1.07–1.52], $\mathbf{P}=0.006$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ .  

Conclusion: Based on our meta-analysis, tenecteplase has similar efficacy and safety to alteplase, with a more promising effect in patients with LVO.  

# 1. Introduction  

Intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase (ALT) has been the standard of care for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) treatment for more than 25 years [1]. Despite that alteplase can dramatically increase the probability of a full recovery without handicap, it has some disadvantages. These include a low recanalization rate, intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) risk, and a short half-life, necessitating constant infusion [2–6]. Accordingly, increasing efforts have been made in recent years to develop new thrombolytic agents with an improved safety, superior efficacy, and a more convenient delivery [7].  

Tenecteplase (TNK) is a recombinant tissue plasminogen activator with enhanced efficacy due to its improved affinity binding to fibrin, resistance to inactivation by plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, absence of procoagulant effects, and extended free plasma half-life [8]. Because of its improved pharmacodynamics, tenecteplase has replaced alteplase as the preferred intravenous thrombolytic treatment for myocardial infarction [9,10]. In the context of AIS, tenecteplase was not superior to alteplase, and it had a comparable efficacy and safety profile according to multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [6,11–13]. However, among stroke patients chosen for treatment based on computed to­ mography perfusion (CTP) imaging, tenecteplase was linked with sub­ stantially improved reperfusion and clinical outcomes compared to alteplase [14,15]. To provide a more nuanced understanding of the benefits and risks of tenecteplase versus alteplase in the management of AIS, we decided to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis on RCTs that reported clinical outcomes following thrombolytic treatment.  

# 2. Materials and methods  

This study was registered prior to a preliminary search in PROSPERO (CRD42022351776) and utilized the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guideline [16]. Furthermore, this study did not require ethical approval, as the data was already published previously. We declare that all data are available in this article.  

# 2.1. Eligibility criteria  

This review and meta-analysis only included RCTs that compared tenecteplase to alteplase and evaluated its effectiveness through excel­ lent and good functional outcomes $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}1$ and $\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2$ , respec­ tively), early neurological improvement (ENI) as defined by each study (Supplementary Table 1), and complete or partial recanalization as defined by each study (Supplementary Table 1). The incidence of any intracerebral hemorrhage (any ICH), symptomatic intracerebral hem­ orrhage (sICH) according to the definition utilized in each study (Sup­ plementary Table 1), and mortality were measured by their frequency during the trials for the safety of both drugs. Patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke and eligible for IV thrombolysis within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ were included. Patients under 18 years, observational studies, and insufficient data for the intended outcomes were excluded. In some RCTs, the fre­ quency of tenecteplase administration was prescribed in different doses, which were included in the subgroup analysis.  

# 2.2. Search strategy  

Our study systematically searched the Medline/PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) data­ bases from database initiation to February 20, 2023, without any re­ striction on date or language. We searched all included databases with keywords including "ischemic stroke," "stroke," “tenecteplase," "alte­ plase," and "randomized controlled trial." Manual searches of reference lists from recent systematic reviews and published studies were also included. The references of the included RCTs were checked for any relevant RCTs that were missed during the systematic search process.  

# 2.3. Study selection and data extraction  

Independently, two reviewers in duplicate performed title and ab­ stract screening, full-text assessment, and data extraction for all the RCTs with the eligibility criteria. Disagreements were discussed with a third reviewer or resolved through consensus before further advancement.  

# 2.4. Risk of bias and quality assessment  

Two reviewers, independently and together, used the Revised Cochrane risk of bias tool to assess the risk of bias in the eligible RCTs [17]. All studies included were reviewed and scored as high risk, low risk, or some concerns. Differences between the reviewers were resolved through discussion until an agreement was achieved. Grading of  

Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria was used to assess the quality of evidence of all reported out­ comes [18].  

# 2.5. Statistical analysis  

Data analysis was performed using RevMan (Review Manager) version 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration). All statistical analyses were per­ formed using the random-effects model. A $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) and $\mathrm{~P~}<0.05$ as a borderline were set for statistical significance. The statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ and P values of the chisquare test. The assessment of dichotomous outcomes was represented as risk ratios (RRs) and pooled using the inverse variance weighting method. Moreover, sensitivity analysis was undertaken to assess the robustness of the results when the heterogeneity was $>50\%$ for an outcome by omitting each study at a time. The dosing of tenecteplase to alteplase administration was included in the subgroup analysis to investigate different dose effects on each outcome. To establish that tenecteplase is non-inferior to alteplase, non-inferiority analysis was performed by determining the non-inferiority margin (ΔNI) based on a previously published trial [22]. The pre-defined ΔNI from the TRACE-2 trial is 0.937. Therefore, the non-inferiority is established when lower boundary of the $95\%$ CI of the pooled RR is greater than 0.937.  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Search results and study selection  

The initial database search by the previously mentioned keywords yielded 1074 results. After using Endnote, a reference management tool, to remove duplicates, the total number decreased to 975 results. After finishing the title and abstract screening, only 61 studies were taken to the next step, full-text screening. Eight articles were included in this study, with an additional two articles that were identified from citation screening of the included articles. Finally, the total number of RCTs that were included in the meta-analysis was 10 (Fig. 1) [6,11–15,19–22].  

# 3.2. Study characteristics  

Ten RCTs met the inclusion criteria and were included in the metaanalysis with a total of 5123 patients; 2676 were allocated to ten­ ecteplase, and 2447 were allocated to Alteplase (Supplementary Table 1) [6,11–15,19–22]. Eight RCTs were multicenter studies, while the others were single-center studies [12,15]. Four of the included trials were non-inferiority trials [19–22]. Different doses of tenecteplase were reported by several RCTs, including 0.1, 0.25, 0.32, and $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ , while the dose of alteplase was fixed to $0{\cdot}9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ in all the RCTs. Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3 show the rest of the demographics, reported comorbidities, and baseline characteristics for all 10 included RCTs.  

# 3.3. Risk of bias and quality of evidence  

The risk of bias of the 10 RCTs was assessed using the Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) (Supplementary Table 4). For the first domain, the randomization process, all the RCTs showed a low risk of bias, except Li et al. [13], which showed a high risk of bias, and Haley et al. [11] and Logallo et al. [6], which both showed some concerns. For the second domain, the deviation from the intended interventions, 5 RCTs showed a low risk of bias [6,13,14,19,20], while Huang et al. [12] showed a high risk of bias. Moreover, Menon et al. [21], Bivard et al. [15], Haley et al. [11], and Wang et al. showed some concerns [22]. For the third domain, the missing outcome data, all the included RCTs showed a low risk of bias. For the fourth domain, the outcome measurement, all the RCTs showed a low risk of bias except Huang et al. which showed some concerns [12]. For the fifth and last domain, the selection of the reported results, all the RCTs showed a low risk of bias except Huang et al. which showed some concerns [12]. The overall risk of bias showed a low risk in 3 studies [14,19,20], some concerns in 4 studies [6,15,21,22], and high risk in 3 studies [11–13]. Finally, the quality of evidence for each outcome in the meta-analysis is shown in Supplementary Table 5.

# 3.4. mRS 0–1 at 3 months  

All the RCTs reported the number of participants with an mRS score of 0–1 at 3 months post-randomization (Fig. 2). The overall effect showed no superiority of either treatment with regard to this outcome $(\mathrm{{RR}}=\mathrm{{}}1.04\$ , $95\%$ CI [0.99–1.10], $\mathrm{P}=0.11$ ), however, the lower boundary of the $95\%$ CI is greater than 0.937 which indicates noninferiority of TNK to ATP. Four doses of TNK were analyzed (i.e., 0.1, 0.25, 0.32, and $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg)}$ , and $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ TNK showed a superior effect to ATP $\mathrm{\bf~R}\mathrm{\bfR}=1.08$ , $95\%$ CI [1.01–1.15], $\mathrm{P}=0.03$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%\dot{}$ ). The remaining subtotals (0.1, 0.32, and $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg})$ showed a similar effect to ATP $\mathrm{\bf\tilde{R}R}=0.96\$ , $95\%$ CI [0.73–1.26], $\mathrm{P}=0.77$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%)$ , $(\mathrm{RR}=1.01\$ , $95\%$ CI [0.75–1.36], $\mathbf{P}=0.94$ , $\operatorname{I}^{2}=$ Not Applicable), $(\mathrm{RR}=0.84\$ , $95\%$ CI [0.58–1.23], $\mathrm{P}=0.37$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=73\%$ ), respectively. (Moderate quality of evidence).

Nine RCTs reported early neurological improvement using the NIHSS (Fig. 3). TNK $0.1\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $(\mathrm{RR}=1.03\$ , $95\%$ CI [0.79–1.35], $\ensuremath{\mathrm{P}}=0.82$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ ${=}0\%.$ , $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $\mathbf{\DeltaRR}{=}1.12$ , $95\%$ CI [0.99–1.26], $\mathrm{P}=0.07$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=41\%\$ , $0.32\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $(\mathrm{RR}=1.06\$ , $95\%$ CI [0.81–1.39], $\mathrm{P}=0.65$ , $\scriptstyle\mathrm{I}^{2}=\mathrm{Not}$ appli­ cable), and $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ $\mathbf{\mathrm{(RR=~0.94}}$ , $95\%$ CI [0.71–1.25], $\mathrm{P}=0.68$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ ${=}68\%.$ ) did not reveal any significant differences in individual doses versus ATP, with $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ having the highest nonsignificant effect and the last dose $(0.4\:\mathrm{mg/kg})$ having the most inferior and heteroge­ neous effect size. (Low quality of evidence).  

# 3.7. mRS score 0–1 at 3 months in LVO patients  

Three RCTs representing 704 patients analyzed the number of participants with LVO who recorded an mRS score of 0–1 at 3 months (Supplementary Fig. 3). TNK demonstrated a significant homogenous effect over ATP $\mathrm{\DeltaRR}=1.28$ , $95\%$ CI [1.07–1.52], $\mathbf{P}=0.006$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%\mathrm{)}$ . (High quality of evidence).

(High quality of evidence).  

# 3.9. Complete recanalization  

Four studies with 840 patients showed a borderline significant  

incidence of complete recanalization in patients receiving TNK versus ATP $\mathrm{{(RR=1}}.17\$ , $95\%$ CI [1.00–1.36], $\mathrm{P}=0.05$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ) (Fig. 4). (High quality of evidence).  

# 3.10. Partial/complete recanalization

# 4. Discussion  

This meta-analysis included ten RCTs with a total of 5123 patients (2676 allocated to tenecteplase and 2447 allocated to ATP) who were enrolled to investigate the efficacy and safety of tenecteplase over alteplase. The pooled analysis revealed no difference regarding excellent functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}1)$ at three months, good functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}0{-}2)$ at three months, ENI, partial/complete recanaliza­ tion, and onset-to-needle time between the two drugs. However, based on subgroup analysis of dosages, tenecteplase at a dose of $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ showed superiority over alteplase in improving excellent functional outcomes at 90 days. Additionally, it was found that tenecteplase $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ was associated with complete recanalization in comparison to alteplase. Regarding safety outcomes, there was no difference in mortality, ICH, or sICH between tenecteplase and alteplase. When the pooled analysis was performed on LVO patients, tenecteplase $0.25~\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathbf{k}\mathbf{g}$ showed significant improvement in excellent functional outcome compared to alteplase; however, good functional outcome, mortality, sICH, and rate of recanalization were similar between the interventions. Based on the non-inferiority meta-analysis result, tenecteplase was noninferior to alteplase, but it was not superior to alteplase.  

A previous meta-analysis was similar to our findings except that they did not find a significant difference in excellent functional outcome between tenecteplase $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ compared to alteplase. They revealed significant improvement in good functional outcomes and early neuro­ logical improvement [23]. This difference in good functional outcomes and ENI results could be explained by the fact that we only included RCTs in our analysis to provide the highest level of evidence. Other systematic reviews and meta-analyses stated that the tenecteplase $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ effect on early neurological improvement was significant, contradicting our findings [24,25]. The difference in sample size could explain this, as our analysis included more patients. Our meta-analysis showed that tenecteplase $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ was associated with improved functional outcomes and early neurological improvement compared to the other doses of tenecteplase; however, the results were not statisti­ cally significant. Although not statistically significant, the overall effect of different doses of tenecteplase demonstrated higher rates of excellent and good functional outcomes, which aligns with previous meta-analyses [23–25]. Regarding efficacy endpoints, we did not find a difference between the other doses of tenecteplase (0.1, 0.32, and $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg)}$ . Tenecteplase $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ was associated with higher mortality risks and sICH, yet the effect was insignificant. The NOR-TEST 2 trial compared tenecteplase $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ against alteplase [20]. They found that tenecteplase $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ did not result in better efficacy out­ comes and was associated with poor safety outcomes (sICH, any ICH, mortality) compared to alteplase. This could be explained by a longer plasma half-life in which a high tenecteplase concentration will increase the risk of bleeding based on its mechanism of action [26,27]. Ten­ ecteplase seems to have a potential beneficial effect over alteplase in achieving excellent functional outcomes for patients in the late window (more than $^{3\mathrm{~h~}}$ of symptom onset). However, this observation was limited since only two trials (AcT and TRACE-2) reported the outcome [21,22]. Evidence in this regard is lacking, and to our knowledge, we are the first meta-analysis to address the favorable effect of tenecteplase in late-window stroke. Thus, future studies are warranted to prove this concept.  

In patients with LVO, thrombolytic treatment with tenecteplase compared to alteplase resulted in a higher frequency of excellent and good functional outcomes without increasing the risk of sICH. These findings align with previous meta-analyses of randomized and non­ randomized studies [28–30,32]. The pharmacological properties of tenecteplase may contribute to its superiority over alteplase in achieving better functional outcomes in patients with LVO [28,29]. In addition, Campbell et al. hypothesized that the practical advantage of adminis­ tering tenecteplase as a single bolus compared to the 1-hour infusion of alteplase might benefit patients affected by major cerebral vessel ischemic stroke [19]. However, these findings should be interpreted carefully, especially in patients eligible for endovascular thrombectomy. Prior studies have shown conflicting results regarding the benefits of bridging therapy with intravenous thrombolytics prior to mechanical thrombectomy [31]. Hence, more evidence is needed to further inves­ tigate the practice of administering bridging therapy and to guide the choice between the two thrombolytics [31].  

Regarding neuroimaging outcomes, our result corroborates the re­ sults of a previous meta-analysis [24]. Tenecteplase $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ contributed to significantly higher rates of complete arterial recanali­ zation than alteplase. This favored effect by tenecteplase could be explained by its pharmacological properties [26,27]. In our analysis, tenecteplase failed to demonstrate a significantly superior recanaliza­ tion rate before mechanical thrombectomy compared to alteplase in LVO patients. The finding mentioned above contradicts the results of the EXTEND-IA trial [19]. This inconsistency could be explained by the differences among the included trials (EXTEND-IA and AcT) in study designs, settings, and patient selection [19,21,28]. In the EXTEND-IA trial, a mismatch in CT perfusion for only anterior circulation was used for patient selection, and $75\%$ of centers were mechanical thrombectomy-capable centers [19]. On the other hand, the AcT trial followed a pragmatic study design, including anterior and posterior circulation strokes, and patient care was primarily performed in comprehensive stroke centers [21].  

This study has limitations. First, all included trials except Haley et al. followed an open-label design and therefore had a high risk of perfor­ mance bias [11]. Second, tenecteplase doses varied across the included trials. Nevertheless, more robust evidence supports using tenecteplase $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ . Third, this meta-analysis was done based on aggregate data instead of individual participant data. Finally, different treatment win­ dows utilized among the included RCTs, medical treatment followed by endovascular thrombectomy which influence the results in some trials, inconsistency in defining sICH and ENI, and whether advanced imaging was used to select patients are possible sources of bias and heterogeneity that we could not control and, therefore, can limit the generalizability of the results.  

# 5. Conclusion  

Our findings established that tenecteplase is non-inferior to alteplase and showed no difference between the two thrombolytics in safety and efficacy endpoints, with more promising effects of tenecteplase in pa­ tients with large vessel occlusion. Furthermore, the ease of administra­ tion supports using tenecteplase as a suitable replacement for alteplase. Future research is warranted to investigate the efficacy of tenecteplase compared to alteplase for patients who presented in a late time window and patients with LVO eligible for mechanical thrombectomy.
