# Tranexamic Acid Inhibits Hematoma Expansion in Intracerebral Hemorrhage and Traumatic Brain Injury. Does Blood Pressure Play a Potential Role? A Meta-Analysis from Randmized Controlled Trials  

Bixi Gao, MD,# Tao Xue, MD,# Xiaoci Rong, MD,# Yanbo Yang, MD, Zilan Wang, MD, Zhouqing Chen, PhD, and Zhong Wang, PhD  

Background: Tranexamic acid (TXA) is an antifibrinolytic agent, which has shown an effect on reducing blood loss in many diseases. Many studies focus on the effect of TXA on cerebral hemorrhage, however, whether TXA can inhibit hematoma expansion is still controversial. Our meta-analysis performed a quantitative analysis to evaluate the efficacy of TXA for the hematoma expansion in spontaneous and traumatic intracranial hematoma. Method: Pubmed (MEDLINE), Embase, and Cochrane Library were searched from January 2001 to May 2020 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Result: We pooled 3102 patients from 7 RCTs to evaluate the efficacy of TXA for hematoma expansion. Hematoma expansion (HE) rate and hematoma volume (HV) change from baseline were used to analyze. We found that TXA led to a significant reduction in HE rate $(P=0.002)$ and HV change $(P=0.03)$ compared with the placebo. Patients with moderate or serious hypertension benefit more from TXA. (HE rate: $P=0.02\$ , HV change: $P=0.04^{'}$ ) TXA tends to have a better efficacy on HV change in intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). $(P=0.06)$ ) Conclusions: TXA showed good efficacy for hematoma expansion in spontaneous and traumatic intracranial hemorrhage. Patients with moderate/severe hypertension and ICH may be more suitable for TXA administration in inhibiting hematoma expansion .  

Key Words: Tranexamic acid—Spontaneous hemorrhage—Traumatic hemorrhage— Hematoma expansion—Hematoma volume change—Meta-analysis $\copyright$ 2020 Published by Elsevier Inc.  

# Introduction  

Spontaneous and traumatic intracranial bleeding commonly caused a high incidence of death and disability.1,2 Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and traumatic brain injury (TBI) are used to describe spontaneous and traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, respectively. Early removal of hematoma and reduction of blood pressure could significantly improve the prognosis of patients.3,4 After bleeding, as the levels of fibrinolysis and coagulopathy become higher, the intracranial hemorrhage may expand.5,6 The intracranial hematoma in half of the patients enlarges after hospital admission, which increased the difficulty of surgical removal and leads to higher mortality and disability.68 Therefore, effectively preventing hematoma expansion after hemorrhage is important for treating intracranial hemorrhage.  

Tranexamic acid (TXA) is an antifibrinolytic agent, which can compete with lysine residues on the surface of fibrin, blocks the interaction of plasminogen and plasmin to inhibit the fibrinolysis and consequently stabilize the clot.10 TXA has shown an effect on reducing blood loss in many diseases.1113 In ICH and TBI, TXA was often administrated by ${1\mathrm{g}}$ within $10\mathrm{min}$ and another $^\textrm{\scriptsize1g}$ during the next 8 hours. Moreover, the effect of TXA on the hematoma expansion of subarachnoid hemorrhage caused by ruptured intracranial aneurysms was confirmed in previous studies.14,15 In 2019, Hu’s study found that TXA could reduce rebleeding and hematoma expansion in cerebral hemorrhage.16 However, Hu’s study compared all types of cerebral hemorrhage, and there was no separate analysis of ICH, TBI, or SAH. The proven effect of TXA on subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) may have an impact on the results. Therefore, there is still a need to explore the influence of TXA on hematoma expansion in ICH and TBI. For ICH and TBI, some relevant RCTs showed that TXA didn’t work for hematoma expansion in intracranial hemorrhage.17,18 Because previous RCTs had inconsistent definitions of hematoma expansion, it was not rigorous to evaluate only the expansion rate of hematoma. It should be analyzed in conjunction with the volume of hematoma expansion. Although previous metaanalyses have found that TXA can inhibit hematoma expansion after cerebral hemorrhage, there is still a lack of quantitative analysis of hematoma volume changes.16,19 Therefore, we did not include patients with SAH into our meta-analysis for better assessing the effect of TXA on hematoma expansion in ICH and TBI. Moreover, there was no analysis of the effect of TXA on different populations before. Since TXA may cause thrombosis or and cerebral ischemia, it is necessary to be more clear about the applicable population when using TXA.  

During our meta-analysis, we increased the sample size (seven RCTs containing 3102 patients) and performed quantitative and subgroup analysis to further evaluate the efficacy of TXA for the hematoma expansion in spontaneous and traumatic intracranial hemorrhage.  

# Methods  

# Study protocol  

In the beginning, we drafted a research protocol by following the Cochrane Collaboration format.20  

# Eligibility criteria  

The inclusion criteria: (a) Type of study: RCT; (b) Language restriction: only English;(c) Participating patients: patients with intracranial hematoma; (d) Intervention: tranexamic acid (TXA) or placebo; (e) Outcomes: hematoma expansion rate and hematoma volume (HV) change from baseline.  

The exclusion criteria: (a) Types of study: retrospective studies, cohort studies, case reviews, and case reports; (b) Control: positive control; (c) Types of hemorrhage: SAH  

# Information sources and search strategy  

Three primary databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library, were systematically searched by one author (XR). The search strategy combined “tranexamic acid and TBI” AND “tranexamic acid and $\mathrm{ICH^{\prime\prime}}$ for MEDLINE. Studies that match the abstracts and titles were searched. The search strategies of EMBASE and Cochrane Library were similar to that search strategy. Besides, two investigators (BG and TX) ensured that all the related studies were included in this study. They independently manually screened the list of references from the RCTs.  

# Study selection and data collection  

Two reviewers (BG and TX) independently evaluated study records from the reference list and electronic database based on the eligibility criteria previously mentioned. If there were disagreements among BG and TX, the disputed studies would be discussed with the third person (XR) who didnt participate in the data collection to determine whether these studies should be included in the meta-analysis. After determining the studies included in the meta-analysis, two reviewers (BG and TX) independently and in duplicate extracted information from each included trial according to our protocol. The extracted variables include hematoma type, sample size, ethnic origin, GCS, systolic blood pressure, delay between initial and FU CT scanning, definition of hematoma expansion, TXA admission time, hematoma expansion rate, and HV change from baseline. After rigorously selected and assessed the literature by the two reviewers, all baseline information was extracted from included RCTs and summarized in Table 1.  

# Risk of Bias  

The risk of bias plot was evaluated based on the Review Manager 5.3 software for each RCT. The unified standard of the Cochrane Collaboration was applied to assess the risk of bias of RCTs, which included: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other potential biases.  

# Summary measures and synthesis of results  

The data were assessed by Revman (Version 5.3) software. Risk ratio (relative risk [RR]; $95\%$ confidence interval [CI]) or mean difference (mean difference [MD]; $95\%$ confidence interval [CI]) were analyzed using dichotomous or continuous outcomes respectively and calculated using a random effect model. Heterogeneity was estimated by the $I_{2}$ statistic. The $I_{2}$ statistic as follows: $I_{2}<$ $30\%$ means “low heterogeneity”; $I_{2}=30$ to $50\%$ denotes “moderate heterogeneity”; $I_{2}>50\%$ represents “substantial heterogeneity”. We implemented subgroup analyses to detect different types of hematoma (spontaneous or traumatic hematoma), different Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) $(<13~\mathrm{or}\ge13)\$ ), different systolic blood pressure $\mathrm{\Omega}(\le160\mathrm{mmHg}$ or $>160\mathrm{mmHg},$ , and different admission time ( $\leq8\mathrm{~h~}$ or $>8\mathrm{~h~}$ ) at baseline. A $\mathrm{P}$ value of less than 0.05 was considered significant, and two-tailed tests were implemented for all analyses.

We identified 365 titles and abstracts through MEDLINE, EMBASE, and CENTRAL (Fig. 1). After removing the duplicates and irrelevant articles, 120 full-text articles were evaluated for eligibility. Besides, 110 articles were excluded because of the restriction of publication types: 12 protocols, six meta-analyses, 26 reviews, 13 editorials, 11 retrospective cohort studies, and 42 different analyses of the same RCT. However, among the RCTs included in qualitative synthesis, three 2123 of ten RCTs $^{17,18,21-28}$ did not compare hematoma expansion rate or hematoma volume change from baseline. Eventually, seven RCTs containing 3102 patients (TXA, $n=1553$ ; placebo, $n=1549$ ) were included in quantitative synthesis. The main characteristics of seven RCTs are listed in Table 1.  

# Outcomes analysis  

All seven RCTs enrolling 3102 patients were pooled to analyze the hematoma expansion rate and hematoma volume change from baseline. Patients in TXA group had a significantly lower rate of hematoma expansion $(23.66\%$ vs $28.16\%$ for TXA vs placebo, $\mathrm{RR}=0.83$ , $95\%$ CI 0.74 to 0.93, $P=0.002$ ; Fig. 2). On the other hand, TXA was seemed to reduce the hematoma volume change from baseline $\mathrm{(MD=-1.06,}$ , $95\%$ CI $-2.04$ to -0.08, $P=0.03.$ ; Fig. 3), compared with placebo. The heterogeneity of hematoma expansion rate and hematoma volume change from baseline was both $0\%$ (Fig. 2 and 3).

Risk of Bias in included studies  

Fig. 4 showed the details of the risk bias for all studies. There was no clinical trial that had an unclear or high risk of bias in random sequence generation. For allocation concealment, the risk of bias was unclear in one study. For the blinding of participants and personnel, the risk of bias was high in two studies. For the blinding of outcome assessment, the risk of bias was unclear in three trials. For the incomplete outcome data, the risk of bias was unclear in two trials. For selective reporting, the risk of bias was unclear in two studies. Apart from these items, one trial had other biases. Overall, the quality of the included studies in this meta-analysis was acceptable (Fig. 5).  

# Discussion  

Our meta-analysis found that TXA could significantly inhibit the hematoma expansion in spontaneous and traumatic intracranial hematoma, consistent with the antifibrinolytic effect of TXA after intracerebral hemorrhage. Previous studies have often used patient prognosis or the need for neurosurgery as the primary outcome. CRASH-3 trial did not perform an analysis of hematoma expansion, which made it impossible for us to incorporate such a larger sample RCT into our meta-analysis.22 However, in addition to the hematoma location, hematoma volume is one of the most direct factors affecting the prognosis of the patient.29 As an antifibrinolytic drug, TXA has a direct effect on hemostasis. Therefore, when evaluating the effects of $\mathrm{TXA,}$ preventing hematoma expansion is the most fundamental and important. If TXA cannot prevent hematoma expansion, it is difficult to prove its significance in improving prognosis. Therefore, our metaanalysis evaluates the effect of TXA on hematoma expansion and the applicable population when using TXA explicitly. Because the included RCTs have different definitions of hematoma expansion, we also use the change in hematoma volume as one of the evaluation indicators.

After analysis, we found that TXA could reduce the hematoma expansion rate after intracranial hemorrhage and significantly reduce the volume of hematoma expansion. However, the possible benefit from TXA appears in specific populations. Interestingly, the effect of TXA on inhibiting hematoma expansion is more pronounced in patients with ICH and moderate/severe hypertension. This indicates that the use of TXA in patients with moderate/severe hypertension seems to be more effective than the use of TXA in patients with no/mild hypertension, which suggests that the patient's blood pressure plays a potential role in TXA inhibiting hematoma expansion. Patients with moderate/severe hypertension have more bleeding and more possibility of rebleeding.30 Because TXA is an antifibrinolytic agent, which can inhibit rebleeding after ICH and TBI, the effect of TXA on inhibiting rebleeding is more significant in moderate/severe hypertension patients. Besides, since hypertensive cerebral hemorrhage accounts for the majority of ICH, the effect of TXA in inhibiting hematoma expansion is relatively more pronounced in ICH. TICH trial is designed to verify the feasibility of the test itself, and the sample size is only twenty-four. Although the TICH trial pointed out that TXA has no significant improvement in hematoma expansion in ICH patients, the results are not persuasive. When it comes to GCS, it seems that patients with severe disorders of consciousness benefit more from TXA. However, patients of both RCTs with the mean ${\mathrm{GCS}}\geq13$ are suffered from moderate/severe hypertension.24,26 Hence, TXA tends to have a better effect on patients with higher GCS. Moreover, the early administration of TXA has a good effect as we expect. But this does not mean that the early administration of TXA is indispensable. Since hemostasis is a spontaneous physiological process, bleeding may stop spontaneously after a period of the hematoma. According to our findings, in patients with ICH and TBI, the benefits of early blood pressure reduction are more pronounced than using TXA. For patients whose blood pressure is difficult to control, the effect of TXA is more significant. Moreover, the early administration of TXA for these patients is more practical. However, for patients without hypertension or only mild hypertension, to avoid the possible ischemic stroke, the use of TXA to inhibit hematoma expansion is unnecessary.

When assessing HV changes from baseline, we found the standard deviations in these RCTs were almost higher than the mean value. Not all hematoma expanded during the early period in ICH and TBI. Besides, differences in hematoma volume change may be influenced by CT scanning timing, different measurement of the volume, other criteria of diagnosing, and methodology of researches.3133 Due to the significant difference in hematoma volume between different individuals, a massive $95\%$ CI was obtained after calculation. This further illustrates that although TXA can inhibit hematoma expansion in some patients, whether it can be applied to all people still lacks sufficient evidence. TXA is only used to inhibit possible hematoma expansion only. Since most patients will not have hematoma expansion, they can not benefit from TXA but will have a higher risk of ischemic stroke. Therefore, it is more critical to choose a more suitable patients group. As mentioned earlier, patients with moderate to severe hypertension may become more suitable for early TXA administration. Moreover, some patients' hematoma can be absorbed during one day of cerebral hemorrhage, and it seems more meaningful only to evaluate the hematoma volume change in patients with hematoma expansion. Our study suggests that future clinical trials can accurately analyze the effect of TXA on hematoma volume changes in hypertensive patients.  

Our study also has several limitations. First, when evaluating HV changes from baseline, the SDs of RCTs are significant so that the results may be biased. Second, there may be some deviations in the results due to the large gap in the sample size of the selected RCT. Moreover, the proportion of men and women in ICH is balanced, but TBI patients are mainly male. We cannot assess whether TXA has different effects on suppressing hematoma expansion in different genders. Nevertheless, the present study was based on 3102 patients from seven RCTs and can be viewed as hypothesis-generating.  

# Conclusion  

TXA showed good efficacy for hematoma expansion in ICH and TBI. Early TXA administration is more effective in inhibiting hematoma expansion in patients with hardcontrolled moderate or serious hypertension. Moreover, ICH patients benefit more from TXA, but the difference was not statistically significant. Future research should focus on the potential role of blood pressure in TXA inhibiting hematoma expansion.
