# Acute Blood Pressure and Outcome After Intracerebral Hemorrhage: The VISTA-ICH Cohort  

Charles L Francoeur, MD, MSc,\*,† and Stephan A Mayer, ${\mathrm{MD}}\ddagger,$ , on behalf of the VISTA-ICH Collaborators  

Background: Recent clinical trials suggest that it is safe to acutely lower systolic blood pressure (BP) to $140~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ after ICH, but uncertainty remains regarding optimal management. We sought to better define the link between BP and outcome in ICH patients using data from the Virtual International Stroke Trials Archive (VISTA). Methods: We performed a retrospective analysis of patients of the VISTAICH trials. We measured the strength of association between systolic and diastolic BP various components at different timepoints with unfavorable 3 month-outcome, defined as death or moderate-to-severe disability at 3 months (mRS of 4-6), after adjustment for known confounders. We also dichotomized BP values obtained at $24\mathrm{{h}}$ at different thresholds to better define an optimal treatment target. The association of BP with hematoma expansion (HE) was also analyzed. Results: A total of 384 patients were included. Higher BP at 24 hours was associated with unfavorable outcome for systolic BP (OR 1.16, $95\%$ C.I. 1.071.25), pulse pressure (OR 1.13, $95\%$ C.I. 1.031.24), and diastolic BP (OR 1.11, $95\%$ C.I. 1.011.23) per $10\mathrm{mmHg}$ increment. The association between higher BP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and unfavorable outcome remained significant down to ${>}140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ . Elevated systolic BP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ was also associated with HE (OR 1.11, $95\%$ C.I. 1.021.21 per $10~\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ increment). Conclusion: Elevated BP after ICH at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ is associated with poor outcome. Our results support the practice of targeting a systolic BP of $140\mathrm{mmHg}$ .

# Introduction  

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a devastating form of stroke, and well-established therapeutic strategies to improve outcome are still lacking.1 The first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after ICH have a well-characterized dynamic evolution, with approximately one-third of patients presenting within $^{3\mathrm{~h~}}$ experiencing significant haematoma expansion (HE), thereby increasing the likelihood of unfavorable outcome or death.2,3 Aggressive blood pressure (BP) control to mitigate this trajectory is an attractive treatment strategy which is supported by observational studies linking higher BP to poor outcome,  and physiological studies rejecting the concept of penumbral ischemia in perihematomal brain tissue.7,8  

Recent randomized controlled trials comparing systolic BP targets of $\le180$ versus ${\leq}140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ have shed light on what may be the optimal BP target after ICH, but discrepancies in their results and interpretation have slowed translation into practice and clinical guidelines. While the INTERACT-2 trial showed marginal improvement in functional outcome with a treatment target of less than $140~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ compared to $180\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg},^{9,10}$ the ATACH-2 trial failed to reproduce these results, despite faster and more effective intervention.11 The 2015 American Heart  

Association Guidelines for the Management of ICH concluded that lowering SBP to levels of $140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ is safe and might be effective for improving outcome, but uncertainty remains.12 For instance, a recent report found that intensive lowering of SBP to ${<}140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ after ICH was associated with an increased risk of remote ischemic lesions on MRI and a higher rate of neurological deterioration.13 We sought to analyze the VISTA-ICH cohort of placebo patients to further explore the relationship between acute BP levels and outcome after ICH.  

# Methods  

# Study population  

The Virtual International Stroke Trials Archive (VISTA)14 is an international collaborative pooled repository of anonymized datasets from randomized clinical trials. We included patients from the placebo arm of the ICH trials, including only adult non-traumatic spontaneous ICH. The VISTAICH steering committee approved the analysis. The data is available from the VISTA-ICH upon reasonable request (www.virtualtrialsarchives.org/vista-ICH)  

# Clinical data  

We obtained basic demographic information including age, gender and medical history such as hypertension, previous stroke, atrial fibrillation, previous myocardial infarct, diabetes, creatinine value and smoking status. Data on ICH location (lobar vs infratentorial), presence of intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), modified Rankin Scale (mRS), and NIH stroke scale score at baseline were also collected. The exposure of interest, BP, was retrieved at baseline, 24, 48, $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ and 90 days (diastolic and systolic) in all patients. The intensity of BP lowering between time points (delta) and the pulse pressure $(\mathrm{PP}=\mathrm{SBP}$ minus DBP) were calculated. Baseline international normalized ratio (INR) was also obtained to exclude coagulopathic patients $(\mathrm{INR}>1.4)$ ).  

# Outcome data  

ICH and edema volumes measured with head CT were available at baseline, 24 and $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ using planimetry by tracing the region of interest and multiplying the area by slice thickness. Ninety-day mRS was used to appraise the primary outcome, with unfavorable outcome defined as an mRS of 46, indicating a state of death or moderate-tosevere disability (i.e. bedbound or unable to walk without assistance). Mortality at day 90 and hematoma expansion defined as absolute growth of $12.5\mathrm{mL}^{15}$ at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ were analyzed as secondary outcomes.  

# Statistical analysis  

Logistic regression was used to measure the association between BP values at different time points and the odds of unfavorable outcome at 3 months. Univariable and multivariable regressions were performed using the five components of the ICH score.16 (age $\ge80$ years, Glasgow Coma Scale score, ICH volume ${>}30\mathrm{mL},$ presence of intraventricular hemorrhage, and infratentorial location) to adjust odds ratios. Confidence intervals $(95\%)$ are provided for every result. Moreover, SBP values were categorized as high or low at different cut-off values, ranging from 110 to $190\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ by intervals of $10\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg},$ to better define the optimal SBP threshold associated with outcome. The association of delta BP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and pulse pressure at baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ with the primary outcome were also assessed and adjusted. The same analyses were performed for the secondary outcomes of HE and mortality.  

# Results  

Patients characteristics, clinical presentation, and outcome  

The sample from the VISTA-ICH database included 385 control patients with a mean age of 66, a male majority, and a large proportion already diagnosed with hypertension (Table 1). They presented generally with good clinical grade ( $66\%$ with GCS 14 or 15) and elevated blood pressure (mean $179/96\mathrm{{mm}Hg})$ . Ninety-day mortality was $21\%$ , $32\%$ were alive with moderate-to-severe disability $(\mathrm{mRS}4\mathrm{or}5)$ ), and $47\%$ of patients had a favorable outcome $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}3)$ . All components of the ICH score were associated with mortality, and all but infratentorial location were associated with unfavorable outcome in our cohort (Table 2).

# Association between BP and outcome  

Admission BP (systolic or diastolic) was not associated with outcome, but values at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ were associated with a $16\%$ increase in the risk of unfavorable outcome for each $10\mathrm{mmHg}$ increment in systolic BP (OR 1.16, $95\%$ C.I. 1.07 1.25) and $11\%$ for each $10\mathrm{mmHg}$ increment in diastolic BP (OR 1.11, $95\%$ C.I. 1.011.23). Higher pulse pressure at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ was also significantly associated with unfavorable outcome with each $10\mathrm{mmHg}$ increment (OR 1.13, $95\%$ C.I. 1.031.24). Change in BP between baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ was not associated with outcome. With dichotomization of SBP into high and low groups at progressive thresholds ranging from 110 to $190~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ at $24\mathrm{~h},$ the adjusted odds ratio between $\mathrm{SBP}>190\mathrm{mmHg}$ and poor outcome was very high (3.23, $95\%$ CI 1.61—6.73). Odds ratios for poor outcome were increased at all levels between ${>}110$ and $>180\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg},$ although only significant at the ${>}140,{>}150$ and ${>}160\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ thresholds (Table 3 and Fig. 1).  

# Association between BP and hematoma growth  

Overall, $22\%$ of patients experienced hematoma expansion at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ . HE was associated with poor outcome (OR 5.18, $95\%$ C.I. 3.488.02) and mortality (OR 3.74, $95\%$ C.I. 2.655.30). Although admission BP did not predict HE, all subsequent time points did, with higher SBP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ associated with an $11\%$ increase in the risk of HE per $10\mathrm{mmHg}$ (OR 1.11, $95\%$ C.I. 1.021.21) (Table 4). We did not detect an association between HE and PP at any time point, or with delta BP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ .  

# Discussion  

In this analysis of control patients enrolled in a variety of ICH trials we found that higher BP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ was associated with unfavorable outcome at 3 months, whereas admission BP was not. There was a $16\%$ increase in the risk of unfavorable outcome for each $10\mathrm{mmHg}$ increment in systolic BP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ . The association was strongest when SBP exceeded $190\mathrm{mmHg}$ and remained significant at the majority of thresholds down to ${>}140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ . We also found that higher PP and DBP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ were related to unfavorable outcome, but the strength of association was slightly weaker than for SBP. Hematoma expansion was unrelated to admission BP but had a strong association with higher SBP at 24, 48 and $^{72\mathrm{h},}$ as well as with mortality and unfavorable outcome at 3 months.

Acute hypertension is known to be the most important causal factor in spontaneous ${\mathrm{ICH}},$ , and it remains plausible that persistent BP elevation may aggravate secondary injury by driving the active bleeding process in the hyperacute phase $(<4\mathrm{~h~})$ , and worsening tissue and microvascular injury in the hours that follow. The fact that admission BP was unrelated to subsequent HE weakens the argument that acute hypertension drives active bleeding in  

ICH to a substantial degree, whereas the association of HE with hypertension at all later time points raises the possibility that subsequent BP elevation is a consequence rather than a cause of HE. Given that neither INTERACT2 or ATACH-2 demonstrated a significant reduction in hematoma growth with BP reduction starting at 3 to $^\textrm{\scriptsize4h}$ after onset,9,11 it would seem that only a trial of ultra-early BP reduction $(<2\mathrm{~h})$ will settle this question definitively.  

The significant association of PP and somewhat stronger association of SBP than DBP with outcome in our study supports the concept that pulsatile shear stress may play a role in aggravating these processes. This concept is supported by studies showing that BP variability, presumably leading to higher shear stress, is associated with worse outcome after $\bar{\mathrm{ICH.}}^{17,18}$ Given that we only had access to BP at four time points, we were not able to calculate more sophisticated measures of BP variability.  

Perhaps the most important finding of this study is the persistent association between higher BP at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and worse outcome down to a threshold of ${>}140\mathrm{mmHg}$ . This should further comfort clinicians who use a SBP target of ${\leq}140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ as their therapeutic goal in the acute phase of ICH, based on the INTERACT-2 and ATACH-2 studies.9,11 The markedly higher odds ratio for unfavorable outcome when SBP persisted above $190~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ should similarly raise alarm if BP is allowed to remain uncontrolled above this level.  

The main strength of our study is the prospective nature and high quality of the randomized controlled trial data that we analyzed, including blinded and standardized assessments of BP, clinical outcomes, and HE. This would be expected to reduce the potential for ascertainment or observer bias which can occur in unblended single center cohort studies. Our patient population also reflects a global and very diverse set of ICH patients. The main limitations to our results include their observational nature and the relatively small sample size. Retrospective, post-hoc analyses remain exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, our study population is skewed towards less disease severity, since many ICH trial protocols exclude poor grade patients in coma or who go for emergency craniotomy. In summary, our results, bearing in mind the above-mentioned limitations, are confirmatory and offer support to an SBP target of ${\leq}140\mathrm{mmHg}$ in patients with acute spontaneous ICH.
