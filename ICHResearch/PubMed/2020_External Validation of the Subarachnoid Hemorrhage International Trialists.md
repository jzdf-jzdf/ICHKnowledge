BACKGROUND: The Subarachnoid Hemorrhage International Trialists (SAHIT) repository is a collection of randomized clinical trials, prospective observational studies, and hospital registries that was used to create a predictive model of unfavorable outcome/mortality following aneurysmal SAH.  

OBJECTIVE: To externally validate the SAHIT model using Barrow-Ruptured Aneurysm Trial (BRAT) data, which was not included in the SAHIT repository.  

METHODS: This is a post hoc analysis of the prospective, randomized BRAT. Three models were created: (1) Core (age, hypertension, World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade), (2) neuroimaging (aneurysm size/location, Fisher score), and (3) full model (model 1 and 2 plus treatment type). The performance of the models was evaluated by measures of model discrimination (area under the curve [AUC]) and model calibration (goodness of fit test, calibration in-the-large, calibration slope).  

RESULTS: A total of 338 patients (average age 54 years; $67.7\%$ good clinical grade; average aneurysm size $6.7\mathrm{mm}$ ; $84.1\%$ anterior circulation) were included. Due to a large number of crossovers, more aneurysms were clipped than coiled $(67.5\%$ vs $32.5\%$ , respectively). A total of $10.1\%$ of the patients died and $29.6\%$ experienced an unfavorable outcome. For unfavorable outcome, the AUCs for the three models were: 0.728, 0.732, and 0.734, respectively. For mortality, the AUCs for the three models were: 0.721, 0.739, and 0.744, respectively. Overall, all models showed good calibration, and the measures of calibration fell within $95\%$ CI of those produced in the SAHIT study.  

CONCLUSION: Using the BRAT data, we have externally validated the SAHIT model for predicting unfavorable outcome and mortality after SAH. The model may be used to counsel patients and families on prognosis following aneurysmal SAH.

Approximately, one-third of patients do not survive and at least $20\%$ of survivors do not regain functional independence.1 Because there are considerable differences in the presenting clinical features, aneurysm characteristics, and clinical course in affected individuals, it is difficult to precisely predict clinical outcome during the acute phase of the disease. Thus, it is important to develop reliable, evidence-based tools to inform outcome expectations to help guide treatment decisions and patient/family counseling.  

The Subarachnoid Hemorrhage International Trialists (SAHIT) repository is a collection of randomized clinical trials (RCT), prospective observational studies, and hospital registries.2,3 Using the SAHIT data, clinical outcome has been linked to features such as hypertension (HTN),4 neurological status,4 loss of consciousness,5 aneurysm characteristics,6 and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH).7 Most recently, the SAHIT data has been used to develop a robust model that is highly predictive of outcome.8 The model includes age, HTN, World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFNS) grade, aneurysm location, aneurysm size, Fisher grade, and treatment type. The SAHIT repository was used to both develop the model and provide internal validation of the model. The purpose of the present study is to provide external validation of this model using the Barrow-Ruptured Aneurysm Trial (BRAT) dataset, which is not part of the SAHIT repository.  

# METHODS  

The original BRAT study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of St. Joseph’s Hospital and Medical Center, Phoenix, Arizona and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov [NTC01593267]. Additional IRB approval was obtained to perform the present post hoc analysis. A detailed description of the original BRAT study protocol has also been previously reported.9 Briefly, all with nontraumatic SAH who were admitted between March 2003 and January 2007 and were deemed eligible were included if permission was obtained. Of 500 eligible patients, 29 were excluded and the remaining 471 patients were randomly assigned: 238 to the intent-to-clip cohort and 233 to the intent-to-coil cohort.  

There were no anatomical exclusion criteria in the original BRAT. Therefore, six categories of nontraumatic SAH were included: (1) nonaneurysmal SAH; (2) aneurysms associated with arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) or fistulas; (3) fusiform aneurysms; (4) blister aneurysms; (5) dissecting aneurysms; and (6) saccular aneurysms. From the initial BRAT data set $(\mathrm{n}=471\$ ), the following exclusion criteria were applied for the present analysis: nonaneurysmal SAH $(\mathbf{n}=57)$ ), patient death prior to treatment $(\mathrm{n}=6)$ ), aneurysms associated with other vascular malformations $(\mathrm{n}=3)$ , and insufficient follow-up $(\mathrm{n}=67).$ ). The final study population included 338 patients.  

The following information was collected as part of the original BRAT: age, past medical history (including HTN), Hunt Hess score, Glascow Coma Scale (GCS), neurological exam, Fisher grade, aneurysm location, aneurysm size, treatment type, and clinical outcome as measured by the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS). As part of the initial BRAT, a research nurse practitioner collected the follow-up data and performed the mRS assessments. For the purposes of this analysis, WFNS was derived from the GCS and neurological exam as follows: grade $\mathrm{I}=\mathrm{GCS}~15$ , no motor deficit; grade $\mathrm{II}=\mathrm{GCS}~13$ to 14, no motor deficit; grade $\mathrm{III}=\mathrm{GCS}~13\$ to 14, motor deficit; grade $\mathrm{IV}=\mathrm{GCS}~7$ to 12; grade $\mathrm{V}=\mathrm{GCS}3$ to 6. Aphasia was recorded in the BRAT-structured data and was counted as a motor deficit. Unfavorable outcome was defined as mRS greater than 2.  

All statistical analyses were performed with the R Statistical Computing software (version 3.4.2, R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). As described in Jaja et al,8 we created 2 sets of binary logistic regression models to predict unfavorable functional outcome and mortality using the following independent variables: patient age, admission WFNS score (1-5), history of HTN $(\mathrm{yes/no)}$ , Fisher grade for SAH (volume of SAH on CT scan), aneurysm size ${\bf\zeta}_{<12\mathrm{mm}}$ , $13{-}24\mathrm{mm}$ , ${>}24\mathrm{mm}$ ), aneurysm location (anterior communicating artery, internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery, and posterior circulation that include the vertebral and basilar arteries), and treatment type (clipping vs coiling), For this particular analysis, the 6-mo follow-up time point was used as this was the closest available outcome time point to that used in the original SAHIT analysis $(3\mathrm{mo})$ .  

In concordance with Jaja et al,8 we created 3 multivariate logistic regression models for our 2 primary outcomes of interest, unfavorable clinical outcome and mortality: (1) Core Model that include patient age, premorbid HTN, and preoperative neurological grade (WFNS scale); (2) Neuroimaging Model with SAH clot volume (Fisher grade), aneurysm size and location; and (3) Full Model with both core and neuroimaging variables as well as treatment modality. The performance of the models was evaluated by measures of model discrimination (area under the receiver operator characteristics curve [AUC]) and model calibration (goodness of fit test, calibration in-the-large (CIL), and calibration slope) applied on the BRAT data. To evaluate the degree of agreement between the predictions and observed outcomes, we then calculated CIL and recalibration slope values and graphed calibration plots for each of the 6 models.  

# RESULTS  

A total of 338 patients with nontraumatic SAH in the BRAT met the inclusion/exclusion criteria and were included in the validation cohort. The baseline demographics are displayed in Table 1. The average age $\pm$ standard deviation) of patients was $54\pm12\mathrm{y}$ r, the majority of patients $(67.7\%)$ were admitted with good clinical grade (WFNS I-III). The average size of the ruptured aneurysms was $6.7\pm3.7\mathrm{{mm}}$ . The vast majority of ruptured aneurysms $(84.1\%)$ were located in the anterior circulation: anterior cerebral aneurysms that include the anterior communicating artery $(\mathrm{n}~=~126\$ ; $37.3\%$ ), internal cerebral aneurysms $'\mathrm{n}=108$ , $32.0\%$ ), and middle cerebral aneurysms $\mathrm{\Deltan}=50$ ; $14.8\%$ ). Although patients were equally assigned to each treatment group, because a large number of aneurysms crossed over from coiling to clipping in the BRAT, more aneurysms were clipped than coiled the present analysis $(67.5\%$ vs $32.5\%$ , respectively). In this cohort, $10.1\%$ of the patients died and $29.6\%$ experienced an unfavorable outcome.  

For predicting unfavorable functional outcome, the core model and neuroimaging model were comparable in predictive strength (AUC: 0.728 vs 0.732, respectively; Figure 1A and 1B). When these were extended to the full model, the predictive value increased slightly (AUC: $0.734$ ; Figure 1C), which was not statistically significant $\begin{array}{r}{\langleP=.09,}\end{array}$ ). The model performed similarly for predicting mortality: the predictive strength for the core model (AUC: 0.721), neuroimaging model (AUC: 0.739) and the full model (AUC: 0.744; Figure 1D-1F) were similar. There were no statistically significance differences when comparing the AUC values among the core, neuroimaging, and full models (core vs neuroimaging: $P=.22$ ; neuroimaging vs full: $P=.24$ ; core vs full: $P=.07\$ ).  

Overall, all of the prediction models for unfavorable clinical outcome and mortality showed good calibration (Figure 2A-2F), and the measures of calibration fell within those produced in the original SAHIT validation study. The models for unfavorable outcome had slight under- and over-estimation of observed unfavorable outcome risk and the models for mortality showed minimal underestimation of observed mortality risk.

# DISCUSSION  

# Main Findings  

Using the BRAT cohort, we were able to externally validate the SAHIT predictive model. Our study population was similar to the SAHIT development cohort in terms of age (BRAT 54 yr vs SAHIT $53\mathrm{yr}$ ), percent of anterior circulation aneurysms (BRAT $84\%$ vs SAHIT $88\%$ ), percent of patient who underwent clipping (BRAT $68\%$ vs SAHIT $68\%$ ), and percent of patients who had a poor outcome (BRAT $30\%$ vs SAHIT $29\%$ ). The two study populations differed in terms of the percent of patients with HTN (BRAT $45\%$ vs SAHIT $63\%$ ), the percent of patients who presented with a poor neurological grade (BRAT $32\%$ vs SAHIT $21\%$ ), the percent of patients who had Fisher Grade 4 SAH (BRAT $64\%$ vs SAHIT $20\%$ ), and the percent of aneurysms that were small (BRAT $92\%$ vs SAHIT $79\%$ ).  

The AUCs generated in our study lay within the $95\%$ CI values in the SAHIT model (Table 2).8 With respect to unfavorable outcome, we found an AUC of 0.734 when using the full model. In comparison, the SAHIT data generated an AUC of 0.81 for unfavorable outcome using the full model. With respect to mortality, we found an AUC of 0.744. In comparison, the SAHIT data generated an AUC of 0.76 for mortality using the full model. These results confirm that the SAHIT predictive model is accurate and usable for predicting both unfavorable outcome and mortality based on clinical and radiographic data points at admission. The model held up even with a number of significant differences in cohorts described above.  

# External Validation  

External validation is an essential final step in the process of developing predictive models. This practice has been used throughout medicine, neurosurgery, and vascular neurosurgery. In 2014, Collins et $\mathrm{al}^{10}$ published a systematic review of methodological conduct and reporting of external validation of multivariate prediction models. Seventy-eight studies describing the evaluation of 120 models were included. The majority of the studies $(\mathtt{n}=45\mathtt{\Gamma}.$ ) were performed separately (from the original study) evaluating an existing model, as is ours. Technical limitations of the included studies included using a simplified scoring system $(57\%)$ , not reporting outcome events $(16\%)$ , no mention of missing data $(54\%)$ , and not evaluating model calibration $\left(67\%\right)$ . The authors concluded that: “the validation studies were characterized by poor design, inappropriate handling and acknowledgement of missing data and one of the most key performance measures of prediction models, ie, calibration often omitted from the publication.” Our study, on the other hand, uses the exact same SAHIT model, reports clinical outcomes, clearly states that our missing data is excluded, and includes model calibration, thus making it, by comparison, one of the better performed validation studies.  

# Application of the Model  

The SAHIT predictive model8 is an addition to a large body of literature devoted to predicting outcome in SAH.11-19 In comparison to previous models (such as FRESH17) and others generated off individual trial data (such as ISAT),14 the SAHIT predictive model has the advantages of power, representativeness, internal validation,8 and, now, external validation. The authors have, in turn, created a web-based prognostic calculator that is accessible to physicians, patients, and families (http://sahitscore.com/).  

Prediction of functional outcome following SAH is more meaningful in modern practice given that many more patients are surviving with improved aneurysm treatment and ICU care. In contemporary practice, clinical discussions with caretakers of SAH patients tend to focus less on survival and have shifted more toward addressing long-term quality of life and neurological function. Patients and their families would benefit greatly with reliable predictive information regarding ultimate prognosis as early as possible, and can assist them in guiding treatment decisions and expectations. The score may even be used in an outpatient clinic, similar to the PHASES score for unruptured aneurysms,20 to help counsel patients and families on what a hypothetical SAH prognosis may be depending on grade.

# Limitations  

Our manuscript has limitations. First, because BRAT was an all-inclusive trial of nontraumatic SAH, the cohort being analyzed was not uniformly saccular aneurysms but instead included atypical aneurysms as well. Second, as stated throughout the BRAT publications, there was a bias toward endovascular to surgical crossovers, which accounts for the lower overall percentage of patients who were coiled. Third, also as stated throughout the BRAT publications, the posterior circulation aneurysms were not well randomized, with many more PICA aneurysms assigned to clipping than to coiling. Fourth, the data is derived from a single center, which may reduce its applicability.  

# Strengths  

The use of the BRAT cohort for external validation has a number of strengths. First, the analysis is being performed on well-acquired and maintained trial data. Second, the clinical and radiographic outcomes were performed in a completely unbiased fashion by research nurses and radiologists, respectively. Third, the same all-inclusive nature as described above means that the study population truly represents a nontraumatic SAH population, unlike most trial data in which patients are highly selected to participate. Fourth, the BRAT cohort differed from the SAHIT cohort in a number of ways (HTN, presenting neurological grade, Fisher grade, and aneurysm size), thus strengthening the applicability of the model. Finally, as discussed above, our study meets the standard for an external validation study in comparison to other previously performed external validation studies.10

# COMMENT  

n the present manuscript, the authors utilize data from the Barrow Ruptured Aneurysm Trial (BRAT) to externally validate the recent Subarachnoid Hemorrhage International Trialists (SAHIT) predictive model. As published in 2018, the SAHIT repository included a multinational population of 10 936 patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage and developed a validated prediction score for clinical outcomes.  

External validation of predictor scales is an essential but underutilized tool for confirming utility in the clinical setting. Scores determined by a single study may of course have bias, simply given the authors’ implicit assumptions. However, external validation of the score with a separate population (and different authors) will challenge the accuracy and thus assess widespread applicability. Therefore, clinical predictors should be challenged more frequently by external validation studies, to enhance the rigor by which we accept these models.
