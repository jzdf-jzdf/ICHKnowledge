# Abstract  



# Objective  

To investigate the efficacy of tenecteplase (TNK), a genetically modified variant of alteplase with greater fibrin specificity and longer half-life than alteplase, prior to endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) in patients with basilar artery occlusion (BAO).  



# Methods  

To determine whether TNK is associated with better reperfusion rates than alteplase prior to EVT in BAO, clinical and procedural data of consecutive patients with BAO from the Basilar Artery Treatment and Management (BATMAN) registry and the Tenecteplase vs Alteplase before Endovascular Therapy for Ischemic Stroke (EXTEND-IA TNK) trial were retrospectively analyzed. Reperfusion ${>}50\%$ or absence of retrievable thrombus at the time of the initial angiogram was evaluated.  



# Results  

We included 110 patients with BAO treated with IV thrombolysis prior to EVT (mean age 69 [SD 14] years; median NIH Stroke Scale score 16 [interquartile range (IQR) 7–32]). Nineteen patients were thrombolysed with TNK $(0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ or $0.40~\mathrm{mg/kg})$ and 91 with alteplase $(0.9~\mathrm{mg/kg)}$ . Reperfusion ${>}50\%$ occurred in $26\%$ ( $\mathbf{\tilde{n}}=5/19$ ) of patients thrombolysed with TNK vs $7\%$ $\binom{\cdot}{\cdot}=6/91$ ) thrombolysed with alteplase (risk ratio 4.0, $95\%$ confidence interval 1.3–12; $p=0.02\$ ), despite shorter thrombolysis to arterial puncture time in the TNK-treated patients (48 [IQR 40–71] minutes) vs alteplase-treated patients (110 [IQR 51–185] minutes; $p=0.004_{,}$ ). No difference in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was observed $\left(0/19\left[0\%\right]\mathrm{TNK},1/91\left[1\%\right]\right.$ alteplase; $p=0.9$ ).  



# Conclusions  

TNK may be associated with an increased rate of reperfusion in comparison with alteplase before EVT in BAO. Randomized controlled trials to compare TNK with alteplase in patients with BAO are warranted.

# Glossary  

$\mathbf{BAO}=$ basilar artery occlusion; BATMAN $=$ Basilar Artery Treatment and Management; $\mathbf{CI}=$ confidence interval; $\mathbf{EVT}=$   
endovascular therapy; EXTEND-IA TNK $=$ Tenecteplase vs Alteplase before Endovascular Therapy for Ischemic Stroke;   
$\mathbf{IOR}=$ interquartile range; $\mathbf{IVT}=\mathbf{IV}$ thrombolysis; $\mathbf{LVO}=$ large vessel occlusion; $\mathbf{m}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{ICI}=$ modified Treatment in Cerebral Ischemia; $\mathbf{NIHSS}=\mathbf{NIH}$ Stroke Scale; $\mathbf{PH}=$ parenchymal hematoma; $\mathbf{RCT}=$ randomized controlled trial; $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}=$ risk ratio;   
$\mathbf{TNK}=$ tenecteplase.  

Basilar artery occlusion (BAO) is a large vessel occlusion (LVO) ischemic stroke subtype associated with high disability and mortality.1 Despite its proven efficacy, alteplase only achieves rapid reperfusion in ${\sim}4\%$ of patients with BAO. Tenecteplase (TNK) is a genetically modified variant of alteplase with greater fibrin specificity and longer half-life, permitting bolus administration, and is cheaper than alteplase in most countries. The Tenecteplase vs Alteplase before Endovascular Therapy for Ischemic Stroke (EXTEND-IA TNK) $\mathrm{trial}^{3}$ demonstrated that TNK led to higher reperfusion rates prior to endovascular therapy (EVT) and improved clinical outcomes compared with alteplase in LVO ischemic strokes. Reperfusion at the initial angiogram occurred in $22\%$ of TNK-treated vs $10\%$ of alteplase-treated patients. It is unclear whether this finding can be extrapolated to BAO as only 6 patients were included with no differences in primary outcome ( $1/3$ in each treatment arm).  



# Methods  

The primary research question is whether TNK is associated with better reperfusion rates than alteplase prior to EVT in BAO (Class IV evidence). Individual patient-level data of consecutive BAOs were pooled from the EXTEND-IA TNK3 (alteplase $0.9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ vs TNK $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg})$ ) and EXTEND-IA TNK part $2^{4}$ (TNK $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ vs TNK $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg)}$ trials and the Basilar Artery Treatment and Management (BATMAN) registry.1 The EXTEND-IA TNK trial (March 2015–July 2019) was a multicenter, prospective, randomized, open-label, blinded outcome trial in patients with ischemic stroke within 4.5 hours after onset who had internal carotid, middle cerebral, or basilar artery occlusion, eligible for IV thrombolysis (IVT) and EVT. The BATMAN registry (October 2009–July 2019) is a multicenter registry of prospectively collected consecutive patients with BAO. Patients presenting within 4.5 hours after onset and eligible for IVT and EVT were considered for this study.  

A neurointerventionalist (P.J.M.) or neurointerventional fellow (C.W.) assessed reperfusion using the modified Treatment in Cerebral Ischemia (mTICI) classification.5 If no retrievable thrombus was found on initial angiogram, the endovascular procedure was terminated. If intracranial angiography could not be performed, reperfusion $250\%$ of the involved territory was assessed on multimodal imaging (CT perfusion/CT angiography) around the time angiography would otherwise have been performed.3,4  

The primary objective was to evaluate substantial reperfusion defined as mTICI 2b–3 on initial angiogram (before EVT was performed) or reperfusion $250\%$ of the involved territory on multimodal imaging. Parenchymal hematoma (PH) was defined as blood clot with mass effect, deemed symptomatic if it occupied $>30\%$ of the infarcted territory and was associated with ${\ge}4$ -point increase in NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score.6 Functional outcome was assessed at 3 months using modified Rankin Scale score dichotomized as ${\le}2$ and ${\le}1$ .  

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (v.16 SE, StataCorp). Analysis of univariable data was performed using Mann-Whitney $U$ test for continuous data and Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Multivariable analyses were performed using Poisson regression models with robust standard error. All $p$ values were 2-sided; $p<0.05$ was considered significant.

# Discussion  

Unlike anterior circulation LVO stroke, current guidelines state that there is uncertainty about the benefit of thrombectomy in BAO (Class IIb; Level of Evidence C). The recent Basilar Artery International Cooperation Study (BASICS) assessing the efficacy of EVT vs best medical management in $300~\mathrm{pa}-$ tients with BAO demonstrated EVT benefit only in patients with $\mathrm{NIHSS}\geq10$ and suggested thrombolysis might be the optimal treatment in those with milder deficits.7 Given this uncertainty, thrombolytic agents that may obviate the need for EVT or have beneficial effect during transfer to endovascular centers are warranted. The use of TNK in stroke is novel and not yet approved by regulatory agencies in most countries. Its supporting evidence largely comes from randomized controlled trials3,4 (RCTs) designed for anterior circulation LVO stroke. This study represents the largest series of TNK-treated patients with BAO and the first to report TNK vs alteplase reperfusion rates before EVT in BAO. TNK led to higher reperfusion rates than alteplase, similarly to the $22\%$ with TNK and $10\%$ with alteplase reported in the EXTEND-IA TNK trial. Functional outcomes were similar between treatment groups, although our study was underpowered to detect such differences. Larger studies are needed to detect treatment effect differences between thrombolytic agents prior to EVT, given the potentially large EVT effect. However, the strong relationship between earlier reperfusion and functional improvement3,8 suggests that TNK could improve outcomes and be beneficial during transfer to endovascular centers.  

Our study has several limitations. The retrospective design and relatively small number of TNK-treated patients reduces the precision of the results. Higher number of “drip and ship” patients in the alteplase group led to longer needle to arterial puncture times, likely due to transfer times between primary stroke centers administering thrombolysis (located in metropolitan and rural areas) and EVT centers. However, this imbalance should have favored reperfusion after alteplase, as it had additional time to act on the occlusion. More than $80\%$ of TNK-treated patients were treated in an RCT setting, whereas $97\%$ of alteplase data were from observational data with slower workflow (which further explains the needle to arterial puncture time imbalance). Although RCTs are considered more reliable than observational studies, previous studies comparing intervention effect size in both types of studies did not show systematic differences.9 TNK-treated patients were older than alteplase-treated patients, likely due to more recent broader age selection criteria for reperfusion therapies, which would favor improved functional outcomes in the alteplase group. Therefore, our results may represent a conservative estimate of TNK benefits. No worse functional outcome or hemorrhagic complications were found in TNKtreated patients despite being older, in accordance with previous studies.8 Although age and clinical severity may influence clinical outcomes associated with reperfusion, recent evidence suggested time from thrombolysis to reperfusion assessment, thrombus location, and permeability to be independently associated with reperfusion.10 The dose of TNK varied. However, no differences were demonstrated between TNK 0.25 and $0.4\mathrm{mg/kg}$ in the EXTEND-IA TNK study.4  

TNK may be associated with increased reperfusion rates in comparison with alteplase before EVT in BAO. Given the dismal prognosis of BAO, RCTs to compare TNK with alteplase in patients with BAO are warranted.
