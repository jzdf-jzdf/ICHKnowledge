# Neuroendoscopic surgery versus craniotomy for basal ganglia hemorrhage: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials  

Kaike Lobo $^1\oplus$ · Cláudia Santos $2_{\textcircled{10}}$ · Pedro Campos ${\mathfrak{s}}_{\oplus}$ · Larah Oliveira $^4\textcircled{\scriptsize{10}}$ · Vithor Ely Bortolin da Silva $5\textcircled{10}$

$\circledcirc$ The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2025  

# Abstract  

Basal ganglia hemorrhage (BGH) is a prevalent site for intracerebral hemorrhage. Although neuroendoscopy (NE) surgery has emerged as a less invasive alternative to craniotomy (CT), the optimal surgical method remains debatable. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of NE versus CT in the management of BGH. A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases was conducted to identify eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing NE surgery with CT in BGH patients. Outcomes included mortality, hematoma evacuation rate, good functional outcome (GFO), operative time, infection, pulmonary infection, and postoperative complication. Risk of bias was assessed with Cochrane’s ROB-2 tool. Four RCTs were included, comprising 423 patients. NE surgery showed no significant benefit in mortality $(p=0.12)$ and GFO $(p{=}0.18)$ . However, NE was associated with a higher hematoma evacuation rate $(p{=}0.007)$ , shorter operative time $(p<0.00001)$ , and lower rates of infection $(p{<}0.0001)$ , pulmonary infection $(p{<}0.0001)$ , and postoperative complications $(p<0.00001)$ . Future research should be designed to assess whether hematoma evacuation using either technique improves outcomes in comparison to optimal medical management in this population.

# Introduction  

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is one of the most lethal forms of stroke, accounting for a significant proportion of stroke-related morbidity and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Basal ganglia hemorrhage (BGH) is a prevalent site for  

ICH, frequently resulting in serious complications, such as increased intracranial pressure (ICP) due to hematoma expansion, acute hydrocephalus, seizures, and infections [3, 4]. Furthermore, acute onset and rapid progression of this condition pose a high risk of severe disability and death, making effective management strategies crucial for improving patient prognosis [5].  

Treatment options for ICH include both surgical and conservative approaches. Conservative treatment typically involves medical management to control blood pressure, reduce ICP, and address coagulopathy [6, 7]. However, surgical intervention, specifically hematoma removal, has been shown to potentially improve outcomes by reducing mass effect, preventing hematoma expansion, and facilitating faster recovery of neurological function [8, 9]. Even so, the optimal approach to surgical intervention remains controversial and is widely debated in the literature, with various techniques being explored over the years, such as craniopuncture, stereotactic aspiration, neuroendoscopy (NE) surgery, and craniotomy (CT) [10, 11].  

CT for hematoma evacuation is an essential intervention in critical situations, such as when large hematomas lead to mass effect, midline shift, altered consciousness, or threat of neurological deterioration [12]. However, since most common sites of ICH are deep brain structures, such as basal ganglia, a large layer of brain tissue must be crossed during surgery, which may cause iatrogenic damage to healthy cerebral tissue [13]. NE surgery has emerged as a less invasive alternative to CT, demonstrating improved outcomes in hematoma clearance, bleeding volume, complications, length of hospital stay, and intensive care unit stay [14]. The development of advanced NE technology has enhanced the precision and efficacy of this procedure, making it a viable option for hematoma evacuation [15].  

Nevertheless, the effectiveness of NE and CT in managing BGH is still a subject of debate due to the lack of sufficient evidence [16]. Although several meta-analyses have been conducted to compare the outcomes of these two treatment strategies [17–19], they have mainly focused on supratentorial ICH, leaving a scarcity of information regarding BGH specifically. Furthermore, a previous metaanalysis comparing NE versus CT for BGH included both randomized and non-randomized studies in pooled analyses [20], introducing potential confounding. To overcome these methodological limitations and incorporate new evidence from a recently published randomized controlled trial (RCT) [21], this systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of NE versus CT for patients with BGH.  

# Materials and methods  

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [22], and was registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration ID: CRD42024556272).  

# Search strategy  

Two authors (K.L, C.S.) independently searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases from inception to May 2024 with the following search strategy: (neuroendoscopy OR endoscopy OR neuroendoscopic OR endoscopic OR “minimally invasive” OR “minimal invasive”) AND (craniotomy OR “open surgery”) AND (“basal ganglia” OR “intracranial hemorrhage” OR “intracerebral hemorrhage” OR “cerebral hemorrhage”) AND (randomized OR randomised OR randomly OR random).  

# Study selection  

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) RCTs comparing NE and CT; (2) patients diagnosed with BGH requiring surgical treatment; and (3) reporting any of the outcomes of interest.  

Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with secondary BGH caused by intracranial aneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, moyamoya disease, traumatic brain injury, coagulopathy or hemorrhagic tumor; (2) supratentorial hemorrhage with an epicenter beyond the basal ganglia; and (3) nonrandomized studies.  

# Outcome measures  

The primary outcomes studied were: (1) mortality; and (2) hematoma evacuation rate. Secondary outcomes were as follows: (1) good functional outcome (GFO); (2) operative time; (3) infection; (4) pulmonary infection; and (5) postoperative complication. GFO was defined as the patient having sufficient functional recovery to perform activities of daily living independently or with minimal assistance, represented by a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) of 0–3 points, Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) of 4–5 Points, and Activity of Daily Living (ADL) of 1–2 points. Infection encompassed intracranial, pulmonary, or incisional infection. Postoperative complications included infection, rebleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding, digestive tract ulcer, hypoproteinemia, and epilepsy.  

# Data extraction and quality assessment  

Two researchers (K.L., C.S.) independently performed data extraction, with disagreements resolved after discussion with a third author (L.O.). Extracted data were as follows: (1) basic information about the included studies, such as first author, year of publication, scales scores used for GFO definition, and time of follow-up; (2) demographic characteristics, including number, gender, and age of study participants, prevalence of hypertension, preoperative Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), preoperative hematoma volume, and time to surgery; and (3) data regarding primary and secondary outcomes. Methodologic quality and risk of bias were assessed independently by 2 authors (K.L., C.S.) using the Cochrane Collaboration Risk of Bias (ROB)-2 tool for RCTs [23]. In case of discordant results, a third reviewer (P.C.) made a final decision after discussion with the team.  

# Statistical analysis  

Meta-analysis of extracted data was performed utilizing Review Manager 5.4 software. Risk ratio (RR) and weighted mean difference (WMD) were calculated as summary measures for dichotomous and continuous outcome variables, respectively, with a $95\%$ confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity was examined with the Cochran Q test and $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ statistics. A fixed effects model was used for outcomes with low heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{<}25\%)$ . Otherwise, DerSimonian and Laird random effects model was used. Sensitivity analysis was used to investigate the sources of heterogeneity. The possibility of publication bias in meta-analysis was determined through funnel plots.  

# Results  

# Search results and study characteristics  

The search yielded 307 results. After the removal of duplicate records and ineligible studies, 12 articles remained and were fully reviewed based on inclusion criteria. Of these, a total of 4 RCTs were eligible for inclusion (Fig.  1) [21, 24–26]. These studies comprised 423 participants, of which 202 $(47.8\%)$ underwent NE and 221 $(52.2\%)$ underwent CT. Table 1 outlines both the study and participant characteristics of each included RCT. Significant between-study variability existed as to the preoperative hematoma volume and the scales utilized to evaluate GFO.  

# Primary outcomes  

Four studies reported the data about mortality (Fig. 2) [21, 24–26]. The test of heterogeneity showed a low difference between studies $\mathrm{T}^{2}{=}0\%$ ; $p{=}0.45$ ). Therefore, we used the fixed effects model. Mortality was $4.95\%$ in the NE group, compared to $9.04\%$ in the CT group. The pooled RR was 0.57 $95\%$ CI 0.28, 1.16; $p{=}0.12)$ , implying that NE did not show a significant benefit in improving the mortality rate.  

Pooled analysis of hematoma evacuation rate included four studies (Fig.  3) [21, 24–26]. High heterogeneity was found between studies $\scriptstyle\mathrm{I}^{2}=91\%$ ; $p{<}0.00001$ ), so the random effects model was used. The pooled WMD of evacuation rate was 6.54 $95\%$ CI 1.77, 11.31; $p{=}0.007)$ , suggesting that NE was superior in hematoma clearance.  

# Secondary outcomes  

Three studies reported data about GFO [21, 25, 26], including 353 patients (Fig. 4). Due to high heterogeneity between studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}81\%$ ; $p{=}0.006)$ , a random effects model was used. The pooled RR was 1.60 ( $95\%$ CI 0.81, 3.16; $p{=}0.18$ , indicating that NE was not able to significantly improve the functional outcome compared with CT.  

In the operative time analysis, three studies were included [21, 24, 26], comprising 239 patients (Fig.  5). Statistical  

heterogeneity was reported to be substantial $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=77\%$ ;   
$p{=}0.01\$ ). Hence, a random effects model was applied.   
The pooled WMD was $-97.36\$ ( $95\%$ CI -116.26, -78.46;   
$p{<}0.00001)$ , implying that NE could shorten the time of surgery.  

Four studies reported data about infection, pulmonary infection, and postoperative complication (Figs.  6, 7 and 8) [21, 24–26]. Low heterogeneity between studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ; $p{>}0.1)$ was found in all these three endpoints, so the fixed effects model was used. The pooled RR was 0.38 for infection $95\%$ CI 0.24, 0.59; $p{<}0.0001.$ , 0.37 for pulmonary infection $95\%$ CI 0.23, 0.59; $p{<}0.0001$ , and 0.40 for postoperative complication rates $95\%$ CI 0.30, 0.53; $p{<}0.00001)$ , suggesting that NE had a positive effect on preventing these outcomes. Regarding intracranial infections and incisional infections, no pooled outcome analysis was possible, as only two studies [21, 24] reported the former and two studies [21, 26] mentioned the latter.  

# Sensitivity analysis  

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis did not show a significant effect in the meta-analysis results except for pulmonary infection, in which the overall effect became insignificant when Feng et al. was removed (RR 0.40; $95\%$ CI 0.16, 1.01; $p{=}0.05$ ; $\scriptstyle\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ) (Supplementary Fig. S1) [25]. In addition, the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ test for heterogeneity decreased from 91 to $0\%$ in the hematoma evacuation rate endpoint upon omitting the study by Cho et al. from the analysis (WMD 3.36; $95\%$ CI 2.72, 4.00; $p{<}0.00001$ ; $\scriptstyle\mathrm{{I}}^{2}=0\%$ ) (Supplementary Fig. S2) [24]. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted for GFO and operative time endpoints, with no significant reductions in heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. S3 and S4).  

# Quality assessment and publication bias  

The four included studies raised some concerns about risk of bias [21, 24–26], mainly due to issues related to domain 1 (randomization process), domain 2 (deviation from intended intervention), and domain 5 (selection of reported results) (Fig. 9). Assessment of publication bias was conducted on the primary outcomes. The funnel plot for mortality was practically symmetrical by visual inspection, indicating low evidence of publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S5). However, the funnel plot for the hematoma evacuation rate had an asymmetrical distribution, indicating a high risk of publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S6).

# Discussion  

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the outcomes of NE versus CT in the management of BGH. Pooled analysis of the results from four studies, enrolling 423 patients, showed that NE was associated with a higher hematoma evacuation rate, shorter operative time, and reduced rates of infection, pulmonary infection, and postoperative complications. However, there was no significant difference between the two approaches regarding mortality and GFO.  

Surgical decision-making for BGH hinges on a thorough assessment of patient selection criteria, timing of intervention, and hematoma characteristics, as these factors directly influence outcomes. Across the studies analyzed, there is a consensus on the critical importance of early intervention and precise patient stratification [21, 24–26]. Cho et al. enrolled non-comatose patients (GCS 9–13) aged 35–70 years with hematomas $\geq25\mathrm{mL}$ , highlighting that timely surgical intervention (within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ from stroke onset) was pivotal for optimizing outcomes [24]. Zhang et al. mirrored these timing and volume thresholds but added the requirement of significant neurological deficits, underscoring the need for careful functional assessment in surgical candidates [26]. Feng et al. focused on older patients (65–79 years) with hematoma volumes $\leq60~\mathrm{mL}$ , emphasizing the role of brainstem compression with midline shift $>1$ cm and consciousness deterioration as surgical indications [25]. In contrast, Lv et al. prioritized moderate hematoma volumes $(20{-}40\mathrm{mL}$ ) in patients with well-documented hypertensive histories and a clear indication for the operation [21]. These criteria collectively emphasize that surgical indications for BGH are shaped by a nuanced interplay of hematoma volume, neurological status, and timing. Tailored approaches that integrate these factors can maximize functional recovery while minimizing complications [1, 16].

The management of ICH, especially in the basal ganglia, remains a subject of debate due to variable outcomes from clinical trials and challenges in patient selection [16]. The ENRICH trial (Early MiNimally-invasive Removal of ICH) offers valuable insights into this ongoing controversy [27]. This multicenter randomized study enrolled 300 patients to compare minimally invasive parafascicular surgery (MIPS) to medical management. For anterior BGH, while the difference in functional outcomes (utility-weighted mRS) between surgery and medical management was modest (mean score difference of 0.013, $95\%$ CI, -0.147 to 0.116), the trial demonstrated significant safety benefits with MIPS, including reduced 30-day mortality $9.3\%$ versus $18.1\%$ with medical management). These findings underscore the potential of surgical intervention in carefully selected patients.  

It is important to emphasize that the successful implementation of NE requires meticulous preoperative planning, technical expertise, and adherence to the principles of minimally invasive surgery (MIS) [28–30]. Unlike CT, which typically involves a larger exposure of brain tissue, NE relies on smaller access pathways, demanding a precise balance between efficient hematoma evacuation and the preservation of surrounding tissue integrity. Planning the ideal trajectory to avoid damage to eloquent areas, minimizing deep tissue coagulation, and respecting the functional anatomy of the surrounding structures are core tenets of MIS that extend beyond the choice of instrumentation. Thus, while NE offers a less invasive alternative to traditional CT, the advantages of MIS depend not just on the use of an endoscope but also on the surgical philosophy that underpins it [29, 30].  

In this meta-analysis, NE was not able to demonstrate a significant improvement in mortality compared with CT.

This finding is consistent with several studies in the literature. For instance, Jianhua et al. reported no significant difference in mortality rates between NE surgery and CT for patients with BGH [31], suggesting that both approaches are similarly effective in terms of survival outcomes. Similarly, another study by He et al. concluded that the overall survival rates between NE and CT groups were comparable [32], indicating that the choice of surgical method might not significantly impact mortality in patients with BGH. However, some studies have highlighted specific advantages of NE surgery that could indirectly influence mortality. A study by Fu et al. demonstrated that NE was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative complications in comparison with CT, positively affecting patient outcomes and potentially decreasing long-term mortality [16]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. highlighted that NE approaches allow for easier identification of the bleeding point and more effective hemostasis, potentially reducing the risk of rebleeding and subsequent complications [26].  

Hematomas can cause a space-occupying effect, which may increase ICP and reduce cerebral blood flow to surrounding brain tissue, potentially resulting in direct brain damage [33]. Consequently, prompt and effective hematoma removal is a critical principle to enhance patients long-term quality of life [34]. A study by Zhu et al. reported that NE could achieve a high rate of evacuation of $88-100\%$ [35]. In an RCT by Zhang et al. [36], the hematoma clearance rate in the NE group $(n=65)$ was significantly higher than in the CT group $(n=65)$ $83.43\%$ versus $72.78\%$ ; $p{<}0.05$ ). Our metaanalysis also confirmed this result. With the advancement of neuronavigation systems and endoscopic equipment, NE has been continuously optimized [37]. This procedure can provide a clear surgical field, facilitating the thorough removal of deep hematomas and resulting in higher hematoma clearance [21]. However, it is important to acknowledge the high heterogeneity of the analysis, which is largely due to different methods of assessing this outcome between the studies. For example, Cho et al. performed computed tomography scans and used the planimetric method to assess hematoma volume by calculating the area and hematoma depth on each scan [24], while Lv et al. employed a 3-dimensional Slicer software to segment and construct a 3-dimensional model of the hematoma [21].  

A recent RCT by Lv et al. showed no significant difference between NE $(n=54)$ and CT $(n=64)$ groups in the percentage of patients with favorable mRS scores in the postoperative sixth month $(79.62\%$ versus $76.56\%$ ; $p{=}0.69,$ [21]. A previous meta-analysis of RCTs and observational studies by Tan et al. showed that the endoscopic approach group had significantly better outcomes than the CT group in postoperative GCS (WMD 1.14; $95\%$ CI 0.22, 2.06; $p{=}0.01$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=76\%$ ) and GOS (WMD 0.44; $95\%$ CI 0.00, 0.88;  

$p{=}0.05$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=62\%$ ) [20]. In this meta-analysis, however, NE was not able to demonstrate a significant improvement in the GFO rate compared with CT $(47.61\%$ versus $37.83\%$ ; $p{=}0.18)$ , despite showing greater hematoma evacuation and a lower incidence of complications. This finding aligns with the existing literature, which suggests that neither surgical technique demonstrates clear superiority in improving functional outcomes in this population [21, 35, 38, 39].  

From a conceptual standpoint, evacuating a BGH endoscopically aligns well with the principles of MIS, aiming to minimize tissue disruption while maximizing functional recovery [21]. However, clinical evidence supporting the superiority of NE remains inconclusive [21, 35, 38, 39]. A significant challenge lies in the outcome measures utilized, which may be too crude to detect subtle yet meaningful differences in recovery or quality of life. Metrics such as the mRS or GOS, while standard, may lack sensitivity in reflecting nuanced recovery trajectories. Moreover, the included populations are often heterogeneous, encompassing diverse hematoma volumes, neurological status, and patient demographics. Current trials aim to address these gaps by focusing on more homogeneous populations, with similar variability in neurological status and hematoma volume thresholds, and incorporating multidimensional outcome measures. For example, the NESICH trial (Efficacy and Safety of NeuroEndoscopic Surgery for IntraCerebral Hemorrhage, NCT05539859) [40] and the MIND trial (Artemis in the Removal of Intracerebral Hemorrhage, NCT03342664) include additional metrics such as Mini-Mental State Examination, Stroke Impact Scale, and EQ-5D- $.5\mathrm{~L~}$ , which provide a more comprehensive assessment of recovery, also encompassing cognitive, emotional, and quality-of-life dimensions.  

The clinical complexity and the nuanced evaluation of patient outcomes in BGH highlight the critical role of specific anatomical involvements, which extend beyond broad categorizations of supratentorial ICH. For instance, the involvement of the posterior limb of the internal capsule, a key pathway for the pyramidal tracts, is strongly associated with increased risks of major disability, particularly in mobility, self-care, and usual activity [41]. Intraventricular hemorrhage also significantly worsens the prognosis, contributing to elevated ICP, hydrocephalus, and a higher likelihood of poor functional outcomes [7]. However, it is important to note that the trials included in this meta-analysis did not consistently provide detailed data on patient selection or stratified results based on anatomical variations surrounding the basal ganglia, which underscores the need for future studies to address this limitation.  

Our analysis results confirm the favorable effects of NE in achieving shorter operative times. This finding is consistent with the available research evidence [14, 42]. In a study by Sun et al. [43], the keyhole endoscopy group $(n{=}46)$ had a shorter mean operative time compared with the CT group $(n{=}43)$ $113\pm14.3$ versus $231\pm26.1$ minutes; $p{<}00.05$ . Considering that, NE procedure has several advantages. Due to its nature of minimal brain tissue invasion, it causes less tissue damage and shortens time of surgery [14, 42]. Additionally, the “brain-hematoma” pressure gradient formed during this procedure allows the surgeon to quickly remove the hematoma and reduce the mass effect [43]. As a result, the reduction in operative time not only decreases the risk of intraoperative complications but also enhances recovery rates [44]. Despite the consistency of our findings with existing evidence, it is important to emphasize the high heterogeneity of the analysis. This heterogeneity is likely influenced by differences in surgeons’ experience with the endoscopic approach, patient-specific factors such as hematoma size, and the diverse types of NE equipment utilized [21, 24–26].  

Several complications can arise following surgery for ICH, including pulmonary infection, intracranial infection, rebleeding, epilepsy, hypoproteinemia, and multiple organ failure. Among these, pulmonary infection is the most common complication [45]. Our study indicated that NE significantly reduced the incidence of pneumonia compared with CT $9.4\%$ versus $24.88\%$ ; $p{<}0.0001$ ). In addition, NE had a significant advantage in reducing the incidence of infection ( $10.39\%$ versus $27.6\%$ ; $p^{<0.0001}$ ) and postoperative complications $(20.29\%$ versus $48.41\%$ ; $p{<}0.00001$ ). These outcomes are closely associated with the attributes of NE, such as reduced brain trauma, smaller skin incisions, and shorter surgical durations. These features not only minimize the extent of brain injury but also lower the risk of postoperative complications, by decreasing the exposure and manipulation of brain tissue [25, 33].  

This study has some limitations that should be considered: (1) a limited number of RCTs were included, with relatively small sample sizes; (2) different measurements in functional outcome analysis were combined, warranting careful interpretation of the results; (3) heterogeneity between studies was considerable in the hematoma evacuation rate, GFO and operative time endpoints, which may impact the results generalizability; (4) the included studies did not examine the application of NE drainage and CT in subgroups of patients with extensive hematoma volume $\left(>70~\mathrm{ml}\right)$ , rendering the comparative effectiveness of these two strategies still debatable in this scenario; and (5) the trials analyzed did not provide stratified results based on anatomical variations, such as the involvement of the posterior limb of the internal capsule or the presence of intraventricular hemorrhage, both of which are critical prognostic factors.  

# Conclusion  

In this meta-analysis comparing NE and CT for patients with BGH, NE was associated with an increased hematoma evacuation rate, shorter operative time, and reduced rates of infection, pulmonary infection, and postoperative complications in comparison with CT. Nevertheless, mortality and GFO endpoints were not significantly improved by NE surgery. Future research should be designed to assess whether hematoma evacuation using either technique improves outcomes in comparison to optimal medical management in this population.
