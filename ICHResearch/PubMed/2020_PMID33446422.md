Keywords: Atrial fibrillation Anticoagulation Intracranial hemorrhage  

Intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a known risk of oral anticoagulation; delineating ICH attributes may provide nuanced guidance regarding atrial fibrillation management. We evaluated ICH characteristics and outcomes from Effective Anticoagulation with Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial FibrillationThrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 48 (ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48), a randomized trial that compared two edoxaban regimens (higher-dose edoxaban regimen $60/30~\mathrm{mg}$ (HDER), lower-dose edoxaban regimen $30/15~\mathrm{mg}$ (LDER)) with warfarin in patients with atrial fibrillation. Patients who suffered ICH vs those who did not were compared and independent predictors of ICH were calculated. We also assessed ICH subtype and etiology. Of 21,105 randomized patients, 322 $(1.53\%)$ had $\geq1$ ICH for a total of 368 events. Intraparenchymal hemorrhage (HDER: HR 0.52 $\left[95\%\mathrm{CI}0.35–0.77\right]$ , LDER: HR 0.22 [0.13–0.38]) and subdural hematoma (HDER: HR 0.29 [0.15–0.55], LDER: HR 0.26 [0.13–0.50]) were lower with both HDER and LDER vs warfarin. Subarachnoid hemorrhage frequency was similar in the HDER vs warfarin groups but lower in LDER. Compared to warfarin, edoxaban was associated with lower risk of spontaneous ICH (HDER: HR 0.47 [0.31–0.69], LDER: HR 0.34 [0.22–0.53]) and traumatic ICH (HDER: HR 0.32 [0.17–0.61], LDER: HR 0.31 [0.16–0.59]). In multivariable analysis, randomization to warfarin, increased age, and risk of falling remained independent predictors of ICH. In ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48, ICH was decreased in edoxaban-treated patients compared to warfarin-treated patients, including ICH of both spontaneous and traumatic causes. Both edoxaban regimens lowered intraparenchymal and subdural hemorrhages compared to warfarin. Patient characteristics and medical history may help guide anticoagulation management.

# 1. Introduction  

The major risk of oral anticoagulation is intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), for which the annual rate in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) is approximately $0.7\substack{-0.8\%}$ in those taking warfarin [1– 3]. Over the past decade, non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs; dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban) that inhibit thrombin or factor Xa have been developed as alternatives to warfarin and appear to be associated with a marked reduction in ICH $(>50\%)$ [4,5]. Effective Anticoagulation with Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation-Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 48 (ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48) was a randomized, doubleblind trial comparing two regimens (higher-dose (HDER) and lower-dose (LDER)) of edoxaban (a direct factor Xa inhibitor) with warfarin in AF patients and showed that both edoxaban regimens were noninferior to warfarin in preventing ischemic stroke or systemic embolism while significantly reducing major bleeding, particularly ICH, and cardiovascular death [6]. While some data on patients with ICH have been described in previous trials of NOACs vs warfarin [7–9], in-depth analysis of ICH events occurring in the setting of edoxaban vs warfarin plus pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic data in the setting of NOAC- and warfarin-associated ICHs have not been previously described. In addition, treatments of ICH in randomized trials comparing NOACs with warfarin are not well-delineated, and it remains unclear if these interventions differ by anticoagulant type [8,9]. Given the high morbidity and mortality of ICH [10], detailed analyses of ICH with NOACs vs warfarin is paramount, especially when counseling patients with AF regarding safety of anticoagulation. Using data from ENGAGE AFTIMI 48, we investigated ICH patients’ clinical characteristics, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, hemorrhage features, and outcomes in order to elucidate specific factors to guide anticoagulation management in AF patients.  



# 2. Materials and methods  



# 2.1. Study population and protocol  

The design and results of ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 have been published previously [6,11]. It was a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, double-dummy trial that ran from 2008 to 2013 and compared 2 dose regimens of once-daily edoxaban (HDER $60~\mathrm{mg}$ , LDER $30\mathrm{mg}$ ) with warfarin (international normalized ratio (INR), 2.0–3.0; median time-in-therapeutic range, $68.4\%,$ in 21,105 patients with AF over a median of 2.8 years. Major eligibility criteria included: AF within prior 12 months, $\mathrm{CHADS}_{2}$ score $\mathbf{of}\geq2$ , and planned anticoagulation therapy for the trial duration. Patients with any previous ICH, recent stroke $\mathord{\left[<30\right.\kern-\nulldelimiterspace}$ days), creatinine clearance $<30\mathrm{mL/min}$ , increased bleeding risk, alternate indication for anticoagulation, or requirement for dual-antiplatelet therapy were excluded. Edoxaban dose was reduced by $50\%$ in both edoxaban arms (HDER $60\rightarrow30~\mathrm{mg}$ , LDER $30\to15~\mathrm{mg}$ ) for patients with creatinine clearance of $<50\mathrm{mL/min}$ , body weight of $\dot{\mathbf{\zeta}}\le60~\mathrm{kg}$ , or concomitant use of a strong P-gp inhibitor [6]. Although only the HDER regimen is approved for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation, we also present data for the unapproved LDER regimen to permit a broader description of edoxaban dose and ICH risk and to provide randomized data regarding the trade-offs that exist when a lower dose anticoagulant is used ‘‘off-label” in patients with AF. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (or guardians) in the study. The protocol stipulated that the blinded study drug be permanently discontinued if a patient experienced an ICH during the trial, although open-label anticoagulation could be initiated post-ICH at the investigator’s discretion. The Brigham and Women’s Hospital Institutional Review Board approved this study.  



# 2.2. Patient and ICH characteristics  

We compared baseline characteristics, laboratory data, and concomitant medications in patients who did and did not experience ICH during follow-up in the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial. Time-intherapeutic range was calculated as percentage of days with INR 2.0–3.0 in the warfarin arm. Using previously described methods [11], edoxaban trough and peak concentration were obtained before and after the edoxaban dose on day 29, respectively, and levels of endogenous factor Xa (FXa) activity were obtained at randomization and on day 29 (peak and trough levels) [12]. Endogenous FXa activity inhibition, which correlated with clinical outcomes in ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 [12,13], is the most biologically relevant pharmacodynamic marker of edoxaban’s anticoagulant effect.  

Analysis of ICH by treatment groups was prespecified. We collected data regarding etiology (spontaneous, traumatic, unknown); ICH type (e.g., intraparenchymal hemorrhage (IPH), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), subdural hematoma (SDH), ischemic stroke with hemorrhagic conversion (ISHT), other (epidural hematoma, intraventricular hemorrhage, unknown); ICH management (surgery, transfusion, prothrombotic therapy); and whether anticoagulation was restarted after ICH. Patients defined as at risk for falling had any of the following at randomization: prior history of falling, impaired cognition, dizziness, lower extremity weakness, poor balance, orthostatic hypotension, psychotropic drug use, severe arthritis [14]. Recommendations to investigators were provided for ICH management including immediate cessation of blinded study drug and use of therapies to reverse anticoagulation (Supplementary Fig. 1). All ICH events were diagnosed based on clinical and written reports of the imaging data, and were adjudicated by an independent clinical end-point committee (CEC) unaware of treatment assignment [6]. One author (S.E.N.) independently evaluated each event, and another author (R.P.G.) evaluated any in which there was a discrepancy regarding ICH type or etiology among investigators, CEC, and initial author review. Multiple simultaneous events in the same patient were either classified under one etiology or multiple etiologies, as appropriate depending on the clinical circumstances, and as initially adjudicated by the CEC. Since the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial was not powered to assess differences between treatments in the rates of ICH, analyses of ICH subtypes were not prespecified, and no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons, findings should be considered hypothesis generating.  



# 2.3. Statistical analysis  

We present results for both the intention-to-treat (ITT) cohort throughout the trial’s duration (whether on or off blinded study drug) and on-treatment analysis, which counted ICHs that occurred within 3 days of the last dose of the blinded study drug among patients who received at least one dose of the study drug. Off-treatment ICHs were considered ICHs that occurred $\geq4$ days after the last dose of the blinded study drug (Supplementary Fig. 2). For comparisons between randomized treatments, the ontreatment cohort was used since we aimed to assess ICH plausibly related to study anticoagulant. Only the 1st ICH event was considered in patients who had $>1$ event when comparing randomized treatments.  

Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables to compare between ICH and non-ICH patient groups. Comparisons among the 3 treatment arms (warfarin, HDER, and LDER) were performed with Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables in the on-treatment cohort, unless otherwise specified. The log-rank test was used to compare ICH event occurrence in each of the 3 treatment arms. Multivariable Cox regression with stepwise selection using alpha $=0.1$ for entry and alpha $=0.05$ for remaining in the model was used to determine independent predictors of ICH. The underlying assumption of proportional hazards for the Cox model was confirmed by examining Schoenfeld residual plots for the treatment arms and testing proportional hazards using the Supremum test and Schoenfeld residual test. Missing data were not imputed. Two-sided testing was used. Statistical significance was set at $\mathtt{p}<0.05$ without adjustment for multiple comparisons. Analyses were performed independently by the TIMI Study Group using Statistical Analysis System version 9.4 (SAS 9.4).  



# 3. Results  

Of the total 21,105 patients who composed the ITT cohort in the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial, 322 $(1.53\%)$ suffered at least one ICH event during the overall study period. Of these 322 patients, 289 had one ICH event and $33\mathrm{~had}\geq2$ ICH events, totaling 368 events. See Supplementary Fig. 2 for a flow chart describing the ICH events that occurred during the trial.  



# 3.1. Demographics and clinical characteristics of ICHs (ITT cohort, first ICH event)  

Among the 322 patients with ICH in the ITT cohort, the median age in those with vs without ICH was 75 vs 72 years $(\mathbf{p}<0.001)$ (Table 1). Compared to patients without ICH, those who suffered ICH had higher systolic blood pressure $\mathbf{\dot{p}}=0.008)$ , increased risk of falling $\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{p}}<0.001\mathbf{\chi}.$ ), more commonly a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack $\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathrm{p}}<0.001)$ , less commonly a history of hypertension $\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime}=0.004\rangle$ , lower platelet levels $\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathbf{p}}<0.001,$ (including a higher frequency of patients with platelets $<100\mathrm{x}10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ $\left(\mathbf{p}=0.033\right)$ ), and more commonly aspirin use $\mathbf{\tilde{\rho}}=0.005,$ . There were some regional differences: a greater proportion of ICHs occurred in Asia-Pacific/ South Africa and a lower proportion in Eastern Europe.  

Of the 322 patients who suffered ICH events in the ITT cohort, 166/7036 $(2.36\%)$ had been randomized to warfarin, 88/7035 $(1.25\%)$ to HDER, and 68/7034 $(0.97\%)$ to LDER (p-value $<0.001$ for each edoxaban arm vs warfarin) groups (Fig. 1). Demographics, past medical history, and laboratory values at randomization in ICH patients were similar among the 3 treatment groups (Supplementary Table 1).  



# 3.2. Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics  

In the on-treatment cohort, median (IQR) of all INR values after randomization and until ICH was 2.42 (2.19–2.63) and did not differ from median INR during the trial in patients without ICH (2.41 (2.26–2.52), $\mathbf{p}=0.37$ ) (Supplementary Table 2). Patients in the warfarin group who suffered ICH had lower time-in-therapeutic range than non-ICH patients (median $63.0\%$ (51.0–73.3) vs $68.5\%$ (56.7– 77.5), $\mathbf{p}<0.001$ ), though time spent with $\mathrm{INR}>3$ was similar in both groups (median $12.5\%$ [4.7–21.0] vs $10.8\%$ [5.9–16.6], $\mathrm{p}=0.10\AA.$ ). Notably, the last INR in 26 $(19.7\%)$ of ICH patients was $>3$ prior to the first ICH.  

There were no significant differences in day 29 peak or trough edoxaban concentrations between ICH and non-ICH patients in either edoxaban regimen, except for lower peak concentration in ICH patients in the HDER dose-reduced group (Supplementary Table 2). Inhibition of baseline endogenous Factor Xa activity was similar between patients with versus without ICH, except for higher peak inhibition of baseline endogenous FXa in patients who received $60~\mathrm{mg}$ edoxaban and lower peak inhibition in those who received $30~\mathrm{mg}$ edoxaban though notably there were $<10$ ICH patients in these groups.

# 3.3. ICH characteristics in on-treatment population  

Of the 234 patients who suffered from ICH during the ontreatment period, there were 125 IPH $(53.4\%)$ , 65 SDH $(27.8\%)$ , 30 SAH $(12.8\%)$ , 23 ISHT $(9.8\%)$ , and 6 classified as other $(2.6\%)$ (Table 2). Notably 7 patients suffered $>1$ ICH event, and some events $(\mathtt{n}=7)$ were present in multiple locations in the same patient. One hundred thirty-two ICH events occurred in the warfarin group, 61 in HDER, and 41 in LDER out of 7012, 7012, and 7002 patients, respectively. Notably, 88 ICH events occurred during the off-treatment period; characteristics of these were largely similar to on-treatment ICHs except some regional differences and possibly higher aspirin use in the on-treatment population (Supplementary Table 3).  

Multivariable regression in the ITT cohort revealed the following independent predictors of ICH in order of importance: randomization to warfarin, age, history of stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), aspirin use at randomization, decreased body mass index, elevated systolic blood pressure, non-Caucasian race, increased risk of falling, low platelet count at baseline, and no history of dyslipidemia (Table 3). History of stroke or TIA was an independent predictor for ICH in the warfarin arm and in the combined edoxaban arms, while other predictors varied between these 2 medications (though increased risk of falling was a predictor only for warfarin) (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).  

Patients in the HDER arm were less likely to suffer IPH $(\mathbf{p}=0.001^{\cdot}$ ) and SDH $(\mathbf{p}<0.001\$ compared to patients in the warfarin arm, but were just as likely to suffer SAH $\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathrm{p}}=0.25)$ or ISHT $(\mathtt{p}=0.54)$ (Table 2). Patients in the LDER arm were also less likely to suffer most ICH types compared to warfarin. Though not statistically significant, the hazard ratio of LDER vs warfarin for ISHT was 2.13 (0.81–5.62); this is possibly due to a higher rate of ischemic strokes occurring in the LDER group (333/7034 $(4.7\%)$ vs 235/7036 $(3.3\%),$ , resulting in more patients at risk for hemorrhagic transformation.

Of the 234 patients who suffered ICH while on-treatment, 141 $(60.3\%)$ were spontaneous, 62 $(26.5\%)$ were due to trauma, and 34 $(14.5\%)$ were of unknown etiology (i.e., not enough information provided to make an etiologic determination) (Table 2). Compared to the warfarin arm, a smaller proportion of patients in each edoxaban group suffered ICH due to both spontaneous (HDER: $0.23\%$ vs $0.49\%$ subjects/year, $\mathbf{p}<0.001$ ; LDER: $0.17\%$ vs $0.49\%$ subjects/year, $\mathbf{p}<0.001$ ) and traumatic causes (HDER: $0.08\%$ vs $0.24\%$ subjects/ year, $\mathbf{p}<0.001$ ; LDER: $0.07\%$ vs $0.24\%$ subjects/year, $\mathbf{p}<0.001$ ). Randomization to warfarin, age, and decreased body mass index were independent predictors for both spontaneous and for traumatic ICH, while other predictors differed between these etiologies (though only traumatic ICH was associated with increased risk of falling) (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7).  



# 3.4. Treatments of ICH  

In the on-treatment cohort, ICH was managed with surgery in 38 patients $(16.2\%)$ ; red blood cell or whole blood transfusions in 9 $(3.8\%)$ , platelets in 5 $(2.1\%)$ , fresh frozen plasma (FFP) in 33 $(14.1\%)$ ; vitamin K administration in 29 $(12.3\%)$ ; and procoagulant drugs in 16 $(6.8\%)$ patients (Table 4). The numbers of patients undergoing surgery were similar in all 3 treatment groups $(\mathtt{p}>0.05$ for each pairwise comparison). Though only 6 patients were formally unblinded for ICH events in the trial, more patients in the warfarin arm received FFP than in the HDER arm (24 patients $(18.2\%)$ vs 4 $(6.6\%)$ ; $\mathbf{p}=0.03$ ) and vitamin K than in the LDER arm (20 patients $(15.2\%)$ vs 1 $(2.4\%)$ ; $\mathrm{p}=0.03$ ). Otherwise, administration of transfusions and other reversal agents were similar among all 3 treatment groups (Table 4).  



# 3.5. Outcomes of ICH  

Percentage of ICH events resulting in death were similar among all 3 treatment arms in the ITT cohort (HDER: 30/61 $(48.2\%)$ , LDER: 18/41 $(43.9\%)$ , warfarin: 59/132 $(44.7\%)$ ; $\mathbf{p}=0.81$ . Comparing the HDER and warfarin groups, there were no differences in frequency of death or time to death after ICH either overall (median time to death for all ICHs: 5.0 (2.0–27.0) days) or when comparing these arms within the traumatic and spontaneous ICH categories. Similar fractions of patients in the HDER (31 patients; $35\%$ , LDER (23; $34\%$ , and warfarin groups (42; $25\%$ ) were restarted on open-label anticoagulation after ICH $\mathrm{p}=0.19$ for 3-way comparison among all treatment groups; $\mathrm{p}=0.10$ for comparison between warfarin and HDER, $\mathbf{p}=0.19$ for comparison between warfarin and LDER), with mean times of starting 24.0, 11.7, and 51.5 days ${\bf\nabla}_{\bf{p}}=0.21$ for overall comparison among treatment arms) after ICH onset, respectively.

# 4. Discussion  

This analysis is the first to provide a detailed description of ICH in patients randomized to NOAC vs warfarin, with data derived from the largest and longest randomized trial of anticoagulants in patients with AF to date. We present an in-depth analysis of ICH etiology, subtype, predictors and treatments of ICH, and include pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data of the anticoagulants studied. ICH occurred rarely $1.53\%$ of patients suffered at least 1 ICH event during 2.8 years of median follow-up), and edoxaban significantly lowered the incidence of ICH compared to warfarin, including both spontaneous and traumatic ICH. Both edoxaban regimens were associated with lower rates of IPH and SDH, and LDER also lowered rates of SAH; ISHT occurred similarly in both edoxaban groups and in the warfarin group. Randomization to warfarin was the strongest independent predictor of ICH in multivariable analysis.  

Similar to other NOACs, edoxaban lowers ICH by approximately $50\%$ compared to warfarin [1–3,6]. Potential reasons for this include NOACs having a shorter half-life than warfarin [15,16] and NOACs affecting only one downstream factor of the coagulation cascade while warfarin inhibits multiple clotting factors [17]. Furthermore, warfarin is associated with cerebral microbleeds (which are associated with increased risk of ICH), while such a relationship has not been demonstrated in NOACs [18–20]. In the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial, only 5 microbleeds were noted (warfarin: 2, HDER: 3, LDER: 0), but MRIs were not systematically performed [21].  

Our multivariable analysis found that increased risk for falling was a predictor of ICH only among patients treated with warfarin (but not edoxaban). Congruent with this, a smaller proportion of ICHs secondary to traumatic causes occurred with edoxaban vs warfarin. Similarly, a post-hoc analysis of the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial suggested that patients at risk for falling may be less likely to suffer ICH with edoxaban than warfarin [14]. However, risk of oral anticoagulation in AF patients more likely to fall may be overestimated [22–24], and risk of traumatic ICH should be balanced with the potential benefit of preventing ischemic stroke [25]. Together, our data suggest ways in which ICH risk can be modified in the setting of oral anticoagulation: managing high blood pressure [26], avoiding concomitant use of platelet inhibitors whenever possible [27], and treating patients with high-risk features for bleeding (older age, high risk for falls, prior stroke/TIA, nonCaucasian race, low body weight, low platelet count) with a NOAC instead of warfarin.  

There were no differences among the 3 treatment arms regarding use of surgery, transfusions, and reversal agents after ICH except that patients in the warfarin group seemed more likely to receive vitamin K (though vitamin K is only effective for VKAs such as warfarin) [28]. Neither an international pooled analysis [29] nor a Japanese observational study found a difference between use of surgery with NOACs vs VKA [30]. A multicenter cross-sectional study of 161 patients found that while use of surgery and external ventricular drains was not different between NOAC- and VKArelated ICH, administration of prothrombin complex concentrate, FFP, and vitamin K was more frequent in VKA-associated ICH [15]. Thus whether differences exist in interventions for ICH in the setting of NOAC vs VKA use remain unclear; however, reversal agents such as idarucizumab (dabigatran only) and andexanet alpha (factor Xa inhibitors) are now available to treat NOACrelated ICH [31,32].  

This study has limitations. First, the population was drawn from a randomized controlled trial with strict inclusion and exclusion criteria; it did not enroll patients at low risk of stroke $(\mathrm{CHADS}_{2}$ score 0–1) and excluded patients who had had an acute stroke within 30 days or previous ICH. Therefore, one should be cautious about generalizing these data to all AF patients. Complete information was not available for each ICH event (e.g., etiology, ICH volume) given variability in reporting, and no central review of radiological scans was performed. Numbers of patients with ICH subtypes were modest and analyses of outcomes comparing treatments were exploratory with limited power, thus confirmation in larger studies is needed. Also, though overall ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 strove to be a blinded study, a markedly elevated INR would likely have revealed allocation to the warfarin group. In addition, platelet function tests were not performed as part of the trial. Nonetheless, the major strength of this study is the large number of patients and variables available in this multicenter trial with 2.8 years median follow-up.  



# 5. Conclusions  

In this detailed analysis of ICH in the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial, edoxaban lowered ICH by $\sim50\%$ in AF patients compared to warfarin, including both spontaneous and traumatic ICH. Both edoxaban regimens lowered IPH and SDH in particular. Clinical factors – including older age, prior stroke/TIA, concomitant use of aspirin, and increased risk for falling – may identify AF patients who could particularly benefit from edoxaban vs warfarin to reduce ICH risk.
