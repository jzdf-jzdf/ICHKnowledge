# Abstract  

# Background and Objectives  

Acute stent thrombosis (AST) is not uncommon and even catastrophic during intracranial stenting angioplasty in patients with symptomatic high-grade intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis (ICAS). The purpose of this study was to investigate whether adjuvant intravenous tirofiban before stenting could reduce the risk of AST and periprocedural ischemic stroke in patients receiving stent angioplasty for symptomatic ICAS.

# Methods  

A prospective, multicenter, open-label, randomized clinical trial was conducted from September 9, 2020, to February 18, 2022, at 10 medical centers in China. Patients intended to receive stent angioplasty for symptomatic high-grade ICAS were enrolled and randomly assigned to receive intravenous tirofiban or not before stenting in a 1:1 ratio. The primary outcomes included the incidence of AST within 30 minutes after stenting, periprocedural newonset ischemic stroke, and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. The outcomes were analyzed using logistic regression analysis to obtain an odds ratio and $95\%$ confidence interval.  

# Results  

A total of 200 participants $(122\mathrm{men}\left[61.0\%\right];$ ; median [interquartile ranges] age, 57 [52–66] years) were included in the analysis, with 100 participants randomly assigned to the tirofiban group and 100 participants to the control (no tirofiban) group. The AST incidence was lower in the tirofiban group than that in the control group $4.0\%$ vs $14.0\%;$ ; adjusted odds ratio, 0.25; $95\%\operatorname{CI}0.08–0.82;p$ $=0.02\$ ). No significant difference was observed in the incidence of periprocedural ischemic stroke ( $7.0\%$ vs $8.0\%$ ; $p=0.98$ ) or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage between the 2 groups.  

# Discussion  

This study suggests that adjuvant intravenous tirofiban before stenting could lower the risk of AST during stent angioplasty in patients with symptomatic high-grade ICAS.

# Introduction  

High-grade intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis (ICAS) is one of the most common causes of ischemic stroke and is associated with a high risk of recurrent stroke.1,2 The current first-line recommended strategy for stroke prevention in patients with ICAS is standard medical therapy.3,4 However, a subset of patients are still at a high risk of stroke recurrence despite aggressive medical treatment. In the Stenting and Aggressive Medical Management for Preventing Recurrent Stroke in Intracranial Stenosis (SAMMPRIS) trial, patients with border zone infarcts and impaired collaterals had a $37\%$ 1-year stroke recurrence rate in the medical management group. As a result, many experienced neurointerventional centers continue to provide percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and stenting (PTAS) for patients with symptomatic severe $\ge70\%$ stenosis) ICAS who have failed medical therapy. But the safety of stent angioplasty has been challenged by the high incidence of periprocedural complications, especially ischemic events.6,7 One critical factor for the periprocedural ischemic events is acute stent thrombosis (AST), which is not uncommon, and may even be devastating if the imaging sign is not timely recognized and disposed. The previously reported incidence of AST was as high as $14.6\%$ during the Wingspan intracranial stent placement despite premedication with aggressive antiplatelet agents, and much higher during stent placement in the middle cerebral artery. The median occurrence time was 20 minutes after stenting.8 However, the true incidence of stent thrombosis in “real world” may be even higher, for some subacute or late stent thrombosis could not be observed during the procedure, but may account for the periprocedural ischemic events. Therefore, the adequate prevention of AST has become a topic of paramount importance.  

Tirofiban, one of the mostly used GP Ⅱb/Ⅲa receptor inhibitors, has been increasingly advocated as a putative agent to counteract different stages of thrombosis mediated by activated platelets. The adjunctive use of tirofiban has been extensively evaluated in percutaneous coronary intervention9 and stent-assisted coiling of intracranial aneurysm 10,11 to prevent periprocedural ischemic complications. Meanwhile, some exploratory studies have tested tirofiban alone12 or in combination with intravenous thrombolysis13,14 or endovascular therapy (EVT)15,16 as an alternative agent in the management of acute ischemic stroke. Our previous case series initially demonstrated the safety and efficacy profile of tirofiban in the management of acute intraprocedural stent thrombosis in patients receiving nonacute intracranial stent angioplasty for ICAS.17,18 To date, no randomized clinical trial has assessed the role of prior use of tirofiban in stent angioplasty for nonacute ICAS.  

Therefore, the Safety and Efficacy of TIrofiban Prior to Stenting for Intracranial Atherosclerotic Stenosis (TIPS) was conducted to investigate whether adjuvant intravenous tirofiban before stenting can reduce the risk of AST and periprocedural ischemic stroke in patients with symptomatic high-grade ICAS. Our primary research hypothesis was whether adding tirofiban before stenting could be effective and safe for patients with symptomatic high-grade ICAS.  

# Methods  

# Study Design  

TIPS was an investigator-initiated, multicenter, prospective, open-label, randomized trial assessing the efficacy and safety of intravenous tirofiban before stenting in patients with symptomatic high-grade ICAS. The trial was conducted from September 9, 2020, to February 18, 2022, at 10 medical centers in China with a high volume of intracranial stenting procedures. We followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) for reporting this study.  

# Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents  

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (Approval No.: YXLL-KY-2019-037) and all the participating centers. All participants or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent. The trial was registered on Clinicaltrials.gov (URL: chictr.org.cn; unique identifier: ChiCTR2000031935). The study protocol is available in the supplemental material (eSAP 1, links.lww.com/WNL/D482).  

# Participant Selection  

We enrolled participants who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) Age 18–80 years old, male or female; (2) Recurrent transient ischemic attack or ischemic stroke ( $\scriptstyle\geq2$ times) after intensive medical therapy, usually dual antiplatelet treatment (aspirin $100~\mathrm{mg}$ and clopidogrel 75 $\mathrm{mg/d)}$ , statin (atorvastatin $20~\mathrm{mg}$ or rosuvastatin $10\mathrm{mg/d,}$ , and the medicines for risk factors (such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus); (3) The last qualifying event occurred between 7 and 90 days before randomization; (4) $70\%-99\%$ stenosis confirmed by digital subtraction angiography according to the warfarin-aspirin symptomatic intracranial disease (WASID) method19 and hypoperfusion determined by Tmax (time to maximum of the residue function) $>6$ seconds on computer tomography perfusion (CTP) or MRIarterial spin labeling studies; (5) The subjects or their legal representatives signed the informed consent form.  

We excluded patients for the following reasons: (1) nonatherosclerotic intracranial stenosis, (2) tandem extracranial or intracranial stenosis $(>50\%)$ or occlusion, (3) concomitant intracranial tumor or aneurysm, or (4) previous angioplasty or stenting in the target lesion. Additional details are listed in eAppendix 1 (links.lww.com/WNL/D481).  

# Randomization and Blinding  

An automated randomization system was used to randomly assign eligible patients to treatment with tirofiban or not in a 1:1 ratio (Figure 1). This protocol was designed as an openlabel treatment assignment. Therefore, we adopted a partial blinding strategy where the imaging and clinical evaluators were blinded but the operators and patients were not. For example, the imaging outcome AST was arbitrated by designated 3 interventionists who were blinded to the randomization. The clinical outcomes were assessed by appointed local neurologists who were also blinded to the randomization and procedure information. But the operators who performed the stenting procedure were unblinded.  

# Premedication  

Dual antiplatelet treatment (DAPT) with aspirin $100~\mathrm{mg}$ and clopidogrel $75\mathrm{mg/d}$ was given at least for 5 days before the procedure. A thromboelastogram (TEG) was recommended to analyze the antiplatelet resistance after strict 5-day DAPT treatment after admission. Antiplatelet resistance was defined either as $<50\%$ inhibition of arachidonic acid-induced platelet aggregation, or as $<30\%$ inhibition of adenosine diphosphateinduced platelet aggregation, or both.20,21 The adjustment of antiplatelets was performed by the appointed local investigators of each center. All the investigators were strictly trained, and followed the results of CHANCE- $\cdot2^{22}$ and 2022 China expert consensus on EVT in patients with symptomatic ICAS. Generally, if clopidogrel was not sensitive enough, we would switch it to ticagrelor ( $180~\mathrm{mg}$ loading dose on day 1 followed by $90~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily).  

# Procedure  

Endovascular procedures were performed under general anesthesia. The patient assigned to the tirofiban group was administered intravenous tirofiban (Hengkang, Lunan Better Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) immediately after femoral artery puncture, with a maintenance dose of $0.1~{\upmu\mathrm{g/kg/min}}$ to the end of the procedure. Systemic heparinization was routinely initiated after placement of the femoral sheath via a bolus of $75\mathrm{U}/\mathrm{kg}$ followed by a half dose 1 hour later to maintain an activated clotting time of 250–300 seconds during the procedure. The degree of stenosis was determined according to the WASID study.19 Endovascular procedures were performed using an 8F guiding catheter or 6F shuttle catheter. The vessel pathway was comprehensively assessed before the intervention, and a 5F intermediate catheter was usually used for support. The lesion was traversed with a coaxial assembly of a microcatheter and microguidewire through the intermediate catheter. Generally, the balloon was not greater than $70\%$ of the size of the normal vessel diameter, inflated slowly to reach the nominal pressure, and kept inflated for at least 60 seconds. The Wingspan or Neuroform EZ self-expanding stent diameter was $0.5{-}1.0\mathrm{mm}$ greater than the target artery and was deployed to an extent of at least $3\mathrm{mm}$ on either side of the lesion.

# AST Assessment  

Serial angiography was performed every 7.5 minutes for a total of 30 minutes after stent placement to assess AST, which was defined as the presence of a thrombus within or immediately adjacent to (within $5\mathrm{mm}$ ) the implanted stent. AST was graded according to the following scale : (1) grade 1: visible thrombus but not flow limiting, (2) grade 2: visible and flowlimiting thrombosis, (3) grade 3: complete stent occlusion. The time of AST occurrence was also noted. Once AST occurred, intra-arterial and intravenous tirofiban was administered. Generally, intra-arterial tirofiban was administered via a slow bolus of $0.1\upmu\mathrm{g/kg},$ accompanied by an intravenous maintenance dose of $0.2\upmu\mathrm{g/kg/min},$ , and was further adjusted according to the thrombus burden.  

# Outcomes  

# Efficacy Outcomes  

The primary efficacy outcome was AST incidence within 30 minutes after stent placement. Periprocedural ischemic stroke was an additional registered primary efficacy outcome. Secondary efficacy outcomes included residual stenosis, new DWI foci (newly added DWI hyperintensity), NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score before discharge, 30-day ischemic stroke, and in-stent restenosis (ISR) at the 6-month follow-up. Ischemic stroke was defined as a new focal neurologic deficit of sudden onset without hemorrhage on CT or MRI.23 ISR was defined as ${>}50\%$ stenosis within or immediately adjacent (within $5\mathrm{mm}$ ) of the treated segment and >20% absolute luminal loss.24  

# Safety Outcomes  

The primary safety outcome was symptomatic intracranial hemorrhagic (ICH) before discharge. Secondary safety outcomes included asymptomatic ICH, peripheral bleeding (including gastrointestinal bleeding and urethremorrhage), thrombocytopenia, and puncture hematoma. ICH was defined as any parenchymal, subarachnoid, or intraventricular hemorrhage detected by CT or MRI with or without a neurologic deficit.25  

# Statistical Analysis  

We conducted a statistical analysis on the intention-to-treat (ITT) population. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson $\chi^{2}$ test or Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were presented as means (SD) or medians (interquartile ranges [IQR]) and were compared using an unpaired 2-tailed $t$ test or Mann-Whitney $U$ test. For comparing the endpoints between the 2 groups, the odds ratios (OR) with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using a binary logistic regression. We adjusted for age, gender, qualifying artery, and time from the qualifying event to randomization. All end points were compared in the ITT population, including all patients who were randomized, and with the per-protocol and as-treated populations as sensitivity analyses. Patients who received the allocated treatment and did not have major protocol violations were included in the per-protocol analysis. Patients in the as-treated population were analyzed according to the actual received treatment, irrespective of the randomization allocation. A 2-sided $p<0.05$ was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted with SPSS, version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY).  

# Sample Size Calculation  

According to a literature review and our previous singlecenter observational studies,8,17 we assumed an AST proportion of $12.3\%$ in the control group, and $2.2\%$ in the tirofiban group, with a target difference of $10\%$ . The minimal clinically important difference was set to $10\%$ . Considering the convenience of accessing patients’ information before discharge, we assumed a dropout rate of $5\%$ . Accordingly, a total sample size of 196 patients (98 patients in each group) would ensure the detection of a significant deviation from the null hypothesis, with a power of $80\%$ and 2-sided $\alpha=0.05$ .

# Acute In-Stent Thrombosis  

A total of 18 $\left(9.0\%\right)$ patients presented with AST within 30   
minutes after the stent, with a median occurrence time of 15   
(13–23) minutes. Of the 18 patients, 4 had AST at the 0–7.5  

minutes interval, 9 at the 7.5–15 minutes interval, 3 at the 15–22.5 minutes interval, and 2 at the 22.5–30 minutes interval. Thirteen $\left(12.4\%\right)$ events occurred with middle cerebral artery stent placement, 3 $(7.0\%)$ with basilar artery, 1 $\left(4.2\%\right)$ with internal carotid artery, and 1 $\left(3.6\%\right)$ with vertebral artery. Seven $(7.4\%)$ events occurred in Wingspan stent-treated patients, and 10 $\left(13.3\%\right)$ events occurred in Neuroform EZ stent-treated patients (Figure 2). Seventeen patients had non–flow-limiting thrombosis (grade 1), 1 had flow-limiting thrombus (grade 2), and 0 had complete stent occlusions (grade 3). All ASTs were successfully recanalized through immediate intravenous and intra-arterial remedial tirofiban. The detailed information on AST characteristics and remedial treatments in the control group is presented in eAppendix 3 (links.lww.com/WNL/D481).  

# Discussion  

This study shows that the adjuvant use of intravenous tirofiban before stenting for patients with high-grade ICAD lowers the incidence of AST without increasing the rate of intracerebral hemorrhage but that there was no difference in the rates of periprocedural ischemic stroke or recurrent stroke at 30 days.

AST, as an imaging indication during stent angioplasty, has a great influence on clinical prognosis. Although the exact pathophysiology of stent thrombosis is not clear, it is primarily considered to be related to platelet activation and endothelial damage caused by stent implantation. In our study, the incidence of AST was $14\%$ in the control group, similar to that of a previous report $\left(14.6\%\right)$ .8 Of importance, the incidence of AST was significantly reduced by tirofiban pretreatment. However, the periprocedural ischemic stroke or recurrent stroke at 30 days was not different between the 2 groups. We speculate that the negative findings were due to the aggressive rescue therapy with tirofiban after AST occurrence between the 2 groups. In other words, the actual incidence of postprocedural ischemic events in the control group would be higher if the aggressive rescue therapy was not used.  

Compared with previous randomized trials,6,7 the incidence of periprocedural ischemic stroke in our study was much lower. The main reasons for the difference may include the following aspects. First, the difference in ethnicity and patient selection. All patients in this study were manifested with hypoperfusion by MRI or CTP, and those with perforator stroke were excluded. A post hoc analysis of SAMMPRIS showed that most periprocedural strokes in the stenting group were perforator strokes.27 Second, the advancement of material and experience of the operator. All included centers in our trial were highvolume centers. This association was also shown in the recent CASSISS trial $(5.1\%),$ ,28 and Weave $\left(2.6\%\right)$ registry,29 suggesting the importance of experience in performing intracranial stenting procedures. Third, the aggressive application of antiplatelet medications, such as tirofiban, might play a significant role in decreasing periprocedural ischemic events.  

The role of an antiplatelet treatment regimen to prevent ICAS-related stroke has improved over the past few years, but questions remain regarding dosing, duration, and a combination of antiplatelet drugs. To reduce thromboembolic complications associated with the placement of neurovascular stents, the use of DAPT (aspirin plus clopidogrel) has become a routine practice in the last decade. However, the antiplatelet resistance was not uncommon.30 In our study, more than $25\%$ of patients presented with antiplatelet resistance monitored by TEG, which was similar to a previous study.31 The antiplatelet regime was changed to aspirin plus ticagrelor when antiplatelet drug resistance occurred in patients (eAppendix 4, links.lww.com/WNL/D481) according to the CHANCE-2 study.22 Despite this, the current intensity of oral antiplatelet strategies is still far from sufficient for patients undergoing stent implantation. Therefore, pretreatment with tirofiban before stenting may be an effective therapeutic strategy.  

Tirofiban inhibits platelet aggregation in a dose-dependent and concentration-dependent manner, but the best administration regimen in the neuroendovascular field remains unclear. In our study, intravenous tirofiban was given $0.1\mathrm{ug/kg/}$ min and was terminated after the procedure, which seems feasible to reduce AST incidence and does not increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage or peripheral hemorrhage. Unlike other GP Ⅱb/Ⅲa receptor inhibitors, tirofiban binds more specifically and reversibly to platelet GP Ⅱb/Ⅲa receptors, with shorter plasma and biologic half-life, which may contribute to its lower rate of hemorrhagic complications.  

This trial has several limitations. First, treatment randomization was open-label and not double-blind, which may lead to bias. Second, the antiplatelet resistance was assessed by local investigators, which could lead to discrepancies in the assessments. Third, we enrolled only Chinese patients, and thus, this result may not be fully generalizable to other populations or settings. In the future, an international multicenter, largesample, double-blind, randomized controlled trial is needed to confirm our findings.  

The findings of this trial indicated that prophylactic intravenous tirofiban before stenting could reduce the risk of AST in patients with symptomatic high-grade ICAS, although there was no clinical benefit in the rate of periprocedural ischemic stroke. The results were still encouraging and hinted at the great potential of tirofiban as an adjuvant agent for stenting in patients with ICAS.
