# Middle meningeal artery embolization following surgical evacuation of symptomatic chronic subdural hematoma improves outcomes, interim results of a prospective randomized trial  

Luca H. Debs \*,1, Fernando L. Vale , Samantha Walker , Diana Toro , Seena Mansouri , Samuel D. Macomson , Scott Y. Rahimi  

Neurosurgery Department, Medical College of Georgia, Augusta, GA, USA

# ABSTRACT

Background: Outcomes after surgical treatment of chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) remain undesirable in a significant proportion of patients. We aimed to show the role of middle meningeal artery (MMA) embolization and to demonstrate its benefits.  

Methods: Thirty-five patients with symptomatic cSDH were enrolled in a prospective randomized trial following evacuation surgery. Participants were randomized to embolization or control group (expectant management following surgical evacuation without embolization). Patients were followed throughout their hospitalization and outpatient follow-ups. The main goals of this planned interim analysis were to assess neurological outcome and resource utilization.  

Results: The groups were comparable in terms of sex, age and follow-up retention rates. Side(s) of intervention(s) and hematoma size were similar. There was no statistical difference in neurological examination improvement at discharge, but at follow-up, we observed a decline in neurologic exam in the control group $(\mathbf{p}=0.03)$ . Control group participants required more re-interventions $(\mathbf{p}=0.02)$ and were followed in clinic and during related readmissions for longer $(\mathbf{p}=0.02)$ . The number of imaging studies obtained in relation to the disease management was higher in the control group $(\mathbf{p}=0.01\$ ).  

Conclusions: Our results suggest a beneficial role for the addition of MMA embolization to surgical intervention in the treatment of symptomatic chronic subdural hematoma. Neurological outcomes were significantly better in the embolization group. This contributed to less need for follow-up, re-interventions, and imaging studies.  

# 1. Introduction  

Chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) is a prevalent neurosurgical condition, but the optimal treatment strategy for surgical candidates remains an area of controversy [3,6,7,12,41,49]. In the United States, cSDH has a projected incidence of 60,000 cases by 2030, becoming the most common cranial surgical condition [1,3,6,7].  

The primary intervention for most cSDHs has traditionally been surgical evacuation, especially those patients which meet generally accepted imaging criteria $\left({>}10~\mathrm{mm}$ maximal thickness, $>5\mathrm{mm}$ of midline shift), or have neurologic symptoms [1,7,13,16–18,25,33,41]. Surgical evacuation is essential and effective for immediate decompression of symptomatic cSDHs. Despite conventional standards in surgical and medical management, at least $20~\%$ of surgically treated patients have poor neurological outcomes requiring long-term healthcare assistance, a recurrence rate of approximately $30{-}39\%$ , and oneyear mortality rates that may reach 32 $\%$ [1,3,4,11,13,15,29,32,33,36,43,49].  

The pathophysiology underlying the formation and maintenance of cSDHs is complex and may necessitate a more targeted approach. Middle meningeal artery (MMA) embolization and its role in the overall treatment strategy for cSDHs remains an active area of clinical investigation. Current review of the literature suggests that a safe and efficacious role exists for MMA embolization as a minimally invasive adjuvant or alternative procedure for treating cSDHs. Numerous clinical studies since 2000 consistently demonstrate the ability of MMA embolization to significantly reduce recurrence rates, prevent hematoma enlargement, reduce cSDH volumes, and promote early brain re-expansion with favorable neurological outcomes at follow-up durations of up to 18 months [4,8,9,16,17,19,21–24,26–28,31,34,35,37,38,40–42,44–48]. Currently, MMA embolization is estimated to reduce recurrence rates to $1.4-3.6~\%$ in comparison to a rate of up to $30{-}39\%$ with conventional surgical management alone [4,19,33,39,41,44,48].  

While MMA embolization appears safe and effective, questions remain over the best timing of MMA embolization as an endovascular adjuvant procedure to optimize reductions in recurrence rates, as well as the impact of adjuvant MMA embolization on neurological outcomes during the initial treatment of symptomatic cSDHs. Recent case series exist which utilize prophylactic postoperative MMA embolization following surgical evacuation to investigate effects on recurrence rates, but none emphasize impact on neurological outcomes [26,41]. Our study represents one of the first prospective, randomized control trial to investigate the impact of postoperative, prophylactic MMA embolization in comparison to surgery alone on neurological outcomes, recurrence, and associated healthcare resource utilization for patients with symptomatic cSDHs presenting for initial treatment.  

# 2. Methods  

# 2.1. Study design, data collection, and enrollment  

The authors followed the CONSORT guidelines. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB# 1496700) and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04272996). Between March 2019 and November 2022, eighty-two cSDH were identified, however only thirty-five patients met the inclusion criteria and consented for the study (Fig. 1). Those patients were enrolled and randomized in a consecutive fashion (1:1) between groups. Eighteen patients were randomized to the control group (observation) and seventeen patients were randomized to the study group (MMA embolization). All participants provided informed consent after initial evacuation surgery. Following consent, patients were randomized 1:1 in a consistent, alternative fashion between control group (observation) and study group (MMA embolization). Every other enrolled patients were alternatively assigned to the control group and embolization group regardless of side of hematoma or number of sides affected, size of hematoma and amount of midline shift, anticoagulation status, sex, baseline mRS, and neurologic examination at admission. Standard demographics were obtained (see Table 1). Minimum requirements of the study included: a post-op CT scan and a full neurologic evaluation at 4–6 weeks after discharge or sooner (if persistent or new symptoms developed). Thereafter, patients were followed as clinically necessary or until radiographic resolution was documented. This manuscript represents an interim analysis to justify further enrollment into the study. In the case of bilateral cSDH, both sides received the same treatment. All metrics were accounted per patient, and not per cSDH.  

# 2.2. Inclusion and exclusion  

The inclusion criteria included ages 18–90, symptomatic cSDH (neurologic deficit, headaches and/or mass effect and midline shift) status post-surgical evacuation within $72\mathrm{h}$ in the form of burr hole(s) or small (trephine) craniotomy $\mathrm{<}6\mathrm{cm}$ in greatest diameter), and Modified Rankin Scale $(\mathrm{mRS})<5$ at presentation. Patients with chronic renal insufficiency (creatinine $>1.8)$ , genetic bleeding disorder, nonmedically induced coagulopathy, and pregnancy were excluded from the study.  

# 2.3. Neurological status  

Full neurologic examination was performed by neurosurgeons certified by the American Board of Neurological Surgery at each time point included in the study (admission (pre-surgery), post-operative (after surgery and/or embolization), discharge, and follow-up). Neurological improvement was defined as an improvement from the neurologic deficit at presentation. Neurologic deficits included impaired orientation, strength, sensation, balance, and/or coordination, $\mathtt{GCS}{<}15$ , and presence of HA.

# 2.4. Computed tomography  

All patients were evaluated at time of presentation with CT head. We only included patients with chronic and/or subacute subdural hematoma collections. These were represented on CT as hypodense to isodense. Size of hematoma was defined as the largest hematoma thickness on coronal sections. Midline shift was measured on axial sections at the level of the foramen of Monroe and represented the shift in midline structures from the center.  

# 2.5. Surgery  

Unilateral or bilateral surgical evacuation in the form of burr hole(s) or small craniotomy was performed by admitting neurosurgeon in standard fashion. Subdural drains were used in most cases at the attending surgeon’s discretion. Coagulation parameters (prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time and international normalized ratio) and cell count were normal(ized) prior to all operations.  

# 2.6. MMA embolization  

Patients randomized to the study group were taken to the endovascular suite for embolization following surgery. All embolization procedures were performed during the same hospitalization, and within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ of surgery. Embolization side always corresponded to side of surgery; in case of bilateral surgical evacuation, patients had bilateral MMA embolization. Following general anesthesia induction and diagnostic angiogram, a working projection of the external carotid artery was obtained and superselective catheterization of the MMA was performed with a microcatheter. The liquid embolic agent Ethylene Vinyl Alcohol Copolymer (OnyxTM) was infused using both blank roadmap and live fluoroscopy technique. In all cases branches of the MMA, including anterior and posterior divisions, were embolized. This was followed by proximal MMA stem embolization. A final control angiogram was performed following embolization to assess for residual vessel filling. All endovascular treatments were performed by senior author.  

# 2.7. Statistical methods  

De-identified data was entered in secured Microsoft Box file. All statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel and associated add-on plugins. Student’s t-test was used to analyze continuous variables. Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze categorical variables. A p value $\leq~0.05$ was used for significance level. Interim analyzes were performed when enrollment reached 35 patients, as dictated by protocol.  

# 3. Results  

The control group and the embolization group were similar in terms of age (mean 70.8 vs 66.1 years, $\mathbf{p}=0.13$ ) and gender distribution (11 males in control group, 12 males in study group, $\mathbf{p}=0.41\$ ). No significant differences of hematoma characteristics were noted between the groups, including affected side, amount of midline shift and clot thickness (Table 1). The use of anticoagulation prior to admission was equivalent between groups (39 vs $35\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.55)$ . Subdural drains were used in most instances and at a similar rate between control and study group patients (78 vs $82\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.53.$ ). Length of stay for initial intervention(s) did not differ amongst groups (mean 11.2 days vs 9.3 days, p $=0.3)$ and subsequent follow up rates were comparable (short-term 100 vs $100\%$ and long-term 78 vs $82\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.53,$ . No complications directly related to the embolization procedure were observed (Table 1).  

Neurological examination showed stable improvement in both groups by time of discharge (56 vs $71\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.29.$ ). However, this was not the case at time of follow up. Indeed, several patients from the control group who had initially improved were found to have new/ recurrent deficits, while the same patients who had showed improvement at discharge in the study group showed continued improvement at follow up (33 vs $71\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.03$ , NNT 2.6, RR 0.44). Further, seven patients from the control group eventually required readmission for repeat evacuation, whereas one patient from the study group required repeat surgery (39 vs $6~\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.02$ , NNT 3.0, RR 0.15). All seven patients from the control group requiring repeat surgery due to poor examination and recurrence of SDH received MMA embolization per request of the primary surgeon. Following repeat surgery and addition of MMA embolization, no differences were noted between the groups in terms of neurological improvement at last follow up (56 vs $65~\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=$ 0.42). Further, when evaluating the mRS at baseline and at time of last follow up, no significant differences were noted amongst the groups (Table 1).  

The time between original intervention and last neurosurgical follow up indicated that patients in the observation group (surgery only) were followed on average for 205 days while patients in the study group (surgery $^+$ embolization) were followed on average for 39 days $\left({\ttp}=\tt\right)$ 0.02). In addition, from the time of admission for cSDH, more cranial imaging studies (CT and MRI) were obtained in the treatment of the control group than that of the study group (mean 6.7 vs 4.6 respectively, $\mathrm{~\bf~p~}=0.0097.$ . Interestingly, the average time between original intervention and last cranial imaging obtained (not necessarily by neurosurgery) revealed no significant difference between groups (370 vs 192 days, $\mathbf{p}=0.1$ ; Table 1).  

# 4. Discussion  

The underlying pathophysiology of cSDH development is an area of continued research interest and debate. A previously accepted theory implicated rupture of cortical bridging veins and slow, venous bleeding [11,30,49]. However, complete support of a venous source theory is limited given the 4–7 week time-frame of cSDH formation, the extension of hematomas away from the superior and inferior sagittal sinuses, and the small proportion $(9\%)$ of patients with enlarging cSDHs who also have an acute haemorrhage [7,12,13,30].  

In light of these limitations and additional studies, the current leading theory describes a continuous, sustained cycle of chronic inflammation and neovascularization following an inciting injury to dural border cells [7,12,14,25]. This activates a cascade of cell proliferation, macrophage infiltration, an inflammatory response, and granulation tissue formation in the subdural space leading to membrane formation and angiogenesis forming anastomotic channels with perforators from the middle meningeal artery [6,7,12,20,40]. The structure of these neocapillaries is fragile and immature with fenestrated basement membranes, lack of smooth muscle, and numerous gap junctions [7,12,40]. As a result, these friable vessels supplied by the MMA leak into the subdural space and produce chronic microhemorrhages [4,12,13,40]. A reactive increase in fibrinolytic activity produces proinflammatory signals (i.e. cytokines) and angiogenic factors (i.e. VEGF) which drive further cycles of inflammation, angiogenesis, membrane formation, and vessel proliferation [4,12,14,20]. This process – driven by formation of leaky vascular membranes supplied by the MMA – continues in a self-sustaining cycle with accumulation of blood products outpacing physiologic reabsorption, resulting in progressive evolution and expansion of a cSDH [7,12,14,20].  

Based on the suggested role of the middle meningeal artery in the updated pathophysiologic mechanism underlying cSDH formation and maintenance, MMA embolization is a potential primary or adjuvant intervention growing in popularity [7,8,40]. By targeting the specific perforator branches supplying the cSDH, MMA embolization prevents the repetitive microhemorrhages causing continued cSDH expansion [4,7,12,40]. Published angiographic findings after MMA embolization support this mechanism with disappearance of characteristic patterns for cSDHs, specifically the “irregular cotton wool staining” of distal MMA vessels and contrast filling in the hematoma cavity [4]. Involvement of the MMA is also supported by the presence of engorged MMAs ipsilateral to cSDHs on imaging studies [6,30].  

Surgical management remains essential and effective for immediate brain decompression and clinical improvement in symptomatic patients. Due to the severity of disease in our patient population, we elected to first treat the mass effect by surgical evacuation. The decision to operate was made by the admitting neurosurgeon prior to study enrollment and randomization to limit selection bias. The use of burr holes or small craniotomy for evacuation was based on surgeons’ preference as both methods are accepted standard of care in these cases [10]. In the study group, MMA embolization was performed as an adjunct to address the issues of recurrence, progression, and neurologic outcomes after surgical evacuation of cSDH.  

A unique emphasis of our study is on assessment in neurologic outcome through serial examination and mRS score. In this study, patients in both groups represented similar baseline functional status prior to their current symptomatology as no significant difference in baseline mRS was identified. At discharge, patients in both groups demonstrated similar rates of clinical improvement on examination, supporting the early benefits of surgical decompression in symptomatic patients $756\%$ in control group, $71\%$ in study group, ${\bfp}=0.29$ ; Table 1). However, significantly fewer patients after surgery alone maintained this improvement on intermediate follow-up examination (33 vs. $71\%$ ), $\mathbf{p}=$ 0.03; Table 1). Further, all seven patients requiring redo surgery from the control group (failure of therapy) crossed over to receive the addition of MMA embolization as an effort to minimize likelihood of further complication. This rational was later supported by the observation of overall neurological improvement in this group at similar rates as the study group at final follow up (56 vs. $65\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.42\$ . The mRS at final known follow-up provided added support $(\mathrm{mRS}{\leq}2$ in 67 vs $71\%$ , $\mathbf{p}=$ 0.45) to this approach. In fact, patients in the control group required a prolonged period of clinical monitoring to achieve this equivalent outcome $(205\pm300$ days vs $39\pm29$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.02$ ; Table 1) and more important, seven patients from the control group required redo intervention. Assessment of neurologic outcome with mRS has been reported in few studies with a trend of early significant improvement after embolization vs. conventional surgical management groups. Understandably, surgical management alone demonstrated a similar clinical outcome but at later follow-up timepoints [34,44].  

Thus, it appears that the addition of MMA embolization promotes earlier sustained improvement in neurologic outcome over surgical evacuation alone by expediting the definitive resolution of the underlying pathophysiologic cycle of cSDHs and preventing recurrence.  

The impact of the addition of MMA embolization on reducing recurrence rates, hematoma progression, and hematoma reaccumulation is well-established and further supported by the results of our prospective, randomized control study. As previously cited, recurrence rates after surgical evacuation alone are reported at rates up to $30{-}39~\%$ [1,3,4,11,13,15,29,33,43,49]. Previous reports demonstrate reduction in this rate to $3.6~\%$ and as low as $1.4~\%$ with MMA embolization [4,19,33,41,44,48]. In this study, recurrence of cSDHs producing symptoms and necessitating repeat neurosurgical intervention occurred at a rate of $39\%$ , which was significantly reduced to $6\%$ (1 patient) with the addition of MMA embolization $(\mathtt{p}=0.02$ , Table 1). While the proportion of patients achieving complete radiographic improvement or $>$ $50\%$ volume reduction by final follow-up appears the same between groups, sustained radiographic resolution or improvement of $>50~\%$ appears to occur more rapidly after embolization, which is consistent with the previously described trend of clinical improvement rates in the embolization group.  

The scarcity of healthcare resources observed in recent years highlights the importance of its conscious and appropriate use. To this end, we evaluated the difference in resource utilization between our control and study groups. We showed that patients treated with surgery only required more repeat interventions, were followed for longer periods of time to achieve equivalent outcome $205\pm300$ days vs $39\pm29$ , $\mathbf{p}=$ 0.02; Table 1) and necessitated more imaging studies (6.7 vs. 4.6 studies, $\mathbf{p}=0.0097.$ ). Cost analysis and quality-of-life evaluation were not the aims of this study, however, our investigation seemed to favor the addition of MMA embolization to reduce overall healthcare burden.  

# 4.1. Limitations  

This study represents a planned interim analysis of the current data. The single-center design is a limitation of our study. In addition, patients were not blinded to the attending neurosurgeon. However, all patients were consented for the study and randomized after surgical intervention according to protocol to reduce selection bias. All neurosurgical encounters pre- and post-discharge involved a complete neurologic examination. The documentation was reviewed by three investigators independently to ensure consistency prior to matching findings. Further, no patient was released from the study prior to evaluation and clearance of cranial imaging by a board-certified neuroradiologist to minimize bias in the clinical assessment.  

MRS and other functional assessments carry an intrinsic bias by emphasizing certain functional components (such as motor function) over other functional domains and are known to introduce a level of subjectivity due to interobserver variability that cannot be disregarded.  

However, mRS is being used more frequently in assessing functional outcomes in the clinical setting and given the substantial impact of motor dysfunction in these patients, it was considered as another tool for assessing neurologic outcome. Measures taken to attempt to limit interobserver variability included all scoring performed by a single assessor and the utilization of a published questionnaire scheme designed to improve reproducibility [2,5].  

# 5. Conclusions  

MMA embolization has increased in acceptance and practice as a safe, valuable surgical adjunct in the management of cSDHs. This study represents one of the first prospective, randomized control trial to investigate the impact of the addition of postoperative prophylactic MMA embolization in the initial treatment of symptomatic cSDH on neurological outcomes, recurrence rates, and associated healthcare resource utilization. Our results suggest that postoperative prophylactic MMA embolization produces an earlier sustained improvement in neurologic outcome by expediting the definitive resolution of the underlying pathophysiologic cycle of chronic inflammation, neovascularization, and microhemorrhage seen in cSDHs. Additionally, our findings provide further support to improvements in cSDH recurrence rates and repeat surgical intervention rates with subsequent decreased need for extended follow up and imaging studies with the addition of MMA embolization. Endovascular applications continue to expand rapidly for neurosurgical patients, and further well-designed larger studies are needed to support the promising benefit and clinical improvements hereby suggested.
