# ABSTRACT  

Introduction: Recent years have witnessed unprecedented progress in stroke care, but unmet needs persist regarding the efficacy of acute treatment and secondary prevention. Novel approaches are being tested to enhance the efficacy of thrombolysis or provide neuroprotection in non-thrombolized patients.  

Areas covered: The current review highlights pharmaceutical agents under evaluation in clinical trials concerning the acute, subacute, and chronic phase post-stroke. We examine the evidence in favor of tenecteplase as an alternative thrombolytic drug to alteplase, nerinetide as a promising neuroprotective agent, and glibenclamide for reducing edema in malignant hemispheric infarction. We discuss the use of ticagrelor and the promising novel category of factor XI inhibitors in the subacute phase after stroke. We offer our insights on combined rivaroxaban and antiplatelet therapy, PCSK-9 inhibitors, and other non-statin hypolipidemic agents, as well as novel antidiabetic agents that have been shown to reduce cardiovascular events in the long-term.  

Expert opinion: Current approaches in stroke treatment and stroke prevention have already trans­ formed stroke care from a linear one-for-all treatment paradigm to a more individualized approach that targets specific patient subgroups with novel pharmaceutical agents. This tendency enriches the therapeutic armamentarium with novel agents developed for specific stroke subgroups.

● Factor XI inhibitors are novel antithrombotic agents currently tested in the subacute phase after minor stroke.

● Novel antidiabetic agents have shown improved cardiovascular out­ comes in clinical trials irrespective of glycemic control.

# 1. Introduction  

Recent developments in acute stroke treatment and second­ ary stroke prevention have revolutionized stroke care. However, acute reperfusion therapies concern only a min­ ority of stroke patients even in developed countries [1]. In the minority of patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis (IVT), a substantial proportion of treated patients do not appear to gain any benefit from IVT and a small minority will develop symptomatic hemorrhage and will deteriorate [2]. Many stroke patients are either elderly, have concomi­ tant diseases or carry high risk of cardiovascular disease, and are at high long-term risk for stroke recurrence or other cardiovascular complication that may be fatal [3]. The current narrative review will present novel pharmaceu­ tical agents that have been tested in randomized-controlled clinical trials (RCTs) and may soon improve stroke prognosis in three distinct time periods: acute, subacute, and chronic phase of stroke (Table 1).

# 2. Acute stroke treatment  

During the last 5 years, acute stroke treatment has developed with unprecedented speed. We have witnessed the substitu­ tion of a decades-old linear treatment algorithm (one reperfu­ sion treatment, one imaging modality, one thrombolytic agent, and one time window) to the proof of safety and efficacy of a second recanalization treatment (mechanical thrombectomy), the use of advanced neuroimaging modalities to extend therapeutic windows and introduction into clinical practice of thrombolytic agents already in clinical use in car­ diology. The modern treatment paradigm is a tree of different diagnostic procedures and reperfusion options that is con­ stantly updated [4] and gives hope for altering the natural course of the disease for exceedingly greater number of stroke patients (Figure 1).  



# 2.1. Tenecteplase for intravenous thrombolysis  

Tenecteplase (TNK) appears advantageous compared to alte­ plase in several pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic prop­ erties: higher fibrin specificity, increased resistance to plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 activity, and longer halflife, allowing for single IV bolus administration [5,6]. TNK work­ flow administration is simpler in many ways compared to alteplase: shorter time to prepare, single bolus administration, no need for an infusion pump, no need for second IV catheter [7,8]. These advantages have recently supported the us,e of tenecteplase to reduce the emergency department spread of Coronavirus 2019 Disease (COVID-19) during the COVID-19 pandemic [9]. Furthermore, orolingual edema that represents a potentially life-threatening complication occurring in approximately $1-2\%$ of patients who receive rt-PA (with a huge range of severity) appears to be exceptional with TNK. This is an essential advantage of TNK compared to rt-PA especially in ‘drip and ship’ patients in an ambulance follow­ ing referral from a primary to a comprehensive stroke cen­ ter [10].  

TNK has replaced alteplase in cardiology without proving clinical superiority. In the ASSENT trial, tenecteplase and alte­ plase showed identical 30-day mortality rates and secondary endpoints were similar. Nevertheless, ease of administration of TNK in combination with non-inferiority were considered enough to justify the transition in thrombolytic regimen in cardiology [11]. Despite its advantages, many clinical trials have failed to prove clinical superiority of TNK to alteplase for stroke patients. The Norwegian Tenecteplase Stroke Trial showed similar efficacy and safety between TNK $(0.4\mathrm{mg/kg})$ and the standard dose of alteplase; however, the trial included mostly mild stroke patients [8,12]. A meta-analysis of five randomized-controlled clinical trials showed non-inferiority of TNK compared with alteplase in terms of functional outcomes and safety [13].  

The Tenecteplase versus Alteplase Before Endovascular Therapy for Ischemic Stroke trial (EXTEND-IA TNK) randomized large-vessel occlusion (LVO)-related acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients, presenting within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ from symptom onset, to alteplase or TNK [14]. TNK doubled successful reperfusion rates at initial angiogram ( $22\%$ versus $10\%,$ $\textsf{p}=0.03)$

compared to alteplase; this translated to a number needed to treat (NNT) of 5 to avert one mechanical thrombectomy (MT). This is an important advantage of bridging therapy as it may provide faster recanalization when MT is not immediately offered [15]. It was also associated with better 3-month func­ tional outcomes without increasing symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) risk. It should be noted that both treatment arms showed very low rates of sICH $(1\%)$ , probably due to perfusion imaging inclusion criteria, as shown by the low baseline ischemic core of randomized patients (median ischemic volume $12.5~\mathrm{~m~}$ and exclusion of patients with ischemic core over $70~\mathsf{mL}$ ). A post hoc within-trial economic analysis analyzed costs of index emergency department and inpatient stroke hospitalization, rehabilitation/subacute care, and rehospitalization due to stroke within 90 days; TNK appeared highly likely to be cost-effective for LVO patients before MT [16]. Most included patients were directly admitted to MT-capable centers (‘mothership’ paradigm), implying short IVT-to-MT delays. Consequently, a retrospective analysis from French centers in patients transferred for MT from a non-MTcapable center (‘drip-and-ship’ paradigm) after propensity score matching for confounders, failed to detect any differ­ ence between TNK and alteplase (recanalization rates $21\%$ vs $18\%$ respectively) [17]. In this ‘drip-and-ship’ study, IVT to angiography took longer than in the EXTEND-IA TNK trial: median 93 min vs. 55 min, respectively. These data suggest that although recanalization rates before MT may be similar with both thrombolytics, recanalization may occur earlier with TNK, a finding that is highly clinically relevant.  

We have performed a systematic review and meta-analy­ sis and included four randomized controlled clinical trials (RCTs) of TNK in 433 patients with LVO; no difference in safety endpoints (mortality, intracerebral hemorrhage) has been observed [18]. There seemed to exist an efficacy superiority of TNK, since functional improvement, recanali­ zation rates, and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores 0–2 were more frequent in the TNK group. We have also pro­ spectively evaluated patients with AIS due to LVO, IVT with or without MT in two tertiary stroke centers in a real-world evidence study. Patients were treated with standard-dose alteplase $(0.9\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{kg})$ or $0.25\mathrm{~mg/kg}$ TNK. The rate of $24~\mathsf{h}$ major neurological improvement was higher in the TNK group $64\%$ versus $33\%$ $\textsf{p}=0.046)$ but the effect ceased to be statistically significant in multivariable analyses [19]. Other study groups also presented real-world data on TNK for AIS; TNK routine use proved feasible and had compar­ able safety and efficacy outcomes to alteplase [20]. Based on these reassuring results, the latest 2019 update on American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) guidelines suggest it may be reasonable to pre­ fer TNK (single IV bolus of 0.25-mg/kg, maximum $25\mathrm{mg})$ over alteplase for MT-eligible AIS [21]. The same position is adopted by the most recent European Stroke Organization (ESO) guidelines for this subgroup (LVO) of AIS patients (quality of evidence: Low; strength or recommendation: weak) [22].  

The non-inferiority of TNK compared to alteplase for the treatment of all AIS patients is currently being evaluated by ongoing RCTs. The ATTEST-2 (NCT02814409) and TWIST (NCT03181360) trials do not require vessel occlusion, but both are collecting data on vessel occlusion status. TASTE (ACTRN12613000243718) is a non-inferiority trial compar­ ing tenecteplase $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ vs standard alteplase among patients eligible for intravenous thrombolysis with radiologically confirmed perfusion mismatch. TEMPO-2 (NCT02398656), TWIST (NCT03181360), TIMELESS (NCT03785678) are also testing the use of TNK in the extended time-window for patients with wake-up symp­ toms, or patients presenting with intracranial vessel occlu­ sions more than $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ from symptom onset, have minor stroke syndromes and/or salvageable penumbra in perfu­ sion imaging.  



# 2.2. NA1 neuro protection  

Nerinetide (NA-1) is a molecule that acts on NMDA brain receptors that has shown substantial neuroprotective effects in vitro [23]. A study in macaques that had occlusion and then reperfusion of the middle cerebral artery, simulating mechanical thrombectomy in humans, has shown reduction of $^{24\mathrm{~h~}}$ infarct volume as measured with MRI and improved neurological function compared to placebo, suggesting that it may enhance the reperfusion beneficial effects [24]. The first clinical trial in humans was the Evaluating Neuroprotection in Aneurysm Coiling Therapy (ENACT) trial, that randomized patients developing iatrogenic stroke during endovascular aneurysm repair to either NA-1 or pla­ cebo [25]. This double-blind study showed that patients in the NA-1 group sustained fewer ischemic infarcts documen­ ted by MRI. However, there was insignificant difference in total infarct volume and no difference in clinical outcome between groups (identical mRS score 0–2 in 3 months, $\mathsf{p}>0.999)$ . The efficacy and safety of nerinetide for the treatment of acute ischemic stroke (ESCAPE-NA1) was eval­ uated in a multicentre, double-blind, RCT [26]. A total of 1105 patients undergoing MT within $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ from symptom onset, presenting moderate to good collaterals in multi­ phase CT angiography (CTA) adopting the neuroimaging protocol used in the ESCAPE trial of MT, were randomized to a single dose of NA-1 or placebo. The drug was adminis­ tered through an IV line for 10 minutes during the proce­ dure and median recanalization time was 20 minutes after start of infusion; the two study arms did not differ in the percentage pre-treated with IVT (bridging therapy) nor in recanalization rates. The study showed neutral results; how­ ever, in the subgroup not pre-treated with IVT $40\%$ of total) a significant mRS shift for improved functional outcomes was documented in the NA-1 group. The authors assumed an interaction between alteplase and NA-1 that leads to subtherapeutic levels to explain this effect modification [27]. Further research will reveal whether NA1 will enhance our therapeutic arms in IVT-ineligible LVO stroke patients treated directly with MT or it will become the latest addition in the ever-expanding cemetery of failed neuroprotective agents [28]. This translation failure of neuroprotective agents that work in preclinical studies but fail in RCTs, questions the validity of existing animal models; one possi­ ble explanation may be that animal models for stroke are devoid of risk factors including aging and comorbidities that are common in stroke patients [29,30]. The former unmodi­ fiable risk factors should be taken into account as potential confounders that may modulate the treatment effect of novel therapeutic agents [29,30].  



# 2.3. Glibenclamide for cerebral edema  

Sulfonylureas have been shown to improve outcome in patients with type 2 diabetes and ischemic stroke [31]. Glibenclamide is a sulfonylurea drug reformulated for intra­ venous injection (BIIB093) that reduced cerebral edema and mass effect after large hemispheric infarction in Phase II trials [32]. CHARM is an ongoing RCT that will evaluate the $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ continuous infusion of IV glibenclamide or placebo in patients suffering from acute ischemic stroke of the middle cerebral artery with 80–300 mL baseline infarct volume (NCT02864953).  



# 3. Short-term secondary stroke prevention  

Early meta-analyses have estimated that the risk of stroke post transient ischemic attack (TIA) was as high as $3.5\%$ and $8.2\%$ in the first 2- and 90-days post stroke, respectively [33]. However, even in these studies, it became clear that expert stroke care greatly reduced risk for stroke early after TIA from $11\%$ to $0.9\%$ [34]. In the meantime, the progress achieved in acute stroke medicine has led to a significant decrease in stroke recurrences in the subacute phase., The development of organized stroke units guarantees that more stroke patients now benefit from evidence-based medical and nursing care to prevent complications and stroke recurrences. with, Optimizing secondary stroke pre­ vention tailored to the specific cause of the index event has led to a significant reduction of recurrent strokes; excep­ tions include non-compliant patients and patients suffering from rare and hard to diagnose (vasculitis) or treat (advanced cancer) medical conditions causing recurrent cer­ ebrovascular events. This is an important point that explains why novel treatments that are directed to enhance protec­ tion from stroke recurrence in the first weeks or months post stroke should prove an excellent efficacy/safety profile to result in a clinical meaningful net benefit for stroke patients. Proving superiority to plain aspirin has proven a challenging task for alternative antiaggregation agents [35] such as cilostazol [36], triflusal [37], and the latest addition, ticagrelor.  



# 3.1. Tigacrelor  

Two RCTs have established a benefit for short-term dual antiplatelet treatment with aspirin and clopidogrel after a minor acute ischemic stroke or TIA [38,39]. One of the two, the Clopidogrel in High-risk patients with Acute Non-dis­ abling Cerebrovascular Events (CHANCE) trial incorporated a genetic substudy that showed that patients who were carriers of the cytochrome P450 (CYP) $2\mathsf{C}19^{*}2$ and $\ast_{3}$ lossof-function (LOF) alleles benefitted more from using aspirin alone than from using dual antiplatelet therapy [40]. Ticagrelor is a direct-acting antiplatelet agent that is not dependent on metabolic activation by CYP2C19, unlike clo­ pidogrel [41]. The Platelet Reactivity in Acute Nondisabling Cerebrovascular Events (PRINCE) suggested improvement of both platelet reactivity and reduction in stroke recurrence risk after a minor acute ischemic stroke or TIA, with the combination of ticagrelor in addition to aspirin compared with the combination of clopidogrel with aspirin [42]. The Acute Stroke or Transient Ischemic Attack Treated with Ticagrelor and ASA [acetylsalicylic acid] for Prevention of Stroke and Death (THALES) was a phase III trial testing the hypothesis that 30-day double anti-aggregation therapy with ticagrelor and aspirin reduces the risk of subsequent stroke or death among patients with acute non-cardioem­ bolic cerebral ischemia compared to aspirin alone [43]. The study had mixed results: 11,000 patients with a mild-tomoderate acute non-cardioembolic ischemic or TIA were included: the risk of the composite of stroke or death within 30 days was lower with ticagrelor plus aspirin than with aspirin alone, but there was no significant difference in the incidence of disability. However, ticagrelor was associated with higher risk of severe hemorrhage and cerebral hemor­ rhage than aspirin alone. The benefit from treatment with ticagrelor plus aspirin as compared with aspirin alone would be expected to result in a number needed to treat of 92 to prevent one primary-outcome event (composite of stroke or death within 30 days) and a number needed to harm of 263 for severe bleeding. Clopidogrel With Aspirin in High-risk Patients With Acute Non-disabling Cerebrovascular Events II (CHANCE-2) is an ongoing RCT (NCT04078737) that will assess the effects of ticagrelor plus aspirin versus clopido­ grel plus aspirin on reducing the 3-month risk of both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke in CYP2Y19 LOF alleles carriers with TIA or minor stroke.  



# 3.2. FXI inhibitors  

Factor XI (FXI) is part of the intrinsic coagulation pathway and takes part in the propagation of the coagulation cascade and thrombin generation, as well as in the downregulation of fibrinolysis [44,45]. The observation that patients with hemo­ philia C, a rare disorder resulting from severe FXI deficiency found with high incidence in Ashkenazi Jewish families, carries a decreased risk for cardiovascular events and venous throm­ boembolism [46], as well as stroke [47], has incited interest in developing FXI inhibitors. Mendelian randomization analysis supported a causal effect of higher, genetically determined FXI levels on risk of any ischemic stroke and, among ischemic stroke subtypes, cardioembolic stroke [48]. Another recent Mendelian randomization analysis showed evidence of reduced risk for both ischemic stroke and venous thromboem­ bolism [49]. Human genetic data suggest that pharmacologi­ cal inhibition of FXI may reduce ischemic stroke risk without evidence for an associated risk of major bleeding [50]. In a phase 2, randomized trial, lowering factor XI levels with an antisense oligonucleotide that specifically reduces human FXI messenger RNA expression in the liver, prevented venous thromboembolism without increasing the risk of bleeding in patients undergoing elective unilateral total knee arthroplasty [51]. A study on BMS-986177 for the Prevention of a Stroke in Patients Receiving Aspirin and Clopidogrel (AXIOMATIC-SSP, NCT03766581) plans to randomize 2,350 participants with acute ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack with motor symptoms who are older than 40 years and have evi­ dence of extracranial or intracranial atherosclerotic plaques relevant to the brain infarct. Participants will be randomized to different doses of study drug or placebo in addition to dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel). The primary efficacy outcome is a composite of new ischemic stroke or new covert brain infarction detected by MRI at 90 days. Another oral FXIa inhibitor (BAY 2433334) is evaluated in the Program of Anticoagulation via Inhibition of FXIa by the Oral  

Compound BAY 2433334 – NonCardioembolic Stroke study (PACIFIC-STROKE) in patients following an acute, noncar­ dioembolic ischemic stroke (NCT04304508).  



# 4. Long-term secondary stroke prevention  

Soon after an ischemic stroke, the primary causes of mortality are stroke related: either complications of the index event or stroke recurrence. However, in the long-term, cardiovascular disease prevails as the major cause of mortality [52]. It is therefore of outmost importance to assess and treat aggres­ sively cardiovascular risk factors and to develop new drugs and strategies for optimizing long-term stroke and cardiovas­ cular events prevention.  



# 4.1. Rivaroxaban for stable atherosclerotic disease  

The risk of myocardial infarction or vascular death after first ischemic stroke was shown to increase with age and in stroke patients with concomitant coronary artery disease in the Northern Manhattan Study [53]. Single antiplatelet therapy was the mainstay of antithrombotic regimen to reduce stroke recurrence of non-cardioembolic stroke for decades. The Cardiovascular Outcomes for People Using Anticoagulation Strategies (COMPASS) trial has demonstrated that the combi­ nation of low-dose novel anticoagulant ( $2.5\mathrm{mg}$ of rivaroxaban twice a day) and aspirin was superior both to aspirin alone and to 5 mg of rivaroxaban twice a day in stable atherosclerotic disease [54]. This study included patients with coronary, per­ ipheral, or carotid artery disease that was either symptomatic at least six months after carotid intervention or asymptomatic with ${>}50\%$ stenosis. A composite outcome of ischemic and hemorrhagic events was significantly in favor of patients in the combined treatment group. Efficacy outcomes were mainly driven by a $50\%$ relative risk reduction in ischemic stroke risk. It is of note that only $7\%$ of included patients had a stroke and that there was an increase in major hemorrhage, mainly gastrointestinal. Net clinical benefit remained significant despite the increase in hemorrhagic risk, and benefit is accen­ tuated in high-risk subgroups: patients with $^{\ge2}$ vascular beds affected with atherosclerosis, impaired renal function, heart failure, and/or diabetes mellitus [55]. Patients with severe heart failure were excluded from the study, but those with mild or moderate heart failure seemed also to gain extra benefit from the combined treatment [56]. In the COMPASS MRI substudy, no differences were noted between the two treatment arms in the number of covert infarcts or the result of cognitive tests [57]. A cost-effectiveness analysis for coron­ ary and peripheral but not carotid artery disease showed that rivaroxaban plus aspirin may be cost-effective compared to aspirin [58]. All the aforementioned promising results are based on a single clinical trial and await confirmation in different cohorts. The combination of $2.5\mathrm{~mg}$ of rivaroxaban twice a day plus aspirin is currently being evaluated in the Combination Antithrombotic Treatment for Prevention of Recurrent Ischemic Stroke in Intracranial Atherosclerotic Disease (CANTOS-ICAD) trial. Patients with recent brain ische­ mia or high-risk TIA attributed to intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis of $30\%{-99\%}$ will be randomized to rivaroxaban plus aspirin or aspirin [59], even though the choice of single anti­ platelet therapy in the control arm would be considered sub­ optimal by many stroke physicians [60] following the promising results of the Stenting and Aggressive Medical Therapy for Preventing Recurrent Stroke in Intracranial Stenosis (SAMMPRIS) trial [61]. Despite failing to prove safety and efficacy for intracranial stenting, the SAMMPRIS trial has shown the importance of dual antiplatelet therapy for treating symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic disease; recurrent ischemic stroke rates were half of those previously reported in the Warfarin–Aspirin Symptomatic Intracranial Disease (WASID) trial that used single antiplatelet therapy (high-dose aspirin) [62].  



# 4.2. Proprotein convertase subtilisin–kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors  

Apart from antithrombotic regimen, dyslipidemia treatment is important in reducing cardiovascular risk in stroke survivors. High-dose atorvastatin therapy post TIA or non-cardioembolic stroke has proven to reduce stroke recurrence and cardiovas­ cular events in the Stroke Prevention by Aggressive Reduction in Cholesterol Levels (SPARCL) trial 15 years ago [63]. This beneficial effect seemed to be preserved in all non-cardioem­ bolic ischemic stroke subtypes [64] and a post-hoc analysis showed that lowering low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) by $\ge50\%$ was associated with greater reduction in the risk of stroke and major coronary events [65]. The Treat Stroke to Target (TST) trial also evaluated lipid-lowering ther­ apy in patients after ischemic stroke and found significant benefit from cholesterol-lowering therapy in preventing vas­ cular events, including stroke [66]. TST confirmed that low­ ering LDL $-C<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ was superior to higher targets for preventing major cardiovascular events. Interestingly, in patients after an acute coronary syndrome, potentiating the hypolipidemic regimen by adding ezetimibe to simvastatin in the Improved Reduction of Outcomes: Vytorin Efficacy International Trial (IMPROVE-IT) further lowered LDL-C levels and reduced the risk of ischemic stroke especially in patients that have already suffered a stroke [67]. In the recent years, a novel class of hypolipidemic drugs, human monoclonal anti­ bodies to proprotein convertase subtilisin–kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, were developed for patients not reaching optimal LDL-C goals despite treatment. PCSK9 inhibitors have proven highly effective in reducing LDL-C levels by more than $50\%$ in two clinical trials: the Further Cardiovascular Outcomes Research with PCSK9 Inhibition in Subjects with Elevated Risk (FOURIER) and the ODYSSEY Outcomes trial [68,69]. In the FOURIER study, the primary endpoint (a composite of cardio­ vascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization for unstable angina, or coronary revascularization) was signifi­ cantly reduced in the treatment arm (absolute risk reduction $1.5\%,$ , median follow-up 2.2 years). The benefit appeared more pronounced in those patients included in the trial post stroke (absolute risk reduction $2.5\%$ from $8.5\%$ to $6\%$ ). Risk of stroke was similarly significantly reduced (absolute risk reduction $0.4\%,$ , from $1.6\%$ to $1.2\%$ ). These results further consolidate the role of LDL-C reduction in improving cardiovascular out­ comes as hazard ratios per 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C were almost identical to those described by the Cholesterol Treatment Trialists Collaboration [70]. The ODYSSEY outcomes trial reported similar results, but only $2.9\%$ of included patients had a history of stroke, since the trial was designed for postacute coronary syndrome patients. Both PCSK9 inhibitors had excellent safety profile with some patients presenting injec­ tion-site reactions. Recent guidelines suggest that it is reason­ able to treat with PCSK9 patients with ischemic stroke who are at very high risk (either concomitant major atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or multiple high-risk conditions) are on maximally tolerated statin and ezetimibe therapy and still present with LDL- $\mathsf{C}>70\mathsf{mg/dL}$ [71].  



# 4.3. Other lipid-lowering agents  

Bempedoic acid is an FDA approved non-statin drug as an adjunct to diet and maximally tolerated statin therapy for the treatment of dyslipidemia [72]. A series of small and shortterm Cholesterol Lowering via Bempedoic acid, an ACL-inhibit­ ing Regimen (CLEAR) RCTs have shown promising results in reducing LDL-C in diverse patient groups. In the CLEAR Tranquility trial, bempedoic acid reduced LDL-C when it was added to a lipid-lowering therapy that included ezetimibe in statin-intolerant patients [73]. The CLEAR Serenity trial rando­ mized statin-intolerant patients with dyslipidemia to bempe­ doic acid or placebo; bempedoic acid was well-tolerated and led to significant reduction in LDL-C [74]. The CLEAR Wisdom and CLEAR Harmony trials tested the addition of bempedoic acid on top of maximally tolerated statin therapy in high-risk individuals and found significant LDL-C reduction [75,76]. Finally, bempedoic acid resulted in significant LDL-C reduction in high-risk individuals who were both on maximally tolerated statins and ezetimibe [77]. All these RCTs recruited a limited number of individuals and had a short follow-up and were therefore unable to evaluate cardiovascular events. This ques­ tion will hopefully be clarified by the ongoing CLEAR Outcomes trial (NCT02993406.). Despite using the same bio­ chemical pathway with statins to inhibit cholesterol biosynth­ esis, conversion to the active form takes place only in the liver and not in the muscles, which renders it a promising alter­ native to statins in patients who present with cramps and other muscle symptoms reportedly associated with statin use. However, it should be noted that data from multiple high-quality RCTs have demonstrated that most if not all of statin-related muscle symptoms are caused by patients’ expec­ tations rather than by statins per se [78,79]. This ‘nocebo’ effect adversely affects many patients’ adherence to statin therapy, and it remains unclear how it should be addressed by the clinicians [80]. Increased awareness of this effect of both clinicians and the public may promote dialogue between physicians and patients and eventually address the cause of this mistaken beliefs that is misinformation. Strategies to reas­ sure patients through a non-paternalistic caring approach may be more effective and are certainly more ethical than devel­ oping a novel class of lipid-lowering drugs to address this common problem. For those patients already on statins and ezetimibe, primary results of enhanced LDL-C reduction are promising and need further evaluation.  

The potential use of triglyceride-lowering medications, and particularly omega-3 fatty acids, including icosapentaenoic acid, to reduce cardiovascular risk remains controversial. After mixed initial results showing some effect only in second­ ary prevention [81], a series of negative RCTs have mitigated hopes [82–84] until the publication of the Reduction of Cardiovascular Events With EPA Intervention Trial (REDUCEIT), which demonstrated that the addition of n-3 fatty acids $(4\mathtt{g}/{\mathsf{d}})$ on top of statins in high-risk patients led to a signifi­ cant reduction in major cardiovascular events. However, the most recent Outcomes Study to Assess STatin Residual Risk Reduction With EpaNova in HiGh CV Risk PatienTs With Hypertriglyceridemia (STRENGTH) was prematurely terminated for futility of benefit from omega-3 fatty acids treatment [85]. Equally important, this study raised important safety concerns of treatment since atrial fibrillation and gastrointestinal adverse events were significantly increased compared to placebo.  



# 4.4. Glucagon-Like peptide-1 receptor agonists and other novel antidiabetic drugs  

Diabetes is a major risk factor for all subtypes of ischemic stroke through its microvascular and macrovascular deleter­ ious effects [71]. The major intervention to reduce stroke risk in diabetic patients is tight control of hypertension [86]. On the contrary, tighter glycemic control had no benefit in cardi­ ovascular outcomes in early clinical trials; a meta-analysis of the Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes (ACCORD), Action in Diabetes and Vascular Disease: Preterax and Diamicron Modified Release Controlled Evaluation (ADVANCE), UKPDS and Veterans Affairs Diabetes Trial (VADT) failed to demonstrate that lowering HbA1c was associated with stroke risk reduction [87]. Novel antidiabetic agents recently changed the treatment paradigm, and among a vari­ ety of available antidiabetic agents some agents have shown a protective effect against stroke. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1 R) agonists are newer antidiabetic medica­ tions that act by reducing glucagon and promoting insulin secretion [88]. Our study group has recently published a sys­ tematic review and meta-analysis that suggests that GLP-1 R agonists have a consistent beneficial effect on primary stroke prevention; further RCTs are needed to evaluate their role for secondary stroke prevention [89]. Another promising class of drugs are sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. In consideration of their positive effect on major cardiovascu­ lar events, the American Diabetes Association recommends treating diabetic patients with established atherosclerotic dis­ ease or with high risk profile, with antidiabetic drugs with proven cardiovascular benefit, either a SGLT2 inhibitor (empa­ gliflozin, canagliflozin, dapagliflozin) or a GLP-1 R agonist (liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide) [90]. Among these agents, a statistically significant reduction in risk of stroke was noted only with two GLP-1 R agonists: semaglutide and dulaglutide [91]. It should be noted that in all aforementioned RCTs, most patients were also on metformin and statin treat­ ment. An important contribution has been the recent Danish study: a Mendelian randomization was performed in patients from two Copenhagen cohorts (the Copenhagen General  

Population Study and the Copenhagen City Heart Study) and the MEGASTROKE study. Effects of eight glucose-lowering drugs on risk of stroke were also summarized by meta-ana­ lyses. The beneficial effect of GLP-1 R agonists was confirmed but not that of SGLT2 inhibitors; thiazolidinediones also showed promise for reducing stroke risk [92]. Similarly, a reanalysis of the Insulin Resistance Intervention after Stroke (IRIS) trial reported a $28\%$ reduction in ischemic stroke in patients with insulin resistance and a recent stroke or TIA treated with pioglitazone [93].  



# 4.5. Colchicine  

Colchicine is a low-cost anti-inflammatory drug with a favor­ able safety profile. In vitro studies support the role of inflam­ mation in the evolution and rupture of an atherosclerotic plaque that may in turn cause stroke through vessel throm­ bosis or arterial embolism [94,95]. Symptomatic carotid pla­ ques from patients undergoing endarterectomy frequently demonstrate inflammatory infiltration [96,97]. Colchicine could prove to have an important role in plaque stabilization [98]. Low-dose colchicine reduces the composite incidence of acute coronary syndrome, out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, or non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke in patients with stable cor­ onary artery disease [99]. A recent RCT of patients with myo­ cardial infarction, showed that treatment with colchicine at a dose of 0.5 mg daily leads to a significantly lower risk of ischemic cardiovascular events than placebo [100]. We have performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of all avail­ able randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reporting on incident strokes during the follow-up of patients with a history of cardiovascular disease randomized to colchicine treatment or control (placebo or usual care). Colchicine treatment was associated with a significantly lower risk of incident stroke during follow-up compared to control [101]. The COlchicine for preventioN of Vascular Inflammation in Non-CardioEmbolic stroke (CONVINCE) is a Prospective Randomized Open-label Blinded Endpoint assessment (PROBE) clinical trial of lowdose colchicine for secondary prevention after non-cardioem­ bolic stroke in patients with atheromatosis (NCT02898610). The RCT will randomize all non-cardioembolic subtypes of ischemic stroke or TIA to low-dose colchicine or placebo on addition to best medical treatment for 36 months [102]. It should be noted that colchicine is frequently associated with severe gastrointestinal symptoms that may lead to interrup­ tion of treatment as well as some rare but severe complica­ tions including peripheral neuropathy, blood abnormalities and drug overdose that could potentially be lethal.  



# 5. Conclusions  

Hopefully, novel pharmaceutical agents will continue to expand our therapeutic armamentarium in the following years and stroke prognosis will further improve in the acute, subacute, and chronic phase. Still, many unmet needs of stroke patients remain. Inequalities in stroke care both between and within countries [103], failure to implement clinical guidelines in prac­ tice [104], absence of organized stroke unit networks in many parts of the world, have more to do with proper and just allocation of resources than the availability of effective treat­ ments for acute stroke care and secondary stroke prevention [105,106]. There is urgent need for Health Services Research to identify gaps in care for the societies to develop strategies to translate knowledge into action [107]. Additionally, novel drugs cannot compensate for the absence of lifestyle modifications after stroke. Over the past three decades, blood pressure and cholesterol control among stroke survivors improved, but rates of obesity, poor diet, and physical inactivity increased [108]. Other comorbidities such as stress, cardiovascular risk factors, and sedentary lifestyle increase stroke risk whereas a combina­ tion of physical exercise, normal weight, healthy diet, avoidance of smoking, and moderate alcohol consumption may act as modulatory factors of any treatment [109]. Long-term preven­ tion of stroke is also limited by decline in adherence to pre­ scribed drugs by stroke patients [110]. Novel drug development in the acute, subacute, and chronic stroke phase in parallel with promoting stroke care globally through collaboration of stroke physicians, stroke patients’ organizations, and decision makers, will have a major impact in improving outcomes for stroke patients in the following years.  



# 6. Expert opinion  

Intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase has remained the only systemic reperfusion therapy in acute ischemic stroke for sev­ eral years; tenecteplase has recently emerged as an alternative thrombolytic agent, which is easier to administer, equally safe (without alteplase-associated allergic reactions), and, possibly, more effective. Neuroprotective agents have a long history of failure to translate preclinical promises into benefit in rando­ mized-controlled clinical trials; nerinetide is a promising agent in acute ischemic stroke that could, supposedly, improve out­ comes in those patients ineligible for intravenous thromboly­ sis. Malignant cerebral infarction has dismal prognosis and medical treatment is of limited value; intravenous sulfonylur­ eas are currently under investigation for reducing life-threa­ tening edema. Secondary stroke prevention in the subacute phase is the emerging challenge in modern stroke medicine requiring detailed diagnostic work-up in dedicated stoke units and pharmaceutical regimens tailored to the specific cause of stroke in each patient. Ticagrelor is an antiplatelet agent that has shown increased efficacy, but widespread use has been hampered by an increased risk of major hemorrhage. Factor XI inhibitors are novel antithrombotic agents that are currently being tested in clinical trials. However, any new drug should demonstrate an excellent risk-benefit profile since stroke recurrence in the subacute phase is no longer common. Combined antithrombotic therapy with aspirin and low-dose rivaroxaban has proven increased efficacy over aspirin alone, especially in high-risk individuals such as those with athero­ matic lesions in multiple arterial beds or heart failure. On the other hand, combined antithrombotic therapy increases hemorrhagic risk and special care should be taken for patients at increased risk for gastrointestinal hemorrhage. PCSK9 inhi­ bitors are extremely effective in lowering cholesterol and, more importantly, have shown benefit in high-risk individuals with dyslipidemia. Other non-statin hypolipidemic agents show conflicting data regarding their efficacy and safety profile. Novel antidiabetic agents have shown improved car­ diovascular outcomes in clinical trials irrespective of glycemic control and current guidelines in diabetology recommend all antidiabetic agents under development to check for and report efficacy on cardiovascular outcomes. Finally, colchicine is a low-cost drug that could prove effective in reducing the inflammatory component of atherosclerosis and, therefore, improve cardiovascular outcomes.  

Even if some of these agents show increased net clinical benefit compared to current treatment paradigms, the effect on stroke care will mainly be determined by the percentage of stroke patients affected by the treatment paradigm shift. In the acute stroke phase, all limitations to alteplase (time limit, contraindications) will continue to apply for tenecteplase, meaning that, even if tenecteplase proves to be slightly more effective than alteplase in ongoing clinical trials, the repercussions on the entire ischemic stroke population would be minimal. Additionally, even if nerinetide proves efficacy in this patient group, the overall effect on stroke population would not be expected to be substantial. Any agent tested for reducing stroke relapse in the subacute phase evidently will affect all ischemic stroke patients, but, since stroke recur­ rence has already been successfully lowered in recent years, the benefit on stroke patients will be, at best, moderate. Stroke survivors unable to optimally lower cholesterol despite best medical treatment with statins or statins with ezetimibe may benefit from PCSK9 but, apart from the fact that this is a small subgroup of patients with minimal impact on the entire stroke population, high treatment cost remains an important barrier to widespread use in clinical practice.
