# ARTICLE   HISTORY  

Abstract: Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH) is a medical emergency with potentially devastating outcomes. It is without doubt that over the past decades, there has been a radical change in the approach towards patients with SAH, both in terms of the surgical as well as of the pharmacological treatments offered. The present review aims to outline the principal data regarding the best practice in the pharmacotherapy of SAH, as well as to sum up the emerging evidence from the latest clinical trials. To date, nimodipine is the only evidence-based treatment of vasospasm. However, extensive research is currently underway to identify novel substances with magnesium sulphate, cilostazol, clazosentan and fasudil, demonstrating promising results. Antifibrinolytic therapy could help reduce mortality, and anticoagulants, in spite of their associated hazards, could actually reduce the incidence of delayed cerebral ischemia. The effectiveness of triple-H therapy has been challenged, yet evidence on the optimal regimen is still pending. Statins may benefit some patients by reducing the incidence of vasospasm and delayed ischemic events. As several clinical trials are underway, it is expected that in the years to come, more therapeutic options will be added to the attending physician’s armamentarium.  

Keywords: Subarachnoid haemorrhage, delayed cerebral ischemia, vasospasm, pharmacotherapy, outcome, therapy, mortality.  

# 1. INTRODUCTION  

Spontaneous subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH) is a medical emergency with potentially life-threatening sequalae. This condition has a high geographical variation, with an annual global incidence ranging from 0.7-12 per 100,000 persons [1]. While it accounts for a minority of all strokes $(<5\%)$ [2], affecting less than 700,000 people globally each year [1], it has gained great attention due to its high morbidity and mortality.  

Importantly, the one-year mortality rate has shown a trend to reduce globally. A study from the UK at the dawn of the new millennium reported a mortality rate of $44\%$ [3]. Studies from Korea report a reduction in deaths from $21.4\%$ in 2013 [4] to $14.4\%$ in the period 2013-2018 [5], while USbased groups working from 2003-2017 found a $22\%$ mortality rate in urban hospitals [6].  

Besides the initial catastrophic event, subarachnoid bleeding can lead to a series of equally detrimental complications. In terms of chronological classification, the complications associated with SAH can be broadly organised into three groups (Fig. 1). Those occurring within 24 hours of ictus are the result of increased intracranial pressure (ICP)  

and, as a consequence, decreased cerebral blood flow (CBF) and cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP). This consists of the acute phase of the condition and has an estimated mortality rate of over $12\%$ [7]. The subacute phase, which lasts until the third day following SAH, is marked by early brain injury due to ischemia-induced inflammation and necrosis, eventually leading to the breakdown of the blood-brain barrier and cerebral edema with similar mortality as the one observed in the acute phase [3]. Following that, starts the chronic phase that can last up to 6 weeks after SAH, when delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) due to vasospasm or formation of microthrombi may occur [8, 9]. Recent mortality rates reported for this period are generally in the range of $13-15\%$ [5, 10]. Each phase presents particular challenges in its management, requiring different pharmacological approaches.  

Even though the overall mortality is currently lower than previously believed, about $40\%$ of patients with SAH die because of the condition, with the chronic complications contributing equally as important as the acute ones in these figures. The pooled estimate of vasospasm and DCI following SAH is $42\%$ and $22\%$ , respectively [11], and thus, their avoidance has attracted significant attention, with extensive research endeavours being currently underway. Cerebral vasospasm is a poorly understood process, with several pathophysiological mechanisms being potential candidates for its pathogenesis [12].

Motivated by the advances in the research and development of new agents, the present review aims to outline the principal data regarding the best practice in the pharmacotherapy of SAH, as well as to sum up the current evidence derived from the latest clinical trials.  

# 2. SEARCH STRATEGY  

A PubMed search for original studies reporting the pharmacological therapy of SAH has been performed through 13 February 2023. Only original manuscripts written in English were considered. The citations of the identified articles were reviewed in order to include additional manuscripts when the relevant criteria were fulfilled. Preclinical studies, including animal studies, were excluded from further processing. The key findings are summarised in this article.  

# 3. NIMODIPINE  

Nimodipine is the only medication that has a Class I; leading to a grade A recommendation by the AHA [13], which remains constant in the latest guidelines published [14]. The laconic, yet succinct, sentence “oral nimodipine should be administered to all patients with aSAH” summarises in the best possible way a practice that is wellestablished globally.  

Several meta-analyses have demonstrated that the calcium antagonist nimodipine reduces the cerebral blood flow velocity in the middle cerebral artery and overall provides a better outcome [15, 16], particularly in patients under 50 years old [17]. Moreover, the effect is so potent that the number needed to treat in order to avoid delayed neurological function deficit has been calculated to be almost 2:1 and for every 3.062 patients that were administered oral nimodipine, one death is being avoided [16]. In a meta-analysis of clinical trials, the relative risk for poor outcomes following treatment with oral nimodipine was estimated to be 0.67. Nevertheless, no study investigating exclusively intravenous administration of the same medication was identified. For this reason, oral nimodipine $60~\mathrm{mg}$ every four hours is strongly recommended, thanks to its efficiency and safety [18]. While continuous intravenous administration, usually at a rate of $2~\mathrm{mg/h}$ is also an option [19], there is no profound superiority of one over the other [20].  

Intra-arterial infusion of $3{-}6~\mathrm{mg}$ nimodipine in the affected vessel and at a rate of $6~\mathrm{mg},$ /hour provided improved clinical outcomes compared to standard of care [21, 22]. In a phase 3 clinical trial (NEWTON-2), single-dose intraventricular administration of $600~\mathrm{mg}$ of nimodipine in its microparticle form provided superior results in terms of vasospasm but had no advantage regarding clinical outcome compared to oral nimodipine alone [23]. Of the agents administered intrathecally, nimodipine was the only one to provide an advantage in vasospasm, as mentioned in the meta-analysis by Grossen et al. [24].  

It should also be stressed that nimodipine has also, in general, a safe profile [16], with the most important side effects associated with continuous intra-arterial infusion being arterial hypotension, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, atrial fibrillation or flutter, infections, acute kidney injury, hepatic and gastro-intestinal irritation [25].  

# 4. ANTIEPILEPTICS  

Post-haemorrhagic seizures are fairly frequent, with an estimated incidence of about $15-20\%$ [26, 27]. Interestingly, some studies support that early-onset seizures are associated with an unfavourable outcome [28], whereas others claim the opposite [29]. While the routine use of antiepileptics is not recommended by the American Heart Association (Class III; Level of Evidence B), their administration should be considered in selected patients, such as those with a known history of seizures, with an intracerebral hematoma, intractable hypertension, infarction, or with an aneurysm at the middle cerebral artery (Class II; Level of Evidence B) [13].  

A recent meta-analysis found that long-term $>3$ days) administration of antiepileptic drugs was associated with poor outcomes [30]. However, it is unclear whether the medication or the disease was the cause of this. In addition, a long-term course of levetiracetam did not show any superiority over a short (3 days) one [31]. When comparing the efficacy of levetiracetam and phenytoin in the prevention of seizures, no superiority of one over the other was found [30]. Nevertheless, phenytoin administration is associated with poorer cognitive outcomes 3 months following hospital discharge [32]. Currently, a multicentre clinical trial is underway to investigate the efficacy of valproate in patients with SAH [33]. Recently, prophylactic administration of perampanel, has shown some potential to reduce the occurrence of DCI, however the need for larger clinical trials is imperative to draw any definitive conclusion [34].  

# 5. ANTICOAGULANTS  

To date, there are no specific guidelines regarding the optimal timing and dosage of anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents for deep venous thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis or for delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) or even their administration per se. Some authors are against their routine use due to lack of clear benefit [35], whereas others challenge this [36].  

In preclinical models of SAH, heparin showed the potential to reduce inflammation, demyelination and neuronal apoptosis [37], setting itself as a candidate for routine clinical application.  

Administration of unfractionated heparin (UFH) for $>48$ hours after securing the aneurysm is associated with a lower incidence of early $\mathit{\Omega}^{\prime}<6$ weeks) cerebral infarction [36] but had no effect either on DCI nor on the quality of life. Importantly, no statistically significant increase in new hemorrhagic episodes was observed. It should be stressed, however, that the therapeutic regimens applied by each research group varied considerably. A more recent study, however, challenges this outcome reporting that early anticoagulation could actually improve outcome one year following the ictus [38].  

While $40~\mathrm{mg}$ of enoxaparin initiated within 12-24 hours following aneurysm occlusion and continued for 10 days is safe and provides significant prophylaxis against thromboses, it does not affect neither on the overall outcome [35], nor on the occurrence of DCI [39] possibly due to its inefficiency in the down-regulation of the coagulation activity [40]. In contrast, another study that investigated the effects of $20~\mathrm{mg}$ of enoxaparin administered for 21 days showed a significant reduction in delayed ischemic neurologic deficit (DIND) and cerebral infarction without an increase in haemorrhagic events [41]. Similar outcomes were obtained following intravenous heparin for 14 days postoperatively [42].  

One major limitation of the administration of heparins is heparin-induced thrombocytopaenia (HIT), which can occur in as many as $5\%$ of patients with SAH [43], even though others report a three times higher incidence [44]. Importantly, this incidence appears to be independent of the selection of the agent as heparin 5000 IU bid intramuscularly and enoxaparin ( $\mathrm{40~mg}$ daily intramuscularly) failed to demonstrate any superiority [43].  

As far as antiplatelets are concerned, no statistically significant effect of any antiplatelet medication in brain ischemia, outcome, complications and mortality was found by Dorhout Mees et al. [45]. Meta-analysis of five clinical trials with 699 patients being enrolled [46] demonstrated that antiplatelets are not associated with an increased risk for poor outcome. Nevertheless, an increase of $0.2\%$ in the incidence of intracranial haemorrhages was observed. On the other hand, the risk for DCI was significantly reduced. The latter, however, contradicts the finding from the metaanalysis conducted by Cagnazzo et al., a study which nevertheless suggests that antiplatelet therapy for over two weeks significantly reduces mortality rates [47]. Considering the above, one could argue that potentially, the overall benefit outweighs the risk, as for every 7 patients treated with antiplatelets, one DCI is being avoided, whereas the corresponding number for the occurrence of devastating sequelae is one in 500. In this context, it should be noted that prehemorrhage aspirin intake, in particular, is associated with a decreased risk for systematic complications such as venous thromboembolism and cardiac events, but has no effect on early mortality [48].  

# 6. ANTIFIBRINOLYTIC THERAPY  

The rationale behind the use of antifibrinolytic therapy is to stabilise the already formed clot and thus avoid de novo haemorrhages. Even though antifibrinolytic therapy is indeed effective in reducing the risk of rebleeding, there is also an increased risk of cerebral ischemia [49, 50]. Another study, however, challenges this claim and supports that tranexamic acid, in particular, is not associated with an increased risk for thrombosis [51]. In addition to this, TXA may be beneficial in reducing mortality in patients with SAH. According to a more recent work [52], it should be stressed, however, that treatment protocols varied significantly among trials from ${1}\mathrm{g}$ thrice daily to $1.5~\mathrm{g}$ every four hours. As data are still inconclusive, routine use of antifibrinolytic therapy is not recommended at the time being [53].  

# 7. SALINE  

The use of triple-H therapy (induced hypertension, hypervolemia and haemodilution) to prevent vasospasm has gradually decreased over the past twenty years [54], mainly because of strong evidence on the potential hazards of aggressive hypervolemia [55], some of the most severe of them being pulmonary oedema and dilutional hyponatremia [56]. Moreover, the absence of any advantage of hypervolemia compared to euvolemia regarding cerebral blood flow [57] and clinical outcome [58], in combination with the association of haemodilution with reduced brain oxygenation [59], has shifted interest to hypertension alone, rendering euvolemia the goal in the management of SAH (Level of Evidence B) [13].  

After securing the aneurysm, it is suggested to keep the patient in a positive fluid balance by ensuring intake of at least 3 litres of saline at a rate of 100 cc/hour [56]. Evidence regarding the selection of the optimal regimen is still lacking. Preliminary results from patients participating in the SMART trial favour saline over balanced crystalloids based on the lower 90-day mortality rate, a difference which is, at the time being, of undetermined cause [60]. Similarly, hydroxyethyl starch does not seem to provide any survival benefit compared to Ringer’s Lactate [61].  

# 8. MANNITOL  

Mannitol is an agent that has established effects against cerebral oedema. It is considered to lower ICP through osmotic diuresis. Mannitol has been extensively studied in the context of traumatic brain injury. Nevertheless, there are a few studies investigating its efficiency in SAH. Depending on the protocol of each study, intravenous administration of $0.5{-}1.5~\mathrm{g/kg}$ of mannitol led to a significant and doseindependent decrease in ICP [62].  

# 9. HYPERTONIC SALINE  

Hypertonic saline is an efficient way to lower intracranial pressure and increase cerebral perfusion pressure, which is particularly useful in patients resistant to mannitol [63]. Even though the quality of evidence is low at the time being, the Neurocritical Care Society suggests administering bolus hypertonic saline in patients with SAH for the management of increased ICP and oedema [64]. However, the optimal dose remains to be determined. The regimens applied vary considerably among research groups still $2~\mathrm{ml/kg}$ of $23.5\%$ NaCl or continuous infusion of $3\%$ NaCl until resolution of symptoms appear to be safe and efficient options. It is evident that at the same time, serum sodium should be closely monitored and kept below $160~\mathrm{mEq/L}$ so as to avoid the risk of developing acute kidney injury [64]. Regardless of its benefits in lowering ICP, strong evidence of its efficiency in overall outcome is still lacking [65].  

# 10. STOOL SOFTENERS/LAXATIVES  

It is well known that defecation is associated with an increase in blood pressure, which is $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ on average and can be as high as $110\mathrm{mmHg}$ for systolic and $80\mathrm{mmHg}$ for diastolic blood pressure [66]. Constipation, in particular, is associated with an increased risk of congestive heart failure, arrhythmia, acute coronary disease and aortic dissection [67]. It is essentially for this reason that vegetable consumption is strongly encouraged (Recommendation Class IIb,  

Evidence Level B [13]) in order to reduce the risk of SAH [68]. Even though stool softeners and laxatives are widely used in clinical practice in order to avoid hypertension associated with defecation [69], currently, to the best of our knowledge, there are no clinical trials assessing their efficiency in the post-SAH setting. However, it should be stressed that the use of laxatives could lead to excessive loss of enteral volume, which is associated with an increased risk of developing DIND [70].  

# 11. ANALGESICS  

When addressing the topic of pharmacotherapy in SAH, analgesics are often overlooked, even though, more frequently than not, treating post-haemorrhagic headaches is extremely challenging. It is noteworthy that two out of three patients report experiencing 10/10 pain at some point during their hospitalisation, in spite of optimal treatment [71]. As specific guidelines are currently lacking [13], any combination of prostaglandin inhibitors, non-steroidal antiinflammatory agents (NSAIDs) and opioid analgesics is applied based on the preferences of the treating physician and the individual needs of the patient. Dexamethasone can also be added in refractory cases, acting indirectly by reducing cerebral oedema, the potentially severe side effects, but, significantly limiting its routine use [72].  

Acetaminophen is typically used in doses of up to 4g/day without significant side-effects. However, monotherapy is considered ineffective [73]. In contrast, its combination with codeine as well as with butalbital and caffeine were found to be two of the most efficient analgesic regimens [73]. Anticonvulsant gabapentin at maximal doses of $900~\mathrm{mg}$ per day has demonstrated a potential to reduce the daily requirements of patients in opioids, acting synergistically with standard of care [74]. Yet, current evidence does not support gabapentin as a standalone option [73]. Opioids, such as $30{\cdot}60~\mathrm{mg}$ codeine every 4 hours [73], are the mainstay treatment for refractory headaches following SAH. Side effects such as ileus and respiratory depression, as well as chronic consumption, which is reported by about half of the patients, are major concerns associated with their use [75, 76]. Previous studies have suggested that magnesium may also be a useful adjuvant to standard-of-care analgesia, especially when treating acute migraine attacks [77, 78] and other various types of headaches as well [79]. With regards to SAH, of all the treatments investigated by Hile and Cook, magnesium showed the largest reduction in mean pain score at a suggested dose of $_{4\mathrm{g}}$ every 4 hours until serum magnesium levels are over $2~\mathrm{mg/dl}$ [73], while another study demonstrated that patients with higher serum magnesium levels required less opioids [80].  

# 12. STATINS  

In a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical trials of patients receiving statins (753 patients Simvastatin $80\mathrm{mg},80$ patients Pravastatin $40~\mathrm{mg}$ , 142 patients Atorvastatin $40~\mathrm{mg}$ ), DIND was found to be significantly reduced, while no difference was observed in mortality and neurological outcomes [81]. Similar conclusions were drawn from Akhigbe et al. in their study published the following year [82]. These results are challenged by Shen et al., who found a statistically significant difference in vasospasm and mortality, yet no effect on DIND was observed [83, 84].  

Another meta-analysis of RCTs investigating specifically the effects of $40~\mathrm{mg}$ or $80~\mathrm{mg}$ of simvastatin daily showed that its administration failed to provide any therapeutic benefit over placebo with regards to ischaemic deficits, vasospasm, disability and mortality [85]. It should be stressed that all of the studies included were assessed as high quality with a Jadad score $>3$ . The superiority of high $(80~\mathrm{mg})$ over low-dose $(40~\mathrm{mg})$ statin administration in the prevention of DIND was also supported by the findings of Choi et al. [86] and To et al. [87], who found a dosedependent effect of statins in risk of vasospasm, DIND and mortality. Another issue of debate is the optimal duration of treatment, with 21 days of administration of statins demonstrating no further therapeutic benefit over a 2-week regimen [88]. Prior statin users may also have an advantage over matched controls as far as functional outcome, delayed ischemia and vasospasm are concerned [89].  

All in all, it appears that high-dose statin administration for two weeks following the event may benefit some patients by regulating cerebral blood flow and thus reducing the incidence of vasospasm and delayed ischemic events. These advantages along with the safety profile of the agents and the high experience gained from the long history of their administration, could warrant more extensive use in the clinical setting. Nevertheless, at the time being and in view of the lack of strong evidence regarding their efficiency in reducing DIND, the Neurocritical Care Society, as well as the American Heart Association, recommend against their routine use [14, 53].  

# 13. CORTICOSTEROIDS  

Corticosteroids are effective in the management of vasogenic oedema, like the one caused by intracranial tumours, by reducing capillary permeability and ultimately stabilising the blood-brain barrier [90]. In spite of the significant attention they received initially for the prevention of DCI [91], driven by their anti-inflammatory properties and ability to reduce plasma volume depletion, to date clinical trials available have failed to establish any positive effect on the relative risk of DCI and poor outcome following SAH. In contrast, they showed that corticosteroids are associated with an increased risk for adverse effects, including hyperglycaemia, which is independently associated with poor outcomes following SAH [92-94]. What is more, it has been postulated that a history of corticosteroid use might be a risk factor for developing SAH [95, 96]. Still, corticosteroids, can be used for the management of natriuretic diuresis ultimately reducing the incidence of hypovolemia [97].  

# 14. BETA BLOCKERS  

Beta-blockers are the mainstay treatment for hypertension and tachycardia, which are both risk factors for SAH and should be managed accordingly (Level of Evidence B [13]). They act by binding to the receptors of catecholamines epinephrine and norepinephrine [98], thus having a direct effect on the sympathetic nervous system [99], which is over-activated following subarachnoid bleeding [99]. Beta-blockers are safe and significantly reduce mortality. However, their effect on vasospasm, DIND and functional outcome is insignificant [100]. It is noteworthy that the studies included in this meta-analysis used different agents, inducing both selective (atenolol, landiolol, and metoprolol) and non-selective (propranolol) blockade. It is stressed that before securing the aneurysm, antihypertensives should be avoided in patients with systolic blood pressure under 140 $\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ [56].  

# 15. MAGNESIUM SULPHATE  

Magnesium sulphate is a calcium antagonist, just like nimodipine. It has been approved for the prevention of seizures in eclampsia/preeclampsia and hypomagnesemia, among others, but its use in SAH remains controversial [101].  

The IMASH study, a phase III clinical trial, failed to show any effect on vasospasm [102], which contradicts the findings of a meta-analysis by Ba et al. [103]. A systematic review found a significant reduction in the occurrence of DCI following SAH. Still, the regimens varied among different studies, ranging from 64 to 190 mmol per day with or without a bolus dose [104], with its therapeutic potential being more potent when co-administered with nimodipine [105]. Yamamoto et al. [106] found intrathecal magnesium sulphate to be effective in combating cerebral vasospasm, and Grossen et al. found this route of administration to significantly reduce DCI [24]. Nevertheless, as hard evidence is still lacking, current suggestions do not support routine administration of IV ${\mathrm{Mg}}{\mathrm{SO}}_{4}$ for the prophylactic or symptomatic management of cerebral vasospasm following SAH.  

# 16. PAPAVERINE  

Papaverine is an opium alkaloid that acts as a smooth muscle relaxant [107]. Cisternal irrigation with papaverine failed to provide any advantage over the standard of care in the relief of vasospasm [108] and in 90-day outcomes [109]. Thus, its efficacy remains questionable, particularly in the absence of large, multi-centre clinical trials.  

# 17. NOVEL AGENTS  

Regardless of the clinical data on its efficiency, nimodipine is not a panacea and some patients do not respond following optimal treatment. In order to improve outcomes, extensive research has focused on the development of additional drugs like cilostazol, a selective inhibitor of phosphodiesterase 3 with anti-platelet and vasodilatory properties and clazosentan, a selective endothelin receptor antagonist.  

Cilostazol, a selective inhibitor of phosphordiesterase type 3, administered at $200\mathrm{mg/day}$ for two weeks, was found to decrease the incidence of symptomatic vasospasm and cerebral infarction and is associated with an overall better outcome compared to placebo, while at the same time, there was no significantly increased incidence of adverse reactions [110-112]. Importantly, it has been suggested that cilostazol may be an effective alternative or adjuvant to nimodipine to achieve a favourable clinical outcome [113].  

As for the latter, $>5~\mathrm{mg/h}$ of clazosentan, a fast-acting endothelin A receptor antagonist, shows reduction in the incidence of new cerebral infarctions, vasospasm and allcause morbidity and mortality [114, 115]. In addition, regardless of dose, clazosentan reduces the incidence of DIND. On the other hand, its administration is associated with a higher incidence of pulmonary complications and anaemia than a placebo, which may ultimately limit its applications [114]. A multicentre phase 3 clinical trial aiming to assess the efficacy of intravenous administration of 15 $\mathrm{mg/h}$ clazosentan for the prevention of delayed cerebral ischemia [116] failed to meet its primary endpoint [117].  

Milrinone is an inhibitor of the peak III cAMP phosphodiesterase isozyme (PDE3) in cardiomyocytes and vascular smooth muscle that is currently under investigation as an adjuvant therapy for cerebral vasospasm. Data available to date suggest that irrespective of its mode of administration (intravenous vs intra-arterial-intravenous), milrinone achieved clinical resolution of DCI in about $90\%$ of patients at the cost of hypotension and hypokalaemia [118]. Although monotherapy is not recommended, co-administration with nimodipine could be reserved for optimal treatment of refractory cases [119].  

Dantrolene is an inhibitor of the ryanodine receptor, which is currently under investigation as a potential neuroprotective agent [120]. Even though pre-clinical trials were promising [121], intravenous dantrolene $1.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ every 6 h for 7 days did not show any superiority over placebo in the 90-day functional outcomes [122]. However, future research is expected to provide more information in the years to come.  

Rho-kinase is involved in vascular physiology, regulating local blood flow through the promotion of smooth muscle cell contraction [123]. Fasudil is a Rho-kinase inhibitor that has been found to induce dilation of cerebral blood vessels and reduce endothelial hyperpermeability [124]. Fasudil $30~\mathrm{mg}$ three times per day has been found to be equally effective as nimodipine in inhibiting vasospasm and preventing cerebral ischemia. Moreover, patients under fasudil had more favourable clinical outcomes than controls one month after hemorrhage [125], an effect which was not observed in individuals over 75 years old [126]. In fact, patients on fasudil were found to be half as likely as controls to present severe complications following SAH [127]. Adverse events observed were exclusively associated with alterations in blood biochemistry without significant sequelae. Interestingly, in spite of its promising qualities, relevant clinical trials are, to date, very limited.  

The potential efficiency of ozagrel, a thromboxane synthetase inhibitor, in preventing cerebral vasospasm was first investigated over 30 years ago [128]. In spite of initially promising outcomes [129], results since then have been contradictory [130, 131], with outcomes being comparable to the standard of care, at the cost of a significantly increased danger for haematoma formation [132].  

Dapsone is widely known as a drug of choice for leprosy, yet thanks to its antagonistic effects on glutamate receptors, and recent research has demonstrated its neuroprotective potential [133]. DCI, favourable outcome and functionality were all improved in patients receiving $100~\mathrm{mg}$ of dapsone daily for about 2 weeks compared to the placebo group [134].  

Cerebrolysin is another example of a substance, having shown promising results as a neuroprotective agent, is undergoing trials that assess its utility in SAH. At the time being, results are mixed, with some groups highlighting its potential in reducing morbidity in poor-grade patients [135] and others finding no difference from placebo [136].  

Following promising results in preventing and reversing cerebral vasospasm in a model of SAH in primates [137], sodium nitrite underwent a phase IIa clinical trial suggesting that an intravenous infusion of $64~\mathrm{nmol/kg/min}$ for 14 days is safe for patients with SAH and has a half-life of less than one hour [138]. Sodium nitrite acts as a pool to provide nitrous oxide, a potent vasodilator, acting preferably at sites of oxidative stress, thus avoiding systematic complications, such as hypotension (Table 1).

# CONCLUSION  

Optimal pharmacotherapy in subarachnoid haemorrhage is still a matter of debate and extensive research endeavours, as underlined by the growing body of literature. The morbidity and mortality associated with the condition, as well as the high socioeconomic impact, render the treatment of its late complications imperative. Even though, at the time being, physicians have in their armamentarium more efficient agents than ever before, and several others are currently under extensive investigation, mortality rates are still high. A more in-depth understanding of the mechanisms associated with the pathophysiology of SAH is expected to provide the necessary tools for the development of novel, more effective compounds in the years to come.
