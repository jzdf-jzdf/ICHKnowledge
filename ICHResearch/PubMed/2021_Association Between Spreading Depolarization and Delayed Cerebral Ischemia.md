# Association Between Spreading Depolarization and Delayed Cerebral Ischemia After Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: Post Hoc Analysis of a Randomized Trial of the Effect of Cilostazol on Delayed Cerebral Ischemia  

Akiko Kawano1, Kazutaka Sugimoto $1^{*}\oplus$ Sadahiro Nomura1, Takao Inoue1,2, Reo Kawano3, Fumiaki Oka1, Hirokazu Sadahiro1, Hideyuki Ishihara1 and Michiyasu Suzuki1,2  

$\circledcirc$ 2021 Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature and Neurocritical Care Society  

# Abstract  

Background:  Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) remains an important problem with a complex pathophysiology. We used data from a single-center randomized trial to assess the effect of a phosphodiesterase inhibitor, cilostazol, in patients with aneurysmal SAH to explore the relationships of DCI with vasospasm, spreading depolarization (SD) and microcirculatory disturbance.  

Methods:  A post hoc analysis of a single-center, prospective, randomized trial of the effect of cilostazol on DCI and SD after aneurysmal SAH was performed. From all randomized cohorts, patients who underwent both SD monitoring and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) on day $9\pm2$ from onset were included. Cerebral circulation time (CCT), which was divided into proximal CCT and peripheral CCT (as a measure of microcirculatory disturbance), was obtained from DSA. Logistic regression was conducted to determine factors associated with DCI.  

Results:  Complete data were available for 28 of 50 patients. Of the 28 patients, 8 $(28.5\%)$ had DCI during the study period. Multivariate analysis indicated a strong association between the number of SDs on the day DSA was performed (i.e., a delayed time point after SAH onset) and DCI (odds ratio 2.064, $95\%$ confidence interval 1.045–4.075, $P=0.037,$ area under the curve 0.836), whereas the degree of angiographic vasospasm and peripheral CCT were not significant factors for DCI.  

Conclusions:  There is a strong association between SD and DCI. Our results suggest that SD is an important therapeutic target and a potentially useful biomarker for DCI.  

Keywords:  Cortical spreading depression, Subarachnoid hemorrhage  

# Introduction  

Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) remains an important problem [1]. The pathophysiology of DCI is complex and seems to be a combination of large vessel vasospasm, microcirculatory disturbance, and inflammation [1, 2]. Furthermore, clinical studies consistently show a strong association between the phenomenon of spreading depolarization (SD) and worse outcomes following SAH [3–5]. SD is characterized by intense neuronal and glial depolarization waves associated with massive transmembrane ion and water shifts, elevated extracellular excitatory amino acid levels, and a tremendous metabolic burden to restore homeostasis [6, 7].  

A recent single-center randomized trial of the effect of a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor, cilostazol, in patients with SAH showed less DCI and improved SD-related metrics in treated patients without reduction of angiographic vasospasm [8]. Another double-blind randomized control trial also showed that cilostazol reduces DCI and poor outcome without reducing angiographic vasospasm [9]. A recent meta-analysis of prospective trials showed that cilostazol reduces new-onset infarction and poor outcomes after SAH [10]. These results suggest that factors other than large vessel vasospasm are  also important in the pathogenesis of DCI and poor outcome.  

To what degree each pathology contributes to development of DCI has not been elucidated. Here, we examined the extent to which large vessel vasospasm, SD, and microcirculatory disturbance contribute to development of DCI. The degree of angiographic vasospasm and proximal cerebral circulation time (CCT) were used as measures of large vessel vasospasm, and peripheral CCT was determined as a measure of microcirculatory disturbance [11].  

# Method  

The study was performed as a post hoc analysis of a single-center prospective randomized trial of the effect of cilostazol on DCI and SD after aneurysmal SAH. The main results of this trial have been published elsewhere [8]. The protocol was approved by the institutional review board and has been disclosed at the University hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000008225). Written informed consent was obtained from legally authorized representatives of patients after a clinical decision had been made to perform clip ligation of an aneurysm.  

# Study Population  

From all randomized cohorts, patients who underwent SD monitoring by electrocorticography (ECoG) and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) on day $9\pm2$ from onset were included in this post hoc study. Fifty patients were randomized, of whom 28 had data required for the current study.  

# Electrophysiology Monitoring and Data Analysis  

Clipping surgery allowed placement through craniotomy of a single, linear, 6-pole platinum subdural electrode (SD-6P; Ad-Tech Medical Instrument Corp, Racine, WI) in the middle cerebral artery area of the frontal lobe ipsilateral to the aneurysm. Electrode 6 served as ground while electrodes 1 to 5 (sealed brain contacts, $1.0{\cdot}\mathrm{cm}$ spacing) were connected in sequential unipolar fashion to a NicoletOne $(0.016{-}45~\mathrm{Hz})$ system (CareFusion, Yorba Linda, CA), with each referenced to an ipsilateral subgaleal electrode (Disposable Subdermal Needle Electrode; Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan). ECoG was reviewed using Chart-8 software (ADInstruments, Bella Vista, Australia), following the consensus of the Co-Operative Studies on Brain Injury Depolarizations [12].  

SDs were identified by the consecutive occurrence of slow potential changes $(<0.05~\mathrm{Hz})$ of neighboring ECoG channels (Fig. 1). In electrically active tissue, SD typically causes “spreading depression” of spontaneous activity in the alternating current range in ECoG $(0.5{-}45\mathrm{Hz})$ . In contrast, SDs measured in a zone of electrically inactive tissue are denoted as “isoelectric SDs.” SDs were counted and depression durations were summed to measure the total number of SDs per recording day (SDs/day) and the total depression duration per recording day (TDDD). The peak SDs/d and peak TDDD (PTDDD) were defined for each patient as the maximal values among all recording days. The number of SDs on the day of DSA and the total depression duration on the day of DSA were also evaluated.  

# Clinical Assessment and Radiology  

Digital subtraction angiography was performed by Japanese Society of Neuro-Endovascular Therapy boardcertified neuroendovascular surgeons. Baseline DSA was performed at admission in all patients. To assess angiographic vasospasm, DSA was conducted on day $9\pm2$ from onset. The ratio of the narrowed vessel diameter was compared with the initial diameter on baseline images. The severity of angiographic vasospasm was categorized as none or mild $\phantom{0}.33\%$ decrease in arterial diameter on follow-up images), moderate $33-66\%$ decrease) and severe ( $\mathrm{\Delta}>66\%$ decrease) [13]. On DSA on day $9\pm2$ , CCT was calculated as described elsewhere [11]. CCT was divided into proximal CCT, the circulation time in the extraparenchymal large arteries (defined as the difference between the mean transit time [MTT] in the vertical intracavernous portion of the internal carotid artery [C5] and MTT in the cortical segment of the Rolandic artery [M4]), and peripheral CCT, the circulation time in the intraparenchymal small vessels (defined as the difference between MTT in M4 and MTT in the Rolandic vein). Overall CCT is the sum of the proximal CCT and peripheral CCT. The arterial diameter of the major cerebral artery correlates well with proximal CCT [11].

A computed tomography (CT) scan was performed at admission and $^{6-12\mathrm{~h~}}$ after aneurysm treatment. Follow-up CT was performed $7\pm2$ and $14\pm2$ days, 1 and 3  months after aneurysmal SAH, and whenever neurological symptoms worsened. Images were assessed by two independent, blinded reviewers. A new cerebral infarction that was due to DCI was evaluated using follow-up CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).  

DCI was defined previously [8, 14] as development of new, focal neurological signs or deterioration in level of consciousness of at least 2 points on the Glasgow Coma Scale or new infarction proven on CT/MRI after the exclusion of procedure-related infarctions that were not attributed to other causes, such as hydrocephalus, electrolyte abnormality, or sedation, by means of clinical assessment, CT or MRI, and appropriate laboratory studies. Clinical assessments were made by two physicians based on clinical records, independent from the treating physicians. Images were also assessed by two independent, blinded investigators. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 3 months after aneurysmal SAH, with favorable and poor outcomes defined as mRS scores of 0–2 and 3–6, respectively, and the extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (eGOS) at 6  months after aneurysmal SAH, with favorable and poor outcomes defined as eGOS scores of 5–8 and 1–4, respectively. These outcomes were assessed by two independent, blinded reviewers.

# Results  

The $\mathrm{DCI}(+)$ and $\mathrm{DCI}(-)$ groups comprised 8 $(28.5\%)$ and 20 $(71.5\%)$ patients, respectively (Table 1). There was no significant difference in age, sex, WFNS grade, Fisher group, aneurysm location, and cilostazol use between the two groups. The severity of angiographic vasospasm was classified as mild (37.5 vs. $45\%$ ), moderate (12.5 vs. $35\%$ ), and severe (50 vs. $20\%$ ), with no significant differences between the groups $P=0.246)$ ). Rescue therapy was performed only in patients who were $\mathrm{DCI}(+)$ (7 of 8, $87.5\%,$ ), and infarction was present in 6 of 8 $(75\%)$ patients in the $\mathrm{DCI}(+)$ group. All SD-related variables, $\mathrm{SDs}/\mathrm{d}$ (34.8 vs. 3.1, $P{=}0.004,$ ), TDDD (418.2 vs. $20.0\mathrm{min}$ , $P{=}0.006,$ ), peak $\mathrm{SDs}/\mathrm{d}$ (9.9 vs. 1.6, $P{=}0.003,$ ), PTDDD (174.8 vs. $10.8~\mathrm{min}$ , $P{=}0.008,$ ), number of SDs on day of DSA (6.1 vs. 0.0, $P{=}0.003,$ , and total depression duration on day of DSA (85.8 vs. $0.0\mathrm{min}$ , $P{=}0.004.$ ) were significantly higher in the $\mathrm{DCI}(+)$ group. Isoelectric SD was detected only in the DCI group (4 of 8 patients, $50\%$ ).  

Time correlations of DCI, DSA, and the incidence of SDs in patients with and without DCI are shown in Fig. 2. The figure gives an overview of 19 of 28 patients who developed SDs after SAH.  

Regarding the circulation time, proximal CCT was shorter (1.34 vs. 1.67  s, $P{=}0.092;$ and peripheral CCT was longer (3.21 vs. $2.23\mathrm{\Omegas},$ , $P{=}0.080^{\circ}.$ ) in the $\mathrm{DCI}(+)$ group, but neither value showed a significant difference. mRS at 3 months (good, 25 vs. $65\%$ , $P{=}0.096$ ) and eGOS at 6 months (good, 37.5 vs. $70\%$ $\phantom{-}_{0}^{\prime}P{=}0.200,$ ) were worse in the $\mathrm{DCI}(+)$ group, but again with no significant difference. We hypothesized that prolonged microcirculation could cause SD, and to the contrary, SD could cause prolonged microcirculation. Therefore, we performed correlation analyses for SD-related values and CCT next. However, correlations between CCT and SD-related values were low in Spearman rank correlation coefficient tests (Table 2).  

The results of univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses of factors with potential relationships with DCI are shown in Tables 3 and 4. In univariate analysis, $\mathrm{SDs}/\mathrm{d}$ , number of SDs on day of DSA, and peripheral CCT were associated with DCI (Table  3). In correlation analyses, SDs/d and number of SDs on day of DSA showed strong correlations $(R{\mathrm{s}}=0.84291$ , $P<0.0001$ , Table  2). Therefore, two multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed (Table  4). These showed a strong and significant association between the number of SDs on day of DSA and DCI (odds ratio [OR] 2.064, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 1.045–4.075, $P{=}0.037$ , area under the curve [AUC] 0.836). SDs/d was also significantly associated with DCI (OR 1.134, $P{=}0.0498$ , AUC 0.863).  

The results of univariate logistic regression analyses of factors with potential relationships with poor clinical outcomes based on mRS at 3  months and eGOS at 6  months are shown in the supplementary material. No variables with a significant association with a poor outcome were identified, except for age.

# Discussion  

Our study showed that the number of SDs on the day DSA was performed (i.e., a delayed time point after SAH onset) and SDs/d were independently associated with DCI, with high discrimination capacity for DCI. In contrast, the degree of angiographic vasospasm and peripheral CCT were not significant factors related to DCI in the present study.  

The first clinical study to demonstrate occurrence of SDs after SAH provided preliminary evidence that delayed ischemic neurological deficits after SAH are associated with a cluster of SDs [15]. The normal hemodynamic response to an SD is hyperemia in humans without acute brain injury; however, in the setting of acute brain injury, an inverse decreased blood flow response is also possible. This phenomenon occurs in patients with SAH in the early and delayed time periods and is referred to as spreading ischemia [6, 16]. SDs are associated with progressive neuronal damage in patients with SAH, with some prospective studies finding a dose dependence between the duration of spreading depression of brain activity and the presence of delayed infarcts [15, 17, 18]. Another case series found that SDs were associated with DCI independent of angiographic vasospasm of the major cerebral arteries [19]. Recent studies have also shown a relationship between SDs and early brain injury (EBI) [20, 21], with an association between SD at an early time point after SAH (i.e., days 1–4 from onset) and a poor outcome. In our cases, the number of SDs at a delayed time point and SDs/d were independent factors associated with DCI, with the former having higher significance. This might have occurred because the number of SDs at a delayed time point does not include early SDs after SAH onset, a biomarker for EBI. Altogether, our results suggest that SDs are a therapeutic target and potentially useful biomarker for DCI.

Microcirculatory disturbance is another potential pathophysiology and therapeutic target for DCI [22]. Prolonged peripheral CCT, which is a surrogate of microcirculatory disturbance, has been associated with decreased regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) independent of the degree of angiographic vasospasm of major cerebral arteries [11]. As for CCT and cerebral perfusion, a prolonged circulation time can be used to diagnose DCI [23, 24], and recent reports suggest that a prolonged circulation time on admission predicts DCI [25–30]. However, it is unclear if prolonged “peripheral” CCT (i.e., microcirculatory disturbance) at a delayed time point can be used to diagnose DCI. In this study, univariate analysis (Table  3) showed that prolonged peripheral CCT was a significant factor for DCI. However, logistic regression analysis showed that the incidence of SDs had higher discrimination capacity for DCI than peripheral CCT and angiographic vasospasm. Also, we expected that peripheral CCT would be associated with SD metrics because SD causes vasoconstriction and vice versa (i.e., vasoconstriction causes SD). However, correlations between peripheral circulation time and SD metrics were weak in the present study.  

There are several weaknesses and limitations in the study. First, we did not assess the microcirculatory disturbance of arteries other than the middle cerebral artery ipsilateral to the subdural electrode. # Conclusions  

In summary, the number of SDs on the day DSA was performed was a strong, independent factor associated with DCI. Vasospasm of major cerebral arteries remains an important factor in the pathogenesis of DCI [2, 31], but we did not find an association between DCI and angiographic vasospasm or proximal/peripheral CCT in this study. Thus, monitoring of SD presents potential opportunities for therapeutic intervention for DCI.
