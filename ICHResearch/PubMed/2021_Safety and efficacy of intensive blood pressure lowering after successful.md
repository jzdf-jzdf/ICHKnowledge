# Safety and efficacy of intensive blood pressure lowering after successful endovascular therapy in acute ischaemic stroke (BP-TARGET): a multicentre, open-label, randomised controlled trial  

Mikael Mazighi, Sebastien Richard, Bertrand Lapergue, Igor Sibon, Benjamin Gory, Jerome Berge, Arturo Consoli, Julien Labreuche, Jean-Marc Olivot, Joseph Broderick, Alain Duhamel, Emmanuel Touze, Adnan I Qureshi, Amélie Yavchitz, Simon Escalard, Jean-Philippe Desilles, Hocine Redjem, Stanislas Smajda, Robert Fahed, Solène Hébert, Benjamin Maïer, François Delvoye, Perrine Boursin, Malek Ben Maacha, Michael Obadia, Candice Sabben, Raphael Blanc, Julien Savatovsky, Michel Piotin, for the BP-TARGET investigators  

# Summary  

Background High systolic blood pressure after successful endovascular therapy for acute ischaemic stroke is associated with increased risk of intraparenchymal haemorrhage. However, no randomised controlled trials are available to guide optimal management. We therefore aimed to assess whether an intensive systolic blood pressure target resulted in reduced rates of intraparenchymal haemorrhage compared with a standard systolic blood pressure target.  

Methods We did a multicentre, open-label, randomised controlled trial at four academic hospital centres in France. Eligible individuals were adults (aged ${\ge}18$ years) with an acute ischaemic stroke due to a large-vessel occlusion that was successfully treated with endovascular therapy. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to either an intensive systolic blood pressure target group $\mathbf{(100-129mmHg)}$ or a standard care systolic blood pressure target group $\mathbf{(130-185mmHg)}$ , by means of a central web-based procedure, stratified by centre and intravenous thrombolysis use before endovascular therapy. In both groups, the target systolic blood pressure had to be achieved within $^{1\mathrm{~h~}}$ after randomisation and maintained for $24\textbf{h}$ with intravenous blood pressure lowering treatments. The primary outcome was the rate of radiographic intraparenchymal haemorrhage at $24-36\mathrm{~h~}$ and the primary safety outcome was the occurrence of hypotension. Analyses were done on an intention-to-treat basis. BP-TARGET is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, number NCT03160677, and the trial is closed at all participating sites.  

Findings Between June 21, 2017, and Sept 27, 2019, 324 patients were enrolled in the four participating stroke centres: 162 patients were randomly assigned to the intensive target group and 162 to the standard target group. Four $(2\%)$ of 162 patients were excluded from the intensive target group and two $(1\%)$ of 162 from the standard target group for withdrawal of consent or legal reasons. The mean systolic blood pressure during the first $24\mathbf{h}$ after reperfusion was $128~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ (SD 11) in the intensive target group and $138~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ (17) in the standard target group. The primary outcome was observed in 65 $(42\%)$ of 154 patients in the intensive target group and 68 $(43\%)$ of 157 in the standard target group on brain CT within $24-36\mathrm{~h~}$ after reperfusion] (adjusted odds ratio 0·96, $95\%$ CI 0·60–1·51; $\mathbf{p}{=}0{\cdot}84)$ . Hypotensive events were not significantly different between both groups and occurred in 12 $(8\%)$ of 158 patients in the intensive target and five $(3\%)$ of 160 in the standard target group. Mortality within the first week after randomisation occurred in 11 $(7\%)$ of 158 patients in the intensive target group and in seven $(4\%)$ of 160 in the standard target group.  

Interpretation An intensive systolic blood pressure target of $\mathbf{100}\mathbf{-129mmHg}$ after successful endovascular therapy did not reduce radiographic intraparenchymal haemorrhage rates at $24-36\mathrm{~h~}$ as compared with a standard care systolic blood pressure target of $\mathbf{130-185~mm~Hg}$ . Notably, these results are applicable to patients with successful reperfusion and systolic blood pressures of more than $130~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ at the end of procedure. Further studies are needed to understand the association between blood pressure and outcomes after reperfusion.

# Research in context  

# Evidence before this study  

We searched MEDLINE for papers in any language published from Jan 1, 2012, to May 1, 2020, with the following terms in the title or abstract: “ischemic stroke”, “endovascular therapy” or “mechanical thrombectomy”, “blood pressure”, “blood pressure lowering” or “hemodynamic”, and “successful recanalization” or “successful reperfusion”. Studies were eligible if they assessed the effect of blood pressure lowering on intracranial haemorrhage rates in patients successfully treated with endovascular therapy. We did not find any randomised controlled trials assessing blood pressure lowering after successful endovascular therapy. Five retrospective studies evaluated the association of blood pressure on intracerebral haemorrhage rates after successful endovascular therapy in patients with acute ischaemic stroke and these studies have been included in a systematic review. However, given several methodological shortcomings, the review could not provide any strong evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of blood pressure lowering. We also did a trial register search on ClinicalTrials.gov and found three ongoing trials evaluating blood pressure targets after successful reperfusion with endovascular therapy.  

# Added value of this study  

To the best of our knowledge, BP TARGET is the first randomised trial to assess the efficacy of blood pressure lowering after successful endovascular therapy on intraparenchymal haemorrhage rates. Being the most serious complication after cerebral reperfusion, radiographic intraparenchymal haemorrhage rates at 24–36 h were considered as a primary endpoint, rather than the more  

clinically relevant and used modified Rankin Scale endpoint, as they could be a marker of reperfusion injury and therefore an appropriate surrogate outcome of such injuries. This study design was by choice because, in 2015, reperfusion rates increased substantially compared with the thrombolysis era, and several questions remained unanswered at that time regarding the management of reperfusion lesions. Given its neutral results, the BP TARGET trial challenges the previously suspected causal relationship between blood pressure values after reperfusion and outcomes such as intraparenchymal haemorrhage. Whether blood pressure has a direct negative affect on outcomes or is an important, but still indirect, marker of worse outcomes is still an unanswered question.  

# Implications of all the available evidence  

Overall, these results point out the absence of efficacy of an intensive blood pressure lowering strategy on radiographic intraparenchymal haemorrhage rates after successful endovascular therapy for acute ischaemic stroke. BP TARGET challenges the current practice to lower blood pressure after successful reperfusion and underlines the need for further trials in this field. Since BP TARGET already evaluated intraparenchymal haemorrhage as a primary endpoint, future trials might consider 3-month functional outcomes as a primary endpoint, but also different blood pressure targets. These trials are currently in progress, including the ENCHANTED 2, OPTIMAL BP, and BEST-II trials. However, as illustrated by BP TARGET, rapid and early blood pressure control after reperfusion remains a challenge, which these studies will need to overcome.

This observation has raised interest in blood pressure management in patients with acute ischaemic stroke after reperfusion therapies. Results from non-randomised studies have suggested an association between increased blood pressure values and sympto­matic intraparenchymal haemorrhage, as well as mortality, notably in the setting of successful reperfusion after endovascular therapy.3,6–8  

Blood pressure management after endovascular therapy is complex, with some stroke centres considering the reperfusion status (ie, successful or not) when defining blood pressure thresholds, despite no convincing evidence to justify this approach.3,6 Blood pressure lowering could prevent ischaemia-reperfusion lesions, including intra­ parenchymal haemorrhage,3 but could also potentially increase the risk of extensive infarct growth with incom­ plete or unsuccessful reperfusion after endovascular therapy.9 Current international blood pressure manage­ ment guidelines do not specify targets based on reperfusion status and recommend a target for systolic blood pressure of less than $185\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ before endovascular therapy and $180~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ within the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after endovascular therapy.10,11 However, several lines of evidence suggested increased intraparenchymal haemorrhage risk or worse functional outcomes with systolic blood pressure values as low as 140 mm Hg after successful reperfusion.3,8 Additionally, some evidence suggested reduced risk of symptomatic intraparenchymal haemorrhage in patients with mean systolic blood pressures of $101{-}120\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ compared with more than $140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}.$ 3 In current practice, data gathered from the StrokeNet survey showed that most US institutions use a systolic blood pressure target in the range of $120{-}139~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ in reperfused patients and choose per­missive hypertension in non-reperfused patients.6 This finding illustrates the current accepted con­ sensus regarding blood pressure management in clinical practice, although no randomised controlled trial (RCT) has yet confirmed this approach.  

We therefore aimed to assess whether an intensive systolic blood pressure target of $100{-}129\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ reduces the occurrence of intraparenchymal haemorrhage after successful endovascular therapy compared with a stan­ dard systolic blood pressure target of $130{-}185\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ .  

# Methods  

# Study design and participants  

We did a multicentre, open-label, randomised controlled trial (the BP-TARGET trial) at four academic hospital centres in France. In brief, eligible individuals were adults (aged ${\ge}18$ years) with an acute ischaemic stroke consecutive to a large-vessel occlusion of the anterior circulation (intracranial internal carotid artery or proximal middle cerebral artery [M1] occlusions, or both) with successful reperfusion by endovascular therapy (defined as modified thrombolysis in cerebral infarction 2b or 3).12 Key exclusion criteria were haemorrhagic complications during endovascular therapy, a spontaneous systolic blood pressure decrease to less than $130~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ after reper­ fusion, severe or fatal comorbidities that would have rendered the procedure unlikely to benefit the individual (eg, malignant cancer with reduced life expectancy), a preexisting stroke disability defined by a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score greater than two, known pregnancy, the absence of written consent from the patient or the family, and the absence of a social insurance scheme. After verification of inclusion and exclusion criteria, the principal investigator or one collaborator explained the objectives and conduct of the study to the patient by using an information form and sought the patient’s consent (appendix pp 14, 19).  

The trial was monitored by an independent data and safety monitoring board, who met three times during the trial’s course. Research ethics approval was obtained from the Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud Méditerranée IV on March 14, 2017 (2016-AO1884-47). Details of the trial design as well as the statistical analysis plan have been published previously,12 and are summarised in the appendix (pp 5–44).  

# Randomisation and masking  

Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) by vascular neurologists or interventional neuroradiologists (the same clinicians who also treated the patients) to either an intensive systolic blood pressure target or a standard systolic blood pressure target within the first hour after reperfusion. Randomisation involved the use of a central web-based procedure provided by an independent statis­ tician using computer-generated random numbers with block sizes of four and stratified by centre and intravenous alteplase use before endovascular therapy. The block size information was not specified in the protocol to ensure that any physician involved in the study could not anticipate the treatment group assign­ment. Given the open-label study design, participants and physicians giving the intervention were not masked to the group assignment.  

# Procedures  

After successful endovascular therapy, individuals were managed in acute stroke units by dedicated vascular neu­ rologists and according to current guidelines.3 For both groups, blood pressure was non-invasively measured with an automatic sphygmomanometer on the arm every $15~\mathrm{min}$ for the first $2\textrm{h}$ , then every $30~\mathrm{min}$ for $6\mathrm{~h~}$ , and subsequently every $^{1\mathrm{h}}$ for the remaining $16\mathrm{~h~}$ , as recom­ mended in French stroke units.12,14  

The treatment goal in the intensive systolic blood pressure target group was to reduce and maintain systolic blood pressure to 100–129 mm $\mathrm{Hg}$ within $^\mathrm{~1~h~}$ after randomisation, as several trials had previously shown the feasibility of achieving blood pressure targets within this timeframe (ENCHANTED and INTERACT2),15,16 and for $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after randomisation. This target was chosen on the basis of an earlier study showing an increased risk of mortality for systolic blood pressure higher than $130~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg},^{17}$ and  the reported U-shaped curve relation­ ship between systolic blood pressure and mortality, symp­ tomatic intraparenchymal haemorrhage, or functional outcomes for patients treated by intravenous thrombolysis.17,18 No observational studies had evaluated the associ­ ation of blood pressure after successful reperfusion at the time the trial was designed, given that the report of the effectiveness of endovascular therapy was published the same year.  

In the standard systolic blood pressure target group, the treatment goal was to maintain systolic blood pressure at $130{-}185\mathrm{mmHg}$ within 1 h and for $24\mathrm{h}$ after randomisation. This upper threshold was chosen according to the French and European guidelines at that time, regarding blood pressure management after reperfusion therapies.3 If blood pressure spontaneously decreased below $130\mathrm{mmHg}$ in the control group, this was accepted and not increased actively as vasopressor therapies cannot be used in stroke units in France, nor were they recommended by European guidelines.13 Nicardipine, administered by intravenous infusion, was the first-line antihypertensive agent recom­ mended, as it is the most commonly used treatment in French stroke units, but investigators were free to choose any antihypertensive medication, based on their current practice, to achieve systolic blood pressure targets.  

Post-procedure medical management in both groups was done according to European and North American guidelines.10,11,13 Neurological and functional examinations (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS] and mRS) were done at baseline. Only the NIHSS was done $24\textrm{h}$ (range 12–36 h) after medical management. Addi­ tionally, any neurological deterioration was evaluated by urgent CT scan. Follow-up after discharge included tele­ phone contact or in-person clinical evaluation at 3 months, or both. The data collection at the 3-month visit consisted of the mRS evaluation and serious adverse events. The assessments were done by a qualified investigator who did not participate in the randomisation, treatment, or in-hospital clinical treatment of the individual.

# Statistical analysis  

We planned to randomly assign 320 individuals (160 per group; appendix pp 16–17) to have $80\%$ power to show, with a two-sided $Z$ -test with pooled variance at 0.05 significance, an absolute $15\%$ decrease in intra­ parenchymal haemorrhage rate (primary outcome) with the intensive systolic blood pressure target (corresponding to an intraparenchymal haemorrhage rate of $25\%$ ). This calculation was based on a assumption of $40\%$ intra­ parenchymal haemorrhage in the standard systolic blood pressure target group, and considering an anticipated rate of $5\%$ of missing follow-up CT scans (due to absence of or uninterpretable scans) for assessment of the primary outcome. The rationale for the effect size was based on findings from a previous study from our group, showing a $40\%$ rate of intraparenchymal haemorrhage after endovascular therapy.19 We considered, as the target difference, a $15\%$ reduction of intraparenchymal haem­ orrhage with the intensive blood pressure control by considering the INTERACT study results, which evaluated blood pressure lowering after acute intracerebral haemo­ rrhage.22 In this study, a mean haematoma growth reduction of $23\%$ was observed with intensive blood pressure control at $24\mathrm{h}$ .

# Results  

Between June 21, 2017, and Sept 27, 2019, 324 individuals were enrolled at four stroke centres in France. 162 $(50\%)$ were randomly assigned to the intensive systolic blood pressure target group and 162 $(50\%)$ to the standard care systolic blood pressure target group. Four $(2\%)$ patients from the intensive target group and two $(1\%)$ from the standard target group were excluded from the intentionto-treat analysis (figure 1). The baseline clinical char­ acteristics were similar between both groups at inclusion (table 1). Of note, one crossover was documented from the control group to the intensive target group, which occurred after treatment allocation and following the occurrence of an intraparenchymal haemorrhage within $24\textrm{h}$ . In total, 236 $(74\%)$ of 318 patients were included with initial isolated middle cerebral artery occlusion. Individuals were included immediately after reperfusion (172 $[54\%]$ of 318 with complete reperfusion), which occurred within a median delay from symptom onset of $285\mathrm{min}$ (IQR 234–357) in the intensive target group and 297 min (220–353) in the standard target group.  

At the time of reperfusion, the mean systolic blood pressure was $155\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ (SD 26) in the intensive target group and $152\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ (25) in the standard target group. During the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after reperfusion, the median number of systolic blood pressure measurements was 33 (IQR 30–35), and the mean systolic blood pressure was $128\mathrm{mmHg}$ (SD 11) in the intensive target group and $138~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ (17) in the standard systolic blood pressure target group (table 2; appendix p 55). Among 290 $(91\%)$ of 318 patients with at least $50\%$ of planned systolic blood pressure measure­ments, the median percentage time spent in the assigned target range of systolic blood pressure was $61\%$ (IQR 41·7–76·2) in the intensive target group and $66\cdot6\%$ $(32\cdot5\ –88\cdot6)$ in the standard target group (table 2). The proportion of patients within the systolic blood pressure target during the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ is provided in the appendix (p 56). The antihypertensive drugs used during the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ to control systolic blood pressure are detailed in table 2. At $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , antihypertensive drugs were used in 128 $(83\%)$ of 154 patients in the intensive target group and 31 $(20\%)$ of 159 in the standard target group. The most frequent drugs used were calcium channel blockers.

# Discussion  

In the BP-TARGET trial, among individuals with acute ischaemic stroke and large-vessel occlusion of the anterior circulation and successful reperfusion after endovascular therapy, an intensive systolic blood pressure target of  

100–129 mm $\mathrm{Hg}$ did not reduce radiographic intra­ parenchymal haemorrhage at $24-36\mathrm{~h~}$ . This finding was consistent across all prespecified subgroups, including age, occlusion site, and intravenous thrombolysis.  

To the best of our knowledge, BP-TARGET is the first RCT specifically designed to evaluate a systolic blood pressure target after reperfusion in the endovascular era of acute ischaemic stroke treatment. The results of BPTARGET do not accord with findings of previous obser­ vational studies that have shown an association between elevated systolic blood pressure and worse functional outcome or intraparenchymal haemorrhage occurrence.3,6,8 The findings of previous observational studies were for different systolic blood pressure targets (<130, 140–159, and $<160\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}),^{25,26}$ all suggesting that the lower the systolic blood pressure the better. BP-TARGET findings agree with previous RCTs of individuals treated with intravenous thrombolysis, with similarly neutral results. The recent ENCHANTED trial, which compared systolic blood pressure targets of $130{-}140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ with targets of less than $180~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ in individuals with acute ischaemic stroke treated with intravenous thrombolysis, found a reduction in any intraparenchymal haemorrhage (OR 0·75, $95\%$ CI 0·60–0·94; p=0·0137) but no significant difference in functional outcome.15  

Several explanations for the findings showing no efficacy of the intensive targeted systolic blood pressure might be discussed. First, the systolic blood pressure difference of $10\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ we observed between groups was modest. However, previous studies have shown an association of systolic blood pressure lowering on sympto­ matic intraparenchymal haemorrhage risk despite modest mean differences in systolic blood pressure, such as 141 mm $\mathrm{Hg}$ versus $130\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg,}^{27} $ or $136~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ versus $129\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}.$ .3 In the ENCHANTED trial, the systolic blood pressure difference was even smaller: a mean systolic blood pressure of 144 mm Hg in the intensive management group versus $149\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ in the control group.15 Of note, in the BP-TARGET trial, $30\%$ of the individuals in the control group had a systolic blood pressure of less than $130~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ which illustrates the well known phenom­ enon of spontaneous systolic blood pressure decrease after reperfusion. The latter phenomenon likely con­ tributed to the narrowed mean systolic blood pressure difference between our groups. Individuals in the inten­ sive management group received substantially more blood pressure lowering drugs than those in the control group ( $83\%$ vs $20\%$ , which confirms the active intervention to reduce systolic blood pressure.  

Second, the optimal systolic blood pressure value to reach after reperfusion might be debatable. A differential effect of systolic blood pressure reduction has been reported, with lower odds of symptomatic intraparen­ chymal haemorrhage for systolic blood pressure of less than $<120~\mathrm{mm~Hg}.$ , but higher odds for mortality.7 This evidence corroborates the high odds of mortality observed for low systolic blood pressure at baseline (ie, before any reperfusion therapy), as compared with systolic blood pressure between $140~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ and $160~\mathrm{mm}$ Hg.28 Intraparenchymal haemorrhage is a marker of ischaemiareperfusion injury;19 so a more stringent control with systolic blood pressure of less than $120~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ might have been more relevant in this population of reperfused individuals. Still, the safety of targeting such low values remains questionable. Mortality was higher in the inten­ sive group, which suggests that a lower systolic blood pressure is probably not better. The rate of primary outcome according to mean systolic blood pressure range by intervals of $10~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ suggested a U-shaped curve with a nadir in the systolic blood pressure range of $110{-}140\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ (appendix p 54), consistent with previous data.17,18 Whether such a relationship would hold true for functional outcomes requires further studies. In this perspective, the ongoing BEST-II (NCT04116112) and OPTIMAL-BP (NCT04205305) trials will evaluate systolic blood pressure targets of $140{-}180~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ and the ENCHANTED 2 trial (NCT04140110) will examine systolic blood pressure of less than $120~\mathrm{mm~Hg}$ in individuals with complete reperfusion after endovascular therapy.  

Third, the observed association between systolic blood pressure and prognosis might be due to the greater elevation in systolic blood pressure for individuals with large acute ischaemic stroke volume or more severe ischaemia, or both. Such large acute ischaemic stroke volume might feature several clusters of focal hypo­ perfusion despite successful endovascular therapy. Such regions might progress toward infarction after intensive systolic blood pressure reduction. Additionally, patients might be successfully reperfused (ie, thrombolysis in cerebral infarction grade 3) after endovascular therapy, but might exhibit persistent venous post-capillary thrombosis, a phenomenon also known as no-reflow.29 In this situation, an intensive systolic blood pressure target even after successful endovascular therapy might negatively affect the prognosis of the acute ischaemic lesion, suggesting that blood pressure could be only an indirect marker of reperfusion injuries (ie, intraparenchymal haemorrhage). Overall, these results support no intensive systolic blood pressure lowering strategy after successful endovascular therapy for clinical practice, at least with a systolic blood pressure target of less than $130~\mathrm{mm}~\mathrm{Hg}$ , to reduce radiographic intraparenchymal haemorrhage rates. The $95\%$ CI (ie, $-11\cdot6$ to 10·1) did not include the target difference $(-15\%$ ; appendix pp 31–32), suggesting that the target effect size is unlikely. These findings also high­ light the difficulty in controlling systolic blood pressure despite best-practice management in dedicated stroke units. Systolic blood pressure variability might be one explanation for the difficulty in controlling this value. This point is important, because blood pressure variability in the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after successful endovascular therapy was found to be correlated more with symptomatic intraparenchymal haemorrhage than with other absolute blood pressure levels.27  

These findings open avenues for future research, to determine if maintaining a stable blood pressure might prevent symptomatic  intraparenchymal  haemorrhage after successful endovascular therapy rather than con­ sidering a unique systolic blood pressure target. Additionally, further studies are needed to evaluate the antihypertensive drugs used in this setting. The present study did not address the role of antihypertensive drugs, which might affect cerebral blood flow (calcium channel blockers that have putative neuroprotective properties30 or $\upbeta$ -receptor antagonists being potentially deleterious by reducing cardiac output). In the BP-TARGET study, $60\%$ of the patients in the intensive target group were treated with intravenous calcium channel blockers, as it is the most prescribed drug in French stroke units.  

We must acknowledge several limitations to the study. First, the non-invasive monitoring could have exposed patients to a hidden blood pressure variability between measures, known to be associated with intraparenchymal haemorrhage after reperfusion?7 and functional outcomes. This non-invasive blood pressure monitoring, with longer intervals for measurements after 2 h (every $30{-}60~\mathrm{min}$ ), could be considered unacceptable in some stroke units, but is explained by national specificities. Unfortunately, such management remains common according to a recent systematic review.8 Second, the feasibility of systolic blood pressure control in the acute phase remains a challenge, and is clearly one of the main weaknesses of the study, as illustrated by the median percentage time in the assigned systolic blood pressure target range $(61\cdot0\%$ [IQR 41·776·2] in the intensive target group and $66\cdot6\%[32\cdot5-88\cdot6]$ in the standard target group). Furthermore, the speed to reach the systolic blood pressure target did not comply with the $1\textrm{h}$ requirement of the protocol (ie, $3\textrm{h}$ in the intensive target group). We also observed an unexpected difference of the primary outcome rate between centres. This difference, due to a low unexpected rate in the standard systolic blood pressure group from the two smaller centres, might have been obtained by chance. Additionally, the NIHSS was available at baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , but not immediately before randomisation (after endovascular therapy), which certainly is another limit to explain differ­ ences in outcomes. Finally, the vast majority of individuals were treated under conscious sedation (156 $[99\%]$ of 158 for the intensive target group vs 153 $[96\%]$ of 160 for the standard target group). Therefore, the results are not applicable for individuals with general anaesthesia.  

In conclusion, our results bring no evidence of any effect of an intensive strategy to lower systolic blood pressure to targets of $100–129\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ to reduce radio­ graphic intraparenchymal haemorrhage at $24-36\mathrm{~h~}$ in individuals with acute ischaemic stroke after successful endovascular therapy as compared with a standard care systolic blood pressure target.
