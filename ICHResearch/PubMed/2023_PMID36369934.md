# Abstract  

Background: Following the results of the PlaNeT-2 randomized controlled trial showing decreased morbidity and mortality in neonates transfused at a threshold of 25 versus $50\times{10}^{9}$ platelets/L, a protocol supporting restrictive platelet transfusions was established in 2019 at the Sainte-Justine Hospital neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). This study aimed to: (1) determine the impact of a local restrictive transfusion protocol on the number of platelet transfusions and donor exposure; (2) compare platelet-transfusion determinants and justifications before and after its implementation.  

Study design and methods: Prospective observational cohort chart-review study comparing all neonates consecutively admitted to the NICU during two 5-months periods: 2013 (before; $N=401$ ) versus 2021 (after; $N=402$ ). Possible determinants were assessed via logistic regressions and justifications via a questionnaire.

# 1 | INTRODUCTION  

Thrombocytopenia, defined as a platelet count $<150$ $\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ , affects up to $35\%$ of infants in neonatal intensive care units (NICU).1 The threshold for platelet transfusion is a matter of debate, with striking variation in transfusion practices among neonatologists.2 4  

A cohort study conducted in the Sainte-Justine Hospital NICU showed a wide disparity in platelet transfusion practices among local practitioners, with several factors besides platelet count influencing transfusion decisions.5 Current literature advocates for standardizing neonatal platelet transfusion practice by adopting and adhering to transfusion protocols.6 Whereas until recently equipoise existed between the clinical repercussions of liberal versus restrictive platelet transfusion thresholds in the NICU, the multicenter randomized controlled trial PlaNeT-2 demonstrated increased survival and decreased major bleeding, including intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), in preterm neonates transfused at a platelet threshold of 25 versus $50\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ .7 Following these results, the division of neonatology of the Sainte-Justine Hospital established local restrictive platelet transfusion guidelines. The primary objective of this study was to determine the impact of restrictive thresholds on the number of platelet transfusions and donor exposure in the SainteJustine Hospital NICU. The main hypothesis was that the new guidelines would reduce platelet transfusions in neonates admitted to the NICU. Secondary aims were to compare the determinants and justifications of platelet transfusions in neonatology before and after protocol implementation.  



# 2 | MATERIALS AND METHODS  



# 2.1 | Design  

All patients consecutively admitted to the SainteJustine Hospital NICU from January 18 to June 22, 2021, were enrolled into this prospective observational cohort study. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was the only exclusion criteria. The comparison cohort used was from a different project conducted in the same NICU in 2013, prior to protocol implementation. The characteristics of this comparison cohort were published in a separate article—please refer to reference5 for details. Setting. The NICU of SainteJustine Hospital in Montreal, Canada, is a tertiary care (level 3B) university-affiliated mother–child health center. Approximately 1000 patients are admitted yearly into this 65-bed NICU.  



# 2.2 | Platelet transfusions and local practice  

Decisions regarding blood product transfusions were at the discretion of the attending clinicians. All blood products were obtained from the Héma-Québec blood bank and leukoreduced by pre-storage filtration. Neonates with birth weight (BW) $\leq1200~\mathrm{g}$ were transfused with gamma irradiated products. The suggested platelet transfusion quantity was $10{-}15\mathrm{ml/kg}$ . Routine platelet transfusion duration was over 1 h. Of note, apheresis-collected platelets from a single donor can be aliquoted into 4–5 ‘pedipacks’ that may be dedicated to one infant, thus potentially reducing donor exposure in transfused infants.  



# 2.3 | Generation and application of the protocol  

The protocol was established by a group of Sainte-Justine Hospital neonatologists based on available literature and local expert's consensus. It was implemented in April 2019. Prior to this, there were no local platelet transfusion guidelines. The established platelet transfusion thresholds were:

The protocol was explained to the division of neonatology's staff, fellows and nurses. The guidelines were available on the hospital protocols secured website for reference.  

Compliance was calculated by dividing the number of platelet transfusions given within the protocol's thresholds by the total number of platelet transfusions.  



# 2.4 | Data collection  

Data abstraction and entry were performed using validated case report forms. Baseline characteristics were collected at time zero for each patient (admission day to the NICU). In transfused patients, daily monitoring of potential platelet transfusion determinants started at time zero and ended upon receiving their first transfusion. In nontransfused patients, daily monitoring ended upon patient death, NICU discharge or 44 weeks of corrected age, whichever occurred first. Validated scores to estimate the severity of illness in NICU patients were recorded upon admission, including the Clinical Risk Index for Babies (CRIB)-II score and the Score for Neonatal Acute Physiology Perinatal Extension (SNAPPE)-II.  



# 2.5 | Determinants of platelet transfusions  

A list of potential determinants of platelet transfusions in neonates was generated for the study conducted in 2013. This list was created from a literature review in collaboration with local experts in neonatology and transfusion medicine. For direct comparison, the same list was used in 2021.  



# 2.6 | Justifications of platelet transfusions  

Justifications for each first platelet transfusion were collected within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ of the transfusion using an online validated questionnaire generated by local experts' consensus (supplemental information). For direct comparison, the same questionnaire was used in 2013 and in 2021. The transfusion-requesting clinician could check one or more justifications from a list, and sort them by order of importance.  



# 2.7 | Statistical analysis  

Descriptive statistics included the number and proportion of patients and mean $\pm$ standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between transfused and non-transfused patients were performed using the Chi-square statistic or the Fisher exact probability test for categorical variables. The Student $t$ -test for continuous variables was used. Univariate analysis was performed for possible determinants using logistic regression and the odds ratio (OR) and $95\%$ confidence interval $(95\%\mathrm{CI})$ were reported. Continuous variables were analyzed using categories, which were separated according to clinically relevant thresholds or quartiles. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical software program (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM  

Corp; Released 2019. https://www.ibm.com/analytics/ spss-statistics-software) with a significance level of $5\%$ .  



# 3 | RESULTS  



# 3.1 | Baseline characteristics  

In total, 402 consecutively admitted infants were included during the 5-month study period. None were excluded. This cohort's neonates were born with a mean gestational age (GA) of $34.9~\pm4.2$ weeks and BW of $2.5\pm1.0~\mathrm{kg}$ . Table 1 summarizes the 2013 and 2021 cohorts' demographic data. The two cohorts were similar, except for statistically significant differences regarding BW (mean $2.3\pm1.0~\mathrm{kg}$ in 2013 versus $2.5\pm1.0~\mathrm{kg}$ in 2021; $p=.039,$ and proportion of bacterial infection as an admission diagnosis (74/401 $(18.5\%)$ in 2013 versus 41/402 $(11.0\%)$ in 2021; $p<.001,$ . Table 2 compares the platelet-transfused subgroups among both cohorts; no significant demographic differences were detected.  



# 3.2 | Platelet transfusion data  

At least one platelet transfusion was given to 20/401 patients $(5.0\%)$ during their NICU stay in 2021, versus $37/402$ patients $(9.2\%)$ in 2013 $(p~=.027)$ . No patient received ${\ge}4$ platelet transfusions in 2021 versus $29.7\%$ in 2013 (shown in Figure 1A). In transfused neonates, the mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ number of platelet transfusions decreased from $2.6\pm1.7$ in 2013 to $1.4\pm0.7$ in 2021 $(p=.045)$ . No patient was exposed to ${\ge}4$ donors in 2021 versus $21.6\%$ in 2013 (shown in Figure 1B). The mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ number of blood donors decreased from $2.2\pm1.2$ to $1.3\pm0.6$ ; $p=.024$ . The mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ platelet nadir within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of the first transfusion was $60\pm26\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ in 2021 versus $64\pm47\quad\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ in 2013. In 2013, 14 nonthrombocytopenic patients received a platelet transfusion, versus none in 2021.  



# 3.3 | Protocol adhesion  

There was $70\%$ concordance with the protocol's criteria. The justifications for transfusions given at platelet counts above the thresholds proposed in the protocol were, in decreasing order: necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) (3), platelet dysfunction (2), post-operative state (1), volemic resuscitation (1), therapeutic hypothermia (1) and extreme prematurity with thrombocytopenia on the first day of life (1). Multiple justifications could be given per transfusion.

# 3.4 | Determinants of platelet transfusions  

Table 3 summarizes the assessed determinants of platelet transfusions in the 2021 cohort. As observed with the 2013 cohort,5 characteristics on the first day in the NICU which predicted platelet transfusion during the NICU stay in 2021 were: $\mathrm{GA}<28$ weeks, BW $\leq1500~\mathrm{g}.$ , mechanical ventilation requirements, hemoglobin nadir $\leq150~\mathrm{g/\upmu}$ L, platelet nadir ${\leq}150\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ and lactate $>5\mathrm{mmol/L}$ . Non-invasive ventilation was associated with fewer platelet transfusions. In 2013, additional determinants on admission included CRIB-II score ${\geq}10$ , SNAPPE-II score ${\ge}24$ and seizures. Variables observed after the first day in the NICU that predicted platelet transfusion during the entire NICU stay in the 2021 cohort were: hemoglobin nadir ${\le}96~\mathrm{g/L}$ , IVH grade 3–4, NEC and ibuprofen treatment. This corresponded to the same determinants as in 2013, with the addition of IVH. Clinically evident hemorrhage, thrombocytopenia and sepsis were shown to be determinants in 2013 but not in 2021.  



# 3.5 | Justifications of platelet transfusions  

Table 4 highlights the justifications for ordering a first platelet transfusion in the 2021 cohort. The most frequently declared reasons were: low platelet counts $(89.5\%)$ , underlying disease $(47.4\%)$ , active bleeding $(31.6\%)$ and disease severity $(21.1\%)$ . Those justifications were selected in the same order of importance as in the pre-protocol cohort.  



# 4 | DISCUSSION  

This study highlighted that within the same NICU, implementing a restrictive platelet transfusion protocol decreased platelet transfusions by $46\%$ . Moreover, transfusions per patient and donor exposure were also decreased. Protocol compliance was of $70\%$ . This might be an underestimate: while ‘instability’ could justify higher transfusion thresholds according to the protocol, an objective measure of instability is lacking in premature babies. This adherence rate is consistent with other studies, which report that even when local protocols exist, up to $36\%$ of platelet transfusions in the NICU are given outside of such guidelines.10 Other potential reasons for protocol deviation might include not knowing of the protocol's existence or whereabouts, urgently transfusing a bleeding patient without preemptive platelet count verification, or exerting clinical judgment not captured by the research protocol.

Other studies have compared platelet transfusion practices and outcomes in the NICU with restrictive versus liberal transfusion protocols; their results are consistent with ours. A retrospective comparison of infants $<32$ weeks GA among two NICUs in 2007–2008 showed that less infants with thrombocytopenia were platelettransfused in the restrictive versus the liberal-transfusion NICU: $15\%$ (21/145) versus $31\%$ (41/141), respectively $(p<.001)$ .11 Another retrospective comparison of two cohorts of preterm infants with BW $<1500~\mathrm{g}$ before (2002–2004, $N=121$ ) and after (2004–2006, $N=134.$

restrictive platelet transfusion guideline implementation in the same NICU reported a $40\%$ decrease in platelet transfusions following restrictive guidelines.12 More recently, a study compared the number of non-indicated platelet transfusions before $(N~=~1388)$ and after $(N=678)$ ) implementation of a restrictive platelet transfusion guideline in the Boston Children's Hospital NICU in January 2019.13 The rate of non-indicated platelet transfusions according to the restrictive guideline decreased from $12.5\%$ to $2.9\%$ . In all three studies, the rates of adverse outcomes such as NEC, IVH, other major bleeding and mortality remained stable. Taken together, these studies support that implementing restrictive neonatal platelet transfusion guidelines significantly reduces potentially harmful platelet transfusions in the NICU, without increasing adverse outcomes.  

The two cohorts compared in this study had similar baseline characteristics. An exception was the lower rate of bacterial infection upon admission in 2021 versus in 2013. This could influence platelet transfusion rates given the association between bacterial infection and thrombocytopenia in neonates. Protocol implementation did not impact characteristics on the first day in the NICU that predicted platelet transfusion. In our study, these were strongly associated with a lower GA and BW, as well as mechanical ventilation requirements, lower hemoglobin and lower platelet counts on admission. These are logical and consistent with other reports.2,14–17 In 2021, higher  

CRIB-II and SNAPPE-II scores on admission trended toward increased platelet transfusions. However, unlike in 2013, statistical significance was not reached for CRIBII score ${\geq}10$ and not demonstrated for SNAPPE-II score ${\ge}24$ (OR non-estimable, p-value non-calculable). Seizures on admission were additionally associated with a higher risk of transfusion in 2013 but were less frequently diagnosed in 2021 and not statistically significant. During the NICU stay, a lower hemoglobin, NEC and ibuprofen treatment were associated with a higher risk of platelet transfusion both before and after protocol implementation. These findings are corroborated in the literature.2–4 Grade $3{-}4\mathrm{IVH}$ was a new determinant following protocol implementation; the protocol's specification of severe IVH for a higher transfusion threshold of $100\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ platelets is a possible explanation. On the other hand, clinically evident hemorrhage and sepsis were less frequent diagnoses in 2021 and association with platelet transfusions was not demonstrated. All platelettransfused patients in 2021 had a platelet nadir $\leq150$ $\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ making the OR not calculable.  

Platelet transfusions in neonates harbor significant risks including increased sepsis, NEC and aregenerative thrombocytopenia,10,16,18 as well as IVH and mortality.7,18,19 Furthermore, the risk of acute febrile, allergic and hypotensive reactions with platelets is nearly threefold compared to red blood cells, and neonates experience a disproportionately high number of transfusion adverse events than other pediatric and adult populations.20 Decreasing unnecessary platelet transfusions promote better patient outcomes, lowers costs and optimizes blood product resources.6  

This study was unicentric, limiting external validity. Physicians were aware that their transfusion habits were monitored throughout the research project, potentially affecting their practice. The stated justifications for transfusions might have been influenced by the multiplechoice questionnaire. This study's scope did not allow to examine the association of reduced platelet transfusion rates with neonatal outcomes such as bleeding or IVH progression.  

The study's strengths include its prospective design (limiting information bias) and universal inclusion (limiting selection bias and improving generalizability). The setting and cohorts were highly comparable between the two periods, as the study was performed in the same NICU within the same timeframe in two different years, using identical case report forms and questionnaires. Moreover, this NICU is a typical North-American multidisciplinary unit, treating preterm and term surgical and medical patients, further enhancing generalizability.  

These results highlight the positive impact of an evidence-based local restrictive protocol on reducing platelet transfusions in the NICU. They contribute to the growing body of evidence emphasizing that adherence to evidence-based guidelines decreases unnecessary and potentially harmful platelet transfusions. Additional education to reinforce guideline adherence is required to further decrease avoidable transfusions in the NICU. Furthermore, the local guidelines were created based on the available evidence at the time as well as expert opinion and included thresholds of $50\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ for certain subgroups. Consideration will be given to further restrict these guidelines following the analysis that showed benefit from a $25\times10^{9}/\mathrm{L}$ prophylactic platelet count threshold across all GA irrespective of predicted baseline risk of major bleeding and/or death in preterm neonates.21 Future studies should focus on evaluating the clinical impact of decreasing transfusions.
