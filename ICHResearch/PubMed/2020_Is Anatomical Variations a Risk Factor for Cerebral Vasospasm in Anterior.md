# Is Anatomical Variations a Risk Factor for Cerebral Vasospasm in Anterior Communicating Complex Aneurysms Rupture?  

Alice Jacquens, MD\*; Eimad Shotar, MD\*; Camille Bombled, MD; Benjamin Glémain, MD; Nader-Antoine Sourour, MD; Aurélien Nouet, MD; Kevin Premat, MD; Stephanie Lenck, MD; Vincent Degos, MD, PhD\*; Frédéric Clarençon, MD, PhD\*  

Background and Purpose—One-third of ruptured aneurysms are located on the anterior communicating complex with high prevalence of anatomic variations of this arterial segment. In this study, we hypothesized that anatomic variations of the anterior communicating complex increase the risk of angiographic vasospasm.  

Methods—Retrospective study of prospectively collected data from a monocentric subarachnoid hemorrhage cohort of patients admitted to neurointensive care between 2002 and 2018. Univariate followed by multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with angiographic vasospasm.  

Results—One thousand three hundred seventy-four patients with aneurismal subarachnoid hemorrhage were admitted to our institution; $29.8\%$ $\scriptstyle(\mathrm{n}{=}410$ ) were related to an anterior communicating complex aneurysm rupture; $9.2\%$ $\scriptstyle({\mathrm{n}}=38$ ) of them showed an anterior communicating artery variation. Angiographic vasospasm was diagnosed in $55.6\%$ of this subgroup (vs $28.1\%$ , $P{=}0.003.$ ). In the multivariate analysis, external ventricular drain (2.2 [1.32–3.65], $P{=}0.003$ ) and anterior communicating artery variation (2.40 [1.2–4.9], $P{=}0.04\$ ) were independently and significantly associated with angiographic vasospasm, while age above 60 years (0.3 [0.2–0.7]; $P{=}0.002$ ) was a protective factor. However, anterior communicating artery variation was not statistically associated with ischemic vasospasm or poor neurological outcome after anterior communicating artery aneurysm rupture.  

Conclusions—Anatomic variation of anterior communicating artery could be a new biomarker to identify patients at risk to develop angiographic vasospasm post-subarachnoid hemorrhage. External validation cohorts are necessary to confirm these results. (Stroke. 2020;51:998-1001. DOI: 10.1161/STROKEAHA.119.026661.)  

Key Words: anatomic variation ◼ aneurysm ◼ angiography $\sqsubset$ multivariate analysis ◼ prevalence ubarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is related to arterial aneurysm rupture in $85\%$ of cases.1 Aneurismal SAH (aSAH) is a potentially devastating condition, associated with a global mortality rate of around $35\%$ .2 Angiographic vasospasm is one of the major risk factors of post-aSAH neurological complications. These complications most often occur between the fourth and 14th day after aneurysm rupture and have the potential to cause delayed cerebral ischemia.3  

The anterior communicating artery complex (AcomC), which is composed of the A1-A2 junction of the anterior cerebral arteries (ACAs) and anterior communicating artery, is a common site of aneurysm formation.4 AcomC aneurysms are often related to AcomC anatomic variations. In particular, hypoplasia or agenesis of the precommunicating A1 segment of the ACA is a known risk factor of AcomC aneurysm formation5–7 (Figure  1). Some studies have described the anatomic variations of the AcomC and their role in the cerebral hemodynamic.8,9  

We hypothesized that AcomC variations, frequently associated with AcomC aneurysms, may alter perfusion pressure and, thus, increase the risk of angiographic vasospasm after SAH. The primary aim of this study was to determine if the anterior communicating artery (AcomA) and A1 ACA variations influence the outcome after AcomC aneurysm rupture.

# Patients  

All adult patients consecutively admitted for a SAH by rupture of an AcomC aneurysm within the first 48 hours were included. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Assistance Publique Hôpitaux de Paris in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and a subgroup of patients were included in a previous study registered on URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov. Unique identifier: NCT01357057.  

# Angiographic Data  

Variations of the AcomC were analyzed on admission digital subtraction angiography in antero-posterior and lateral projections. A1 ACA hypoplasia was defined as a vessel $\le1\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter.7,12 AcomA hypoplasia was defined as a vessel present but barely visible and agenesis as the complete absence of a vascular segment. Hypoplasia or agenesis of the AcomA (∆AcomA) and A1 ACA (∆A1) segments were recorded by an experienced neuroradiologist (Dr Clarençon). To verify the reproducibility of the quotation, 192 cases (half of the data) were analyzed at random by a second senior neuroradiologist (Dr Shotar).  

A digital subtraction angiography was performed when significant (Materials and Methods I in the online-only Data Supplement) vasospasm was suspected clinically, on trans-cranial Doppler sonography and on computed tomography angiography-computed tomography perfusion. Angiographic vasospasm and its localization were recorded prospectively on digital subtraction angiography as any significant arterial caliber reduction on angiography leading to intraarterial chemo-dilation (Materials and Methods II in the online-only Data Supplement).  

# Intensive Care Unit Management  

All patients with SAH were followed in neurointensive care unit and managed according to a standardized medical protocol.10  

# Outcome  

The primary outcome was occurrence of angiographic vasospasm anywhere in the brain arteries. The secondary outcome criteria were ischemic vasospasm in the ACA territory, duration of stay in the intensive care unit, Glasgow Outcome Scale extended, and modified Rankin Scale at discharge, at 6 months, and 1 year. Ischemic vasospasm was defined by cerebral infarction in the territory of the vasospasm territory identified on computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging after excluding procedure-related infarction. The long-term outcome was evaluated by a physical examination or a phone interview.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata software (StataCorp, College Station, TX).  

Discrete variables were compared with a $\chi^{2}$ test and continuous variables with a Student $t$ test. All statistical tests were 2-tailed, and statistical significance was considered at $P{<}0.05$ . To identify factors associated with angiographic vasospasm, univariate followed by stepwise multivariate logistic regression models were constructed. A variable was included in multivariate logistic regression if it was positively or negatively associated with vasospasm in univariate analysis with a $P$ threshold $<0.1$ . Agreement between the 2 neuroradiologists was obtained for a subset of the angiographic data with the Cohen’s Kappa $(\upkappa)$ test.13  

# Results  

# Patients’ Characteristics  

Between 2002 and 2018, 1374 patients with aSAH were admitted to our institution (Figure 2). Four hundred and ten were related to an AcomC aneurysm rupture (Table I in the online-only Data Supplement).  

# AcomA and A1 Segment Variations and Outcome  

Interobserver correlation between the 2 readers was excellent, with a κ test of 0.85.  

Patients with ∆AcomA demonstrated increased frequency of angiographic vasospasm $(55.6\%$ versus $28.1\%$ ; $P{=}0.003$ ), whereas patients with ∆A1 did not (28.1 versus $32.6\%$ ; $P{=}0.06,$ ). None of these subgroups demonstrated statistically significant difference in terms of outcome (Table $\mathrm{II}$ in the online-only Data Supplement). Ischemic vasospasm was present in 27 patients $(6.6\%)$ with a median ischemic volume of $69.5~\mathrm{mL}$ (44–153.3 mL).

# Discussion  

AcomA hypoplasia or agenesis was associated with increased incidence of angiographic vasospasm. However, AcomC anatomic variations were not associated with increased incidence of ischemic vasospasm or poor clinical outcome in this study.  

This is the first study to test the hypothesis that ACAs A1 segment and AcomA variations could alter tolerance to vasospasm by limiting possibilities for collateral flow in case of vessel narrowing. A1 dominance of ACA as a risk factor for AcomC aneurysm formation has been hypothesized to be related to wall shear stress, A1-A2 angulation, and flow patterns related to arterial geometry.14 Moreover, A1 dominance has been suggested to increase risk of AcomA aneurysm rupture and aneurysm recurrence after endovascular treatment.15 A possible explanation is that this anatomic condition modifies local rheological and flow conditions, leading to increased vessel susceptibility to vasospasm; or patients with ∆AcomA indeed have diminished clinical tolerance to vasospasm, leading to more frequent referral to angiography and thus a higher but artificial reported frequency of vasospasm through referral bias.  

The main limitations of this study are the retrospective nature of the analysis and the monocentric design. The analysis was nevertheless conducted on prospectively collected data, limiting memorization bias. A second limitation is the small sample size of both ∆AcomA and delayed cerebral ischemia. Moreover, anatomic variations may be different depending on the ethnicity of the patients. This single-center study might, therefore, be underpowered to identify subtle effects of anatomic variations on incidence of ischemic vasospasm.  

# Conclusions  

Hypoplasia or agenesis of the AcomA were associated with increased incidence of angiographic vasospasm. It could be a new biomarker to identify patients at risk to develop  

angiographic vasospasm post-SAH. External validation studies are necessary to confirm these results.
