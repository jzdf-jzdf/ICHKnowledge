# Clinical outcomes of very elderly patients treated with ultrasound‑assisted catheter‑directed thrombolysis for pulmonary embolism: a systematic review

Jathniel Panneflek2 · Mauricio Vazquez‑Guajardo1   
Accepted: 16 February 2021 / Published online: 4 March 2021

# Abstract  

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a significant cause of death in the very elderly $(\geq75$ years) population. Ultrasound-assisted catheter-directed thrombolysis (USCDT) emerges to improve thrombolysis safety and efficacy. However, outcomes in very elderly patients are unknown, as randomized controlled trials exclude this population. Recently, we demonstrated acute kidney injury (AKI) and ischemic hepatitis in an octogenarian intermediate-risk PE patient treated with USCDT. Considering the lack of evidence, we undertook a systematic review to evaluate the clinical outcomes in very elderly PE patients treated with USCDT. We searched for very elderly PE patients treated with USCDT from 2008 to 2019. Additionally, we conducted another systematic review without age restriction to update previous evidence and compare both populations. We also did an exploratory analysis to determine if thrombolysis was followed based on current guidelines or impending clinical deterioration factors. We identified 18 very elderly patients (age 79.2, 75–86), mostly female and with intermediaterisk PE. We found an intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and a right pulmonary artery rupture. Additionally, two significant bleedings complicated with transient AKI, and one case of AKI and ischemic hepatic injury. The patients who survived all had clinical and echocardiographic in-hospital improvement. Despite low rt-PA doses, ICH and major bleeding remain as feared complications. Thrombolysis decision was driven by impending clinical deterioration factors instead of international guideline recommendations. Our data do not suggest prohibitive risk associated with USCDT in very elderly intermediate and high-risk PE patients. Despite long-term infusions and right ventricular dysfunction, AKI and ischemic hepatic injury were infrequent.

# Introduction  

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is the third cause of cardiovascular mortality after ST-elevation myocardial infarction and stroke [1]. Also, PE remains a significant cause of death in special populations (pregnancy, cancer, trauma) [1–4]. Another growing community with high incidence and mortality in the elderly and very elderly $(>75$ years) PE patients [5]. Although thrombolysis plus anticoagulation, in intermediate and high-risk PE, improves outcome and mortality, the evidence in the very elderly population is scarce [6–8]. Additionally, randomized controlled trials systematically exclude elderly patients [9] due to frailty [10], multiple comorbidities, and a higher risk of intracranial or significant bleeding complications [11]. Currently, the interventional approach called the EkoSonic ultrasound accelerated thrombolysis system (EKOS) is a new alternative for intermediate and high-risk PE patients to improve thrombolysis safety. The EKOS system combines high-frequency, low power ultrasound waves, and lower thrombolytic doses inducing fibrin structure changes, increased thrombus permeability, and possibly fewer bleeding complications [12]. Considering that organic dysfunction is a consistent clinical element in elderly and very elderly patients, a potential disadvantage is the long-term infusions $(\sim24\mathrm{~h~})$ , inducing sustained hypoperfusion states in the setting of severe right ventricular dysfunction (RVD). Recently, we demonstrated acute kidney injury (AKI) and ischemic hepatitis, possibly secondary to long-term infusion, in an octogenarian intermediate-risk PE treated with ultrasound-assisted catheter-directed thrombolysis (USCDT) [13]. Although USCDT shows safety and efficacy in the younger population [14–17], elderly and very elderly patients are underrepresented [6–8]. Considering our previous experience and the lack of evidence, we undertook a systematic review to evaluate the clinical outcomes in very elderly intermediate and high-risk PE patients treated with USCDT.  



# Methods  

We searched for systematic reviews, cohorts, case series, case reports, editor letters, and abstracts related to very elderly PE patients treated with USCDT according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [18] and prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD42020152632). The objective was to assess the clinical outcomes of USCDT in very elderly PE patients. Additionally, we analyzed the clinical presentation, risk stratification, and diagnostic approach. We included patients with confirmed PE by ventilation-perfusion lung scan, computed tomography pulmonary angiogram, or pulmonary angiography [19]. The outcomes of interest were the safety and effectiveness of USCDT. We performed a systematic review from 2008 to 2019 through PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and OpenGrey and provided the complete search strategies in the e-Appendix 1. We used the snowballing method [20], a manual search to avoid lost reports, controlled vocabulary, and no language restriction. We contacted authors to obtain the information of very elderly patients enrolled in previous trials. Additionally, we conducted a systematic review without age restriction to update the latest systematic review [15] and analyzed and compared it with very elderly patients.  

We identified potentially eligible studies by examining titles and abstracts. We obtained full papers to assess eligibility criteria before the critical appraisal and extracted cases that met the eligibility criteria. All investigators analyzed data extraction of every case report to improve quality data extraction. The corresponding author was a cardiologist with expertise in the field (C.JS.). A group discussion was conducted daily to assess all the information extracted from the cases included in a database. Disagreements were solved posteriorly by consensus. For the analysis, the primary safety outcomes were in-hospital mortality, major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), minor bleeding, and procedural complications. The primary effectiveness outcomes were clinical, thrombus burden, and right ventricular to the left ventricular (RV/LV) ratio improvement. We defined major bleeding according to the International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria [21]. Other definitions, including high-risk PE and successful thrombolysis, are available in the e-Appendix 2.  

Based on our experience [1, 22, 23] and previous evidence [24, 25], we analyzed whether thrombolysis indication was based on current recommendations (persistent hypotension or shock) [19, 26, 27] or whether physicians in charge used other impending clinical deterioration factors historically related with poor outcome as persistent chest pain [24], syncope [28], hypoxemia $<90\%$ [26], heart rate $\geq120/\mathrm{min}$ [26], respiratory rate $>20$ breaths/min [26], right bundle branch block [27], atrial fibrillation [27], saddle pulmonary embolus [29], B-type natriuretic peptide $\mathrm{(BNP)}{>}600~\mathrm{pg/mL}$ [19], N-terminal-pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) $\mathrm{>600pg/mL}$ [19], standard cardiac troponin ${>}0.05~\upmu{\mathrm{g/mL}}$ , High sensitivity cardiac troponin I $(\mathrm{hs-cTnI})>99\%$ percentile upper reference limit, right ventricular hypokinesis, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) $<13~\mathrm{mm}$ [19, 26, 27], and treatment failure (clinical worsening and or increasing biomarkers measurements despite adequate anticoagulation) [26].  

We used summary statistics for continuous and categorical variables according to their types and distributions. We report the frequency and percentage $(\mathtt{n}>20)$ for categorical variables and continuous variables, median and standard deviation. No hypothesis testing was performed, given the quality of the information obtained.  



# Results  

We carried out the systematic review from August 30 to September 30, 2019. Figure 1 shows the flowchart, including the four phases of PRISMA, and obtained, eliminated, and excluded duplicated papers. In the identification phase, we collected 1,498 papers from PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Opengrey. We carefully reviewed the full text for eligibility criteria and selected a total of 55 papers for the quality assessment. Finally, we extracted the data for this review from 15 studies [12, 13, 30–42].

We identified 18 very elderly patients (age 79.2, 75–86), and most were female (11 patients). Two patients had high-risk and 16 intermediate-risk PE; of them, two were stratified as high-risk PE during in-hospital followup. Table 1 shows recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) doses $(10{-}43~\mathrm{mg})$ ), infusion times $(12-48\mathrm{~h~})$ , and outcomes. The most prevalent comorbidities were hypertension, cancer, and previous venous thromboembolism. The clinical presentation was driven by dyspnea and tachycardia alone or associated with chest pain and clinical stability (e-Appendix 2). All patients had pulmonary hypertension and RVD characterized by an RV/LV ratio $\geq0.9$ , right ventricular hypokinesis, and McConnell sign, and four had in-transit thrombi. Most patients were diagnosed based on computed tomography pulmonary angiogram and had extensive bilateral thrombosis. The biomarkers measurements (troponin, BNP, D-dimer) were low (e-Appendix 2), and the primary venous access was femoral or jugular, and most had bilateral catheter placement. Adjunctive treatment was unfractionated heparin, and rt-PA was the thrombolytic agent.  

We identified two serious adverse events related to mortality; an ICH associated with thrombolysis and a right pulmonary artery rupture secondary to the procedure (Table 1). Additionally, we found two significant bleedings related to venous puncture complicated with transient AKI. We identified a single case of AKI and ischemic hepatic injury [13], possibly secondary to long-term infusion and a systemic hypoperfusion state. Of the patients who survived, all had echocardiography and in-hospital clinical improvement.

We identified five new studies [8, 43–46] to update the latest systematic review [15]. We obtained 31 studies [6–8, 30, 43–69], including $1539\mathrm{PE}$ patients treated with USCDT (Table 2). Most of the patients were $<60$ years-old with the same proportion for the male and female genders. We identified at least five very elderly patients, and we contacted the authors to obtain more clinical information without response. A minority of patients had high-risk PE $(12.5\%)$ , being underrepresented in all the primary studies. Table 2 shows infusion times and rt-PA doses. When we excluded OPTALYSE patients, the mean infusion time was one-hour longer $(18.3\mathrm{~h})$ . We identified a low rate of ICH and major bleeding.  

Two patients had high-risk in the exploratory analysis, and 16 had intermediate-risk PE, while two evolved to highrisk despite adequate anticoagulation. Upon admission, we identified a wide range of systolic blood pressure (Table 3), and the decision to USCDT was based on several impending clinical deterioration factors (RVD, anticoagulation failure, severe hypoxemia, increased biomarkers, saddle PE and intransit thrombus) in intermediate-risk PE patients (Table 3). In no case, the use of USCDT was decided following international recommendations [19, 26, 27] (systolic blood pressure ${<}90\mathrm{mmHg}$ ) or high bleeding risk. Most patients treated with USCDT, including high-risk PE patients, had clinical and echocardiographic improvement (Table 3).  



# Discussion  

Our systematic review had several relevant findings; first, overall, these data do not suggest prohibitive risk associated with USCDT in very elderly intermediate and high-risk PE patients. Second, despite long-term infusions and RVD, AKI and ischemic hepatic injury were infrequent. Third, despite low rt-PA doses, ICH and other significant bleedings related to the procedure emerge as the most feared complications. Finally, the exploratory analysis suggests that the thrombolysis decision was driven by the combination of several impending clinical deterioration factors instead of the international guideline recommendations [19, 26, 27].  

Catheter-directed reperfusion techniques (local thrombolysis, mechanical embolectomy, and pharmaco-mechanical) receive increasing attention for use in PE patients [70]. These advanced therapies act primarily by reducing obstruction, restoring pulmonary circulation, and improving RVD through a low-dose of a thrombolytic agent [70]. Recently, the EKOS system approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2014 is an emerging and potentially promising approach [71]. The EKOS system consists of 3 components: a drug delivery catheter, a removable US device, and the control unit. The thrombolysis mechanisms are the combination of high-frequency, low power ultrasound waves, and lower thrombolytic doses inducing fibrin structure changes [71], reducing thrombus resistance, thrombolysis failure, and bleeding complications. The procedure is performed at the cardiac catheterization laboratory with continuous hemodynamic and ECG monitoring. Venous access is obtained at the common femoral vein, usually with a 6F introducer sheath (unilateral device placement) or a 10F double-lumen introducer sheath (for bilateral device insertion). A standard diagnostic angiographic catheter is used to cross the embolic occlusion. With the guidewire tip in a safe position, the angiographic catheter is exchanged for the drug delivery catheter with the US device inserted. A continuous infusion of rt-PA at $1\mathrm{mg/h}$ per catheter for $12–24\mathrm{h}$ [6], or 2, 4, or $^{8\mathrm{~h~}}$ [8].  

Recently, we performed a successful USCDT with rt-PA low-dose $(24\mathrm{mg)}$ in long-term infusion $(24\mathrm{~h})$ in borderline blood pressure, large bilateral thrombus, and RVD octogenarian PE patient. Unexpectedly, despite initial clinical and RVD improvement, we identified AKI (creatinine $4.3~\mathrm{mg/}$ dL) and ischemic hepatitis (AST 5812 U/L, ALT 3084 U/L), possibly secondary to the long-term hypoperfusion state [13]. The combination of aging, RVD, and USCDT using long-term infusion could be an excellent model for acute liver and kidney injury. AKI related to clinical instability, hypoxia, and hypoperfusion is highly associated with mortality and in-hospital adverse events in elderly PE patients [72]. Additionally, ischemic hepatitis is likely to be more common in older high-risk PE patients [73].  

This review provided remarkable information on the outcomes in very elderly patients treated with USCDT. This therapeutic approach is more frequent in female intermediate-risk PE patients (Table 1), and despite the high-risk, AKI, and ischemic hepatitis were infrequent compared with procedure-related significant bleedings. The hemorrhagic complications related to AKI resulted from the procedure, and one patient died from a ruptured pulmonary artery. Therefore, it is essential to keep in mind that USCDT should be used in expert centers to avoid related complications. Despite the lower doses of rt-PA, ICH remains the most feared complication in the very elderly population (Table 1). However, considering previous evidence, the incidence of bleeding complications was similar to non-very elderly PE patients [74]. Additionally, USCDT is high operator dependence, and it is not exempt from catheter-related complications or malfunctioning [70]. Finally, although patients who survived had clinical and echocardiographic improvement, we suggest caution in this population considering the small sample.  

Our findings on the non-very elderly systematic review confirmed the results of previous evidence [17, 75, 76], including the latest systematic review [15], which demonstrated similar safety. This systematic review update includes the OPTALYSE results [8] and shows that patients receiving USCDT in long-term infusions are young with intermediate-risk PE. The low incidence of major bleeding complications is likely attributed to the patient’s age and the total dose of rt-PA (Table 2). However, despite this favorable combination, major bleedings, including ICH, remain possible severe complications, especially in older patients with endothelial dysfunction, even in the absence of traditional risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, obesity, hypercholesterolemia, and smoking [77] (Table 2). A cohort study recently demonstrated that patients who received short-term $(4\mathrm{~h~})$ compared to longterm infusions had a shorter in-hospital stay and a trend towards a lower incidence of bleeding complications [78]. It seems that short-term infusions and technological development may substantially decrease bleeding and USCDT related complications [15].

To better understand the differences between very elderly and non-very elderly patients, we compared bleeding complications, rt-PA doses, and infusion times of all PE patients treated with USCDT (Table 4). Although ICH incidence is low, regardless of age and the use of lower doses of rt-PA, it is present in all age groups, and we observed a trend towards a higher incidence in elderly patients. Also, we identified that there seems to be a relation between prolonged infusions and a higher rate of ICH and significant bleedings, suggesting that long-term infusions are an excellent model for hemorrhagic complications. These findings also suggest the need to explore short-term infusions (2 or $^{4\textrm{h}}$ ), as indicated by the OPTALYSE study results [8].  

A relevant finding in the exploratory analysis was that thrombolysis decision-making was driven by the combination of several impending clinical deterioration factors, historically related to poor outcomes, instead of international recommendations [19, 26, 27] (Table 3). To date, the incidence and risk factors of early clinical instability in patients admitted with intermediate-risk PE are very limited [79]. The USCDT decision was based on guideline recommendations in only two intermediate-risk very elderly PE patients and based on several impending clinical deterioration factors in the rest of PE patients (Table 3). These findings suggest the need for further research to understand better how clinical factors might predict clinical instability over time [24]. Recently, in a post-hoc analysis of the PEITHO trial [28], systolic blood pressure $(110\mathrm{mmHg}$ ), respiratory rate $(>20~\mathrm{bpm})$ , and chronic heart failure had a significantly higher risk on the PE-focused clinical outcome. Additionally, previous evidence suggests that the systolic blood pressure threshold of $\leq110{-}120\mathrm{mmHg}$ seems to have the same impact on mortality as the threshold of $\leq90\mathrm{mmHg}$ [28, 80, 81]. Finally, these findings serve as an indication to reconsider and perhaps redefine current PE risk categories, considering the presence, persistence, and worsening of impending clinical deterioration factors over time without improvement [24].

This study’s limitations were as follows: our results confirm that the evidence for USCDT in very elderly patients is based on a summary of case reports. A potential loss of case reports from search engines is possible. Even though we contacted the authors of extensive USCDT studies, we could not obtain very elderly patients’ information. Therefore, the number of reviewed patients was very low, and we cannot give a definitive recommendation regarding these patients’ treatment. Finally, there is a trend to not report patients with poor in-hospital outcomes or serious adverse events.  

In conclusion, overall, these data do not suggest prohibitive risk associated with USCDT in very elderly PE patients. Management of intermediate and high-risk PE in this population should be individualized to each patient and reserved for centers with high expertise. In the data presented, AKI and ischemic hepatitis were infrequent complications. Finally, future efforts should systematically collect and report data in very elderly PE patients to improve evidencebased clinical practice.
