# ABSTR ACT  

# BACKGROUND  

Patients receiving standard treatment for chronic subdural hematoma have a high risk of treatment failure. The effect of adjunctive middle meningeal artery embolization on the risk of treatment failure in this population remains unknown.  

# METHODS  

We randomly assigned patients with symptomatic chronic subdural hematoma to undergo middle meningeal artery embolization as an adjunct to standard treatment (embolization group) or to receive standard treatment alone (control group). Either surgical or nonsurgical standard treatment had been chosen for each patient before randomization. The primary efficacy outcome was a composite of the following events: recurrent or residual chronic subdural hematoma (measuring ${>}10~\mathrm{mm}$ ) at 180 days; reoperation or surgical rescue within 180 days; or major disabling stroke, myocardial infarction, or death from neurologic causes within 180 days. The primary safety outcome was a composite of major disabling stroke or death from any cause within 30 days.  

# RESULTS  

Among 310 enrolled patients, 149 were randomly assigned to the embolization group and 161 to the control group; 189 patients were to receive surgical standard treatment and 121 nonsurgical standard treatment. The mean age of the patients was 73 years, and $70\%$ were men. In the primary efficacy analysis, a primaryoutcome event occurred in 19 of 120 patients $(16\%)$ in the embolization group, as compared with 47 of 129 patients $(36\%)$ in the control group (odds ratio, 0.36; $95\%$ confidence interval, 0.20 to 0.66; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.001$ ). In the primary safety analysis, 4 of 144 patients $(3\%)$ in the embolization group and 5 of 166 patients $(3\%)$ in the control group either had a major disabling stroke or died within 30 days. Through 180 days, 12 patients $(8\%)$ in the embolization group and 9 patients $(5\%)$ in the control group had died, with death from neurologic causes occurring in 1 patient $(1\%)$ in the embolization group and in 3 patients $(2\%)$ in the control group.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

Among patients with symptomatic chronic subdural hematoma, adjunctive middle meningeal artery embolization resulted in a lower risk of treatment failure than standard treatment alone, without resulting in an increased incidence of disabling stroke or death in the short term. Further study of longer-term safety outcomes is warranted. (Funded by Balt USA; STEM ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT04410146.)

hronic subdural hematoma is a common neurosurgical disease. The condition is most prevalent among older patients, and the incidence continues to increase.1,2 Standard treatment can consist of either surgical evacuation or nonsurgical management.3-9 Although no evidence-based guidelines regarding the treatment of chronic subdural hematoma are widely accepted, surgical evacuation is generally favored for larger and more symptomatic collections, whereas nonsurgical management is favored for smaller collections, particularly in patients with multiple coexisting conditions or in patients receiving anticoagulant therapy.10,11 Both treatment strategies are associated with a relatively high risk of treatment failure (ranging from 5 to $50\%$ ) as indicated by the persistence, progression, or recurrence of chronic subdural hematoma, which often leads to reoperation or surgical rescue, as well as by complications such as stroke, myocardial infarction, or death, which are related to coexisting conditions and the withholding of anticoagulant therapy.2,12-21  

It has been hypothesized that chronic subdural hematoma is primarily caused by inflammation of the meninges, which stimulates neovascular ingrowth that is susceptible to bleeding, with the blood leaking into the subdural space. Such leakage may incite a positive-feedback cycle of further inflammation, neovascular ingrowth, and bleeding, in which blood products may ultimately accumulate faster than the vasculature in the central nervous system can absorb them.22,23 Embolization of the middle meningeal artery with liquid agents is an endovascular procedure that can occlude the pathologic meningeal neovasculature,24,25 shifting the balance in favor of physiologic reabsorption of the collection.24,26  

The Squid Trial for the Embolization of the Middle Meningeal Artery for the Treatment of Chronic Subdural Hematoma (STEM) investigated the effect of adjunctive middle meningeal artery embolization with Squid (Balt USA), a nonadhesive liquid embolic agent, on the risk of treatment failure among patients receiving either surgical or nonsurgical standard treatment for chronic subdural hematoma.  

# Methods  

# Trial Design and Oversight  

STEM was a prospective, international, multicenter, open-label, interventional, randomized, controlled trial involving patients with symptomatic chronic subdural hematoma who were to receive either surgical or nonsurgical standard treatment. Complete details regarding the trial design and statistical analysis are provided in the protocol, available with the full text of this article at NEJM.org. The trial was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and with the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The trial protocol was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each site. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients. The trial was performed under an investigational device exemption (G190038).  

All potential clinical primary-outcome events were reviewed and adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee. An independent data and safety monitoring board received data from the clinical events committee and provided safety oversight. All imaging studies were adjudicated by an independent core laboratory (Eppdata). Data were entered by the participating sites with the use of an electronic data-capture system (iMednet Solutions). The trial sponsor (Balt USA) manufactured the injected agent, funded and conducted all operational aspects of the trial, and assisted with the statistical analysis and preparation of the data for publication. The principal investigators (the first and last authors) designed the trial and wrote the first draft of the manuscript without input from the sponsor. All the authors vouch for the accuracy and completeness of the data and for the fidelity of the trial to the protocol.

# Treatment  

Either surgical or nonsurgical standard treatment was chosen for each patient before randomization, with the decision based solely on the surgeon’s judgment. After stratification according to the standard treatment strategy, patients were given the opportunity to participate in the trial. Enrolled patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to undergo middle meningeal artery embolization as an adjunct to standard treatment (embolization group) or to receive standard treatment alone (control group). Overall, there were four treatment strategies: embolization plus surgical standard treatment, embolization plus nonsurgical standard treatment, surgical standard treatment alone, and nonsurgical standard treatment alone (Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Appendix, available at NEJM.org).  

Among the patients in the embolization group who were to receive surgical standard treatment, middle meningeal artery embolization was performed before surgical evacuation. Patients in the embolization group underwent the embolization procedure within 24 hours after randomization. Middle meningeal artery embolization with the injected agent Squid was performed with the use of standard transarterial techniques under the guidance of digital subtraction angiography (Fig. S2 and Section S2). Squid is a nonadhesive liquid embolic agent that is composed of ethylene vinyl alcohol copolymer dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide with suspended micronized tantalum powder added for radiopacity (Section S3). Among the patients who were to receive surgical standard treatment, either burr-hole evacuation or subdural evacuating port system (SEPS) drainage was performed on the target side or sides.  

# Trial Outcomes  

The primary efficacy outcome was a composite of the following imaging or clinical events:  

recurrent or residual chronic subdural hematoma on the target side measuring greater than $10~\mathrm{mm}$ at 180 days after the intervention; reoperation or surgical rescue within 180 days after the intervention, with the indication for surgery based on the surgeon’s discretion (not specified in the protocol); or major disabling stroke, myocardial infarction, or death from neurologic causes within 180 days after the intervention. The primary safety outcome was a composite of major disabling stroke or death from any cause within 30 days after the intervention. Prespecified secondary outcomes included the score on the modified Rankin scale at 180 days after the intervention, as well as any adverse events or serious adverse events related to the investigational device or procedure.  

# Adjudication and Blinding Procedures  

On-site external monitors reviewed medical records to verify that the source data supported the entries made in the electronic data-capture system at each site. There was no blinding procedure for the monitors; they had no role in the primary-outcome determinations except for verifying that the reoperations and surgical rescue procedures were performed on the target side. Adjudication of potential imaging primary-outcome events was conducted by the independent core laboratory and could not be performed in a blinded manner, given the radiopacity of the injected agent. Adjudication of potential clinical primary-outcome events, neurologic serious adverse events, and deaths was performed by the clinical events committee.  

# Statistical Analysis  

We calculated that a sample of 286 patients (143 evaluated in each trial group) would provide the trial with $90\%$ power to show superiority in the embolization group over the control group with respect to the primary efficacy outcome, at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. The calculation was based on the assumption of a primary-outcome event occurring in $19\%$ of the patients in the embolization group and in $36\%$ of the patients in the control group, with randomization performed in a 1:1 ratio. In the primary efficacy analysis, a Cochran– Mantel–Haenszel test was used to compare the embolization group with the control group, with stratification according to surgical or nonsurgical standard treatment. Multiple imputation was used to address data that were assumed to be missing at random; additional tipping-point sensitivity analyses were prespecified to address data that were potentially not missing at random.28 Analyses of the primary efficacy outcome were performed with and without imputation for missing data in the intention-to-treat population (defined as all patients who had provided consent and undergone randomization) and in the modified intentionto-treat population (defined as all patients who had provided consent and undergone randomization and in whom the assigned intervention had been started). The results from the intention-to-treat analyses are reported here. Analysis of the primary efficacy outcome according to the standard treatment strategy was not prespecified as part of a hierarchical statistical analysis plan.  

The primary safety analysis was performed in the safety population, in which patients were grouped according to the actual treatment received, regardless of the randomized group assignment. The safety population was used for the analysis of all additional safety outcomes, including death from neurologic causes and death from nonneurologic causes. Statistical analysis was performed for only one key secondary outcome, the score on the modified Rankin scale at 180 days, with the analysis contingent on superiority being shown in the embolization group in the primary efficacy analysis to control the type I error rate for multiple testing. The methods used for the secondary-outcome analyses, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses were described in the statistical analysis plan (see the protocol). For secondary-outcome and subgroup analyses, only point estimates with $95\%$ confidence intervals are reported. The widths of the confidence intervals have not been adjusted for multiplicity and should not be used in place of hypothesis testing.  

# Patient Enrollment and Characteristics  

From November 2020 through May 2023, a total of 310 patients were enrolled at 32 participating sites. Among these patients, 149 were randomly assigned to the embolization group and 161 to the control group. On the basis of stratification performed before randomization, 189 patients were to receive surgical standard treatment (91 in the embolization group and 98 in the control group), and 121 patients were to receive nonsurgical standard treatment (58 in the embolization group and 63 in the control group). Overall, 264 patients completed all the clinical and imaging follow-up assessments required by the protocol. Data were missing for $15\%$ of the patients in the intention-to-treat population and for $13\%$ of those in the modified intention-totreat population (Fig. S1). Of the 264 patients with complete follow-up data, 15 died from nonneurologic causes, and the remaining 249 were included in the primary efficacy analysis.  

The mean age of the patients was 73 years (range, 39 to 97), and $70\%$ were men. The embolization group and the control group were well balanced in terms of age, sex, coexisting conditions, use of anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications, and neurologic status at baseline (Table 1). Information regarding the representativeness of the trial population is shown in Table S1. Characteristics of the chronic subdural hematoma were similar in the embolization group and the control group (Table 2). In the surgical stratum, the subdural hematoma collections were larger and associated with greater midline shift than those observed in the nonsurgical stratum.

# Discussion  

STEM showed that among patients with symptomatic chronic subdural hematoma, adjunctive middle meningeal artery embolization with the injected agent Squid resulted in a lower risk of treatment failure than standard treatment alone, without resulting in an increased incidence of disabling stroke or death in the periprocedural period. Adjunctive embolization was not associated with an increased incidence of disability (as assessed with the modified Rankin scale) or death from neurologic causes at 180 days, but overall mortality was numerically higher in the embolization group than in the control group $8\%$ vs. $5\%$ , with a difference of 3 percentage points $[95\%$ CI, $^{-3}$ to 9]).  

The benefit of adjunctive embolization with respect to the primary efficacy outcome appeared to be driven by the effect among patients receiving nonsurgical standard treatment; the analysis in this stratum showed an absolute difference between the embolization group and the control group in the percentage of patients with primary-outcome events of 37 percentage points. Although the trial was not designed to compare strata, a benefit of adjunctive embolization was also observed among patients receiving surgical standard treatment, but the absolute betweengroup difference in this stratum was smaller (9 percentage points).  

Although adjunctive embolization resulted in a lower risk of treatment failure than standard treatment alone, it was not associated with a lower risk of disability or death at 180 days. In previous retrospective studies, reoperation for recurrent chronic subdural hematoma was associated with an increased risk of complications such as seizures, empyema, and pneumonia, as well as substantially increased costs.2 These findings indicate that reducing the risk of reoperation may be key to reducing the risk of complications and costs associated with caring for patients with chronic subdural hematoma.  

Our trial has several limitations. Data were missing for $15\%$ of the patients in the intentionto-treat population, most likely because the trial was conducted among older patients during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic. Unlike other interventional trials, this trial had no upper age limit, which might have influenced patients’ ability to attend follow-up visits. In addition, primary-outcome events were assessed at 180 days, and therefore, adherence to the protocol might have been more burdensome in this trial than in trials with shorter follow-up. Analyses with multiple imputation based on prespecified clinically relevant covariates, as well as other sensitivity analyses, were performed to explore the effect of missing data, but the potential for bias due to missing data cannot be ruled out.  

A component of the primary outcome, reoperation or surgical rescue, was assessed by operators who were aware of the group assignments, which introduced the potential for bias. However, only a minority of patients underwent surgical rescue on the basis of clinical data alone,

Although the incidence of death from neurologic causes at 180 days was numerically lower in the embolization group than in the control group, overall mortality was numerically higher. Moreover, the evidence supporting the use of adjunctive embolization for patients receiving surgical standard treatment was not as strong as that for patients receiving nonsurgical standard treatment. These findings may indicate important areas of focus for pooled analyses in other, contemporaneous studies of adjunctive middle meningeal artery embolization for chronic subdural hematoma.  

This trial showed that adjunctive middle meningeal artery embolization with the injected agent Squid reduced the risk of treatment failure among patients receiving standard treatment for chronic subdural hematoma, without causing excess deaths or disabling strokes in the short term. Further study of longer-term safety outcomes associated with middle meningeal artery embolization is warranted.
