# Structured abstract  

Purpose of review: The role of lipids in spontaneous, non-traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) remains controversial, as some studies suggest that lower levels of total and LDL cholesterol could increase the risk of this disease. Because of their random assortment during meiosis, genetic variants known to associate with lipid levels can be used as instruments to evaluate this relationship from a causal perspective. The purpose of this review is to summarize the existing literature related to genetically-determined low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels and risk of ICH.  

Recent findings: A number of studies have demonstrated that lower LDL levels are associated with a higher risk of ICH and a higher burden of neuroimaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease, such as microbleeds and white matter hyperintensity volume. As for genetically-elevated lipid levels, several studies confirmed an inverse association between LDL levels and ICH. However, a number of observational studies and large meta-analyses of clinical trials of statins have failed to show such association.  

Summary: Observational studies and clinical trial of statins have yielded inconsistent results regarding a possible link between LDL levels and the risk of ICH. Genetic studies focused on genetically-elevated LDL levels and risk of ICH have, for the most, found an inverse association.

# Introduction  

Spontaneous, non-traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is responsible for $10\%$ of all strokes and significantly contributes to the morbidity and mortality of this disease. Because novel targets for preventive and therapeutic strategies are urgently needed in ICH, there is a growing interest in investigating the role of lipids and lipid-lowering drugs in ICH pathophysiology (1, 2). The pivotal Stroke Prevention by Aggressive Reduction in Cholesterol Levels (SPARCL) trial (3, 4) examined the role of statins in secondary prevention after a stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA). The study demonstrated that $80~\mathrm{mg}$ of Atorvastatin per day reduced the incidence of stroke and cardiovascular events; surprisingly, it also found a statistically significant increase in the incidence of ICH. Since the publication of these results, several observational studies, genetic studies, and meta-analyses have attempted to elucidate the meaning of this possible association. These follow-up studies examined the role of different lipid fractions in ICH, evaluating not only risk, but also disease severity, outcome and recurrence of this disease.  

The purpose of our review is to summarize the existing literature on genetically determined low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels and ICH. We start by describing observational studies that evaluated this relationship and then discuss genetic studies on this topic. Genetic studies provide important evidence to support a causal role between LDL levels and ICH. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) found to be associated with lipid levels can be used as instruments to evaluate the causal relationship between lipid levels and risk of ICH because these variants are randomly distributed during meiosis and are exempt from confounding by post-natal exposures.  

# Observational studies related to lipids levels and ICH  

# Studies reporting an association between LDL and ICH  

A number of observational studies and clinical trials have demonstrated that lipid levels influence the risk of ICH (Table 1). In addition to the SPARCL results outlined above, one of the most important observational studies on this topic to date examined the relationship between LDL levels and ICH risk using a long follow-up period of 6-years and different thresholds of LDL (2). The authors observed a significant association between lower LDL concentration and higher risk of ICH when LDL level was $<70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , and the association became nonsignificant when LDL level was $\triangle70\mathrm{mg/dL}$ . Valappil et al. (5) assessed the relationship using a case/control design, prospectively recruiting patients with ICH and comparing them to a control group of sex-matched individuals who attended routine preventive health check-ups at a primary care center. They confirmed that lower LDL cholesterol in both men and women were associated with a higher risk of primary ICH, particularly in older individuals. Similarly, meta-analysis by Cheng et al. (6) explored the relationship between a dose-dependent statin therapy and ICH risk. The authors showed that low-dose statin therapy is a safe and effective ICH treatment, whereas high-dose statin therapy is associated with increased ICH risk. Finally, Pandit et al. (7) performed a metaanalysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to assess the association between high-dose statins and risk of ICH among patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD). The researchers found an increased risk of ICH in subjects on a high-dose statin, but there was no difference in all-cause mortality between groups.  

Mounting evidence indicates that factors that influence the risk of sustaining an ICH also modify the severity and outcome of this disease (8). Supported by this evidence, some studies evaluated the role of LDL as a determinant of neuroimaging severity and outcome in ICH. Rodriguez-Luna et al. (9) investigated the relationship between LDL levels and clinical outcome in patients with acute ICH. They prospectively reviewed data of 108 consecutive patients with supratentorial ICH presenting within 6 hours from symptoms onset. Lower serum LDL level independently predicted hematoma growth (a powerful neuroimaging predictor of outcome), early neurological deterioration, and 3-month mortality after acute ICH (10).  

# Studies finding no association between LDL levels and ICH  

In contrast to the results outlined above, a number of studies did not find a significant association between LDL levels and risk of ICH (Table 1). Lee et al. (11) evaluated the clinical characteristics of subjects with different levels of LDL, including the risk for ICH. They were particularly interested in the subgroup of patients with low values of LDL (≤ $40\mathrm{mg/dL}$ . About $1\%$ of the patients had a LDL level $\leq40\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , and about half of these subjects had no history of hypolipidemic therapy. Importantly, ICH incidence was not related to LDL level or statin use in this subset. Another comparative study by Mustanoja et al. (12) explored whether blood lipid profiles and statin use before ICH were associated with outcome. They used data from the Helsinki ICH Study, a single-center observational registry of consecutive ICH patients, and studied the associations between premorbid statin use, baseline lipid levels, and clinical outcome. Premorbid statin use did not affect the outcome of ICH, but lower LDL levels were associated with higher in-hospital mortality. Similarly, Bozloucay et al. (13) aimed to investigate patients with ICH hospitalized in a neurology department within 5 years. They observed that hypercholesterolemia, but not low lipids levels, was one of the most common risk factors in patients with ICH as compared to controls, along with hypertension. McKinney et al. (14) performed a meta-analysis of 31 randomized controlled trials of statins involving 91,588 subjects in the active group and 91,215 in the control group without finding a significant association between statins and risk of ICH. Finally, a meta-analysis of 39 trials, which included 287,651 study participants, did not find a significant association (15). However, lipid lowering interventions were associated with an increased risk of ICH in secondary prevention trials (15).  

# Genetic studies evaluating the role of LDL in ICH pathophysiology  

A handful of studies evaluated the role of genetically determined lipid levels on ICH (Table 2). Akoudad et al. (16) reported the results of a study that included several genetic loci for serum lipid fractions (total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides) and their influence on ICH risk. The investigators also evaluated the role of these genetic risk factors in the occurrence of cerebral microbleeds, a well-established neuroimaging trait of cerebral small vessel disease, which is the vasculopathy that causes ICH. Genetic susceptibility to high  

serum total cholesterol and LDL was positively associated with incidence of ICH, and borderline associated with multiple lobar microbleeds.  

In contrast to the aforementioned positive associations between genetic risk variants for LDL and risk of ICH, several genetic studies found evidence of an inverse correlation between this exposure and outcome. Anderson et al. (17) examined the role of known genetic risk variants for circulating lipid levels on ICH using a candidate gene approach. Specifically, the investigators evaluated genetic risk variants within CETP, a gene that codes for the cholesteryl ester transfer protein, a plasma protein that facilitates the transport of cholesteryl esters and triglycerides between the lipoproteins. Importantly, the evaluated genetic variants within CETP are known to be associated with higher HDL and lower LDL levels, mirroring the effect of statins. The study tested $390~CETP$ variants in a discovery cohort of 1,149 ICH cases and 1,238 controls from 3 different genetic studies, finding that 12 were associated with ICH risk after accounting for multiple testing (strongest association at rs173539). These results were replicated in 1,625 cases and 1,845 controls from 5 studies. Subsequently, the investigators modeled the aggregate burden of genetic variants within CETP by constructing a genetic risk score comprised of 7 independent variants within this gene, which was strongly associated with ICH risk. Because genetic variants at CETP are associated with increases in HDL and decreases of LDL, these results provide genetic evidence to support a role of lipid metabolism in ICH, but cannot determine whether the mediating lipid fraction is HDL or LDL.  

Sun et al. (18) analyzed causal associations of blood lipids with risk of stroke in an Asian population. The authors examined the associations of biochemically measured circulating lipids with ischemic stroke and ICH in a nested case-control study using the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB). Importantly, the study involved both observational and genetic analyses in 5,475 ischemic stroke cases, 4,776 ICH cases and 6,290 healthy controls. In the observational analysis, the investigators found a positive association between LDL levels and risk of ischemic stroke and an inverse association with risk of ICH. The genetic analysis involved the implementation of Mendelian Randomization techniques using a polygenic risk score of 46 genetic risk variants known to strongly associate with higher LDL levels as the instrument. With this approach, it was found that genetically-elevated LDL levels were associated with a higher risk of ischemic stroke. As for ICH, the direction of effect for genetically-elevated LDL levels was negative, consistent with the findings from the observational part of the study, but this did not reach statistical significance.  

Finally, Falcone et al. (19) evaluated whether genetically-elevated lipid levels were associated with risk of ICH in a predominantly European population. The investigators used a polygenic risk score that included genetic information on 339 genetic risk variants for LDL to conduct Mendelian Randomization analyses. This polygenic risk was found to be strongly associated with observed circulating LDL levels in 316,428 individuals enrolled in the UK Biobank. Combining these results with data from 1286 ICH cases and 1261 matched controls enrolled in 3 case-control genetic studies of ICH, the authors found that genetically-elevated LDL levels were significantly associated with lower risk of ICH. The underlying pathophysiology of ICH varies with the location of the hemorrhage within the brain, with lobar bleeds being mainly related to cerebral amyloid angiopathy and deep bleeds (compromising the deep brain nuclei, brainstem and cerebellum) being caused by long-standing hypertension. As a result, this study also pursued analyses stratified by hemorrhage location, finding that the inverse association between genetically-elevated LDL and ICH risk remained significant for both lobar and deep hemorrhages.  

# Conclusion  

ICH is a devastating condition with limited preventive and therapeutic options. Given the need for new targets for drug development, lipid metabolism has been extensively studied as possible pathophysiological mechanism for this disease. In particular, there has been significant interest in determining whether higher levels of total and LDL cholesterol are protective in this disease, in contrast to most cardiovascular disease. Clinical trials, observational studies, meta-analyses, and genetic studies have reported conflicting results about the influence of lipids in general, and LDL in particular, on the risk of ICH. These inconsistencies may be driven by lack of statistical power, confounding, and misclassification due to inappropriate ascertainment of ICH. The ongoing Statins In Intracerebral Hemorrhage (SATURN) trial (NCT03936361), sponsored by the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, constitutes an important step to more definitively establish whether lipid levels and statins play a role in ICH. This study aims to determine whether continuation vs. discontinuation of statin drugs after spontaneous lobar ICH is the best strategy, and whether the decision to continue/discontinue statins should be influenced by an individual’s apolipoprotein-E (APOE) genotype.

# Key points:  

Because spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage is in urgent need of novel treatment targets, lipid metabolism has been extensively studied as determinant of both risk and outcome.   
In contrast to most cardiovascular diseases, evidence from clinical trials of statins, observational studies, and genetic analyses point to an inverse association between circulating LDL levels and risk of ICH (lower LDL levels are associated with a higher risk of ICH).   
However, not all studies have found this inverse association between LDL and ICH; some have found a null result and a few have found a positive association.
