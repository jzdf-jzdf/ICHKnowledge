# abstract  

Study objective: This study compares the safety and efficacy of a fixed dose of 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4FPCC) to the FDA-approved variable dosing for reversal of warfarin-induced anticoagulation.  

Methods: This was a single-center, prospective, open-label, randomized controlled trial with subjects randomized to 4FPCC at a fixed dose of 1500 IU or the FDA-approved variable dosing regimen. The primary efficacy outcome (reversal success) was defined as a post-intervention international normalized ratio (INR) of less than or equal to 1.5. Given that 4FPCC is the standard of care for reversal of warfarin-induced anticoagulation an active-controlled approach was employed with the two dosing regimens compared based on efficacy, cost, and safety outcomes. Results: 71 subjects (34 in the fixed dose group and 37 in the variable dose group) completed the study. There were no significant differences in age, gender, weight, initial INR, or indication for 4FPCC administration between the two treatment groups. Reversal success in the fixed-dose group was $61.8\%,$ while in the variable dose group reversal success was $89.2\%$ . Reversal success in the fixed-dose group was significantly lower than the rate of reversal success in the variable dose group $27.4\%$ lower, $p=0.011$ ).  

Conclusion: The results of this study provide evidence that fixed dosing results in lower reversal success rates as compared to variable dosing of 4FPCC for warfarin-induced anticoagulation.

# 1. Introduction  

Warfarin, first approved for medical use in the United States in 1954, is a common oral anticoagulant utilized for prevention and treatment of thromboembolic events and conditions. It has been shown to treat blood clots and to prevent stroke in patients with atrial fibrillation and valvular heart disease [1]. Although effective, warfarin use is complicated by an increased risk of major bleeding events, particularly with long-term administration [2]. These events may occur in patients receiving chronic warfarin therapy at a rate of $1.5\%$ to $5.2\%$ annually with a resultant mortality exceeding $10\%$ in these bleeding events. Among patients with intracranial hemorrhage, the mortality rate may exceed $40\%$ [2]. In the setting of these life-threatening complications of warfarin use, it is essential to reverse the pharmacologic effects of the drug rapidly in order to minimize bleeding and reduce mortality [3].  

Traditional warfarin reversal agents in the setting of major bleeding are fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and vitamin K (phytonadione). The use of FFP is limited by requisite volume of administration and the time delay related to thawing this product prior to use. The effectiveness of vitamin K is hindered by delayed onset of action [4]. More recently, a four factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4FPCC) commonly described by its trade name, Kcentra®, was introduced as an alternative for warfarin reversal containing all of the inhibited vitamin K dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X) and proteins C and S. 4FPCC has a rapid onset of action and may reverse INR changes caused by warfarin within 10 min. As the duration of action of this 4FPCC is up to $^\textrm{\scriptsize8h}$ , it is often given with vitamin K to prolong the procoagulant effect [5].  

The first commercially available 4FPCC was approved for vitamin K antagonist reversal in the United States in 2013. In less than five years, these agents emerged as a standard of care for patients with lifethreatening hemorrhage after warfarin administration and are supported by guideline statements due to ability to rapidly and effectively achieve hemostasis [6]. Despite expansion in the use of 4FPCC, the optimal strategy for dosing and administration of these agents remain unclear. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved dosing algorithm for 4FPCC is based on pretreatment INR and body weight (Table 1). More recently, there has been an increase in the number of publications reporting success with fixed dose 4FPCC regimens with lower doses than recommended by the package insert which is based on the FDA dosing algorithm [7]. Unfortunately, clinical data does not consistently demonstrate the efficacy and safety of a fixed dose strategy [8]. Existing data is often limited by lack of direct comparison to FDA approved dosing and potentially subtherapeutic fixed dosing regimens [7,8].  

A variety of fixed dose strategies utilizing 4FPCC to maximize efficacy and minimize adverse events, cost and time to administration have been proposed. The first benefit of a fixed dose strategy is improved time to drug administration in patient populations where time is essential for optimal outcome [9]. An observational study in 2006 documented that time to initiation of vitamin K antagonist reversal was highly predictive of INR normalization where every $30\mathrm{min}$ of delay in administration of the first dose of FFP was associated with a $20\%$ decrease in the odds of INR reversal at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ . A fixed 4FPCC dose administration strategy offers the potential to begin administration rapidly [10]. Another recent trial noted a significant decrease in medication order to medication administration time following implementation of a fixed dose 4FPCC protocol. Time reduction in this trial was 51 min with variable dose administration of 4FPCC to an average of $38~\mathrm{min}$ with a fixed dose protocol. A fixed dosing strategy removes barriers such as dosage calculation which may hasten both normalization of INR and achievement of hemostasis [5].  

A second benefit of fixed dose administration for 4FPCC is decreased pharmaceutical cost. Financial evaluation of variable dosing strategies based on patient weight and INR have placed the median cost of 4FPCC administration between $\$2817$ and $\$4213$ per patient. Financial analysis of a fixed dose strategy for 4FPCC administration had an associated cost of $\$2264$ perpatient [11].Asimilartrialfounda $\$982$ per patient cost reduction in favor of a fixed dose strategy for administration of 4FPCC [12].  

At present, the FDA approves a variable dosing protocol for administration of 4FPCC for warfarin-induced anticoagulation based on patient weight and presenting INR [3]. We compared a 1500 IU 4FPCC fixed dosing strategy to the standard variable strategy based on presenting INR and patient weight in a set of patients receiving warfarin for anticoagulation. The study investigators hypothesized that we would find the 4FPCC fixed dosing strategy to result in similar successful reversal rates as compared to the variable regimen. Cost of each dosing strategy was examined along with the occurrence of thromboembolic events within 7 days after drug administration.  



# 2. Methods  



# 2.1. Selection of participants  

All patients aged 18 years or older on chronic anticoagulation with warfarin having an INR ≥2 (initially identified by iSTAT device-Abbott, Princeton, NJ) presenting to the emergency department with emergent bleeding or requiring emergent surgery were eligible for participation in the study. Indications for 4FPCC administration included, but were not limited to: intracranial hemorrhage, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm, intrathoracic hemorrhage, spinal cord hemorrhage, cervical hematoma, and emergent surgical indications such as debridement for necrotizing soft tissue infection, fasciotomy for compartment hypertension, external fixator placement, and exploratory laparotomy. Patients were excluded if they had an initial INR less than 2 or did not have an initial or post-treatment INR reading. Other exclusion criterion included a history of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, current pregnancy, or incarceration. Patients were enrolled between March 2017 and April 2019.

# 2.2. Study design and setting  

This was a single-center, prospective, open-label, randomized controlled trial. It was conducted at Regions Hospital, an urban, level I trauma center in St. Paul, MN. This study was approved by the HealthPartners Institutional Review Board. Due to the time-sensitive nature of hemorrhage in these patients and clinical equipoise in optimal 4FPCC dosing strategy, informed consent was waived.  

Eligible patients were randomized to receive either a fixed dose of approximately 1500 IU prepared by administering three full approximately 500 unit vials (amount of factor IX varies by lot number) or FDA-approved variable dosing based on body weight and presenting INR (Table 1). This study utilized a research randomizer tool to create a blocked randomization schedule to ensure equal allocation of subjects in each arm as enrollment progressed. Randomization occurred using the prepared randomization list and was initiated by a research team member with physician approval. Exact doses of 4FPCC, based on factor IX component, administered varied slightly from the calculated doses as the amount of factor IX differs based on characteristics of the vials utilized. The dosing strategy could not be blinded by the research team at the time of administration, as patients receiving a fixed dose of 4FPCC typically received a smaller volume of study drug.  

Following randomization and medication administration, each patient was monitored for continued bleeding by the treating physician. If a patient receiving the 1500 IU fixed dose had an INR remaining above goal, an additional 500 IU could be administered to reduce bleeding risk and/or attempt to achieve hemostasis. Supplemental administration of the 4FPCC in the fixed dose group occurred at the discretion of the treating physician. If a patient was in the variable 4FPCC dose group, they were not considered eligible for additional dosing of 4FPCC. If a patient received the additional 500 IU dose, the patient was included in the analysis as a treatment failure for fixed dosing as the INR would have to be ${>}1.5$ to receive the subsequent dose.  



# 2.3. Outcomes  

Successful INR normalization was defined as a lab INR value of less than or equal to 1.5 fifteen minutes after the completion of 4FPCC administration. Secondary objectives were the occurrence of thromboembolic events within 7-days after 4FPCC administration as well as cost associated with each dosing strategy. All patients had both an initial INR value obtained via the iSTAT (Abbott, Princeton, NJ) point of care test and a confirmatory INR in the clinical laboratory. In consideration of risk vs benefit of earlier administration of the 4FPCC, the laboratory INR may not have been evaluated prior to randomization. After enrollment and randomization, each patient received study medication and had a laboratory INR drawn $15\mathrm{min}$ after infusion of 4FPCC. This $15\mathrm{min}$ time frame was chosen based on the peak onset of action for the 4FPCC [13] and possible need to re-dose in potentially life threatening situations. The laboratory INR results were utilized in all cases to evaluate the change in INR with administration of the 4FPCC for the primary endpoint. All enrolled patients were evaluated for development of a thromboembolic event within 7 days after 4FPCC administration. A 7-day follow-up period was chosen based on prior studies evaluating complications with a 4FPCC fixed dose [12,14]. The last patient progress note written within the 7-day follow-up period was evaluated to determine patient status. This allowed inclusion of all patients who may have been discharged prior to the end of the 7-day period but were readmitted with a thromboembolic event. Records from other area health facilities are typically available through our EMR as well, resulting in the ability to discern any thromboembolic events for which care was sought at an outside facility within the follow-up period. Cost of treatment was assessed based on the amount of 4FPCC doses administered during admission, using the study-contemporary rate of $\$1.57$ /unit.

# 4. Limitations  

There are several limitations to this study. We chose the successful reversal of INR elevation as our primary outcome. A more patientcentered outcome such as mortality or clinical hemostasis would have also been appropriate. In addition, our selection of an INR less than or equal to 1.5 was a conservative goal in comparison to previous literature in this area. Goals for target INR have ranged from ${<}2.1$ to ${\leq}1.3$ [11]. There may be an INR target between 1.5 and 2.0 that is clinically relevant and more easily achieved with fixed dosing. This was a single center study involving a high number of overweight patients, as $28.2\%$ of patients were ${>}100~\mathrm{kg}$ Along with a significantly elevated INR, obesity has been shown to be an independent risk factor for reversal failure [16]. This may limit applicability of our results to centers outside of countries like the United States where obesity is not as prevalent. Although this is unlikely to affect our primary outcome, we were also unable to blind the dosing of 4FPCC given variability of volume (always $25\mathrm{IU/mL}$ and strict administration criteria [13]. Finally, enrollment was closed prior to goal of 100 subjects due to feasibility constraints, resulting in a smaller sample 79 subjects which lead to post-hoc changes to the analysis approach.

# 5. Discussion  

Our study examined the reversal of warfarin using fixed dose versus variable dose administration of 4FPCC. The rate of reversal success, defined as an INR $</=1.5$ after 4FPCC administration, was $61.8\%$ with a fixed dose of 4FPCC while the rate of reversal success where variable dosing of 4FPCC was utilized was significantly higher at $89.2\%$ . While a noninferiorty trial is needed to formally assess non-inferior status, our results signal that the fixed dosing regimen does not perform as well as the previously endorsed variable dosing approach. The odds of reversal success increased as the initial INR decreased with fixed dose administration of 4FPCC. Although statistical significance could not be established for the initial INR subgroups, likely due to insufficient number of patients, there was a trend toward increased rate of reversal success for patients with all levels of initial INR when the variable dosing regimen was used (Table 3). From the cost perspective, the fixed dosing schedule is appealing as the median pharmaceutical cost was significantly lower ( $\$3372$ to $\$2524$ , $p<0.001$ ) although these benefits failed to persist in the efficacy analysis. As there were no thromboembolic events in any patient for the first 7 days after 4FPCC administration, no differences in safety could be reported.

After our planned initial analyses, we followed up with chart reviews of all patients in effort to better describe the cohort for our own quality improvement purposes. We noted slightly higher vasopressor use and transfusion requirements in the fixed dosing arm. However, the incidence of hematoma expansion and mortality was slightly greater in the variable dosing arm. These exploratory outcomes were helpful in better understanding care for our patients although not included in our main analyses. While formal conclusions could not be drawn from this additional review, we hope that it will inform and encourage more patient centered outcomes for future work.  

Important questions remain about the use of fixed dose 4FPCC. A recent international survey demonstrated that only a minority of health systems have implemented an emergent warfarin reversal protocol based on fixed dose 4FPCC with noted interfacility variability, suggesting a hesitancy to adopt a straightforward fixed dose 4FPCC administration strategy [19]. Current literature consists of retrospective studies which are at high risk of bias inherent to that study design. Findings of these studies are complicated by heterogeneity of patient population, type of bleeding, intervention utilized, administration of other blood products, severity of illness, and delays in time to therapy [11].  

Doses are highly variable in available literature describing use of 4FPCC for reversal of warfarin effect. Reported doses range from 200 to 1500 IU. In a clinical trial utilizing a fixed dose regimen of a 4FPCC, patients receiving a 500 IU dose were less likely to reach INR reversal than patients who were treated using a variable dosing regimen. Thus, while 500 IU represents under dosing for many patients, the dose which optimizes efficacy, safety, and cost is still not clear [14]. More recent retrospective work evaluating fixed doses ranging from 1000 to 1500 IU, appears to provide more effective reversal of patients to an INR ${\leq}1.5$ . For example, a study by Gilbert et al showed no difference in rates of post-4FPCC infusion INR of ${<}1.6$ between fixed and variable groups of patients with acute life threatening bleeding associated with warfarin therapy [20]. Dosing algorithms utilizing a fixed dose followed by additional doses driven by clinical response may provide rapid administration while containing pharmaceutical cost [5]. Data on these strategies is limited.  

An early report describing fixed dose 4FPCC administration for INR reversal came in 17 patients with hemorrhagic complications associated with warfarin therapy. The protocol began with administration of a fixed dose of 4FPCC followed by additional administration of drug if correction of the INR was not identified. Based on this early work, the National Advisory Committee on Blood and Blood Products in Canada recommended a dose of 1000 IU of a PCC with vitamin K administration where the PCC was utilized in bleeding associated with use of a vitamin K antagonist [17]. This guideline was updated in 2011 after a survey revealed continued concern for thrombotic complications. The Committee then adjusted its recommendation to a tiered, fixed dose regimen based on INR [2]. A subsequent update to the recommendation in 2014 advocated dosing of the 4FPCC based on weight and INR [13]. Advocates for fixed dose administration of 4FPCC suggest a 1500 IU fixed dose protocol in patients with a wide range of INR values. In a recent efficacy analysis, an INR value of 2 or less was noted in a wide range of patients after administration of a fixed dose of a 4FPCC [18].  

The risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) is regarded as a critical factor favoring adoption of fixed dose regimens. With traditional dosing, the incidence of VTE varies in the literature from $1.8\%$ to $13.8\%$ . Fixed dose regimens describe a $0\%$ to $4.9\%$ incidence of VTE [6]. However, there are inconsistencies in analyses of this VTE data in regard to time to event, presence of risk factors, and comparison to traditional dosing programs. Studies that included a comparator group were likely underpowered to detect thromboembolic safety outcomes [6,19].  

Available literature differs with regard to efficacy endpoints and there is controversy regarding the optimal clinical endpoint. Target INR values range from ${<}1.5$ to ${<}2$ . Differences in the definition of successful reversal cloud a clearer risk to benefit analysis as reversal to an INR of 2 may carry risk of continued bleeding. Conversely, reversal to an INR ${<}1.5$ may carry a greater risk of thromboembolic events [3]. While INR serves as a surrogate for bleeding risk, it has been argued that direct measurement of hemostasis or neurologic outcomes may be more important. Retrospective studies are not well suited to examine these outcomes as time to hemostasis is not routinely and effectively recorded [21].  

The relationship between the speed in which a 4FPCC is delivered, attainment of goal INR values, and the impact on patient outcomes has not been clearly reported. Both time to administration and time to completion of infusion may be reduced with fixed doses secondary to improved operational efficiency. Older studies comparing fixed dose to variable dose administration of 4FPCC may be at risk for residual confounding due to the differences in time to administration between dosing programs.  

The American College of Cardiology Expert Consensus Decision Pathway for management of bleeding in patients on oral anticoagulants provides two dosing options for fixed dose administration of a 4FPCC in emergent bleeding: 1000 IU for major hemorrhage and 1500 IU for intracranial bleeding [22]. Full discussion in the body of the guideline is not provided. The clinician is left to discern which evidence optimally supports these recommendations. Little information is provided on risks, benefits, and limitations.  

Administration of a fixed dose 4FPCC is a promising intervention with the potential to rapidly restore hemostasis while minimizing adverse events and optimizing use of available resources. Before fixed dose 4FPCC strategies can be recommended for routine practice, however, additional data is needed. Further studies must be comparative in nature as interest in fixed dose regimens gains popularity and acceptance. Institutions utilizing this practice should have an ample comparator group to study. Outcomes should include meaningful measurement of clinical success rather than simple attainment of laboratory targets. Thromboembolic events should be characterized by temporal relationship to 4FPCC administration. Utilization of additional blood products should also be documented and included for comparison, particularly for cost effectiveness analysis.  

There is evidence to support consideration of fixed dose 4FPCC administration based on observations and case reports [3,5,7,8,14]. While the goal of the present study was to validate this practice, it failed to further support the use of fixed dose 4FPCC. Additional studies addressing limitations of previous work are necessary. An appropriately powered clinical trial designed to compare fixed dose and variable dose 4FPCC administration may provide insight into many of the outstanding gaps in current clinical practice. Our work highlights some of the factors to consider in comparison of fixed and variable dose 4FPCC protocols. Findings from additional studies may produce recommendations for the use of a fixed dose 4FPCC which optimize efficacy, safety, and cost.
