To the editor,  

We read the article published in your journal with great enthusiasm and highly appreciate the efforts that the authors put into conducting the research [1]. It provides significant insights into the discussed cause and helps better understand the defined topic. For sure, it lays out a good foundation for subsequent studies; however, we have a few doubts, which have prompted us to write this letter, and we firmly believe that if these doubts were well addressed, the overall yield of the study would have been enhanced many folds.  



# Letter  

To start with, the authors in the study have mentioned various surgical techniques for the treatment of chronic subdural hematoma, some of which are traditionally used (craniotomy) while others are contemporary (like neuroendoscopyassociated surgery). It is a well-known fact that contemporary techniques are proving to be more beneficial in terms of delaying or terminating unfavorable outcomes [2], and when considering an intervention postoperatively for efficacy, these techniques should be used while operating to have a clear idea of whether the procedure was associated with adverse events, if any, such as the two complications reported in the intervention group in the given study, or if it was due to an older technique that is associated with adverse events per se, irrespective of the intervention or control group.  

Secondly, comorbidities play an important role in the pathogenesis of many diseases and almost always result in poor outcomes. While reading the article, we did not find the authors addressing comorbidities like diabetes mellitus or any malignancy beforehand, which can result in poor outcomes [3] regardless of intervention or control group and can be a significant cause of bias.  

Third, in addition to comorbidities, a patient’s general health before surgery also affects how effectively he or she reacts to therapy after surgery and whether he or she develops problems, particularly when the issue at hand is neurosurgical, as in our instance. According to research by Tomohisa Ishida [4], regardless of the kind of intervention employed, patients who had previously had treatment for cSDH and had low modified Rankin Scores (mRS) were more likely to experience postoperative problems.  

Finally, the authors did not give enough consideration to the patients with bilateral hematoma or unilateral hematoma and any discrepancy in functional outcomes in either case. We have found that, while diving deep into the literature regarding CSDH, patients with bilateral hematoma have an increased risk of recurrence [5] and these cases need special attention when any study is conducted with them.  



# Conclusion  

Overall, the yield of the article is very substantial, regardless of the arguments made above, but as avid researchers, we should believe there is always scope for improvement, and if any confounding factors are ever found, these need to be accounted for, boosting our overall understanding regarding any study.
