# The effect of delayed cord clamping on early cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics, mortality, and severe intraventricular hemorrhage in preterm infants $<32$ weeks: a systematic review and meta‑analysis of clinical trials

$\circledcirc$ The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2025  



# Abstract  

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the impact of DCC $(>30\mathrm{s})$ compared to immediate cord clamping (ICC) or umbilical cord milking (UCM) on early cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics, mortality, and severe intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) in very preterm infants. We searched Ovid Medline, EMBASE, and Cochrane CENTRAL from inception to September 28, 2023, and included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing preterm infants $<32$ weeks who received DCC to ICC or UCM. The results were obtained using the Mantel–Haenszel and pooled with a random-effects model. Fifteen articles (2967 patients) were selected, comparing DCC to ICC (10), DCC to UCM (4), and one three-arm study. DCC resulted in a slight increase in superior vena cava (SVC) flow compared to ICC (MD $16.09\mathrm{ml/kg/min}$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}=4.03$ to 28.15, $I^{2}{=}20\%$ ; low-certainty evidence). There was little to no difference in right ventricular output (RVO) after DCC compared to ICC $\mathrm{(MD-2.09ml/kg/min}$ , $95\%$ $\%{\mathrm{CI}}=-26.20$ to 22.02, $I^{2}{=}17\%$ ; low-certainty evidence). DCC resulted in a large reduction in mortality compared to ICC (RR 0.64, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}0.47$ to 0.88) but was very uncertain compared to UCM. DCC may reduce severe IVH compared to UCM (RR 0.54, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}0.28$ to 1.06).  

Conclusion: DCC improves outcomes in preterm infants $<32$ weeks when compared with ICC, as indicated by an increase in SVC flow and regional cerebral oxygenation (rSO2) (moderate- and low-certainty evidence) and reduced mortality. There is low- and very-low-certainty evidence to suggest little to no difference in mortality and cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics after DCC compared to UCM.  



#  

What is Known:   
• DCC or UCM assists the physiological transition from intrauterine to extra-uterine life by increasing the amount of circulating blood at birth in preterm neonates.   
• In comparison to ICC, DCC or UCM were shown to reduce mortality, NEC, and infection in preterm infants $<32$ weeks; however, a higher incidence of severe IVH was a concern in the UCM group.   
What is New:   
• Although DCC has been associated with a large reduction in mortality for preterm infants $<32$ weeks compared to ICC, the current evidence is of moderate certainty. However, there appears to be little or no difference in early cardiac hemodynamic parameters and cerebral nearinfrared spectroscopy parameters (low or very-low certainty evidence).   
• Current evidence, which is of low and very low certainty, suggests that there is little or no difference in cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics, mortality, and severe IVH with DCC compared to UCM.

# Introduction  

The very preterm infants continue to encounter prematurityrelated complications, particularly intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) [1]. The risk of IVH increases as the delicate balance of cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics shifts from intrauterine to extrauterine transition [2, 3]. Monitoring cardiac input, specifically superior vena cava (SVC) flow, has proven to be a reliable indicator of cardiovascular changes and in predicting IVH [4–6]. The impact of impaired cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics extends beyond immediate survival and has been linked with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes [7].  

Enhanced placental transfusion through delayed cord clamping (DCC) or umbilical cord milking (UCM) is a key strategy to support transition [2] with recommendations in various clinical guidelines [8, 9]. The mechanism of benefit of DCC or UCM has been related to increased placental blood received supporting the physiological transition [3, 10]. A systematic review of 12 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in 2014 involving 531 preterm infants $<32$ weeks gestational age (GA) concluded DCC, UCM, or a combination of both to have lower mortality, necrotizing enterocolitis, and infection compared to immediate cord clamping (ICC) [11]. Recent studies have raised important questions regarding the safety and efficacy of these techniques. The Australian Placental Transfusion Study, which compared DCC vs ICC in 1566 preterm infants $<30$ weeks, showed that DCC did not result in a lower incidence of the combined outcome of death or major morbidity at 36 weeks GA than ICC [12]. A systematic review in 2017 involving 998 preterm infants $<28$ weeks found DCC to reduce mortality but not severe IVH [13]. Furthermore, a recent RCT in $<32$ weeks GA found a significantly higher rate of severe IVH (a signal of harm) in the UCM group, which led to the early termination of the trial [14]. These conclusions were supported further by a systematic review involving preterm infants $<37$ weeks, which showed that UCM, as opposed to DCC, significantly increased severe IVH [15].  

To gain a better understanding of these differences in the effects of DCC and UCM on mortality and severe IVH, it becomes prudent to explore underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and examine the effects of DCC and UCM on early cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics such as ventricular outputs, superior vena cava (SVC) flow, and cerebral perfusion using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) measurements in the early postnatal period. These indicators can provide insights into neonatal circulatory adaptation and oxygenation status during the transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life. Some clinical trials have suggested that DCC and UCM may affect these hemodynamic parameters [16–19]. However, a systematic review is required to summarize the existing evidence regarding the effect of enhanced placental transfusion on cerebral and cardiac hemodynamics, as well as to update the available evidence regarding their impact on important neonatal outcomes such as mortality and severe IVH.  



# Materials and methods  



# Included studies  

We included only RCTs comparing preterm infants born at GA less than 32 weeks receiving DCC after birth and a parallel group receiving either UCM or ICC/DCC vs UCM and DCC vs ICC.  



# Participants  

Preterm infants born at $\mathrm{GA}<32\$ weeks of any birth weight (BW), born via cesarean section or normal spontaneous delivery. We excluded cases with placental or cord abnormalities (vasa previa, placenta previa, abruptio placentae, or abnormally invasive placentas), severe birth asphyxia, or life-threatening congenital malformations.  



# Types of interventions  

DCC was defined as clamping the cord at least $30~\mathrm{s}$ after delivery. ICC was defined as clamping the cord at no later than 15–20 s after delivery. UCM was defined as squeezing and pulling the umbilical cord blood toward the newborn at least three times after delivery.  



# Outcome measures  

Early cardiac hemodynamics included SVC flow $\mathrm{(ml/kg/}$ min) and right and left ventricular output (RVO/LVO) measured in $\mathrm{ml/kg/min}$ . Early cerebral hemodynamics included regional cerebral oxygenation (rSO2) measured by NIRS. Mortality was recorded before hospital discharge and severe IVH was defined as grades III or IV hemorrhage according to Volpe classification [20].  



# Search strategy  

We searched Ovid Medline (from 1946 to September 28, 2023), Ovid EMBASE (from 1974 to September 28, 2023), and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) (Searched on September 28, 2023). The search strategies for MEDLINE, EMBASE, and CENTRAL can be found in the Supplementary File. The RCTs search filter for therapy, Embase, and Medline best balance of sensitivity and specificity by Health Information Research Information Unit, McMaster University applied. We did not limit the search to any specific language, any publication date, or any other restrictions. The clinical trial registry (ClinicalTrials. gov, www.clinicaltrials.gov) was searched on September 28, “Google Scholar” was searched for articles that might not have been cited in the standard medical databases, and reference lists were screened for ongoing and unpublished studies. The review was not registered.  



# Selection of studies  

Two independent reviewers (EA and MG) screened the titles and abstracts for potentially relevant studies through the Covidence software. The screened studies were then reviewed for full-text screening by both reviewers. Any conflict between the reviewers at each stage was resolved through discussion.  



# Data extraction and management  

Study information and results were abstracted by two independent reviewers (EA and MG) after piloting the process for one study using the Covidence software data abstraction form. Then, the outcome data were inputted into the Review Manager Website (RMW) [21]. The conflict between the reviewers regarding the abstracted data was resolved by discussion. The gathered data included study characteristics such as RCT design, number of participants, inclusion and exclusion criteria, methods of randomization, concealment allocation, blinding, and the analysis plan. Patients’ characteristics such as GA, sex, birth weight, mode of delivery, duration of DCC or ICC, and the number of times UCM was performed were also abstracted. Outcomes data were collected as per the primary and secondary analysis if reported. Attempts were made to contact authors of excluded studies if abstracts lacked sufficient data.  



# Assessment of risk of bias (ROB) in included studies  

Two authors (EA and MG) independently assessed ROB using the Cochrane ROB tool for RCTs 2.0 [22]. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus.  



# Measures of treatment effect  

We used RMW to perform the data analysis. For dichotomous outcomes (severe IVH and mortality), we expressed the difference as risk ratios (RRs) with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs). For cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics as continuous data, we expressed results as mean differences (MD) with $95\%$ CI. For analysis of continuous data in which the mean and SD were not reported, we estimated the mean and SD from the median, interquartile range (IQR), and sample size using Wan’s method. [23]  



# Assessment of heterogeneity  

For this assessment, we used three statistical criteria: visual inspection of the forest plot, Chi-squared test, and the $I^{2}$ . We considered $p<0.05$ as statistically significant for the Chi-squared test. Based on the Cochrane Handbook, $\varLambda2$ values were interpreted as 0 to $40\%$ might not be important, 30 to $60\%$ moderate heterogeneity, 50 to $90\%$ substantial heterogeneity, and 75 to $100\%$ considerable heterogeneity. Clinical heterogeneity was also addressed to complement the statistical one.  



# Assessment of reporting biases  

Since the meta-analysis included fewer than ten RCTs, we had to rely on the comprehensiveness of the search strategy and the range of databases used.  



# Data synthesis  

We obtained the results using Mantel–Haenszel and the random-effects model in RMW.  



# Subgroup and sensitivity analysis  

We could not perform pre-planned subgroup analyses for preterm infants born at $\mathrm{GA}<28$ weeks vs GA 28–32 weeks and for $\mathrm{BW}<750\mathrm{~g~}$ vs $\mathbf{BW}{>}750\mathrm{~g~}$ due to small numbers. Also, due to insufficient high-quality studies, we could not conduct sensitivity analysis removing the high ROB studies.  



# Assessment of the certainty of the evidence  

The certainty of the evidence was assessed by two independent reviewers (EA and MG) using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [24]. For mortality and severe IVH, we considered at least five fewer events per 1000 patients $(0.5\%)$ as MID. For cardiac hemodynamics, we considered a $15{-}\mathrm{ml/kg/min}$ increase in SVC flow, RVO, and LVO as MID after DCC compared to UCM or ICC. For cerebral hemodynamics, we considered a $5\%$ increase in regional cerebral oxygenation as MID after DCC compared to UCM or ICC. Tables for “Summary of findings” were prepared using the GRADE pro Guideline Development Tool [25].  



# Results  

A total of 1484 studies were retrieved from different databases: Medline (366), Embase (501), and CENTRAL (617), and 15 studies were retrieved from other sources. In total, 783 duplicate studies were excluded, leading to 716 studies. The titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers, leading to 52 full-text articles. Fifteen articles were selected for inclusion in the analysis based on our predefined inclusion criteria, as shown in Fig. 1.

# ROB assessment  

We assessed ROB in all included studies using ROB 2.0, which showed some concerns and a high ROB in most of the included studies. The primary source of bias was mainly due to the lack of blinding to those administering the intervention, the assessors of outcomes, and deviations from the intended intervention. Figure 2 shows ROB in each study by domain for the included outcomes. Detailed comments to support each decision are presented in the Supplementary File.  



# Effects of interventions and assessment of the evidence  



# SVC flow  

Four studies with 380 patients provided data for SVC flow with DCC compared to ICC (Fig. 3). Meta-analysis showed an increase in the SVC flow in the DCC group when compared with the ICC group $\mathrm{(MD~16.09~ml/kg/min}$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}4.03$ to 28.15). The statistical heterogeneity was low $(I^{2}{=}20\%$ ; $p{=}0.29,$ . The evidence was rated low quality and suggests that DCC may result in a slight increase in SVC flow compared to ICC. Three studies with 207 patients provided data for SVC flow with DCC compared to UCM (Fig. 3). Meta-analysis demonstrated a range of small reduction to moderate increase in SVC flow between the two interventions $\mathrm{MD}8.92\mathrm{ml/kg/min}$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}=-23.26$ to 41.1). The statistical heterogeneity was high $(I^{2}=78\%$ ; $p{=}0.01\$ ), possibly attributed to variations in the methods employed for DCC and UCM across the various studies. The evidence was rated as very low quality. Hence, at present, the evidence is very uncertain about the effect of DCC on SVC flow compared to UCM.  



# LVO  

Only one study with 14 participants (seven in the DCC group and seven in the ICC group) provided data for LVO after DCC compared to ICC, so no meta-analysis was performed for this outcome and rated very low quality (Fig. 3). At present, the evidence is very uncertain about the effect of DCC on LVO compared to ICC. Three studies with 208 patients provided data for LVO after DCC compared to UCM (Fig. 3) with a trivial reduction to the large increase in LVO between the two interventions $\mathrm{(MD25.36ml/kg/}$ min, $95\%\mathrm{CI}=-5.68$ to 56.40). The statistical heterogeneity was not important $(I^{2}{=}33\%$ ; $p{=}0.22,$ , and overall evidence is very low quality and currently very uncertain about the effect of DCC on LVO compared to UCM.

# RVO  

Four studies with 380 patients demonstrated a range of slight reduction to a slight increase in RVO after DCC compared to ICC $\mathrm{(MD-2.09ml/kg/min}$ , $95\%$ $\%~{\calCI}=-26.20$ to 22.02, $I^{2}{=}17\%$ ; $p{=}0.31$ , low quality). At present, DCC may result in little to no difference in RVO compared to ICC with uncertain evidence. Three studies with 208 patients showed a range of large reduction to a trivial reduction in RVO after DCC compared to UCM $\mathrm{(MD-}34.70\mathrm{ml/kg/min}$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}=$ $-68.97$ to − 0.44, $I^{2}{=}20\%$ ; $p{=}0.29$ , very low quality). DCC may reduce RVO when compared to UCM, but the evidence is very uncertain (Fig. 3).  



# rSO2  

Three studies with 111 patients showed a trivial increase in rSO2 with DCC when compared to ICC (MD $4.32\%$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}3.48$ to 5.16, $I^{2}{=}0\%$ ; $p{=}0.78$ , very low quality). The evidence indicates DCC may increase rSO2 compared to ICC, but the evidence is very uncertain (Fig. 3). Only one study with 30 participants (13 in the DCC group and 17 in the UCM group) provided data for rSO2 with DCC compared to UCM, so no meta-analysis was performed for this outcome. The evidence is very uncertain about the effect of DCC on rSCO2 compared to UCM.  



# Mortality before hospital discharge  

The meta-analysis showed a significant reduction in mortality before hospital discharge in the DCC group compared to the ICC group (RR 0.64, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}0.47$ to 0.88). In absolute terms, there was a $3.2\%$ reduction (equivalent to 32 per 1000 patients) in mortality events after DCC compared to ICC. The absolute $95\%$ CI ranged from 47 to 11 fewer per 1000 patients $(-4.7~\mathrm{to}-1.1)$ . This implies a significant benefit without harm considering the MID of $0.5\%$ . There was no clinical or statistical heterogeneity $(I^{2}=0\%$ ; $p{=}0.83)$ . The result was rated as moderate quality suggesting that DCC likely results in a large reduction in mortality before hospital discharge compared to ICC (Fig. 4).  

Four studies with 718 patients provided data for the outcome of mortality before hospital discharge after DCC compared to UCM (RR 1.11, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}0.66$ to 1.86, Fig. 4) In absolute terms, there was a $0.8\%$ increase (equivalent to 8 per 1000 patients) in mortality events after DCC compared to UCM. The absolute $95\%$ CI ranged from 24 fewer to 61 more per 1000 patients $(-2.4\$ to 6.1). This implies the potential for a moderate benefit to significant harm considering the MID of $0.5\%$ . There was no clinical or statistical heterogeneity $(I^{2}{=}0\%$ ; $p{=}0.46,$ ). The evidence was rated as very low quality. Overall, the evidence is very uncertain about the effect of DCC on mortality before hospital discharge compared to UCM.

# Severe IVH  

Six studies with 1885 participants provided data for the outcome of severe IVH after DCC compared to ICC (RR 1.00, $95\%$ $5\%\mathrm{CI}=0.61$ to 1.63, Fig. 4). In absolute terms, there was a $0\%$ reduction in severe IVH events after DCC compared to ICC. The absolute $95\%$ CI ranged from 13 fewer to 22 more per 1000 patients $(-1.3$ to 2.2). This implies the potential for a small benefit to a small harm considering the MID of $0.5\%$ . The statistical heterogeneity was low ${{T}^{2}}\mathrm{{=}}0\%$ ; $\scriptstyle{p=0.45}$ . The evidence was rated low quality and suggests that DCC may result in little to no difference in the outcome of severe IVH compared to ICC.  

Four studies with 709 patients provided data for the outcome of severe IVH after DCC compared to UCM (RR 0.54, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}0.28$ to 1.06, Fig. 4). There was a $3.2\%$ reduction (equivalent to 32 per 1000 patients) in severe IVH events after DCC compared to UCM. The absolute $95\%$ CI ranged from 50 fewer to 4 more per 1000 patients $(-2.4$ to 6.1). This implies the potential for a significant benefit to trivial harm (clinically unimportant harm) considering the MID of $0.5\%$ . The statistical heterogeneity was low ${{T}^{2}}=0\%$ ; $p{=}0.46,$ . The evidence was rated low quality, concluding that DCC may reduce the outcome of severe IVH when compared to UCM.  

We summarize in Tables 2 and 3, current evidence comparing DCC vs ICC, and DCC vs UCM for outcomes of early cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics, mortality, and severe IVH in preterm infants born at $\mathrm{GA}<32\$ weeks.  



# Discussion  

In preterm infants $<32$ weeks’ gestation, DCC may result in a slight increase in SVC flow compared with ICC, but it may result in little to no difference in RVO. By pooling data yielded from 380 patients who were included in four RCTs, we found that the related evidence suffers from the ROB and imprecision as the CI of the pooled estimate crossed the MID of $15\mathrm{ml/kg/min}$ . So, it had a low level of certainty. On the other hand, the evidence is very uncertain about the effect of DCC on LVO compared to ICC. Similarly, we found the certainty of evidence for increasing rSO2 after DCC compared to ICC was very low. Pooled data from three RCTs done on 111 patients showed that DCC may increase rSO2 compared to ICC, but the evidence is very uncertain. In contrast to the findings related to cerebral and cardiac hemodynamics, our analysis revealed that the level of evidence regarding mortality before hospital discharge after DCC compared to ICC was of moderate certainty. Pooled data from six RCTs done on 2191 patients showed a moderate level of certainty in the evidence indicating that DCC compared to ICC, likely results in a large reduction in mortality before hospital discharge. However, for the outcome of severe IVH, the evidence was rated low and suggests that DCC results in little to no difference in the outcome of severe IVH compared to ICC. There were five RCTs investigating the effect of DCC compared to UCM in preterm infants $<32$ weeks. For the outcomes of cerebral and cardiac hemodynamics, the certainty of the evidence was rated as very low because of the ROB across all the studies and the very serious imprecision. This was mainly due to the small sample size, a low number of events, and the wide confidence interval around the pooled estimate, which crossed the MID. For the outcomes of severe IVH, the evidence suggests that DCC may reduce severe IVH compared to UCM; the certainty of the evidence was rated as low because of ROB and serious imprecision.

However, for the outcomes of mortality, the evidence is very uncertain about the effect of DCC on mortality before hospital discharge compared to UCM. This uncertainty arises from similar concerns about bias and very serious imprecision in the available data.  

The inclusion criteria and population were consistent across the studies, primarily including infants born before 32 weeks [12, 16, 34]. Most trials commonly exclude participants with major congenital anomalies, placental or cord anomalies, or monochorionic multiples. The rationale behind excluding this population lies in the fact that they are ineligible for enhanced placental transfusion. Based on these observations, the results of the review can be generalized to the real-world clinical setting. However, we could not evaluate the effect of enhanced placental transfusion on cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics in preterm infants $<28$ weeks because we found no RCT done in this age group assessing these outcomes, which limits the applicability of the evidence in those fragile and high-risk populations. Our review is limited by the absence of protocol registration.

The main strength of our study is that this is a unique systematic review that pooled the data of RCTs exclusively done to assess the effect of enhanced placental transfusion on cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics. We tried to decrease the risk of publication bias in this review to the minimum by searching the related articles in all major databases and clinical trial registries. We did not limit our search to any specific language or region. All processes of article selection, screening, and ROB were done by two reviewers. One of the important limitations of this review is the high ROB in the majority of included trials due to the lack of blinding among participants, those administering the intervention, and assessors of outcomes, as well as deviations from the intended intervention. Another limitation is the low number of studies done exclusively to assess the outcomes of cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics in preterm infants $<32$ weeks and most of the included studies have smaller sample sizes than the optimal information sizes which caused imprecision while evaluating the level of evidence. Additionally, variations were observed in the techniques employed for UCM and in the timing of DCC. This led us to use a randomeffects model for the meta-analysis, as opposed to a fixedeffects model. Also, there was an insufficient number of trials to conduct subgroup analysis, particularly for the outcomes of cerebral and cardiac hemodynamics. There were several estimations conducted to pool the data [23]. Lastly, the threshold for minimally important differences has relied on expert opinion.  

We could not find any systematic review of RCTs done on preterm infants assessing the outcomes of cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics after DCC to compare. Fogarty et al. [13] performed a systematic review of 18 RCTs, compared DCC with ICC in 2834 preterm infants $<37$ weeks, and concluded that DCC significantly lowered the risk of all-cause mortality before discharge. However, there were no reductions in any other neonatal morbidities, including severe IVH. While the population included in their review differs somewhat from ours, our analysis results also suggest similar findings. Despite the low certainty of the evidence regarding the effect of DCC on SVC flow and rSO2 compared to ICC, this significant reduction in mortality may be associated with the increase in SVC flow and rSO2 after DCC. This is in line with earlier studies highlighting the predictive value of SVC flow in determining mortality and other significant neonatal morbidities. [4–6] Controversy exists regarding the safety of DCC in non-vigorous preterm infants who need immediate resuscitation with concerns of delay in stabilizing ventilation. UCM has been suggested as an alternative to DCC. However, the evidence pertaining to its safety and efficacy in very preterm infants is still lacking. [35] Balasubramanian et al. performed a systematic review of RCTs and compared UCM with DCC in preterm infants $<37$ weeks and found no significant effect on mortality with UCM when compared with DCC, however, UCM significantly increased the risk of severe IVH in preterm infants (4 RCTs; 718 infants; low-certainty evidence) [15]. This finding was primarily driven by the results of a multicentre non-inferiority trial [14] involving preterm infants born at less than 32 weeks GA randomized to UCM or DCC at birth; however, the trial was prematurely terminated, since the first interim analysis revealed a significantly increased risk of severe IVH with UCM among infants born at less than 28 weeks of gestation. In our review we compared DCC to UCM and the evidence suggests that DCC may reduce severe IVH compared to UCM (RR 0.54, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}{=}0.28$ to 1.06), in absolute terms $3.2\%$ fewer (5 fewer to 0.4 more; low-certainty evidence).  



# Conclusion  

DCC improves outcomes in preterm infants $<32$ weeks when compared with ICC, as indicated by the significant reduction in mortality and increase in SVC flow and rSO2 (moderate and low-quality evidence). There is low and very low-quality evidence to suggest little to no difference in cardiac outputs and severe IVH after DCC compared to ICC. Similarly, there is low and very low-quality evidence to suggest little to no difference in cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics and mortality after DCC compared to UCM. DCC may reduce the outcome of severe IVH compared to UCM (low-quality evidence). Well-designed, multicenter trials with adequate power assessing the effect of enhanced placental transfusion on early cardiac and cerebral hemodynamics particularly, comparing DCC to UCM are warranted.
