# Enhancing the Informed Consent Process Using Shared Decision Making and Consent Refusal Data from the CLEAR III Trial

# Abstract  

Background:  The process of informed consent in National Institutes of Health randomized, placebo-controlled trials is poorly studied. There are several issues regarding informed consent in emergency neurologic trials, including a shared decision-making process with the patient or a legally authorized representative about overall risks, benefits, and alternative treatments.  

Methods:  To evaluate the informed consent process, we collected best and worst informed consent practice information from a National Institutes of Health trial and used this in medical simulation videos to educate investigators at multiple sites to improve the consent process. Clot Lysis: Evaluating Accelerated Resolution of Intraventricular Hemorrhage Phase III (CLEAR III) (clinicaltrials.gov, NCT00784134) studied the effect of intraventricular alteplase $(n=251$ ) versus saline (placebo) injections $'n=249)$ for intraventricular hemorrhage reduction. Reasons for ineligibility (including refusing to consent) for all screen failures were analyzed. The broadcasted presentation outlined best practices for doctor–patient interactions during the consenting process, as well as anecdotal, study-specific reasons for consent refusal. Best and worst consent elements were then incorporated into a simulation video to enhance the informed consent process. This video was disseminated to trial sites as a webinar around the midpoint of the trial to improve the consent process. Pre- and post-intervention consent refusals were compared.  

Results:  During the trial, 10,538 patients were screened for eligibility, of which only three were excluded due to trial timing. Pre-intervention, 77 of 5686 $1.40\%$ ) screen eligible patients or their proxies refused consent. Post-intervention, 55 of 4849 $(1.10\%)$ refused consent, which was not significantly different from pre-intervention $\primeP{=}0.312.$ ). The incidence of screen failures was significantly lower post-intervention ( $'P=0.006)$ , possibly due to several factors for patient exclusion.  

Conclusion:  The informed consent process for prospective randomized trials may be enhanced by studying and refining best practices based on trial-specific plans and patient concerns particular to a study.  

Keywords:  Best practice, Consent, Randomized controlled trial, Simulation  



# Introduction  

Randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trials are fundamental to advancing science by measuring different effects between two arms of a study [1]. However, the informed consent process becomes challenging for the patient in emergency neurologic trials, since the patient has neurologic damage and may not be able to understand the risk versus benefit ratio of a randomized intervention. Furthermore, the patient’s legally authorized representative, or proxy, may not have healthcare-related decision-making authorization or may not be psychologically prepared or have the medical literacy to ask questions about the goals of care in emergency situations to fully understand the risks and benefits to speak confidently on the patient’s behalf. These are just a few factors inherent to the shared decision-making (SDM) process that can lead to failure to obtain consent in randomized trials. In addition, there is a perception that placebo is inferior to the trial treatment arm intervention. The impact of these concerns is likely underestimated and poorly quantified in many randomized, placebocontrolled trials, and may be a reason for reluctance or outright refusal to consent and enroll in a trial, and these consent refusals are costly in the sense that they limit effective recruitment in randomized trials [2, 3]. The consent process, in terms of the elements discussed with the patient (proxy) that lead to successful or failed consent, also remains poorly studied [4–7], despite a considerable amount of National Institutes of Health funding for randomized clinical trials. We hypothesized that identifying study-specific reasons for informed consent failure during a prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial could provide best practice information that could be later used to enhance the informed consent process using medical simulation.  



# Methods  



# Study Design and Participants  

The Clot Lysis: Evaluating Accelerated Resolution of Intraventricular Hemorrhage Phase III (CLEAR III) trial was a prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled multicenter trial (clinicaltrials.gov, NCT00784134) investigating the effect of intraventricular alteplase $\overset{\prime}{n}=251,$ ) versus saline (placebo) injections $(n=249)$ for intraventricular hemorrhage reduction and clinical outcomes. Access to the study treatment (alteplase) was only available to the patient through enrollment in the CLEAR III trial. Further patient inclusion and exclusion $\mathrm{(I/E)}$ criteria can be found in the original CLEAR III trial article [8]. The primary hypothesis was that an intervention using the actual screening trial data of best and worst informed consent collected in the trial could be used as an intervention to reduce informed consent refusal in eligible patients or via their proxy.  



# Data Collection  

Screen and consent failure reasons were collected prospectively as part of the trial in an electronic data capture system, which asked patients (proxies) to provide one reason from preselected drop-down menu options, including an option for “other,” where the failure can be defined using free text. Data by menu options were categorized in groups, and similar free text options were allowed for consent refusal. One preselected option was “refused consent.” Screening was defined similar other trials in which sites review a subject candidacy for a trial based on I/E criteria. Screen failure is defined as the conversion of subjects screened and excluded based on I/E criteria similar to other trials [2, 3]. The term enrollment yield is sometimes used to describe the yield of screening to actual consent and study enrollment into a trial. Screen failure reasons were ranked in terms of absolute frequency among all data collected from the screening process. One major interest in this trial was patients deemed study eligible (screened and eligible by I/E criteria) who later refused consent into the trial which is defined as consent refusal [9], which is a subset of all screen failures. The clinical trial committee formulated responses to patient (proxy) ethical, placebo-related questions and other failed consent questions and reviewed the literature for evidence-based, best-practice informed consent elements.  



# Medical Simulation  

Using prospective frequency data from the trial, we ranked the top ten most successful informed consent elements (Table  1) and the top ten reasons for consent refusal (Table 2). We then wrote two scenarios to convey the best and worst elements of informed decision making observed in the trial. With a medical simulation actor performing the role of the patient (proxy) and a site principle investigator (W.D.F.), we recorded and edited two videos (Videos 1 and 2) for dissemination via webinar hosted by the study center at Johns Hopkins University to the other 55 trial sites. In addition, we presented a best informed consent PowerPoint (Video 3) during a webinar.

# Results  

CLEAR III ultimately screened 10,535 patients who met criteria within the time frame allowed for symptom onset. Of these patients, 8575 $(81.4\%)$ did not meet study inclusion criteria. Among the remaining 1960 patients, 132 $(6.7\%)$ refused consent (i.e., consent refusal), which is $1.3\%$ of all screened patients (132/10,535). Further stratification of the trial population is shown in Fig. 1. Further patient demographic data for the full study population can be found in the CLEAR III article [8]. The number of patients excluded was measured pre- and post-intervention. Patients were excluded from the study for a variety of reasons, including medical conditions that were not favorable for the study intervention, staffing inadequacies, and consent refusal (Box  1). Among the 5686 patients screened prior to the June 21, 2012, webcast of the simulated video consent, 77 $\left(1.4\%\right)$ refused consent (consent refusal). Of the 4849 screened after the webinar, 55 $(1.1\%)$ refused consent. The difference between preand post-intervention consent refusals was not statistically significant $(P=0.31\$ ) (Table 2 and Box 1). However, our study may have been underpowered to detect a significant difference, since consent refusal was a relatively low-frequency event. There was a statistically significant lower incidence of overall screen failures after the webinar intervention $\left(P=0.006\right)$ (Table  2) which may represent overall improved trial enrollment yield.

# Discussion  

To our knowledge, the CLEAR III trial was the first randomized neurocritical care clinical trial to examine its own informed consent process, and in particular report its screen failure rates and consent refusals in order to educate enrolling sites about data-driven best practice elements to improve the informed consent process. While our original hypothesis (consent refusal) did not meet statistical significance and was lower than our original anecdotal concerns, we did glean important knowledge about the informed consent process itself in a large multicenter randomized clinical trial, and how this might enhance future randomized trial consent methods and clinical trial enrollment.  

Overall, few studies have scientifically measured their informed consent process elements [2, 9], and even fewer directly link these elements with consent success rates [10], consent refusal. The FAST MAG investigators did analyze their prehospital stroke enrollment based on I/E criteria and were able to enroll about $40\%$ . The Vancouver Stroke Project [9] analyzed consent failure and refusal rates and, interestingly, found that there are many reasons patients or their surrogates refuse enrollment into a clinical trial, some being similar to the concerns of “wanting surgery” over a placebo (i.e., seen as inferior to medical or observation). There were similar major concerns early in the CLEAR III trial, since it involved a minimally invasive brain catheter injection procedure similar to surgery (i.e., perceived as more than placebo), and concerns that alteplase was more effective than saline as placebo, even though that was the very purpose of the Phase III trial. Therefore, the steering committee felt to minimize this bias, and we should collect reasons for consent refusal, as well as factors that seemed associated with improving enrollment yield (e.g., best consent elements). Many psychosocial and SDM factors can impact the informed consent process beyond factual information about a clinical trial. Training on communication techniques has been shown to increase consent rates in organ procurement processes [10]. Patients prefer their physician to sit down during the informed consent process, and see this body language as more compassionate and encouraging [11]. Patients also favor a friendly and open communicator [12], with a reassuring attitude [13]. Patients who understand the study characteristics are more likely to be satisfied with the consent process and provide consent [14]. Use of multimedia decision aids during the informed consent process, as opposed to traditional inperson verbal informed consent, may improve patient understanding and consent rates [15–18]. Even though our study analyzed the top ten elements of good and poor informed consent during the trial, it is important to recognize there are still more factors and dimensions to the complex, relational informed consent process, including socioeconomic status, health literacy, and time constraints inherent to emergency trials compared to trials in the outpatient setting.  

Other literature has emerged highlighting the presumptive approach to obtaining informed consent, primarily in the organ donation process. This approach emphasizes the initial assumption that consent will be achieved from the patient’s medical legal proxy. Presumptive consent uses language with an underlying tone that participation should not be a difficult decision when it is the “right thing to do,” such as donating an organ to help another patient in need. We believe that most randomized trials obtain consent under a value-neutral approach when there is an intervention and a placebo arm that are seen as relatively equivalent. However, this value-neutral approach may still seem unbalanced to the patient (proxy). For example, the placebo option could be perceived as inferior to whatever active intervention is being offered in a trial. In contrast, a presumptive informed consent approach could shift consent conversations toward a seemingly morally altruistic decision to join a clinical trial, fulfilling an innate desire to help others to find a scientific answer and help future patients in a similar condition [19]. Nevertheless, this presumptive approach is not without some criticism [20–23]. In emergency treatment trials, a presumptive informed consent approach may be confusing to those of low health literacy in early phase clinical trials for which the benefit of an intervention is less well known. Similarly in phase III clinical trials, the intervention itself may seem superior against placebo based on promising phase I/II data, especially when the placebo is seen as “doing nothing.” We did not employ a presumptive approach in our study, but instead used real-world factors gleaned from the clinical trial itself to make improvements to the consent process. Other aspects of our best practices process included allowing the patient (proxy) to ask open-ended questions, emphasizing the study’s relevance, and exploring apprehensions the patient, family or proxy might have about potential risks.  

As patients and proxies declined trial participation, clinical trial personnel formulated suggested balanced or best element responses to the most common reasons for refusal in an attempt to improve the consent interaction as the trial progressed. In time-restricted, intense medical situations, it was thought to be beneficial for trial personnel to have a framework with which to respond to patients and their families when barriers to consent arose. We hoped to combat cognitive overloading in helping patients and proxies better understand the elements of the trial that they were most concerned about. Examples to be explored are shown in Table 2 but uncertain of many of the significant $P$ value variables playing a role in consent refusal or not (e.g., DNR status, mycotic aneurysm). Therefore, this requires further exploration and better capture of exclusion and reasons for consent refusion used in this study.  

Anecdotally, trial-site investigators in this study reported that many consent refusals were related to the perception of the inferiority of placebo (Table  1). Whether or not this reflected some unconscious bias of the site principal investigator from the earlier CLEAR II data or represented the “inferiority of placebo” perception by the patient’s proxy remains unknown. The counterargument to the inferior placebo perception by patients (or proxies) to the investigational drug is not entirely rational, and was in fact the purpose of the trial. Also, we cannot be entirely certain that the placebo arm was framed in the consent process like another form of intervention against alteplase intervention. In retrospect, framing the placebo arm as intraventricular saline irrigation might have been a better psychological tactic for equipoise than calling it purely placebo. Another issue is that decision makers may be uncomfortable with the idea of randomization being in control of choosing between two options, as the patient may not wish to receive one of the two options. This underscores the reason randomization is performed, which is to remove unidirectional bias that can confound the ultimate outcomes of a clinical trial. Study coordinators and principal investigators may need to communicate that the placebo arm is critical to measuring treatment differences and that there are other benefits to participation in a clinical trial, including “collateral benefits” such as longer term follow-up and “aspirational benefits” such as the ability to contribute to the scientific knowledge base [24, 25].  

Our study used National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke randomized clinical trial informed consent data to enhance the informed consent process using medical simulation videos and various psychosocial and logistical factors. We aimed to address difficult-to-measure variables to help us understand why study enrollment rates may not be as high as desired. Further exploration of subjective measures, such as perceived friendliness, hurriedness, and team cohesiveness, could be performed in the future to determine how a randomized, placebocontrolled trial is perceived by patients and caregivers. Further investigation of consent elements and improving the SDM informed consent process using provider interventions, such as simulated demonstrations, is critical to further develop the process of enrolling patients in clinical trials, maximize study population diversity, and increase study recruitment.  

The strengths of this study include a large prospective study population (10,535 patients initially), as well as collection of the most common best practice and failed consent elements in this trial (Box 2, Table 2). Data showed an overall lower incidence of screen failures which could be due to several patient- and site-specific factors inherent to patient I/E criteria. We are uncertain of the meaning of this finding unless it represents either better clinical referrals from other institutions into CLEAR III trial sites or improved patient screening by trial personnel as the study progressed over time. There was no significant decrease in consent refusal post presentation, despite sharing suggested best practice consent methods.  

There are several limitations inherent to our study, the most important being the midpoint interventional design and failure to reject our empiric hypothesis based on the low incidence of consent refusal $(1.4\%)$ . However, a relative strength of this study is the large multicenter design examining several facets of the consent process itself, which are important for future randomized controlled trial design. A major challenge in randomized trials for patients (proxies) in SDM is the placebo arm, which may be perceived as inferior treatment or outcome. However, we noted that one reason for consent refusal was worsening intracranial bleeding, which within the placebo arm presumably would have counter balanced this placebo arm risk. Yet, despite having potentially higher bleeding, alteplase risk might have been seen with a higher chance of success of intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) thrombolysis and better outcome. This creates a complex risk vs benefit equation for decision making, where the placebo arm carries some bleeding risk (lower than alteplase), but with unclear IVH thrombolysis benefit compared to alteplase, which has higher benefit of clearing IVH products, but higher risk. Therefore, the “value proposition” for either option may have seemed too risky to some who refused consent and instead perhaps wished to “opt out” of the trial or ask the physician for a third option outside of the trial.  

Another possible limitation is the implementation of the consent intervention around the midpoint of a clinical trial. While the best practice information was shared to all sites and an informal quiz done at the end after the video displays, we did not retain the information among the sites which may or may have not performed better after such trial update online meeting and will consider doing that going forward. Ironically, to study the effect of sharing informed consent best practice knowledge and obtaining formal surveyed feedback from each site PI and coordinator likely would require a separate consent process in the future before joining a multicenter trial. Admittedly, studying the informed consent process of the informed consent process we must acknowledge on some level the possibility of the Hawthorne effect, in which “studying something changes the thing you are studying.” Unfortunately since the trial concluded, we cannot practically go back and further analyze several of these factors on consent screening and refusal. However, this knowledge could be used in a future clinical trial to measure the effect of our intervention on the consent process. Despite these limitations, we felt the design was unique and practical, since it used the randomized trial data available to potentially improve the informed consent process we found lacking in other randomized trials. As consent refusal was not statistically altered post-intervention, it is possible that the designed intervention was not effective or that consent refusal could have been impacted significantly if a higher percentage of trial site personnel could have participated in our midpoint presentation. Other limitations include the lack of a standardized clinical decision aid beyond the educational materials provided at the intervention and post-intervention surveys from the sites about use and implementation; we will plan to add these study tools in future randomized trials. We will also incorporate the growing literature around SDM to create standardized decision aids in the beginning of future trials, similar to creating study consent forms, and checkpoints for efficacy of such tools going forward.  

To our knowledge, this was the first use of video simulation to enhance a National Institutes of Health-funded study to reduce consent refusal rates. Although our data were derived from a large, prospective study, the simulation intervention was initiated more than halfway into the study design, making outcomes challenging to interpret. We cannot explain the decrease in screen failure rate described other than the Hawthorne effect, or influence on behavior secondary to awareness of being observed, impacting the ability to apply the I/E criteria.

# Conclusion  

Informed consent is a complex medical SDM process from both a medical researcher standpoint and a patient (proxy) standpoint. Further prospective SDM interventions and process measurements are needed to improve trial recruitment.
