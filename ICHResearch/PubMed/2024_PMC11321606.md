# Association of Fluid Balance and Hemoglobin Decline With Neurological Outcome After Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage\*  

OBJECTIVES: To explore the relationship between fluid balance and hemoglobin decline with secondary infarctions and neurologic outcome in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) patients.  

DESIGN: Secondary analysis of the Earlydrain trial, a prospective randomized controlled study investigating prophylactic lumbar drain use in aSAH patients.  

SETTING: Patients with aSAH treated in ICUs at 19 tertiary hospitals in Germany, Switzerland, and Canada.  

PATIENTS: From January 2011 to January 2016, 287 patients were enrolled in the Earlydrain trial. Only files with complete information on both daily hemoglobin and balance values were used, leaving 237 patients for analysis.  

INTERVENTIONS: Investigation of fluid balance management and hemoglobin levels during the initial 8 days post-aSAH to establish thresholds for unfavorable outcomes and assess their impact on secondary infarctions and 6-month neurologic outcome on the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).  

MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS: Patients with unfavorable outcome after 6 months $({\mathsf{mRS}}>2)$ ) showed greater hemoglobin decline and increased cumulative fluid balance. A significant inverse relationship existed between fluid balance and hemoglobin decline. Thresholds for unfavorable outcome were $10.4\mathfrak{g}/$ dL hemoglobin and $4894\mathrm{mL}$ cumulative fluid balance in the first 8 days. In multivariable analysis, fluid balance, but not fluid intake, remained significantly associated with unfavorable outcome, while the influence of hemoglobin lessened. Fluid balance but not hemoglobin related to secondary infarctions, with the effect being significant after inverse probability of treatment weighting. Transfusion was associated with unfavorable outcomes.  

CONCLUSIONS: Increased fluid balance influences hemoglobin decline through hemodilution. Fluid overload, rather than a slight decrease in hemoglobin levels, appears to be the primary factor contributing to poor outcomes in aSAH patients. The results suggest aiming for euvolemia and that a modest hemoglobin decline may be tolerated. It may be advisable to adopt a restrictive approach to transfusions, as they can potentially have a negative effect on outcome.

# KEY POINTS  

Question: This study explores the interplay between hemoglobin decline, fluid balance, and neurologic outcome in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage patients.  

Findings: Patients with unfavorable outcome (modified Rankin Scale $>2$ ) displayed more significant hemoglobin decline and increased fluid balance. Fluid balance, impacting hemoglobin through hemodilution, correlated with worse neurologic outcomes and secondary infarctions, while the influence of hemoglobin lessened. Transfusions were linked to unfavorable outcomes.  

Meaning: Fluid overload, rather than slight hemoglobin decline, appears to be the primary contributor to poor outcomes. The results suggest targeting euvolemia and tolerating modest hemoglobin decline. Restrictive transfusion approaches may be prudent, as they could negatively impact outcomes.

Therefore, maintaining euvolemia is commonly recommended, which may require careful fluid augmentation in patients with true hypovolemia to sustain adequate circulation (5). Fluid augmentation, coupled with vasopressors, may additionally be necessary to achieve induced hypertension, which remains an integral part of the treatment strategy to prevent cerebral ischemia in patients with aSAH (10, 11). Surprisingly, the interaction between liberal fluid administration and hemoglobin decline did receive limited attention and causal relations remain uncertain.  

It remains unclear whether hypervolemia, through its dilution effects, can trigger a decline in hemoglobin levels and whether anemia acts as a surrogate parameter for fluid overload rather than an independent predictor of poor neurologic outcome (12). Previous studies, including a secondary analysis of the CONSCIOUS-1 study, a phase 2 trial assessing the impact of clazosentan on angiographic vasospasm in patients with aSAH, focused separately on hemoglobin levels and fluid administration (6, 13). However, the potential influence of a patient’s fluid balance on the decrease in hemoglobin levels and an explanation for this decline was not recognized. Limited understanding of the relationship between liberal fluid management and anemia has led to significant variations in transfusion and fluid management (14, 15). Packed RBC (PRBC) transfusions had been associated with organ dysfunction and increased mortality, and decision-making on treating anemia after aSAH and transfusion remains controversial (12, 16, 17).  

The Earlydrain study was a randomized controlled trial investigating the prophylactic use of lumbar drains in patients with aSAH (18). In the current analysis, we investigated daily treatment data from Earlydrain on the relationship of daily and cumulative fluid balance, fluid intake, and hemoglobin decline on the occurrence of secondary infarctions and neurologic longterm outcomes. Second, we considered the potential adverse effect of PRBC transfusions as means of hemoglobin augmentation.  

# METHODS  

# Study Design  

We conducted a secondary analysis on 287 participants from the Earlydrain trial, a prospective, multicenter, open-label randomized study across 19 hospitals in Germany, Switzerland, and Canada (18). The trial included adults 18 years old or older with aSAH confirmed by CT. Intracranial aneurysms were verified by CT angiography or digital subtraction angiography. Aneurysm treatment (coiling or clipping) had to be performed within 48 hours, following local standards and international guidelines. Patients were then randomized to the additional use of a lumbar drain or standard of care only. Earlydrain is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT01258257). Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Erlangen (vote no. 4171, June 16, 2010), with additional approval from each participating center’s ethics board. Procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.  

# Data Collection  

Data were obtained from the Earlydrain database, encompassing baseline demographic and care process information from the initial 8 days, and descriptive radiological images and 6-month outcomes. Variables included daily hemoglobin levels, fluid intake, and fluid balance. In case of neurologic deterioration due to cerebral ischemia or high flow velocities on transcranial Doppler, digital subtraction angiography was performed to confirm vasospasm. Secondary infarctions, not evident on postprocedural CT scans after initial treatment of the aneurysm, either through microsurgical or endovascular intervention, were diagnosed using the last cerebral imaging (CT or MRI) before acute care discharge. For the secondary analysis, the 6-month functional outcome assessed with the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) was dichotomized as 0–2 (favorable) or 3–6 (unfavorable). Adherence to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational study reporting was ensured (19).  

# Statistical Analysis  

Numeric variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and categorical variables as absolute and relative frequencies. Imputation of missing values was not performed. Differences in demographic, radiological, and clinical factors were calculated using Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Pearson chi-square test.  

A linear mixed-effect model was used to investigate hemoglobin and fluid management over the first 8 days. Correlations between hemoglobin, fluid intake, and fluid balance were determined using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were used to define hemoglobin, cumulative fluid intake, and balance levels on day 8 to predict poor clinical outcomes on the dichotomized mRS after 6 months.  

Generalized estimation equation (GEE) models with an independent correlation structure evaluated the impact of hemoglobin, fluid intake, fluid balance, and PRBC transfusion on neurologic outcome and secondary infarctions, adjusting for relevant confounders. The quasi-likelihood information criterion was used to select the best fitting model and the working correlation structure in the GEE models (20). Interaction terms between variables were probed, but showed no model improvement and therefore were not included. The GEE models were adjusted for age, Hunt & Hess (HH) grade greater than 3 at admission, presence or absence of intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and the use of an early lumbar drainage. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine multicollinearity among variables. Variables exhibiting correlation coefficients greater than 0.7 were excluded from further analysis.  

We performed a sensitivity analysis with inverse probability of treatment weighting using logistic regression-derived propensity scores to account for the possibility that patients with a more severe disease course may receive increased fluid volumes. Covariates included age, sex, HH grade greater than 3, presence or absence of IVH and ICH, utilization of an early lumbar drainage, mean hemoglobin level, and patient intubation status. Propensity scores were calculated to estimate the likelihood of patients experiencing increased fluid balance status, defined as exceeding the threshold values determined in the ROC analysis. These scores were then used to recalculate the effect of hemoglobin, fluid intake, and fluid balance on neurologic outcome and secondary infarcts. Subsequently, propensity scores were recalculated to estimate the probability of patients receiving transfusions, and the effect of transfusions on neurologic outcomes and secondary infarcts was assessed.  

Results are displayed as odds ratio (OR) with $95\%$ CIs. All statistics were performed with $\textrm{R4.1.0}$ (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Significance level was set at $\boldsymbol{p}$ value of less than 0.05 for all models.  

# RESULTS  

# Patient Characteristics  

This is a secondary analysis on the 287 patients in the Earlydrain study that were enrolled from January 2011 to January 2016. Only files with complete information on both daily hemoglobin and balance values were used, leaving 237 patients for analysis. The median age was 54 (IQR, 48–63) with a male $(73/237,31\%)$ to female $(164/237,69\%)$ ratio of 1:2.2. Detailed patient characteristics are shown in Table 1.  

# Correlation of Hemoglobin Decline, Cumulative Fluid Balance, and Cumulative Fluid Intake  

To investigate potential links between hemoglobin level decline, fluid balance, and fluid intake, we analyzed the trends in patients’ hemoglobin levels, cumulative fluid balance, and cumulative fluid intake. The median hemoglobin decline was $-0.12{\bfg}/{\bfdL}/{\bfd}$ (IQR, $-0.26$ to $0.01\mathrm{g/dL/d}.$ ), with significantly steeper declines in patients with unfavorable neurologic outcomes (mRS scores, 3–6) compared with those with favorable outcomes (mRS scores, 0–2), measuring $-0.20\mathrm{g/dL/d}$ (IQR, $-0.34$ to $-0.06\mathrm{g/dL/d)}$ vs. $-0.09\mathrm{g/\Omega}$ dL/d (IQR, $-0.21$ to $0.05\mathrm{g/dL/d)}$ (Fig. 1A; $p<0.001\$ ). The median increase in cumulative fluid balance was $446\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{d}$ (IQR, $110{-}1017\mathrm{mL/d)}$ . Patients with mRS 3–6 exhibited a significantly higher slope in cumulative fluid balance $\left(659\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{d}\right.$ ; IQR, $332\mathrm{-}1262\mathrm{mL/d},$ ) compared with those with mRS scores 0–2 $\mathrm{(331mL/d}$ ; IQR, $39{-}792\mathrm{mL/d}_{.}$ ) (Fig. 1B; $p<0.001.$ ). The median increase in cumulative fluid intake was $4332\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{d}$ (IQR, $3749-5037\mathrm{mL/d};$ ), with no difference between outcome groups $(\mathrm{mRS}3–6;4454\mathrm{mL/d}$ [IQR, $3875{-}5067\mathrm{mL/d}]$ and mRS 0–2: $4232\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{d}$ [IQR, $3619{-}5004\mathrm{mL/d}]$ ; $p=0.13\$ ) (Table 1 and Fig. 1C). Patients with worse neurologic outcomes had a more prolonged duration of noradrenaline usage (7 d [IQR, 4–8 d] vs. 4 d [IQR, 1–8 d]; $p<0.001\$ ) (Table 1). Both individual hemoglobin declines and cumulative fluid balance increases demonstrated a significant inverse correlation (Pearson correlation coefficient, $-0.41$ ; CI, $-0.51$ to $-0.30$ ; $p<0.001\$ ), as did the absolute daily values of hemoglobin and cumulative fluid balance (Pearson correlation coefficient, –0.36; CI, $-0.41$ to –0.32; $p<0.001,$ ) (Fig. 2A). We found no significant correlation observed between daily hemoglobin values and fluid intake (Pearson correlation coefficient, 0.04; CI, $-0.01$ to 0.09; $p=0.10\AA,$ , nor between individual hemoglobin declines and cumulative fluid intake increases (Pearson correlation coefficient, –0.03; CI, –0.16 to 0.10; $p=0.65)$ (Fig. 2B).

# ROC Thresholds for Unfavorable Neurologic Outcome $(\mathsf{mRS}>2)$ ) and Secondary Infarctions Following aSAH  

Optimal thresholds of lowest hemoglobin levels recorded during the first 8 days after aSAH were $10.4\mathrm{g/dL}$ (area under the curve [AUC], $62.8\%$ ; specificity, $40.8\%$ ; and sensitivity, $81.1\%$ ) for predicting an unfavorable outcome and $10.4\mathrm{g/dL}$ (AUC, $60.9\%$ ; specificity, $39.5\%$ ; and sensitivity, $82.7\%$ ) for predicting secondary infarctions. Analysis of ROC curves to establish the optimal threshold for the cumulative fluid balance 8 days after aSAH revealed a threshold of $4894\mathrm{mL}$ (AUC, $69.2\%$ ; specificity, $71.4\%$ ; and sensitivity, $58.9\%$ ) associated with poor neurologic outcome and $5873\mathrm{mL}$ (AUC, $65.3\%$ ; specificity, $76.5\%$ ; and sensitivity, $57.3\%$ ) associated with the development of secondary infarctions (Fig. 3). ROC curves assessing thresholds for cumulative fluid intake are shown in Supplement Figure 1 (http://links.lww.com/CCM/H545).

# Hemoglobin Decline, Cumulative Fluid Balance, and the Development of Secondary Infarctions  

The development of secondary infarctions after aSAH is a major predictor and the most important preventable cause of poor neurologic outcome. On univariate regression analysis, higher hemoglobin levels were significantly associated with a reduced risk for secondary infarctions at the time of discharge (OR, 0.83; CI, 0.73– 0.95; $p=0.005^{\cdot}$ ), but not on the adjusted model (OR, 0.91; CI, 0.78–1.05; $p=0.2^{\cdot}$ ). Elevated fluid balance significantly increased the risk for new infarction at discharge on unadjusted univariate analysis $(\mathrm{OR},1.2;\mathrm{CI},1.08-1.34\$ ; $p=0.001,$ ), but misses significance on the adjusted model (OR, 1.11; CI, 0.99–1.25; $p=0.07$ ). The nonsignificant effect of hemoglobin and fluid balance remained consistent after entering both hemoglobin and fluid balance as well as transfusion of PRBC into the same adjusted model. Summary can be found in Table 3.  

After inverse probability of treatment weighting based on the likelihood of patients experiencing a cumulative fluid balance exceeding $5873\mathrm{mL}$ , fluid balance (OR, 1.17; CI, 1.03–1.34; $p=0.02\$ ) showed a significant association with secondary infarctions. The association of hemoglobin, fluid intake, and transfusion with increased risk for secondary infarctions remained without significance (Table 3).  

# DISCUSSION  

We were able to demonstrate an inverse correlation of positive fluid balance and hemoglobin decline after aSAH, suggesting a link between hemoglobin reduction after aSAH and the dilution effects of liberal fluid administration. Positive fluid balance was associated with an increased risk for secondary infarctions and worse neurologic outcome, while the effect oxygenation and anemia is thought to increase the risk for secondary brain injuries due to impaired oxygen delivery (6, 12, 22–24). Various factors contribute to declining hemoglobin in ICU patients, including frequent blood sampling and diagnostic phlebotomy, with daily blood loss ranging from 10 to $377\mathrm{mL}$ . This is particularly relevant in severe cases undergoing frequent blood analysis (25). Impaired erythropoiesis, stemming from inflammation, oxidative stress, and hormonal changes postaSAH, exacerbates anemia (21). Notably, the relationship between liberal fluid administration and dilution anemia has not been explored (26). This is the first study examining the interplay between fluid administration, hemoglobin decline, and their collective impact on secondary infarctions and long-term neurologic outcomes in aSAH patients.

Anemia is a common condition among critically ill patients and hemoglobin concentrations can decline by more than $0.5\mathrm{g/dL/d}$ during the first days on the ICU (21). In patients with aSAH, the prevalence of anemia was reported in $28.3\substack{-82.6\%}$ (12). In neurocritical patients, especially in patients with aSAH, treatment strategies aim at improved cerebral perfusion and brain rologic outcome. The findings align with works indicating a connection between developing anemia in the aftermath of aSAH and unfavorable neurologic outcome (6, 12). However, the association of hemoglobin levels and neurologic outcome lost significance when fluid balance was taken into account. This might be attributed to the inverse relationship between hemoglobin levels and increased fluid balance, suggesting a dilutive effect of liberal fluid administration leading to reduced hemoglobin levels. Therefore, the reduction in hemoglobin might serve as an indicator of augmented fluid intake rather than an independent prognostic factor for worse neurologic outcome.

Prophylactic hypervolemia, once part of the outdated triple H therapy for aSAH, is no longer considered standard practice (12, 27–29). In randomized trials, hypervolemia not only failed to reduce secondary infarctions and improve neurologic outcome but also led to more in-hospital complications such as edema and cardiac failure (30–32). Our study suggests that, in contrast to anemia, fluid balance is the primary factor associated with secondary infarctions and worse long-term neurologic outcomes. Notably, previous retrospective studies on aSAH identified fluid intake, but not fluid balance, to be linked to poor neurologic outcome and delayed cerebral ischemia (5, 7). In contrast to our study, euvolemia was primarily targeted in these works, making it challenging to identify hypervolemia as a direct predictor of adverse neurologic outcome. Instead, significant differences in fluid intake between groups indicated a more unfavorable clinical course. In their sensitivity analysis, fluid balance also showed a significant association when only new infarctions on CT was the outcome (7). In the Earlydrain trial, volume management was at the discretion of local investigators, allowing us to identify hypervolemia as a potential predictor of poor outcome. Fluid balance alone may be a poor indicator of volume status, and therefore, goal-directed hemodynamic management using transpulmonary thermodilution was suggested (7, 9). Nevertheless, prospective data suggests that volumetric parameters depend on age and sex, raising concerns on the use of fixed thresholds for individual treatment decisions in the predominantly younger and female SAH population (33, 34). Higher fluid balance may indicate increased fluid retention in extravascular compartments due to capillary leak, suggesting disease severity. Conversely, polyuria can signal disease severity, too, which may not necessarily be reflected in higher fluid balance, but rather in increased fluid intake (35). It remains unclear which factor is the better predictor, and future studies will need to elucidate this point.  

The correlation of hypervolemia with secondary infarctions after aSAH is believed to arise from cerebral edema, venous congestion with hindered cerebral venous outflow and elevated intracranial pressure (7, 10, 27). However, it is important to note that in patients with aSAH, induced hypertension often remains a crucial treatment approach for those with perfusion deficits or vasospasm (10, 11). In those cases, the increased demand for vasopressors might prompt clinicians to administer more fluids, which should only be done in hypovolemic patients to achieve euvolemia. Considering the patient’s hemodynamic status is important, as excessive vasopressor use with concurrent fluid overload can lead to adverse effects. We observed patients with worse outcomes and more secondary infarctions to have a greater cumulative fluid balance and a longer duration of noradrenaline usage. However, the results on hemodynamic augmentation and induced hypertension remains controversial (11, 36). The HIMALAIA trial, a randomized controlled trial comparing induced hypertension to no induced hypertension in aSAH patients with cerebral ischemia, found no benefit from induced hypertension, while a considerably larger, but retrospective analysis from the same group of authors published in the same year indicates a reduced rate of secondary infarctions in selected patients (37). Although most recommendations are against hypervolemia, there is insufficient evidence for or against induced hypertension, and treatment strategies should be made individually. Our ROC analysis suggests that a modest positive fluid balance is acceptable and possibly essential in critically ill patients. Nevertheless, our findings are in line with treatment strategies maintaining euvolemia (5). These findings are particularly noteworthy as fluid administration stands as one of the few modifiable factors in the aftermath of aSAH.

In contrast to fluid balance, our study emphasizes the secondary role of hemoglobin levels, offering insights into anemia treatment for aSAH patients. Previous analyses present conflicting views on transfusions (13). Although higher hemoglobin levels are associated with improved outcomes, anemia is linked to worse outcomes (38–40). The secondary analysis of the CONSCIOUS-1 study proposed an early transfusion benefit, advocating a liberal transfusion threshold (6). Conversely, transfusions may raise oxidative stress, worsen neuronal injury, disrupt microcirculation, and impact oxygen delivery (38, 41, 42). Our study shows PRBC transfusion significantly associates with worse outcome, suggesting a moderate hemoglobin decrease may be tolerated, and a restrictive PRBC transfusion approach is advisable unless specific triggers are present. The ongoing SAHaRA trial, assessing the impact of PRBC transfusion strategies on outcomes in aSAH patients, might provide more in-depth data on this matter (43).  

Limitations should be acknowledged in interpreting our study. First, data originated from a secondary analysis of Earlydrain, lacking randomization for euvolemia/hypervolemia and transfusions. As a secondary analysis, we did not conduct a priori sample size calculation or power analysis. However, our primary aim was to maximize data utilization, so we included as many patients as possible. Second, our data focuses on the initial 8 days post-aSAH, leaving effects beyond this period unknown. Third, we did not document noradrenaline doses or use echocardiography/ transpulmonary thermodilution for hemodynamic monitoring, limiting a comprehensive assessment of intravascular/extravascular volume status. Most importantly, our study cannot establish causality between increased fluid administration and worse outcomes. Elevated fluid balance may simply indicate a more severe course in these patients. We attempted to address this through a sensitivity analysis. Prospective investigations are needed to explore targeted euvolemia and a restrictive PRBC transfusion approach for improved patient outcomes.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

The hemoglobin decline after aSAH appears to be influenced by the dilution effect of extensive fluid administration. Fluid overload, rather than a slight hemoglobin decline, appears to be the primary factor influencing secondary infarctions and poor neurologic outcomes. Further, the data suggest that a modest hemoglobin decline may be tolerated, and it may be advisable to adopt a more restrictive approach to transfusions. Future research may identify subgroups with different thresholds for fluid administration and transfusion. Until then, aiming for euvolemia and an overall conservative approach to volume management may be warranted.
