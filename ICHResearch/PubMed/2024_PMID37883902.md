# ABSTRACT  

Keywords: Propofol Sevoflurane Desflurane Isoflurane Brain swelling Brain edema  

Study objective: This meta-analysis aimed to compare the risk of brain swelling during craniotomy between propofol-based and volatile-based anesthesia.   
Design: Meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs).   
Setting: Operating room.   
Intervention: Propofol-based anesthesia.   
Patients: Adult patients undergoing craniotomy.   
Measurements: Databases, including EMBASE, MEDLINE, Google Scholar, and Cochrane Library, were searched from inception to April 2023. The primary outcome was the risk of brain swelling, while the secondary outcomes included the impact of anesthetic regimens on surgical and recovery outcomes, as well as the risk of hemody­ namic instability.   
Main results: Our meta-analysis of 17 RCTs showed a significantly lower risk of brain swelling (risk ratio [RR]: 0.85, $p=0.03$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=21\%$ , $n=1976.$ ) in patients receiving propofol than in those using volatile agents, without significant differences in surgical time or blood loss between the two groups. Moreover, propofol was associated with a lower intracranial pressure (ICP) (mean difference: $-4.06\mathrm{mmH}g$ , $p<0.00001$ , $\bar{\Gamma}^{2}=4\bar{4}\%$ $n=409.$ as well as a lower risk of tachycardia $(\mathrm{RR}=0.54\$ , $p=0.005$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ , $n=822.$ ) and postoperative nausea/vomiting (PONV) $(\mathrm{RR}=0.59\$ , $p=0.002$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=19\%$ , $n=1382$ ). There were no significant differences in other recovery outcomes (e.g., extubation time), risk of bradycardia, hypertension, or hypotension between the two groups. Subgroup analysis indicated that propofol was not associated with a reduced risk of brain swelling when  

compared to individual volatile agents. Stratified by craniotomy indications, propofol reduced brain swelling in elective craniotomy, but not in emergency craniotomy (e.g., traumatic brain injury), when compared to volatile anesthetics.  

Conclusions: By reviewing the available evidence, our results demonstrate the beneficial effects of propofol on the risk of brain swelling, ICP, PONV, and intraoperative tachycardia. In emergency craniotomy for traumatic brain injury and subarachnoid hemorrhage, brain swelling showed no significant difference between propofol and volatile agents. Further large-scale studies are warranted for verification.  



# 1. Introduction  

Brain relaxation, defined as the balance between intracranial content volume and intracranial space capacity, plays a critical role in neuro­ surgical procedures [1–3], because achieving optimal brain relaxation may improve surgical exposure, reduce retraction injury, and poten­ tially lead to better patient outcomes [3–6]. Inadequate brain relaxation during craniotomy may be attributed to an increased cerebral blood volume, increased volume of intracranial contents caused by brain le­ sions, and a blockade of cerebrospinal fluid outflow [3,7]. The reported incidence of unsatisfactory intraoperative brain relaxation varied based on the criteria used and the specific patient population studied. For instance, while a survey reported a $0.7\%$ incidence of severe cerebral swelling [8], other investigations have noted incidences ranging from 5.0 to $6.1\%$ for significant or pronounced swelling during elective cra­ niotomies [9,10]. Several maneuvers are employed to promote brain relaxation during surgery, including hyperventilation, drainage of ce­ rebrospinal fluid, administration of intravenous anesthetic agents (e.g., propofol), optimizing venous drainage through patient positioning with the head elevated, and the use of osmotic agents [3,11,12].  

Neurosurgical anesthesia maintenance involves the use of intrave­ nous or volatile anesthetics, each with distinct effects on cerebral vasomotor tone and blood flow [1,13,14]. Propofol can reduce cerebral blood flow (CBF) and cerebral blood volume (CBV) as well as cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen consumption $\mathrm{(CMRO_{2})}$ , potentially providing neuroprotection [1,13,14]. In contrast, inhalational anesthetics, while reducing $\mathrm{{CMRO}_{2}}$ , increase CBF and CBV due to their vasodilatory properties [1,15–17]. Hence, intravenous anesthetic agents like propo­ fol may be considered ideal in neurosurgical anesthesia due to this distinction. However, clinical trials have not consistently demonstrated differences in brain relaxation outcomes between various anesthetic techniques [12,18,19]. For example, a previous study involving 411 patients undergoing elective craniotomy reported similar brain relaxa­ tion scores between patients receiving sevoflurane-based and propofolbased anesthesia [19]. Despite indicating a lower risk of moderate herniation in patients receiving propofol-based anesthesia compared to volatile-based anesthesia, a previous meta-analysis had limitations due to the inclusion of only five randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [20], thus highlighting the need for further research to strengthen the evi­ dence. Recently, several RCTs have investigated the impact of propofolor volatile-based anesthesia on brain relaxation [4,21,22], thereby presenting an opportunity to update the current evidence. Therefore, we conducted this updated systematic review to evaluate the efficacy of propofol-based anesthesia in preventing the occurrence of tense brain in patients undergoing craniotomy, which may provide guidance for clin­ ical care.  



# 2. Methods  

This meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses. In addition, the protocol has been registered with the International Prospective Register of System­ atic Reviews (number CRD42023415731).  



# 2.1. Search strategy  

We conducted a thorough search of Ovid EMBASE, Ovid MEDLINE, and Cochrane Central Register of controlled trials using predefined criteria to find randomized controlled trials (RCTs) related to the impact of anesthetic agents (i.e., propofol- and volatile-based anesthesia) on the risk of brain swelling in adult patients undergoing craniotomy from inception to April 10, 2023. The following keywords were used for the search: (“Neurosurgery” or “Brain surgery” or “Craniotomy” or “Brain operation” or “Brain procedure” or “Intracranial surgery” or “Stereo­ tactic neurosurgery” or “Brain surgery” or “Skull surgery” or “Cranial surgery” or “Intracranial operation” or “Neurosurgery”) and (“Diprivan” or “Disoprivan” or “Propofol” or “Total intravenous anesthesia” or “TIVA” or “Total IV anesthesia” or “Continuous intravenous anesthesia”) and (“Xenon” or “Methoxyflurane” or “Isoflurane” or “Sevoflurane” or “Desflurane” or “Halothane” or “Enflurane” or “Volatile anesthesia” or “Inhaled anesthesia” or “Gaseous anesthesia” or “Gas anesthesia”) and (“Brain herniation” or “Cerebral herniation” or “Transtentorial hernia­ tion” or “Uncal herniation” or “Tonsillar herniation” or “Hydrocepha­ lus” or “Intracranial pressure” or “Intracranial hypertension” or “brain edema” or “brain relaxation score” or “brain swelling” or “dural tension” or “brain relaxation” or “Cerebral Edema”). The search was performed without language restrictions. To ensure thoroughness, we manually searched reference lists of eligible articles and meta-analyses and reached out to the authors of the included studies with missing data up to three times. Details on the used search strategies are presented in Supplemental Table 1. We also searched Google Scholar for additional eligible studies.  



# 2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria  

Studies that met the following criteria were included in the current analysis: (a) population: adult patients aged ${>}18$ years who underwent elective or emergency craniotomy; (b) intervention: propofol-based regimens for anesthetic maintenance irrespective of the propofol dosage (i.e., propofol group); (c) comparison: volatile-based regimens for anesthetic maintenance (i.e., volatile group); and (d) outcomes: overall risk of brain swelling. Other surgery-related outcomes (e.g., duration of surgery), postoperative recovery, and hemodynamic pro­ files, if available, were analyzed, as well as the (e) study type: only RCTs were included. Through randomization, both the intervention and control groups exhibited comparable baseline characteristics such as average age and comorbidity profiles. This methodological strength minimizes the potential for confounding factors, particularly age and comorbid conditions.  

The exclusion criteria were (1) the use of propofol in combination with other volatile agents (e.g., nitrous oxide) for anesthetic mainte­ nance; (2) studies published as case reports, reviews, or conference abstracts; (3) studies with potentially duplicated data; and (4) studies not available in full-text format.  



# 2.3. Study selection and data extraction  

After deduplication, two independent authors separately reviewed the titles and abstracts of the obtained records, ensuring that they met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Conflicts were resolved through discussion. The articles that passed the title and abstract screening were included in the full-text review, and after the authors reached a consensus, they were selected for final analysis.  

Relevant data was rigorously and independently extracted by two authors. Specifically, data related to the first author's name, publication year, participant characteristics (e.g., gender), details on the use of volatile-based regimens (e.g., sevoflurane, desflurane), sample size, outcomes (e.g., incidence of brain swelling), and country of origin were extracted. We communicated with the corresponding authors via email in case of missing or incomplete information to obtain the necessary data. This was to ensure the completeness and accuracy of the data collected for our analysis.  



# 2.4. Outcomes and definition  

The primary outcome was to determine the overall risk of brain swelling, which was defined based on the criteria used in individual studies. For studies that scored brain swelling using a scale of 1 to 4 (where 1 indicated perfectly relaxed brain and 4 indicated bulging brain) [23], patients with a scale ${\ge}3$ (i.e., firm brain or bulging brain) were considered to have clinical brain swelling. If brain swelling was assessed both immediately after opening the dura and at the closure of the dura in a study, we used the data from the initial assessment for our analysis.  

The secondary outcomes included the impact of propofol-based or volatile-based regimens on surgical outcomes [i.e., the volume of blood loss, duration of surgery, and intracranial pressure (ICP)] and recovery outcomes [i.e., risk of agitation, seizure, nausea and vomiting (PONV), extubation time, time to emergence, and time to verbal commands], as well as the risks of intraoperative hemodynamic instability (i.e., hy­ pertension, hypotension, tachycardia, and bradycardia). Given the subjectivity of the cerebral edema assessment, we incorporated ICP as a quantifiable indicator to substantiate the findings associated with brain swelling. In addition, cerebral swelling can complicate the surgical field and necessitate judicious hemostasis, thereby affecting blood loss and the duration of the surgery. Therefore, we further examined these spe­ cific secondary outcomes to validate the impact of brain swelling on surgical outcomes.  



# 2.5. Assessment of methodological quality and certainty of evidence  

Each trial included in the analysis underwent bias assessment by two authors according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Any disagreements were resolved through discussions. The potential risks of bias for each individual study were categorized as “low,” “high,” or “some concerns.” The certainty of evidence was eval­ uated by two authors using Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations [24] to grade the quality of evidence and recommendation strength. Discrepancies between the assessments are resolved through discussion, and a third author may be consulted if needed. The certainty of the evidence is rated as high, moderate, low, or very low.  



# 2.6. Data synthesis  

We used RevMan 5.4 (Cochrane IMS, Oxford, United Kingdom) to conduct all statistical analyses. To account for potential sources of variation and uncertainty, we used a random-effects model as the basis for our analysis. We expressed the analysis results as risk ratios (RR) for dichotomous data and mean differences (MD) for continuous variables, accompanied by $95\%$ confidence intervals (CI) to reflect the precision of estimates. For studies with two control groups, each pairwise compari­ son was included separately, with the shared intervention group size divided approximately evenly among the different comparisons. For dichotomous outcomes, both the number of events and total number of patients were partitioned. For continuous outcomes, only the total number of participants was divided, whereas the means and standard deviations remained unaltered. This approach aimed to prevent the arbitrary omission of pertinent groups and any potential duplication in participant counting.  

To overcome publication bias, we created funnel plots for outcomes reported in 10 or more studies/datasets. We examined the plots for asymmetry and, if necessary, adopted the Egger regression test to identify potential sources of bias. Furthermore, we conducted sensitivity analysis using the “leave-one-out” approach to evaluate the influence of each study on the overall results. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the type of volatile (i.e., sevoflurane, desflurane, or isoflurane), indications for craniotomy (i.e., elective craniotomy vs. emergency craniotomy), and nitrous oxide use in the volatile group (i.e., use of nitrous oxide vs. nonuse). Meta-regression was performed to evaluate the impacts of mean age and proportion of males on the risk of brain swelling between the propofol and volatile anesthesia groups. The mean age and proportion of males were entered as covariates in a randomeffects meta-regression model. We considered $p<0.05$ to indicate sta­ tistical significance for all analyses.  



# 3. Results  



# 3.1. Search results and study characteristics  

The search conducted in various databases, including Ovid Medline, Ovid Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar identified a total of 257 records (Fig. 1). Additionally, five records were acquired from screening of the reference lists of relevant review articles, yielding a total of 262 records. After removing 66 duplicates and 166 records based on the title and abstract, 30 reports with full-text were reviewed for eligibility. Finally, 17 studies published between 1993 and 2023 were included in the review [4,14,18,19,21–23,25–34]. The reasons for exclusion varied, such as being review articles, studies with duplicate data, studies where propofol was combined with nitrous oxide, or studies where propofol was not used.  

The characteristics of the eligible studies are summarized in Table 1. Out of the 17 studies, 15 employed a two-arm study design [4,14,18,21–23,26–34], and the remaining two used a three-arm study design [19,25]. The 17 studies involved patients of different ages, ranging from 29.8 to 58.1 years. The percentage of males varied be­ tween $14.8\%$ and $81\%$ across the studies, while the number of patients included in each study ranged from 42 to 411.The indications for craniotomy included elective craniotomy (e.g., brain tumors or aneu­ rysm clipping) (nine trials) [4,18,19,23,25,30–33] and emergency craniotomy (eight trials) such as subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) or severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) [14,21,22,26–29,34]. In the propofol group, fentanyl and remifentanil were the two most commonly used opioids to reduce surgical stimulation. In the volatile group, sevoflurane, isoflurane, and desflurane were often combined with fentanyl or remi­ fentanil to provide balanced anesthesia. The studies were conducted in different countries, namely India $(n=6)$ [14,21,26–28,33], Italy $(n=3.$ ) [19,31,32], China $\left(\mathtt{n}=3\right..$ ) [4,30,34], Malaysia $(n=1)$ ) [29], Egypt ${\bf\rho}({\bfn}=$ 1) [22], Denmark $(\mathtt{n}=1)$ [25], the United Kingdom $(\mathtt{n}=1$ ) [18], and the United States $(\mathtt{n}=1$ ) [23].

# 3.3. Outcomes  



# 3.3.1. Primary outcome: Impact of propofol on risk of brain swelling  

Sixteen studies provided information on the association between the use of propofol and brain swelling, resulting in 18 data sets involving a total of 1976 patients (propofol group: $n=905$ patients; volatile group: $n=1071$ patients). The incidences of brain swelling in the propofol and volatile groups were $25.9\%$ and $31\%$ , respectively. The meta-analysis revealed a significantly lower risk of brain swelling with the use of propofol compared to volatile agents (RR: 0.85, $95\%$ CI: 0.74 to $0.98,p$ $=0.03$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=21\%^{\circ}$ (Fig. 3) [4,14,18,19,21,23,25–34]. Sensitivity ana­ lyses using the leave-one-out approach consistently supported this finding. Additionally, an inspection of the funnel plot indicated a low risk of publication bias (supplemental Fig. 1).

# 3.3.3. Subgroup analysis  

The results of subgroup analysis based on the type of volatile agents (i.e., sevoflurane, desflurane, and isoflurane), the use of nitrous oxide (i. e., use vs. not use), the indications for craniotomy (i.e., elective crani­ otomy vs. emergency craniotomy), and assessment methods (e.g., the brain relaxation scale versus others) are summarized in Table 2. Pro­ pofol did not decrease the risk of brain swelling compared to individual volatile agents (i.e., sevoflurane, desflurane, and isoflurane). In patients undergoing elective craniotomy, the use of propofol was associated with a lower risk of brain swelling, whereas no significant difference in brain swelling was observed between propofol and volatile agents in patients receiving emergency craniotomy. Furthermore, the use of propofol was found to correlate with a reduced risk of brain swelling when the brain relaxation scale was used to evaluate the severity of brain swelling. However, no significant difference was noted when a non-validated assessment method was used.  



# 3.3.4. Meta-regression  

Meta-regression showed that neither mean age nor proportion of males were significantly associated with the risk for brain swelling when comparing propofol to volatile anesthesia. The regression coefficient for the mean age was $-0.029$ $(p=0.11).$ ), indicating no significant effect (Supplemental Fig. 14). The regression coefficient for the proportion of males was 0.008 $(p=0.4)$ ), suggesting no significant impact (Supple­ mental Fig. 15).  



# 3.3.5. Certainty of evidence  

Supplemental Table 2 summarizes the certainty of evidence for both primary and secondary outcomes. # 4. Discussion  

The relaxation of the brain is a critical component of anesthetic care during intracranial surgery. In addition to improving the surgeon's operating environment and minimizing the severity of retraction injury, optimal brain relaxation may have a positive impact on the post­ operative outcome. A variety of intravenous and inhalational agents have been utilized for maintaining anesthesia in neurosurgery; however, a consensus has not been reached regarding the most suitable method for achieving brain relaxation during craniotomy [1]. Our metaanalysis, involving 1976 patients, showed a significantly lower risk of brain swelling in patients receiving propofol than those using volatile agents without significant differences in surgical time or blood loss be­ tween the two groups. In addition, propofol was associated with lower ICP as well as a lower risk of PONV. There were no significant differ­ ences in extubation time, time to verbal commands, time to emergence, agitation, or seizure risk. Propofol was also linked to a lower risk of intraoperative tachycardia, while the risks of intraoperative brady­ cardia, hypertension, and hypotension were comparable between the two groups.  

The results of our meta-analysis, which were derived from a sub­ stantial sample size of 1976 patients, revealed a significant reduction in the risk of brain swelling associated with propofol use compared to volatile agents. Despite the inclusion of studies with diverse clinical settings (e.g., brain tumor or trauma) and various volatile agents (i.e., sevoflurane, isoflurane, and desflurane), the observed heterogeneity was relatively low $(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}^{2}=11\%)$ . Supplemental Table 4 provides a summary of published meta-analyses that have reported the risk of brain swelling related to the clinical use of propofol compared to volatile agents. Notably, our meta-analysis represented the most large-scale report to date focusing on this issue. It is worth mentioning that we found no significant differences in surgical outcomes (e.g., surgical time) and postoperative recovery (e.g., extubation time or risk of seizure) between the propofol and volatile groups. This suggests that while propofol correlated with the benefit of a reduced risk of brain swelling, this effect had no additional impact on other particular clinical outcomes. Another noteworthy finding was the association between propofol use and lower ICP with a mean difference of $-4.06\mathrm{mmHg}$ compared to volatile agents. This finding is consistent with that of a previous meta-analysis that re­ ported a link of propofol-based anesthesia to a reduced ICP (i.e., $-5.2$ mmHg) [20]. Therefore, for patients anticipated to have increased ICP postoperatively, the use of propofol may be recommended as a favorable option. In summary, our meta-analysis provides robust evidence sup­ porting a reduced risk of brain swelling associated with propofol use compared to volatile agents. However, it should be acknowledged that this benefit may not translate into notable differences in surgical out­ comes or postoperative recovery.  

In our subgroup analysis, we did not find a significant difference in brain swelling between propofol- and volatile-based anesthesia in pa­ tients receiving emergency craniotomy for SAH or TBI. Consequently, our findings emphasize the necessity of individualizing anesthetic se­ lection according to distinct surgical indications, as opposed to a generic reliance on the inherent properties of the anesthetics. This lack of dif­ ference may be attributed to impaired cerebral vessel autoregulation in these patient populations, which could diminish the impact of the anesthetic agents on brain swelling. In fact, in patients with acute supratentorial intracerebral hemorrhage, dynamic cerebral autor­ egulation is bilaterally impaired for at least 10 to 12 days [35]. Another interesting finding from our subgroup analysis was that propofol was not associated with a lower risk of brain swelling than the use of individual volatile agents. Based on these findings, along with consideration of other clinical outcomes, there is no strong evidence suggesting that the use of volatile agents may be inferior to the use of propofol. Neverthe­ less, it is important to note that further large-scale studies are required to validate our findings. Additional research will help strengthen the evi­ dence base and provide more definitive conclusions regarding the optimal choice of anesthetic agents for brain swelling prevention.

Various grading methods have been employed to assess the degree of brain relaxation during craniotomy procedures. Some studies classified brain relaxation simply as satisfactory or not [36], while others cate­ gorized it into tight, adequate, or soft [18]. More recent studies have utilized a four-point scale, assessing brain relaxation as completely relaxed, satisfactorily relaxed, firm, or bulging [9,10,18,19,23,25]. Our subgroup analysis demonstrated the efficacy of propofol in reducing brain swelling when using a validated brain relaxation scale. However, when non-validated methods were used, no significant difference was observed. These findings highlight the importance of implementing an accessible and standardized method for grading brain relaxation during craniotomy.  

Our study findings align with those of previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses [2,20], indicating no statistically significant differences in extubation time, time to emergence, and time to verbal commands between patients receiving propofol-based intravenous anesthesia and those undergoing inhalational anesthesia for mainte­ nance during craniotomy. Early post-operative recovery is critical for both patients and healthcare providers following craniotomy [37,38], as delayed return to consciousness can impede neurological examinations and impair patient care [39]. Therefore, our findings suggest that both propofol and inhalational anesthesia can be utilized to promote early recovery, highlighting the importance of this issue in clinical practice.  

On the other hand, the current study indicated a higher incidence of PONV in patients who received inhalational anesthesia compared to those subjected to intravenous anesthesia. However, there were no significant differences observed in the incidence of seizures and agita­ tion between the two groups. These results align with those previously reported by Zhou et al. [2] and Chui et al. [20], which also demonstrated a decreased incidence of PONV associated with propofol-based anes­ thesia. The prevention and control of PONV in post-craniotomy patients are essential aspects of perioperative care [40,41]. First, the elevated pressures generated during the ejection phase of vomiting, exceeding $100~\mathrm{mmHg}$ in intra-abdominal and intrathoracic regions, can directly impact the intracranial cavity, resulting in increased ICP [42]. Second, neurosurgical patients, given their compromised neurological state and weakened airway reflexes, face an increased risk of aspiration [43]. Therefore, considering the potential benefits, the utilization of propofol as an anesthetic agent in post-craniotomy patients may provide an additional advantage by reducing the risk of PONV.

Our meta-analysis included a subgroup of patients with SAH/TBI. While this choice may raise concerns about variability within our study population, we believe that the inclusion of patients with SAH/TBI is crucial for two reasons. First, emergency craniotomy for SAH/TBI is associated with high rates of both disability and mortality, making it a particularly pressing clinical issue. Second, patients with SAH/TBI often experience brain swelling, a life-threatening complication that makes the choice of anesthetic particularly critical. Our study offers valuable insights into how the selection of propofol and volatile agents affects the risk of brain swelling in these patients. By including this subgroup, we further addressed the gap in the existing research and provided practical guidance for clinicians treating patients with SAH/TBI.  

Regarding the clinical implications of our study, our meta-analysis advocates the preferential use of propofol over volatile agents, partic­ ularly when the clinical objective is optimal brain relaxation. Propofol is effective in minimizing brain edema, which is especially advantageous in patients undergoing elective craniotomy. Moreover, our findings indicate a reduced risk of PONV when using propofol compared to volatile agents. This is particularly relevant for post-craniotomy patients for whom vomiting carries significant risks, such as aspiration or a further increase in ICP. For specific patient populations, such as those with TBI or SAH, our results showed that propofol did not offer a sig­ nificant advantage in reducing brain swelling compared to volatile agents. Thus, an individualized approach to anesthetic selection, considering surgical indications, is advised.  

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this systematic re­ view. First, the small sample sizes in most of the included studies as well as the significant variations in the concentration, dosage, and supple­ mentary medications may affect the accuracy of our results. Second, differences in healthcare systems, surgical practices, and patient pop­ ulations among the countries in which the studies were conducted may introduce bias to our findings. Moreover, there was considerable heterogeneity in patient characteristics (e.g., age, male proportion), indications for craniotomy (i.e., elective craniotomy vs. emergency craniotomy), anesthetic protocols, and study settings across the included studies. Third, some studies were identified as low quality, which may impact the accuracy of the outcomes. Therefore, further high-quality RCTs are needed to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of propofol-based intravenous anesthesia versus inhalational anesthesia in patients undergoing craniotomy.  

In conclusion, our meta-analysis showed an association of propofol use with a lower risk of brain swelling compared to volatile agents without significant differences in surgical time or blood loss. Propofol also correlated with a lower ICP, reduced risk of PONV, and decreased incidence of tachycardia. Subgroup analysis demonstrated that propofol decreased brain edema in patients undergoing elective craniotomy, but not in those receiving emergency craniotomy (e.g., TBI), compared to volatile anesthetics. Despite the encouraging findings, larger-scale studies are warranted to investigate the long-term neurological out­ comes related to propofol-related brain relaxation.
