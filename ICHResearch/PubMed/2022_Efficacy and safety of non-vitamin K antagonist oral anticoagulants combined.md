# Efficacy and safety of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants combined with antiplatelet drugs for patients with peripheral artery disease: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials  

Meina $\mathsf{L}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}^{{\mathsf{I}},2}$ , Shaojun Jiang1,2, Tingting $\mathbf{w}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{1},2$ , Wenjun Chen1,2 and Jinhua Zhang ID  

# Abstract  

Background: The efficacy and safety of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) combined with antiplatelet drugs in patients with peripheral artery disease remain largely unknown.  

Objective: The aim of this meta-analysis was to explore the effects of NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs versus antiplatelet drugs alone in this population.  

Methods: A comprehensive search of randomized controlled trials published in PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library in 30 September 2020 and before. According to the I2 statistic, a random or fixed-effect model was used to analyze the safety and effectiveness of NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs in peripheral artery disease patients.  

Results: Three RCTs met the inclusion criteria, with a total sample size of 11,761 participants. Compared with antiplatelet drugs alone, NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs resulted in lower risk of ischemic stroke events ( $\mathrm{\Delta}OR=0.75$ , $95\%\mathsf{C}|0.57–0.98,$ $\pmb{\triangleright=0.03}$ ), while other treatment effects were not worse than those of single antiplatelet drugs $(p\ge0.05)$ . In addition, although compared with single antiplatelet drugs alone, NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs had a higher risk of major bleeding and clinically related nonmajor bleeding, their risk was not higher for intracranial hemorrhage, which may endanger the life of patients, or for fatal bleeding.  

Conclusions: In summary, for peripheral artery disease patients, a combination of NOACs plus antiplatelet drugs may offer additional benefit in reducing ischemic stroke outcome, yet it may increase the risk of bleeding.  

# Keywords  

Anticoagulation therapy, antiplatelet therapy, atherosclerosis, peripheral artery disease  

# Introduction  

About 200 million people worldwide suffer from peripheral arterial disease (PAD),1 which is characterized by an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. The incidence of cardiovascular events in patients with PAD is higher than that in patients with coronary heart disease or cerebrovascular disease.3 In addition, due to extensive atherosclerosis, patients with PAD have a significantly increased risk of major adverse limb events and mortality.4 For patients with severe PAD, including ischemic rest pain or gangrene, the two-year mortality rate is $40\%$ , and nearly $50\%$ of deaths are due to cardiovascular causes or sudden death.5 Studies have shown that repeated hospitalizations, re-interventions, and limb loss associated with PAD have been shown to have a negative impact on the quality of life and increase medical costs to society.6–8 T herefore, it is very important to adopt active secondary prevention strategies to reduce this risk. In addition to correcting risk factors through lipidlowering therapy, antihypertension treatment, antihyperglycemic treatment, and lifestyle improvement including smoking cessation, it is essential to optimize antithrombotic therapy.  

Related guidelines recommend that a key component of the treatment strategy for patients with PAD is antiplatelet therapy. In large-scale trials, there is evidence that effective P2Y12 inhibitors plus acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) can reduce clinical events, but the number of new or recurring cardiovascular adverse events including deaths is still high, and the bleeding risk is also proportional to the strength of the antiplatelet regimen.10–13 Therefore, it is very necessary to seek better treatment.  

In recent years, many non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants (NOACs) have been developed and put into clinical use, including factor Xa antagonists (apixaban, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban) and factor II antagonist (dabigatran). NOACs have good compliance and do not require routine monitoring, providing a new option for clinical antithrombotic treatment.14 Many researchers want to explore whether NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs are more effective for PAD patients than antiplatelet drugs alone, so the effectiveness and safety of the combination have received attention,15–16 but it is still controversial. Related meta-analyses of NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs have discussed mainly the effects of the combination on coronary artery disease (CAD) and PAD, but have focused on CAD, ignoring PAD;17–18 so far, no meta-analysis that independently explores PAD has been found. Although PAD and CAD have many common risk factors, including age, male, smoking, diabetes,19–21 the role of each risk factor in the two disease processes is different. Studies have shown that smoking and diabetes are the two main risk factors for PAD, rather than the main risk factors for CAD.22  

Therefore, a separate meta-analysis of the effectiveness and safety of NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs for patients with PAD is necessary. The current study carries out such a systematic review and meta-analysis using methods from the Cochrane Collaboration in order to guide clinical applications.  

# Methods  

# Data sources and search strategy  

The research follows the guidance of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses (PRISMA).23 We conducted a comprehensive literature search on databases including PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. We searched for relevant research published in 30  

September 2020 and before. The search keywords are as follows: (1) dabigatran OR rivaroxaban OR apixaban OR edoxaban OR betrixaban OR factor IIa inhibitors OR factor Xa inhibitors OR direct thrombin inhibitors OR non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants OR NOACs OR direct oral anticoagulants OR DOACs OR novel/new oral anticoagulants; (2) peripheral arterial disease OR peripheral artery disease OR PAD. For comprehensiveness, we also examined the reference lists of the studies found by the database searches.  

# Inclusion criteria  

We included all studies that met the following requirements: (1) The study design was a randomized controlled trial (RCT). (2) Participants suffered from PAD. (3) Patients in the intervention group received combined treatment with NOACs and antiplatelet drugs. (4) Patients in the control group were treated with antiplatelet drugs. (5) The main outcome indicators include efficacy indicators (acute limb ischemia, major amputation, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, cardiovascular mortality, all-cause mortality) and safety indicators (major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, fatal bleeding, clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding).  

# Exclusion criteria  

We excluded the following studies: (1) The effects of NOACs or antiplatelet drugs on PAD patients were discussed independently, and no comparison was made. (2) The publication duplicated another based on the same research. (3) The research data as originally published were incomplete or unavailable and could not be obtained by contacting the original author.  

# Data extraction  

The data come from the selected articles. The extracted data include study information (author, publication year), study characteristics (study population, sample size, follow-up time), participant characteristics (age, gender, BMI), and outcome indicators (acute limb ischemia, major amputation, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, cardiovascular mortality, all-cause mortality, major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, fatal bleeding, clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding). The required data were extracted by two researchers independently and cross-referenced to avoid potential data extraction errors. If there was a dispute, it was discussed by a third researcher to reach an agreement.  

# Quality assessment  

Two independent researchers used the Cochrane Collaboration risk of bias tool24 to evaluate the quality of the selected literature. The evaluation items included the following six points: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, blinding of personnel, and outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, other bias. During the evaluation process, if there was a dispute, another researcher conducted an evaluation to help solve the problem.  

# Statistical analysis  

We used NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs as the intervention group, and single antiplatelet drugs as the control group. Review Manager version 5.3 software (the Cochrane Collaboration) was used for meta-analysis, with odds ratio (OR) and $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) as the statistics of interest. The heterogeneity among the included studies was analyzed by the $\chi^{\bar{2}}$ test, and the test level was $\alpha=0.10$ . If $p\geq0.1$ and $\mathrm{I}^{2}\le50\%$ , a fixed-effects model was used for meta-analysis. If $p<0.1$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}>50\%$ , we analyzed the source of heterogeneity, and after excluding the differences between the studies caused by the quality of the study design, treatment plan, etc., we used a random-effects model for the meta-analysis.  

# Results  

# Study selection  

According to the above search strategy, a total of 866 studies were retrieved. After screening, three studies were finally included,25–27 with a total of 11761 patients,  

including 5878 patients in the intervention group and 5883 patients in the control group. The literature screening process and results are shown in Figure 1.  

# Study characteristics and quality assessment  

The baseline characteristics of the included studies are shown in Supplement Table 1. The included studies are RCTs evaluating either rivaroxaban $2.5\mathrm{mg}$ twice a day) plus aspirin ( $100\mathrm{mg}$ once a day) or edoxaban ( $60\mathrm{mg}$ once a day) in conjunction with aspirin ( $100\mathrm{mg}$ once a day). All patients in the study control group received antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or/and clopidogrel). Most studies had a low risk of bias, according to the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool (Figures 2 and 3).  

# Efficacy outcomes  

# Myocardial infarction  

For myocardial infarction outcome indicators, the three included $\mathrm{RCTs}^{25-27}$ have been reported (Figure 4). There was no statistical heterogeneity among the studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%)$ ), so a fixed-effects model was used for metaanalysis. There was no statistically significant difference $(\mathrm{OR}=0.85\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI~}0.69\ –1.03$ , $p{=}0.10$ ).  

# Ischemic stroke  

The three included $\mathrm{RCTs}^{25-27}$ also reported on the occurrence of ischemic stroke in PAD patients (Figure 4). The statistical heterogeneity was low among the studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=41\%$ ), so a fixed-effects model was used for meta-analysis. The occurrence of ischemic stroke events in the two groups was comparable. The results showed that the number of ischemic stroke events in PAD patients receiving NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs was lower than in those receiving antiplatelet therapy alone, and the difference was statistically significant $\mathrm{\DeltaOR}=0.75$ , $95\%{\mathrm{CI~}}0.57-$ 0.98, $p=0.03$ ).

# Cardiovascular mortality  

Cardiovascular mortality 28(Deaths due to sudden cardiac arrest, acute myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, heart failure, cardiogenic shock, immediate complications from a cardiovascular procedure, and other cardiovascular causes (including pulmonary embolism, other thromboembolic events, and peripheral arterial disease)) was analyzed by including three RCTs25–27 (Figure 4). The statistical heterogeneity was low among the studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=48\%$ ), so a fixedeffects model was used for meta-analysis. The results showed that there was no significant difference in cardiovascular mortality between the NOACs combined with antiplatelet drug treatment group and the antiplatelet treatment group only $\mathrm{\DeltaOR}=1.05$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.88-$ 1.26, $p=0.56$ .

# All-cause mortality  

For all-cause mortality, three RCTs were included25–27 (Figure 4). The statistical heterogeneity was low among the studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=35\%)$ ), so a fixed-effects model was used for meta-analysis. The results of the study were similar to cardiovascular mortality, and there was no significant difference between the two groups $(\mathrm{OR}=1.03\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI~}0.90\%1.19$ , $p=0.63$ ).  

# Safety outcomes  

# Major bleeding  

We analyzed the incidence of major bleeding (Figure 5) in the three included RCTs,25–27 according to International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria29 (defined as fatal bleeding, bleeding into a critical site, a hemoglobin level $\geq2\:\mathrm{g/dL}$ , or transfusion of at least two units of packed red cells or whole blood). The statistical heterogeneity was low among the studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=45\%)$ ), so a fixed-effects model was used for metaanalysis. The results showed that the bleeding risk of NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs was greater than that of using antiplatelet drugs alone, and the difference was statistically significant $(\mathrm{OR}=1.43\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 1.15–1.77, $p=0.001$ .

# Fatal bleeding  

The analysis of fatal bleeding included two large RCTs25–26 (Figure 5). There was no statistical heterogeneity between studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ), so meta-analysis was performed using a fixed-effects model. The results showed that the NOACs combined with antiplatelet drug treatment group did not increase the risk of fatal bleeding compared with the antiplatelet treatment group only $\mathrm{{OR}}=1.11\$ , 95%CI 0.45–2.74, $p{=}0.82,$ ).  

# Clinically relevant non-major bleeding  

In addition, two RCTs25,27 reported on clinical nonmajor bleeding (defined as any sign or symptom of hemorrhage that does not fit the criteria for the ISTH definition29 of major bleeding, but does meet at least one of the following criteria: requiring medical intervention by a healthcare professional, leading to hospitalization or increased level of care, prompting a face-to-face evaluation 30) (Figure 5). The statistical heterogeneity was low between studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=45\%$ ), so a fixed-effect model was used for meta-analysis. The results showed that the risk of clinical non-major bleeding in the NOACs combined with antiplatelet drug therapy group was greater than that of the antiplatelet therapy group alone, and the difference was statistically significant $(\mathrm{OR}=1.49\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}1.20\small{-}1.86.$ $p=0.0004)$ .  

# Intracranial hemorrhage  

For intracranial hemorrhage, we included two large RCTs 25–26 ( Figure 5). There was no statistical heterogeneity among the studies $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%)$ ), so a fixed-effects model was used for meta-analysis. The results showed that there was no significant difference in the risk of intracranial hemorrhage between the NOACs combined with antiplatelet drug treatment group and the antiplatelet treatment group only ( $\mathrm{{OR}}=0.69$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.38–1.26, $p=0.23,$ ).  

# Discussion  

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, data from three RCTs25–27 comparing NOACs in combination with antiplatelet drugs and antiplatelet drugs alone in the treatment of PAD patients were compiled. The main finding is that the incidence of ischemic stroke events of NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs is lower than that of antiplatelet drugs alone, and the therapeutic effects of other aspects are similar to those of antiplatelet drugs alone. We also found that although NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs have a higher risk of major bleeding and clinically related nonmajor bleeding than do antiplatelet drugs alone, for intracranial hemorrhage, which may be life-threatening, and for fatal hemorrhage, there is no difference between NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs and antiplatelet drugs alone. The results of this meta-analysis suggest that NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs may be worth considering as a treatment option for PAD patients at high risk of ischemic stroke and provide further evidence to support the current guidelines,9 yet it may also increase the risk of bleeding.

Figure 5. Forest plot of safety outcomes.  

As far as we know, we are the first to independently explore the effectiveness and safety of NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs in patients with PAD. The previous researches all analyzed the combination of CAD and PAD, focusing on the analysis of $\mathrm{CAD^{17-18}}$ while ignoring PAD. Our study included 11,761 patients, and the heterogeneity between the study groups was generally low, making the study group clinically representative. We found that compared with antiplatelet drugs alone, NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs have a relatively low risk of ischemic stroke events. The combination of low-dose aspirin and NOACs can be considered to reduce the risk of ischemic stroke in patients with PAD, which is consistent with the results of Bauersachs et al.18 Their meta-analysis showed that for patients with CAD or PAD, rivaroxaban plus aspirin significantly reduced the risk of ischemic stroke compared to aspirin alone, although major bleeding with rivaroxaban plus aspirin increased. This result is also consistent with the guidelines.  

The combined application of NOACs with antiplatelet drugs had a reported risk of major bleeding and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding that was higher than that for antiplatelet drugs alone, which indicates that this combination may be avoided for PAD patients with high bleeding risk. Although the overall result is that NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs have a higher risk of major bleeding and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding than antiplatelet drugs alone, in the study of Moll et al.,27 edoxaban combined with antiplatelet drugs for major bleeding overall, the incidence rate did not increase compared to the use of antiplatelet drugs alone. It is unclear whether this conflicting study result is related to the specific pharmacodynamics of edoxaban. Although there is no faceto-face comparison between NOACs in PAD patients, observational studies in patients with atrial fibrillation or venous thrombosis have shown that some NOACs may have a lower bleeding risk than other NOACs.31–33 Another possible explanation is that the study by Moll et al.27 was different from the other two studies25–26 in that its control group patients used dual antiplatelet drugs, which may also be a reason for the increased risk of bleeding in the control group. As noted, the risk of major bleeding and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding is higher for NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs than for antiplatelet drugs alone. However, for intracranial hemorrhage, which may endanger the patient’s life at any time, and for fatal hemorrhage, there is no significant difference between the two groups. This shows that compared with the single use of antiplatelet drugs, the combined application of NOACs with antiplatelet drugs does not increase the risk of fatal bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage in patients, and endanger the lives of patients.  

In addition, there is an issue worthy of attention. Included RCT (Anand et al.’s study25 from 558 centers) showed that low-dose NOACs plus aspirin reduced cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke and limb events when compared with aspirin alone, but the limb events are very low (about $1\%$ ) in both groups. When large groups are analyzed, the statistics may show a difference but clinically the number needed to treat is very high for the added risk of bleeding. In addition, the price of NOACs is relatively expensive.34 Therefore, NOACs plus antiplatelet drugs is not necessarily be better than using antiplatelet drugs alone. For PAD patients, before receiving combined treatment with NOACs plus antiplatelet drugs, it is still necessary to balance efficacy, safety, and medical expenses. This issue is also worthy of further study and even economic evaluation in the future.  

In addition, there are several limitations worth discussing. First, this meta-analysis only evaluated data on rivaroxaban and edoxaban, which may also lead to publication bias because of the lack of available data on the effects of apixaban and dabigatran on patients with PAD. Second, the study by Moll et al. 27 did not specifically assess the carotids, which may have some impact on the outcome of ischemic stroke, but the study accounted for a small proportion (weight: $0.4\%$ ) of stroke outcome, and had no reversal effect on stroke outcome. We still expect more real-world studies to further confirm it in the future. Third, because RCTs of NOACs combined with antiplatelet drugs for the treatment of PAD patients are currently limited, the number of included studies is small, which makes it impossible to conduct further subgroup analysis on the potential effect of NOACs dose. Because different doses of NOACs may benefit PAD patients somewhat differently, so more research is needed to better understand the impact of different doses of NOACs and antiplatelet drugs on the safety and efficacy in PAD patients. Future research on the optimal dose of NOACs may be necessary.  

# Conclusion  

For PAD patients, a combination of NOACs plus antiplatelet drugs may offer additional benefit in reducing ischemic stroke outcome, and the therapeutic effects of other aspects (myocardial infarction, cardiovascular mortality, all-cause mortality) are similar to antiplatelet drugs alone, yet it may increase the risk of bleeding.
