# What’s new?  

• We provide an accurate, updated assessment of the efficacysafety profile of dual antithrombotic therapy (DAT)—including a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) plus a single antiplatelet agent—vs. triple antithrombotic therapy (TAT)—involving a vitamin K antagonist (VKA) plus two antiplatelet agents—in patients with atrial fibrillation and acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).   
• A significant reduction of bleeding rates with DAT as compared to TAT is observed, with a trend towards reduced rates of intracranial haemorrhage with DAT and an estimated number needed to treat of about 300 which became significant after exclusion of DOACs from TAT and VKA from DAT.   
• A non-significant difference in trial-defined major adverse cardiovascular events, including all-cause death, cardiovascular death and stroke, with DAT compared to TAT, was marred by a $60\%$ significant increase in the risk of stent thrombosis and by a trend towards an increased risk of myocardial infarction $(\mathsf{M}|)$ , with numbers needed to harm of 274 and 151, respectively.   
• Our meta-analysis is the first to include a subgroup analysis by index event (ACS/PCI vs. elective PCI), showing, in ACS/PCI patients, a significant $40\%$ increased risk of MI and a clear numerical increase of ST (absolute risk $1.46\%$ vs. $0.6\%$ ) with DAT compared to TAT.   
• Basing on clinical and procedural features a tailored strategy should be adopted on a case-by-case basis and a very early DAT initiation should not be viewed as the default strategy.  

# Introduction  

Atrial fibrillation (AF) prevalence is increasing, with 15 million Europeans estimated to be affected by 2030.1 Atrial fibrillation is a risk factor for acute coronary syndromes (ACS), but also a consequence, with one in five patients developing AF within 5 years postACS.2 Coronary artery disease affects about $30\%$ of AF patients, and about $10\%$ of all AF patients undergo percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).2 While oral anticoagulants (OACs) are required to prevent AF-related thromboembolic events, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) is crucial to reduce ischaemic events such as myocardial infarction (MI) or stent thrombosis (ST) in patients with recent ACS or PCI.3  

Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) testing triple antithrombotic therapy (TAT) with ${\mathsf{OAC}}+{\mathsf{DAPT}}$ against dual antithrombotic therapy (DAT) with OAC $^+$ single antiplatelet therapy have reported reduced rates of bleeding with DAT without excess of ischaemic outcomes. However, none of the trials were adequately powered for ischaemic endpoints, stimulating a number of metaanalyses.10,11 Moreover, trial designs were heterogeneous: WOEST and ISAR-Triple had vitamin K antagonist (VKA) in the DAT arm,5,6 whereas PIONEER AF-PCI and RE-DUAL4,7 had rivaroxaban or dabigatran; in ISAR-Triple, DAT was compared to TAT after 6 weeks of triple therapy in both arms6; AUGUSTUS8—the only trial comparing TAT or DAT with a VKA vs. TAT or DAT with a full-dose direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC)—demonstrated significantly reduced bleeding rates with DOAC vs. VKA, regardless of dual or triple therapy.10  

Whether a full-dose DOAC plus a single antiplatelet agent is sufficient to prevent ischaemic events remains a lingering question: two recent meta-analyses of three trials has indeed shown increased rates of ST with DAT compared to TAT.12,13 The very recent ENTRUSTAF-PCI trial,14 showing non-inferior bleeding rates for DAT with edoxaban compared to a triple VKA-based strategy, offers additional important data on the safety-efficacy profile of the DAT strategy. Here, we provide an updated extensive meta-analysis of the four randomized controlled trials aimed at balancing the risks of intracranial and other major haemorrhages against the risk of MI and ST.  

# Methods  

# Study selection and eligibility criteria  

The authors followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Inclusion criteria were (i) randomized trials comparing treatment with DAT vs. TAT; (ii) patients with AF undergoing PCI or presenting with ACS; (iii) follow-up of ${\ge}6$ months; snf (iv) reported rates of bleeding, as well as composite and single efficacy outcomes. Exclusion criteria were (i) observational or nonrandomized studies and registry data; (ii) studies not including DOACs in the DAT arm; and (iii) DOAC dose never tested for stroke prevention in AF patients (i.e. rivaroxaban $2.5\mathsf{mg}$ bis in die, bid).  

We searched PubMed, Embase, EBSCO, Cochrane database of systematic reviews, and Web of Science from database inception to 3 September 2019, using the following terms in various combinations: ‘atrial fibrillation’, ‘acute coronary syndromes’, ‘percutaneous coronary intervention’, ‘triple antithrombotic therapy’, ‘dual antithrombotic therapy’, ‘anticoagulants’, ‘antiplatelets’, ‘dabigatran’, ‘rivaroxaban’, ‘apixaban’, ‘edoxaban’, ‘DOACs’, ‘NOACs’, ‘aspirin’, ‘clopidogrel’, and ‘randomised trial’. Previous meta-analyses and abstracts/presentations from annual meetings of major cardiovascular (CV) societies (European Society of Cardiology, American Heart Association, American College of Cardiology and Transcatheter Cardiovascular Therapeutics) were also screened, as well as reference lists of included articles, using a snowball approach. The full search strategy is available in Supplementary material online, Table S2. All authors independently selected the trials that potentially fulfilled the inclusion criteria, based on title and abstract. Full-text articles were retrieved to verify in detail their eligibility.  

# Risk of bias and endpoint definitions  

Methodological quality and risk of bias of the selected trials were assessed using Cochrane’s Collaboration tool (Supplementary material online, Table S3). The primary safety outcome was ‘trial-defined primary safety outcome’, including International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) major or clinically relevant non-major (CRNM) bleeding, thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) major and minor bleeding, or bleeding requiring medical attention (Supplementary material online, Table S4). Secondary safety outcomes were TIMI major bleeding, TIMI major and minor bleeding, CRNM bleeding, and intracranial haemorrhage. The primary efficacy outcome was ‘trial-defined primary major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)’ according to the definition of MACE in the respective trials (Supplementary material online, Table S4).  

Secondary efficacy outcomes were all-cause death, cardiac death, MI, stroke, and ST. The trial definitions of ST were ‘definite’ in ENTRUST-AF$\mathsf{PCI}^{14}$ and RE-DUAL PCI,4 ‘definite’ or ‘probable’ in AUGUSTUS,8 and not provided in PIONEER-AF.7  

# Statistical analysis  

Odds ratio (OR) with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs), using both random and fixed-effect models according to heterogeneity among the included studies, were calculated using RevMan software version 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration) and the R statistical software meta-package (R project for statistical computing 3.3.3 version). The $\mathsfQ$ Cochran test and Higgins $I^{2}$ statistics (with $1^{2}<25\%$ , $25-50\%$ , or $>50\%$ indicating low, moderate, or high) were used to determine heterogeneity among trials. The randomeffects model with the inverse variance weighting was used when the degree of heterogeneity was high or moderate, otherwise, the fixed-model with the Mantel–Haenszel method was preferred. Funnel plots were examined for publication bias (Supplementary material online, Tables S5– S15). Meta-regressions were not considered given the small number of included studies $(<10)$ .  

Sensitivity analyses were run: (i) excluding rivaroxaban $15\mathsf{mg}$ daily, which is not an approved dose for stroke prevention in patients with AF; (ii) excluding dabigatran $110\mathsf{mg}$ bid, which is not an approved dose in the USA; (iii) considering only the DAT with DOAC and the TAT with VKA arms of the AUGUSTUS trial; (iv) removing each study one at a time to test whether the exclusion of a specific trial could significantly affect the results of the analysis. A subgroup analysis for patients undergoing ACSrelated PCI vs. those undergoing elective PCI was performed including data from RE-DUAL PCI and AUGUSTUS.15,16 Finally, the ‘number needed to treat’ (NNT) or the ‘number needed to treat for an additional harmful outcome’ (NNTH) to prevent or cause and adverse event were calculated according to the absolute risk differences.  

# Results  

# Studies and patient characteristics  

Using our search strategy, we screened 11 161 potentially relevant articles. The PRISMA flow diagram describing the search and study selection process is available in Supplementary material online, Table S16. Four relevant studies were identified. Our analyses included a total of 10 234 patients: 1389 from PIONEER AF-PCI,7 comparing DAT with rivaroxaban 15 mg daily vs. TAT with VKA; 2725 from REDUAL PCI,4 comparing DAT with dabigatran 150 or $110\mathsf{mg}$ bid vs. TAT with VKA; 4614 from AUGUSTUS, comparing DAT with either apixaban 5 mg bid or VKA vs. TAT with either apixaban 5 mg bid or VKA; and 1506 from ENTRUST-AF-PCI,14 comparing DAT with edoxaban $60\mathsf{mg}$ daily vs. TAT with VKA. The main trial characteristics and respective treatment arms are shown in Supplementary material online, Table S17. The PIONEER AF-PCI arm including TAT with rivaroxaban $2.5\mathsf{mg}$ bid (709 patients) was not included in the analysis, as this dosage has never been tested for stroke prevention in AF. All patients had documented AF, and anticoagulation was used for stroke prevention in all. Acute coronary syndrome was present in 5786/10 234 patients $(56.0\%)$ ; 1097/10 234 $10.7\%$ of them (all from the AUGUSTUS trial) were medically managed, while the others all underwent PCI. Elective PCI was performed in 5164/10 234 patients $(50\%)$ . Most PCI-treated patients received a drug-eluting stent, and clopidogrel was the most commonly used $\mathsf{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor $>90\%$ of patients).  

# Safety outcomes  

# Trial-defined primary safety outcome, TIMI major bleeding, TIMI major plus minor bleeding, and CRNMB  

Overall, DAT regimens were associated with a significantly lower risk of trial-defined primary safety outcome compared to TAT (OR 0.63, $95\%\subset10.50–0.79,I^{2}=71\%)$ (Figure 1). DAT regimens were also associated with significantly lower risks of TIMI major bleeding compared to TAT ( $\mathsf{2R}0.54,95\%\subset10.41\substack{-0.70,l^{2}=0\%}$ (Figure 2). Overall rates of TIMI minor plus major bleeding and of CRNM bleeding were also significantly lower with DAT regimens compared to TAT (OR 0.60, $95\%\mathrm{~C}|0.44-0.81$ , $I^{2}=69\%$ , and OR 0.62, $95\%$ CI 0.51–0.76, $I^{2}={}$ $62\%$ , respectively) (Figure 2).  

Excluding rivaroxaban $15\mathsf{mg}$ daily or dabigatran $110\mathsf{mg}$ bid did not significantly alter the bleeding outcomes (Supplementary material online, Tables S18 and S19). Considering only DAT with DOACs vs. TAT with VKAs also did not influence the main results (Supplementary material online, Table S20). Omitting one trial at a time showed that individual study data did not influence the main safety results (Supplementary material online, Table S18). Subgroup analysis showed significantly reduced bleeding rates with DAT vs. TAT for both ACS and elective PCI patients (Supplementary material online, Table S21).  

# Intracranial haemorrhage  

Dual antithrombotic therapy regimens were associated with a non-significantly lower risk of intracranial haemorrhage compared to TAT $(\mathsf{OR}0.50,95\%\mathsf{Cl}0.21-1.19,l^{2}=47\%)$ (Figure 4). Excluding rivaroxaban $15\mathsf{mg}$ daily or dabigatran $110\mathsf{mg}$ bid did not significantly alter the result (Supplementary material online, Tables S18 and S19). Omitting one trial at a time showed significantly reduced rates of intracranial haemorrhage with DAT vs. TAT when AUGUSTUS was excluded (OR 0.32, $95\%$ CI 0.15–0.69) (Supplementary material online, Table S18); this effect was probably linked, at least in part, to the weight that AUGUSTUS had on the analysis $(33\%)$ and to the inclusion of both apixaban and VKA in the AUGUSTUS DAT arm. When DAT with DOACs only was compared to TAT with VKA only, a significant reduction of intracranial haemorrhage favouring DAT with DOACs was observed (OR 0.31, $95\%$ CI 0.15–0.64) (Supplementary material online, Table S20).  

# Efficacy outcomes  

# Trial-defined primary major adverse cardiovascular events, all-cause death, cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, and stroke  

Overall, DAT regimens were associated with a non-significant difference of trial-defined primary MACE compared to TAT (OR 1.09, $95\%\mathsf{Cl}0.94\pounds1.26,l^{2}=0\%)$ (Figure 1). DAT regimens were also associated with non-significant differences in all-cause mortality (OR 1.11, $95\%\mathsf{Cl}0.9\mathrm{-}1.36,l^{2}=0\%)$ or CV death (OR 1.10, $95\%$ CI 0.86–1.42, $I^{2}=0\%,$ ) compared to TAT (Figures 1 and 3). Dual antithrombotic therapy regimens were associated with a non-significant trend towards an increased risk of MI (OR 1.23, $95\%$ CI 0.99–1.54, $1^{2}=0\%$ , $P=0.06\$ ) with no significant differences in stroke rates ( $\mathrm{OR}1.00,95\%$ $\mathsf{Cl}0.69\mathrm{-}1.46,l^{2}=0\%)$ compared to TAT (Figure 3).

# Stent thrombosis  

Overall, DAT regimens were associated with a significantly increased risk of ST compared to TAT (OR 1.60, $95\%$ CI 1.02–2.52, $I^{2}=0\%$ ) (Figure 4). Excluding rivaroxaban $15\mathsf{mg}$ daily did not significantly alter this result (Supplementary material online, Table S18). Excluding dabigatran $110\mathsf{mg}$ bid showed an overall non-significant increased risk of ST (Supplementary material online, Tables S19). Omitting each trial one at a time showed that the increased risk of ST was no longer significant when AUGUSTUS or RE-DUAL PCI were excluded (Supplementary material online, Table S18); this effect was probably linked to the weight that these two trials had on the analysis $35\%$ and $32\%$ , respectively) and to the low number of events reported in each trial. Subgroup analysis showed a non-significant but clear trend towards increased risk of ST in ACS/PCI patients receiving DAT therapy (OR 1.92, $95\%$ CI 0.98–3.75) (Supplementary material online, Table S21).

# Discussion  

The present meta-analysis confirms a significant reduction of trialdefined primary safety outcomes with DAT using DOACs vs. a triple antithrombotic regimen in patients with AF and recent ACS and/or PCI. There was a trend towards reduced rates of intracranial haemorrhage with DAT compared to TAT, with an estimated NNT of about 300, with at sensitivity analysis including only DAT with DOACs vs. TAT with VKA showing a significantly reduced risk of intracranial haemorrhages, consistent with the superior safety of DOACs vs. VKA.8 On the other hand, the non-significant difference in trial-defined MACE, including all-cause death, CV death, and stroke, with DAT compared to TAT, was marred by a $60\%$ significant increase in the risk of stent thrombosis and by a trend towards an increased risk of MI, with numbers needed to harm of 274 and 151, respectively (Figure 6). Of note, our meta-analysis is the first to include a subgroup analysis by index event (ACS/PCI vs. elective PCI), showing, in ACS/PCI patients, a significant $40\%$ increased risk of MI and a clear numerical increase of ST (absolute risk $1.46\%$ vs. $0.6\%$ )15,16 with DAT compared to TAT.  

Despite numerous randomized trials comparing dual vs. triple antithrombotic regimens in AF patients with recent ACS or PCI, the optimal drug combination and duration for these patients is still unclear.17 Meta-analyses can provide useful information on rare events—such as intracranial bleeding and stent thrombosis—and on overall outcomes for which individual trials may be under-powered. In the present meta-analysis, all patients received oral anticoagulation for AF and all patients were randomized to DAT vs. TAT within one week from PCI or ACS diagnosis. We focused on DAT regimens involving a DOAC plus an antiplatelet agent as compared to triple antithrombotic regimens because the use of VKA within both DAT and TAT will probably be discouraged by future guidelines18 on the basis of AUGUSTUS8 and of a recent network meta-analysis.10 Previous meta-analyses,10,11 instead, included trials testing DAT regimens based entirely on VKA plus a single antiplatelet agent, as well as patients receiving anticoagulation for reasons other than AF; one previously included trial compared DAT against TAT 6 weeks after $\mathsf{PCI},^{6}$ providing no information on DAT during the early antithrombotic management of AF-ACS/PCI patients; no subgroup analysis by acute or chronic presentation has ever been done; finally, the very recent ENTRUST-AF-PCI14 trial was not included in previous detailed meta-analyses.10–13

Our finding of an increased risk of stent thrombosis with DAT is coherent with that of two recent meta-analyses12,13 that did not include ENTRUST-AF-PCI. At sensitivity analyses, the exclusion of dabigatran $110\mathsf{mg}$ twice daily showed only a non-significant increase in the risk of stent thrombosis with DAT, whereas the trend towards increased rates of MI with DAT vs. TAT became significant after excluding the rivaroxaban $15\mathsf{mg}$ daily regimen, and in the subgroup analysis of $\mathsf{ACS/PCI}$ patients; thus, not all DAT regimens including DOACs may be equal in terms of ischaemic outcomes,4,8,15 and acute clinical presentation is likely to expose AF/PCI patients to higher ischaemic risk with DAT compared to TAT.  

Our NNT analysis shows that 76 patients have to be treated with a DAT strategy involving a DOAC, compared to a TAT strategy, to avoid a TIMI major bleeding event, whereas 274 patients need to be treated to cause a stent thrombosis event; while not all TIMI major bleeds are life-threatening, stent thrombosis carries a high acute mortality risk and is more frequent in the first weeks after PCI. Interestingly, our estimates showed that a similar number of patients (314) would need to be treated with DAT using DOACs to prevent the most feared bleeding complication, i.e., intracranial haemorrhage. Admittedly, recent-generation stents are less thrombogenic compared to older stents; however, contemporary patients, increasingly, present intrinsic prothrombotic features, such as advanced age, comorbidities, prior stent thrombosis, complex coronary anatomy, proximal or extensive disease involving the left main stem or bifurcations, that enhance the risks and consequences of stent thrombosis. Thus, our data suggest that clinicians may opt for early DAT in patients in whom the risk of bleeding is clearly predominant, but may delay the initiation of DAT, at least during the first weeks after PCI, in the remaining patients, especially if presenting with ACS.

# Limitations  

This meta-analysis has limitations. First, trial rather than individuallevel data were used to assess outcomes. Second, owing to the limited size of specific groups, we did not perform detailed subgroup analyses of outcomes in relation to type of stent (drug-eluting vs. bare-metal), $\mathsf{CHA}_{2}\mathsf{DS}_{2}\mathsf{VASC}$ risk score, or $\mathsf{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor used. However, we performed an original subgroup analysis by type of index event (ACS vs. non-ACS), based on the recently published subgroup data of RE-DUAL PCI and AUGUSTUS.15,16 Third, the $2\times2$ factorial design of AUGUSTUS included both VKA and DOAC in the DAT and TAT arms. To overcome this limitation, for the main outcomes, we performed a sensitivity analysis including only the DAT with DOAC and the TAT with VKA arms of AUGUSTUS and found no relevant differences in the overall main results, exception made for intracranial haemorrhages. Finally, a degree of heterogeneity was found across trials in terms of trial design, type and duration of antiplatelet/antithrombotic therapy used. To face this, the more rigorous random effects analysis was applied in the presence of moderate to high heterogeneity.  

# Conclusions  

Despite the proliferation of statements17,19,20 and meta-analyses10,11 proposing a direct early dual antithrombotic strategy for most patients, our meta-analysis justifies caution with such an approach,9,12,18,20 suggesting that a more tailored strategy, based on clinical and procedural features, should be adopted on a case-by-case basis, and that very early DAT initiation should not be viewed as the default strategy, especially in ACS patients. Our analysis indicates that treating about 300 patients with early DAT as compared to TAT may prevent an intracranial haemorrhage while causing one stent thrombosis and two MIs.
