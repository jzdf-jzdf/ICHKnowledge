# CLINICAL TRIAL  



# Phase 1/2a Trial of ISPASM  

Mario Zanaty, MD; Lauren Allan, DO; Edgar A. Samaniego, MD, MS; Anthony Piscopo, BS; Eleanor Ryan, BS; James C. Torner, PhD, MS; David Hasan , MD  

BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Microthrombosis could play a role in delayed cerebral ischemia after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. Tirofiban has shown promising results in reducing delayed cerebral ischemia in retrospective studies. However, the safety of using tirofiban in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage is not rigorously established.  

METHODS: A phase 1/2a double-blinded randomized controlled trial (2:1 randomization) to assess the safety of a 7-day intravenous infusion of tirofiban compared with placebo, in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage treated with ventriculostomy placed in the operative room and coiling was conducted. The primary end point was any intracranial hemorrhage during the hospital stay. The secondary end points were: incidence of radiographic and clinical vasospasm, incidence of delayed cerebral ischemia, and incidence of cerebral ischemic changes noted on magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography.  

RESULTS: Eighteen patients received intravenous tirofiban and 12 received placebo. There was no difference in baseline characteristics except for higher male proportions in the tirofiban group. There was no difference in death, in development of new or change in existing intracranial hemorrhages, in thrombocytopenia, and need for shunts in the two arms. However, the tirofiban arm had a lower incidence of delayed cerebral ischemia compared with placebo $(6\%$ [1/18] versus $33\%$ [4/12]; $P{=}0.04.$ ), and less radiographic vasospasm as detected by catheter angiogram or computed tomography angiography $(P=0.01$ ) and computed tomography perfusion $(P=0.01$ ).  

CONCLUSIONS: The above preliminary results support proceeding with further testing of the safety and efficacy of 7-day intravenous infusion of tirofiban in a pragmatic (placing external ventricular drain by the bedside), multicenter setting, and using a larger population.

ntracranial aneurysm is a prevalent disease affecting ${\approx}2\%$ to $5\%$ of the population.1,2 Aneurysm rupture leads to aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH), which accounts for $2\%$ to $5\%$ of all new strokes and affects 21 000 to 33 000 patients each year in the United States.3–6 It poses a high rate of mortality $(\approx60\%)$ and morbidity, with a large proportion $(30\%-40\%)$ of the survivors becoming functionally dependent.7 Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) is a prevalent and dreadful complication of aSAH, occurring in $30\%$ to $40\%$ of cases.8–10 It is the most consistent treatable predictor of morbidity, $11-14$ and plays an essential role in the persistent cognitive, social, emotional, and functional morbidity of survivors.15–19  

So far, only nimodipine, by an unclear mechanism, has been effective in preventing DCI. Nimodipine reduces clinical vasospasm by $13\%,^{20}$ DCI by $34\%$ , and overall poor outcomes by $40\%_{,}^{21}$ compared with placebo. Recently, evidence from animal and human studies has surfaced implicating the role of microthrombi in the pathophysiology of DCI.10,22,23 As a result, several antiplatelet and anticoagulation agents have been evaluated.24–26 A

Tirofiban, a GpIIb/IIIa reversible inhibitor, emerged as a suitable option for emergent treatment of ruptured aneurysms using stents,26,29–32 given the a rapid time of onset of 10 minutes (for $90\%$ platelet suppression), short half-life (2 hours) and a return to normal platelet function within 4 to 8 hours of therapy cessation.33,34 Tirofiban was found to reduce the odds of DCI by $72\%$ , without affecting the risk of hemorrhagic complications in a retrospective study of patients with aSAH, when compared with matched group treated with coiling without the use of any antiplatelet therapy.  

These data lead to the conduction of the ISPASM trial (Induced Suppression of Platelets Activity in aSAH Management) to evaluate the safety and efficacy of tirofiban in preventing DCI in patients with SAH treated with coils only, a double-blind placebo-controlled phase 1/2a randomized controlled trial.  



# METHODS  

The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon request.  



# Trial Design and Oversight  

This is an investigator-initiated Food and Drug Administration Investigational New Drug Application (137923) approved single-center, parallel-group, double-blind randomized trial that compares the safety of tirofiban with placebo in patients with aSAH. Ethics approval was obtained in 2018 from the University of Iowa Hospital and Clinics Institutional Review Board. Because the trial enrolled patients with aSAH, written informed consent was obtained from the legally authorized representative to give consent on the admission of the patient. An independent data safety monitoring board and ethics committee (DSMB) reviewed the trial regularly to assess conduct, progress, and safety.  

The trial protocol was designed in a collaborative fashion by the departments of Neurosurgery, Neurology, Interventional Neuro-radiology, Epidemiology, Surgical Intensive Care Unit, at the University of Iowa Hospital and Clinics. The investigators vouch for the completeness and accuracy of the data, analyses, fidelity of this report to the trial protocol, and the statistical analysis plan.  



# Participants and Trial Sites  

To undergo randomization in the trial, patients had to be between 18 and 85 years of age, have an aSAH documented by an angiogram, have an external ventricular drainage (EVD) placed in the operative room on admission, a Hunt-Hess Grade35 of ${\le}4$ after correction of the electrolytes and the hydrocephalus, a modified fisher grade ${\le}4.^{36}$ The patients had their aneurysm(s) secured with coiling ${<}48$ hours from the rupture and ${<}24$ hours from admission.  

Patients who underwent a craniotomy or craniectomy were excluded from the study. Similarly, patients who were taking any form of antithrombotic therapy (antiplatelets or therapeutic dose of anticoagulation) were excluded from the study. That included patients who underwent stent deployment were also excluded requiring the use of antiplatelet therapy. Patients who required ${>}1$ pass with the ventricular catheter during placement of the EVD were also excluded for safety. In addition, patients who experienced postcoiling significant strokes and postventriculostomy hemorrhages of ${>}7\mathtt{CC}$ (or clinically significant) were excluded. The volume of 7 cc was based on Roa et al’s37 study, which reported after a receiver operating characteristic analysis that volume of $<1.10$ cc has $91.7\%$ sensitivity to rule out symptomatic EVD related hemorrhage, whereas a volume of ${>}7.59$ cc has $95.5\%$ specificity to predict symptomatic hemorrhages. Postcoiling significant stroke was defined as restricted diffusion associated with worsening neurological exam detected by calculating the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale and can explain the patient symptoms (ataxia, blindness, aphasia, paresis, sensory loss, etc) Additional inclusion/exclusion criteria are found in the submitted online protocol.  



# Interventions and Randomization  

Upon presentation to the hospital, all patients underwent a baseline computed tomography (CT) angiography (CTA) with CT perfusion (CTP) to evaluate for the presence of aneurysm and obtain baseline perfusion and angiographic parameters (Figure). Nimodipine was then started for all patients as standard of care. Patients presenting with hydrocephalus or drowsiness were taken immediately for EVD placement in the operating room, to allow for accurate homeostasis and for the use of the Ghajar guide (CODMAN) so that the ventriculostomy can be performed with a single pass only. Patients who did not undergo EVD placement on presentation were not included in this study. The patients were then admitted to the surgical and neuroscience intensive care unit. A cerebral digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed within 24 hours from admission, during which the patient underwent endovascular coiling of the aneurysm(s). The patient’s legally authorized representative was then approached for consent. Randomization was performed in permuted blocks of random sizes without stratification. To ensure concealment, the block sizes were not disclosed. Randomization was computerized and performed by the pharmacy department and began at consent. Patients were then treated in the surgical intensive care unit following the institution's routine SAH protocol (details online). After treatment of the hydrocephalus with the EVD and correction of metabolic abnormalities, the Hunt-Hess Grade and World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grading scale were recorded. This is typically recorded 12 hours after the EVD placement. Patients underwent a postangiogram magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) after enrollment in the study to assess for postoperative strokes or hemorrhages (baseline MRI). If the selection criteria were met, the patients underwent a 2:1 randomization to tirofiban versus placebo. The developed tirofiban protocol has been previously published11 and is described in the online protocol. Tirofiban or placebo $(0.9\%$ normal saline) infusion $(0.10~\upmu{\mathrm{g}}/$ [kg·min]) was initiated within 24 hours from the angiogram but at least 12 hours after EVD placement and following the MRI. Patients who cannot undergo MRI were not recruited. The infusion was used for a total of 7 days. If the creatinine clearance is ${<}30$ mL/min, the dose was decreased to 0.05 mcg/(kg·min). Thus, the timeline for the patients included in this study is as follows: Patients must present on day of ictus to the emergency department. An EVD is placed emergently and the patient is transferred to the intensive care unit. A noncontrast CT scan is obtained post-EVD to ensure adequate placement and rule out EVD related hemorrhage. An angiogram is performed either simultaneously with the EVD or within 24 hours to coil the aneurysm. An MRI is obtained postangiogram to rule out stroke or hemorrhages and to serve as a baseline MRI. The patient is then started on the study drug 12 hours after the EVD and within 48 hours from ictus. After 7 days, the infusion was stopped and the EVD(s) were weaned over the course of 4 days (see protocol for weaning details). If the patient developed hydrocephalus after clamping the EVD, then a ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) was performed; otherwise, the ventricular drain was removed at the bedside. Thus, the EVD removal or VPS placement were performed at least 4 days after the cessation of the infusion. If the patient required any urgent surgery during the 7 days of infusion, the infusion was stopped for 2 hours and resumed after 4 hours, based on the tirofiban protocol published elsewhere.11 In the case of emergent surgeries, the patient was unblinded per a preestablished safety protocol (required by the institutional review board and DSMB) to assess the need for tirofiban reversal or platelet transfusion. Hemoglobin and platelet counts were obtained daily during the 7-day infusion. Further details of the SAH and tirofiban protocol can be found in the online protocol. A secondary MRI was performed on the day before discharge to assess for development of stroke or new hemorrhages. See schedule of events in the Data Supplement. To ensure appropriate blinding, the research pharmacy prepare the investigational drug for the patients and are the only group that are unblinded. However, unlike the clinical pharmacy group, they do not round or interact with any member of the research team. In addition, the drug came in a black bag so that the nurses, patients, and physicians are blinded. Finally, the data was analyzed blindly by an independent person not involved in the study.

# Outcomes  

The primary end point was to assess whether a 7-day infusion of tirofiban increases the risk of intracranial hemorrhage secondary to EVD placement (and removal) or VPS placement. CT scans were obtained for weaning of the ventriculostomies, after removal of the ventricular catheter, or after placement of a VPS to assess for new hemorrhage, and when clinically indicated (new neurological deficit). The clinical changes were determined by the blinded treating neurosurgeon(s). A second MRI obtained before discharge was compared with the baseline MRI for safety evaluation. The secondary end points were the incidence of moderate or severe angiographic vasospasm found on DSA or CTA, Incidence of clinical vasospasm, incidence of ischemic changes noted on CT or MRI, and incidence of DCI between the 2 arms. DCI was defined as clinical vasospasm with cerebral infarct based on CT or MRI (see protocol). Clinical vasospasm was defined as a change in neurological status that was not attributable to other causes and corresponds radiographically with a CTP positive for a decrease in mean cerebral blood flow, preservation of cerebral blood volume, and increase in mean transit time. The CTP findings were confirmed with a presence of angiographic vasospasm on DSA. DCI was treated with rescue therapy (see online protocol) which included intravenous nicardipine and mean arterial pressures ${\tt>}90$ for 5 days and with endovascular intervention when feasible. For definitions of symptomatic hemorrhage from EVD/VPS, asymptomatic hemorrhage from ventriculostomy/VPS, new symptomatic hemorrhage, new asymptomatic hemorrhage, worsening existing hemorrhage, and radiographic vasospasm, see online protocol.  

The angiographic measurements were based on CTA (which is compared with the admission CTA) and DSA. A decrease in diameter by $25\%$ was classified as mild, a decrease by $25\%$ to $50\%$ was classified as moderate. A decrease by ${>}50\%$ of the original diameter was classified as severe. The changes should involve at least $15~\mathrm{mm}$ in one vessel. The imaging findings were read by 2 independent neuro-interventionist with a third to adjudicate for disagreement.  

Other secondary end points included the following: (1) CTP changes suggestive of vasospasm or ischemia; (2) thrombocytopenia; (3) mortality; (4) the proportion of patients requiring VPS before discharge or at follow-up; (5) length of stay in the surgical intensive care units (days); and (6) mortality. Secondary hydrocephalus requiring ventriculoperitoneal shunt was defined as the inability to wean the EVD due to symptomatic high intracranial pressure or increased size of the ventricles after clamping the drain. The protocol can be found online. The weaning starts 7 days after an aneurysmal rupture unless the patient developed DCI, then the weaning begins 24 to 48 hours after the resolution of DCI.  

For data safety monitoring, adverse events, and serious adverse events, please see online protocol and Data Supplement.  

The drug infusion was stopped if one of the following occurred: symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, asymptomatic intracranial hemorrhage ${>}15$ cc, thrombocytopenia ${<}20000$ the need for major invasive procedures (hemicraniectomy), any new symptomatic hemorrhage requiring transfusion of ${>}2$ units of packed red blood cells (gastrointestinal bleed, retroperitoneal bleed, menorrhagia), or the EVD is no longer functioning and require placement. In the any of the conditions above, unmasking was required by the DSMB and the IRB and if the subject was receiving intravenous tirofiban then platelets and fresh frozen plasma were infused and the patient infusion was not resumed.  



# Statistical Analysis  

The primary outcome was any intracranial hemorrhage during the hospital stay with an expected average of 3 weeks. We expect to observe $5\%$ of the patients in the control group to experience any intracranial hemorrhage during the hospital stay. The risk of asymptomatic intracranial hemorrhage in the tirofiban group is expected to be within $30\%$ difference when compared with control and that of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage to be within $10\%$ difference from the control group. Thus, we assumed an event rate in the tirofiban therapy group to be ${<}35\%$ for asymptomatic and $<15\%$ for symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, with a total event rate of ${<}50\%$ . With a 2-sided $\chi^{2}$ test with a 0.05 type I error rate, the sample size of 20 tirofiban and 10 control patients (30 patients total) allowed us to compare this safety outcome between groups with $80\%$ power. The proportion of patients with hemorrhagic changes classified as intracranial hemorrhage was characterized by $95\%$ confidence limits to determine if there was a difference between the control group and the tirofiban group.  

Other primary safety outcomes included the analysis of adverse events or death related to tirofiban, compared by a 2-sided $\chi^{2}$ test with a 0.05 type I error rate. Secondary end points were compared by a 2-sided chi-square test with a 0.05 type I error rate. The length of stay in the surgical intensive care unit using student’s t-test. The statistical analysis was performed by a blinded (bio)statistician and epidemiologist.  



# RESULTS  



# Patient Characteristics  

From February 2019 to July 2020, we screened 59 patients with aSAH. We excluded 29 patients for the following reasons: The legally authorized representative of 6 patients declined participating in the study, 8 patients required hemicraniectomy microsurgical clipping, 5 patients could not undergo MRI due to history pacemaker placement, 4 patients had blister aneurysms required treatment with flow diverter and dual antiplatelet therapy, and 6 patients had a Hunt-Hess Grade of 5 on presentation and persisted despite EVD placement. A total of 30 patients were thus enrolled in this study. The DSMB met 3 times (at the end of enrolling first 10 subjects, at the end of enrolling second 10 subjects, and at the end of enrolling the third 10 subjects). During these meetings, they evaluated stop points for the study which are outlined below and detailed in the protocol:  

•	 The risk of any intracranial hemorrhage is ${\ge}30\%$ when compared with control   
•	 The risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage is $\geq10\%$ when compared with control.   
The risk of asymptomatic intracranial hemorrhage is ${\ge}30\%$ when compared with control.   
•	 The rate of thrombocytopenia (platelets ${<}20000;$ ) is $\geq10\%$ when compared with control.   
•	 The rate of hemorrhagic bleeding requiring ${>}2$ units of red blood cells transfusion is $\geq10\%$ when compared with control (eg, gastrointestinal, retroperitoneal, etc).   
•	 The threshold of $20\%$ as maximum allowable symptomatic hemorrhage per each quarter. If the threshold is reached, the study will be stopped, and all adverse events are reviewed.   
If all the symptomatic hemorrhage reported is due specifically to the agent being tested, then the DSMB will make recommendation to stop the study.   
•	 If DCI or symptomatic clinical vasospasm is significantly less in one group versus the other during interim analysis.  

Of these subjects, 18 were assigned to the tirofiban group and 12 to the control group. Three patients had to have the study infusion unblinded: one after developing a large EVD hematoma (placebo subject), another after developing asymptomatic thrombocytopenia (tirofiban subject), and the third after developing femoral artery dissection (placebo subject). All patients completed their 6-week follow-up and all scheduled events of the trial. All patients were analyzed for the primary outcomes of the trial. There was no difference at presentation in hydrocephalus, subdural hematoma, intraparenchymal hemorrhage, and intraventricular hemorrhage between the 2 arms (Table 1). There was also no difference in the Fisher Grade or Hunt-Hess Grade at presentation or after EVD placement between the two arms. There were more males in the tirofiban arm $_{\mathrm{P}=0.02}^{\prime}$ ), but no difference was noted in race or ethnicity. Table 1 lists the baseline characteristics. For more details on baseline characteristics and demographics, refer in the Data Supplement.  



# Outcomes  



# Primary Outcomes  

There was no difference between the 2 arms in terms of any intracranial hemorrhage related to EVD placement (all hemorrhages included: tirofiban: $11\%$ [2/18]; placebo: $8\%$ [1/12]; $\mathrm{\simeq}0.80$ ). The tirofiban arm had less DCI incidence compared with placebo $(6\%$ [1/18] versus $33\%$ [4/12]; $P_{=0.04}$ ). This confers an absolute risk reduction of $27\%$ and a number needed to treat of 3.7. For details on the outcomes, please refer to Table 2. Tirofiban also reduced the incidence of clinical vasospasm $(6\%$ [1/18] versus $42\%$ [5/12]; $\widetilde{P{=}0.01}$ ).  



# Safety Outcomes  

There was no statistical difference in death (related to tirofiban or not), symptomatic or asymptomatic hemorrhages related to VPS, development of new hemorrhage, and change in existing hemorrhages (Table  1). Similarly, there was no difference in the development of serious adverse events in the 2 arms. We encountered 24 serious adverse events (10 patients) with tirofiban and 13 with placebo (7 patients). For details on the type of adverse events and serious adverse events, please refer in the Data Supplement.  



# Secondary Outcomes  

We did not find any difference in the incidence of patients (1) requiring VPS between the tirofiban arm and control arm $33\%$ versus $25\%$ respectively; $_{P=0.62}$ ); (2) incidence of thrombocytopenia $(6\%$ versus $0\%,$ $_{P=0.40}$ ); and (3) morality $(6\%$ versus $0\%$ , $_{P=0.40.}$ ). When comparing tirofiban to placebo, there were marked decreases in angiographic vasospasm noted on DSA and or CTA $(6\%$ versus $42\%$ ; $\widetilde{P{=}0.01}$ ), and CTP changes $(6\%$ versus $33\%$ ; $\widetilde{P{=}0.01}$ ). There was a trend toward decreased intensive care unit stay in tirofiban group ${\bf\zeta}_{1}\mathrm{1}\pm\mathrm{3.9}$ days versus $13{\pm}5.26\$ days; $_{P=0.17}$ ). There was a reduction in the ischemic changes noted on MRI, but it did not reach significance $(6\%$ versus $17\%$ $\scriptstyle{\stackrel{\circ}{=}}0.32.$ )  



# DISCUSSION  

The results of this trial support proceeding with further testing of the safety and efficacy of 7-day intravenous infusion of tirofiban in a pragmatic (placing EVD by the bedside), multicenter setting, and in a larger population.

Continuous intravenous tirofiban infusion for 7 days did not increase the risk of any intracranial hemorrhages in this cohort with aSAH treated by primary coiling and had their EVD placed in the operative room. In addition, it reduced the risk of asymptomatic vasospasm, clinical vasospasm, and DCI. However, the trial was a single-center study and enrolled small sample size of subjects who had their EVD placed in the operative room. Further safety and efficacy testing need to validate these findings.  

DCI is known to occur typically between day 4 and 14 in patients with aSAH. When it occurs, it is treated with intraarterial vasodilators or invasive endovascular procedures using balloon angioplasty in combination with vasodilators.38,39 The treatment efficacy is around $40\%$ to $60\%$ with a complication rate of $5\%$ , including vessel perforation.38,39 The difficulty in treating DCI led to the search for a preventive strategy. There has not been any improvement in the prevention of DCI since the introduction of nimodipine in the 1980s,20,21,40 despite multiple clinical trials.41 The narrowing of the large-caliber arteries associated with ischemia after SAH remains a prominent, measurable symptom of the post-SAH syndrome but is only apparent in a minority of DCI patients. DCI has been targeted mechanically (endovascular intervention) and pharmacologically (endothelin-1 antagonist,42 Rho-kinase inhibitor,43 nitric oxide donors44,45) with no clear effect on the outcome. Other therapies attempted to clear the blood to reduce oxy-hemoglobin and other breakdown products46 or to target the procoagulation milieu using antifibrinolytic therapy in the cerebrospinal fluid47; however, they also failed. So far, only nimodipine, a calcium channel blocker, has been effective in decreasing clinical vasospasm by $13\%,^{20}$ DCI by $34\%$ , and overall poor outcomes by $40\%_{,}^{21}$ compared with placebo. Interestingly, nimodipine reduces DCI sometimes without affecting the incidence of radiographic vasospasm, highlighting again the complex underlying pathophysiology.40 Similarly, clinical trials using other vasodilators or calcium channel blockers have demonstrated a lack of improvement in outcome despite improvement in angiographic vasospasm.16,48 Nimodipine increases endogenous fibrinolytic activity, which may reduce the incidence of microthrombosis49 and antagonizes cortical spreading ischemia in rats, which may be one of the culprits in DCI.50 Those properties are not shared by other calcium channel blockers. No other drug so far has been successful in preventing DCI. Thus, for the first time since nimodipine was introduced, a new treatment focusing on platelet inhibition seems to be promising at preventing DCI.  

Soon after aSAH, microthrombi begin to form when platelet aggregates are seen tracking through intraparenchymal microvessels and within pial arterioles.51–53 These platelet aggregates form early after rupture as distal microvasculature constricts to limit the flow, as a form of an adaptive response to control the bleeding. Platelet aggregates also release thromboxanes, which worsen the vasoconstriction,54 and increases resistance to flow. The peak period for the formation of microthrombi is ${\approx}48$ hours in parenchymal and pial vasculature,55 and the number of microthrombi has been shown to correlate with nearby apoptotic neurons.52 Postmortem studies showed patients with large areas of ischemia to have a significantly increased number of microthrombi.55 Microthrombi increases the resistance to flow contributing thus to cortical spreading ischemia56 and DCI, which can potentially be resolved or prevented with antiplatelet therapy.55 We postulated that 7 days of treatment with tirofiban should suppress the hyperactive platelets and reduce the incidence of DCI. The 7-day therapy is based on the retrospective study where tirofiban was used until the EVD was weaned or a shunt had to be placed and the patients were subsequently switched to DAPT. In that study, however, tirofiban was used not to prevent DCI but out of necessity to avoid stent occlusion. It was used as a bridge to oral DAPT as it was found to be safer29 in the acute setting where those patients would require additional emergent procedures. Procedure-related asymptomatic hemorrhage occurred in $8\%$ of patients on tirofiban29 compared with $22\%$ in patients on DAPT,57 with no mortality and no symptomatic hemorrhage. In the current trial, tirofiban is used for 7 days after which the EVD weaning begins over a few days. Thus, by the time the VPS is planned, the patients have been off their infusion for 5 to 6 days. Others have shown tirofiban to be safe and effective at reducing the rate of thromboembolic events and risk of hemorrhage to $1.9\%$ compared with DAPT alone.26 To avoid DAPT, others have used intravenous eptifibatide (GPIIb/IIIa [glycoprotein IIb/IIIa] inhibitor). Although eptifibatide reduced thromboembolic complications, it was associated with $6.3\%$ of intracranial hemorrhage and an accompanying mortality rate of $4.7\%.^{58}$ We postulate that a short half-life, the efficacy of desmopressin $(0.3~{\upmu\mathrm{g}}/{\upk\mathrm{g}}$ IV over $15\min$ ), and the no bolus protocol make tirofiban an attractive alternative to other GPIIb/IIIa inhibitors.  

We recognize the limitations of this study, which was performed in a single institution and consisted of a small sample size, serving as a phase 1/2a study. The small sample size does not allow for a multivariate analysis and leads to the treatment group having a higher rate of hydrocephalus, which was not statistically significant in this small sample. Thus, a pragmatic, multicenter, and larger population of subjects is needed to establish safety and efficacy. This study excluded patients who underwent microsurgical clipping for fear of increased risk of postoperative hemorrhage if randomized to the tirofiban arm. In addition, patients who underwent stentassisted coiling and patients on DAPT for other reasons were not included as they are considered to be already on platelet suppression therapy and cannot be randomized to the placebo or placed on tirofiban as a third antiplatelet suppression agent. As such, there is uncertainty whether these findings can be applied in a larger cohort of patients with aSAH treated with different modalities, and perhaps different standards of care. Future studies should examine other markers, such as platelet inhibition, D-dimers, fibrinogen-degradation products to measure the drug effect and to evaluate whether the underlying protective mechanism is effectively due to platelet suppression. Obtaining these in a randomized controlled study would lead to unblinding if the data is entered in the electronic medical record. Finally, the study sample size did not account to detect a difference in other tirofiban side-effects, but this would be analyzed in a future multicenter study to better judge the overall safety profile versus efficacy in reducing DCI.  

Strength of this study includes a rigorous methodology, including a standardized definition of what constitutes DCI. There are considerable inconsistencies in the definitions of DCI in both the clinical trials and observational studies. A major cause of this is the difficulty of combining radiographic evidence of vasospasm with clinical features of cerebral ischemia.59 The second issue is the variability and overlap of terms used to describe each phenomenon.59 In this trial, we used clear definitions,59 accounting for a more comprehensive model of DCI that includes factors other than the angiographic vasoconstriction of vessels, such as clinical deficits and CTP objective parameters.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

The results of this trial support proceeding with further testing of the safety and efficacy of 7-day intravenous infusion of tirofiban in a pragmatic (placing EVD by the bedside), multicenter setting, and in a larger population.
