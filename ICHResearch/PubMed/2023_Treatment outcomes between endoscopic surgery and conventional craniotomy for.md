# Treatment outcomes between endoscopic surgery and conventional craniotomy for spontaneous supratentorial intracerebral hemorrhage: a randomized controlled trial

# Abstract  

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has been repeatedly evaluated in patients with ICH as a promising procedure for improved survival and functional outcome. Among MIS techniques, endoscopic surgery (ES) has shown superior efficacy for ICH removal due to rapid clot evacuation and immediate bleeding control. However, the results of ES are still uncertain due to insufficient data. In this study, participants with spontaneous supratentorial ICH who were indicated for surgery were randomly assigned (1:1) to undergo ES or conventional craniotomy (CC) between March 2019 and June 2022. The primary outcome was a difference in favorable modified Rankin Scale (mRS) outcome (0 to 3) at 180-day follow-up evaluated by blind assessors. There were 188 participants, 95 in the ES group and 93 in the CC group, who completed the trial. At 180-day follow-up, 46 $(48.4\%)$ participants in the ES group achieved favorable outcomes, compared to 33 $(35.5\%)$ in the CC group (risk difference [RD] 12.9, $95\%\mathrm{CI}-1.1{-}27.0$ , $p{=}0.07;$ . After covariate adjustment, the difference was slightly higher and significant (adjusted RD 17.3, $95\%$ CI [4.6–30.0], $p{=}0.01$ ). Moreover, the ES group had less operative duration and less intraoperative blood loss than the CC group. Clot evacuation rate and complications were similar between the two groups. Subgroup analyses showed a potential benefit of ES in age $<60$ years, time to surgery $\geq6\mathrm{h}$ , and deep ICH. This study showed that ES was safe and effective in ICH removal and provided a better functional outcome compared to CC.  

Keywords  Conventional craniotomy $\cdot$ Endoscopic surgery $^{\ast}$ Intracerebral hemorrhage $\cdot$ Functional outcome  

# Introduction  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a common problem in neurosurgery with an incidence of 24.6 per 100,000 person-years [1]. The initial treatment of ICH aims to prevent the expansion of the hematoma by controlling blood pressure and reversal of bleeding disorders. In addition, surgical evacuation of ICH is indicated to relieve intracranial pressure in patients with large hematoma. Despite appropriate treatment, patients with ICH carry poor prognosis with a mortality rate of 40 to $50\%$ and a dependency rate of $60\%$ [2].  

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses have evaluated the benefits of surgery for supratentorial ICH; however, none of these have shown improved functional outcomes compared to the best medical treatment [3–6]. Among these studies, the meta-analysis of Mendelow et al., which included the results of both the Surgical Trial in Intracerebral Hemorrhage (STICH) I and II, reported a significant advantage of surgery for ICH in their updated meta-analysis [4]. They also found that the effect of surgery could affect the included studies that used minimally invasive surgery (MIS). Furthermore, advanced MIS techniques for ICH have been continuously developed, including stereotactic aspiration (SA) with/without thrombolysis, keyhole surgery, and endoscopic surgery (ES) to achieve rapid clot removal with better access and less surrounding brain injury [7]. To date, MIS techniques have been repeatedly evaluated in patients with supratentorial ICH and have demonstrated an improvement in both functional outcome and survival, while conventional craniotomy (CC) is suggested as a life-saving procedure due to the failure to improve functional outcome [8–13].  

Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus rt-PA for Intracerebral Hemorrhage Evacuation (MISTIE) III trial, using SA plus thrombolysis, was recently published and failed to validate the improved functional outcomes [14]. This trial suggested that removal to $\leq15~\mathrm{ml}$ of ICH volume or $\ge70\%$ volume reduction could increase in the probability of achieving a favorable outcome. However, the MISTIE III trial achieved residual ICH volume $\leq15\mathrm{ml}$ at $59.7\%$ and an average reduction in hematoma size at $69\%$ . This was related to the performance of SA, which was less effective in removing the adherent clot and required a stable clot period of at least $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ before thrombolysis. In contrast, ES has shown superior efficacy for ICH removal due to earlier clot evacuation with direct visualization and immediate bleeding control. Several authors reported a clot evacuation rate of ES ranging between 70 and $90\%$ [5, 15–17], and the data showed that ES resulted in improved functional outcomes compared to SA and CC [6, 7, 18–20].  

Nevertheless, current evidence is uncertain to conclude the benefit of ES for ICH removal due to the lack of highquality clinical trials, the heterogeneity of patient selection, and the difference in surgical techniques and instruments. Therefore, we conducted this study to compare the safety and efficacy between ES and CC for the treatment of supratentorial ICH. In addition, subgroup analyses were performed to identify patients who benefit from ES.  

# Materials and methods  

This RCT was conducted with the approval of the local institutional review board in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was registered with the Thai Registry of Clinical Trials, TCTR20200116002. Patients with spontaneous ICH between March 2019 and June 2022 at our institution were enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria included (1) age 18 to 80 years; (2) newly diagnosed supratentorial $\mathrm{ICH}\geq30\mathrm{ml}$ ; (3) Glasgow coma scale (GCS) 5 to 14 or $\mathrm{NIHSS}>6$ before treatment; (4) onset less than $^{24\textrm{h}}$ ; and (4) baseline-modified Rankin Scale (mRS) 0 to 1 before the onset.  

Exclusion criteria were (1) ICH associated with vascular malformations, tumors, or trauma; (2) bilateral fixed dilated pupils or extensor motor posturing; (3) presence of intraventricular hemorrhage $(\mathrm{IVH})>50\%$ in the lateral ventricle or obstructive hydrocephalus; (4) infratentorial hemorrhage; (5) uncorrectable coagulopathy; (6) active bleeding in the gastrointestinal, retroperitoneal, or respiratory tract; (7) platelet count $<100{,}000$ per microliter or internaltional normalized ratio $>1.3$ ; (8) end-stage renal or liver disease  

or other disease contraindicated for surgery; (9) pregnancy;   
and (10) unidentified subjects or lack of caregivers.  

# Randomization  

After informed consent was obtained, participants were randomly assigned to receive ES or CC. The randomization sequence was created using Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) with a 1:1 allocation using random block sizes of 2 and 4 by a biostatistician. Outcome measurements were collected by assessors who did not involve in the treatment. The assessors were kept blinded to the allocation.  

# Treatment protocol  

After the diagnosis of ICH, all participants were treated using the recommendations of the American Heart Association for the treatment of spontaneous ICH [9]. An intravenous nicardipine infusion was initiated to maintain blood pressure at $140/90\mathrm{mmHg}$ . Hyperosmolar and antiepileptic agents were administered if required. In addition, fluid level, electrolyte, and coagulation were normalized before surgery.  

# Procedure  

ES for ICH removal was performed by surgeons who experienced more than 50 independent cases. The surgical approach was planned using the image guidance system. The surgical entry point was chosen according to the location of the hematoma and the shortest distance between the entry point and the hematoma, typically the frontal (Kocher’s) point for the basal ganglia hematoma, the parietal (Keen’s) point for the thalamic hematoma, and the closest surface cortical point for the lobar hematoma (Fig. 1). Then, a burr hole or a small craniotomy was performed to make the surgical trajectory cover the width of the hematoma. After opening the dura, a transparent sheath (Neuroport, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a $4\mathrm{mm}$ endoscopic lens (Karl Storz Endoscopy, Tuttlingen, Germany) was introduced, reaching the hematoma. The clot was then removed by 8- and 10-Fr monopolar suction coagulators until the surrounding brain gradually collapsed into the sheath. During clot removal, the sheath advanced in an outside-in direction, using its side wall to protect the brain while keeping the tip of the sheath at the center of the hematoma, allowing the clot to continuously migrate into the sheath. For bleeding control, an attempt was made to avoid using electrocauterization, especially in the deep part of the hematoma, to decrease the injury to the surrounding brain tissue, as previously described by the authors [16]. Warm saline irrigation or application of hemostatic agents was used instead. After hemostasis, the hematoma cavity was circumferentially explored with continuous saline irrigation to inspect the residual bleeding point and clot, and then the sheath was gradually removed along the surgical trajectory [21]. The dura was then closed, and the opening of the bone was covered with a plate.

For CC, a transcortical approach was planned at a sulcus or gyrus that has the shortest distance to the hematoma. After craniotomy and dura opening, the hematoma was accessed through a small transsulcal or transcortical corridor and removed under microscopic visualization. The bleeding was stopped with a bipolar coagulator and hemostatic materials. After adequate decompression of the ICH, the closure was performed in a routine manner.  

# Postoperative management  

After surgery, participants were managed in the neurosurgical intensive care unit for at least $^{24\textrm{h}}$ and subsequently transferred to the general ward until discharge. Participants were scheduled to follow up at 30 days, 90 days, and 180 days. The assessment, including neurologic function, GCS, mRS, and NIHSS, was examined by blinded assessors at each visit.  

# Radiographic analysis  

All participants received postoperative computed tomography (CT) scans on days 1 and 3, or as clinically indicated. The CT image data in the DICOM format were analyzed using OsiriX software (version 4.1, Pixmeo; Geneva,  

Switzerland). Hematomas were identified using semi-automated segmentation in each slice at a threshold of 40–100 Hounsfield units. The hematoma volume was then calculated by the sum of the surface area of each slice to measure the preoperative and postoperative ICH volumes. The hematoma removal rate was subsequently measured as the percentage reduction of the total volume of the preoperative hematoma.  

# Outcomes  

The primary outcome was the difference in favorable mRS outcome, defining as the score 0 to 3, at 180 days. The secondary outcomes in this study included the difference in mRS outcomes at 30 days and 90 days, the mortality rate, the complication rate, and the surgical outcomes including operative duration, blood loss, clot reduction rate, and hospital stay.  

# Statistical analysis  

Based on previous studies, we assumed that ES and CC could approximately result in favorable outcomes of $45\%$ and $25\%$ , respectively [4, 6, 14]. A sample size of 200 patients (100 patients in each treatment group) would provide $80\%$ power to detect a $20\%$ difference in favorable outcomes between ES and CC groups at a type I error rate of 0.05. Data monitoring was carried out to ensure the efficacy and safety of interim analyses after 60, 120, and 180 participants were enrolled. The stopping guidelines for the trial were based on O’Brien-Fleming to determine the critical boundaries in each interim test [22].  

Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and were analyzed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, while categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and were analyzed using chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests. The average treatment effect comparing ES and CC was estimated using the intention-to-treat principle. For the primary analysis, we estimated the difference in a favorable outcome and the $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) using a generalized linear model with adjustment of the baseline characteristics that affected severity to reduce the variability in the outcome, as recommended by the guidelines for RCTs [23]. The variables included in the adjusted analysis were age, GCS, ICH volume, ICH location (superficial vs. deep), side of hematoma (right vs. left), and presence of IVH. In addition, post hoc analysis was performed to explore the heterogeneity of the treatment effect across each stratum of baseline variables using interaction tests. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (Version 14.1, StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Statistical significance was set at $p<0.05$ for the two-tailed test.  

# Results  

The flow chart for study enrollment is shown in Fig. 2. Two hundred participants were randomized to ES $(n=100),$ ) and CC groups $(n=100)$ ). There were 95 participants in the ES group, and 93 participants in the CC group completed the trial at 180 days of follow-up. For the entire cohort, the median age was 50.5 (16) years with 130 $(69.1\%)$ male participants. The median duration from onset to hospital arrival was 4 (1.8) hours. For baseline clinical status, the median GCS was 10 (5) and the median NIHSS was 18 (9). The ICH locations were the basal ganglion in 174 $(92.6\%)$ , thalamus in 5 $(2.7\%)$ , and the cerebral lobe in 9 $(4.8\%)$ with 99 $(52.7\%)$ located on the left side. Median ICH volume was 50 (30.7) ml with the presence of IVH in 40 $(20.3\%)$ . There were no significant differences between the groups in demographic and baseline characteristics (Table 1).  

The surgical outcomes of the ES and CC groups are shown in Table 2. Participants in both groups underwent surgery after onset at an equivalent time period of 6.8 (2) and 6.6 (2.5) hours in ES and CC groups, respectively $\left(p=0.91\right.$ ). In the ES group, the median diameter of the bone opening was 2.3 (1.3) cm, while the median craniotomy size was 5.5 (5.7) cm in the CC group $(p<0.01)$ . The most common entry site was equivalent between the two groups at the frontal point due to the most common location of ICH at the basal ganglion $(p=0.35)$ . ES group had less operative duration (107 [24] minutes vs. 136 [25] minutes, $p<0.01\$ ) and less intraoperative blood loss (113 [26] ml vs. 300 [10] ml, $p<0.01;$ ) than the CC group. Participants in the ES group achieved a similar degree of ICH volume reduction to those in the CC group (87.6 $[26]\%$ vs. 81.0 [24.8] $\%$ , $p{=}0.18\$ . In ES and CC groups, 83 $(87.4\%)$ and 75 $(80.6\%)$ participants achieved volume reduction $\ge70\%$ $\protect\overrightarrow{p}=0.21)$ . As a result, the median residual ICH volume was 6.5 (8.3) ml and 10.1 (11.6) ml in ES and CC groups, respectively $(p=0.05)$ . There were 79 $(83.2\%)$ participants who achieved clot removal to $\leq15~\mathrm{mL}$ in the ES group, compared to 67 $(72.0\%)$ in the CC group $(p=0.07)$ . ICU stay (2 [27] days vs. 3 [10] days, $p{=}0.94,$ and hospital stay (11 [28] days vs. 10 [15.6] days, $p{=}0.72,$ ) were similar between the two groups.  

At 180-day follow-up, 46 $(48.4\%)$ participants in the ES group achieved favorable outcomes (mRS 0 to 3), compared to 33 $(35.5\%)$ in the CC group; however, the difference was not statistically significant (risk difference [RD] 12.9, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}-1.1{-}27.0$ , $p{=}0.07^{\cdot}$ ). After covariate adjustment, the difference in favorable outcome between ES and CC was slightly higher and the treatment effect was significant (adjusted RD 17.3, $95\%$ CI [4.6–30.0], $p{=}0.01$ ). In addition, there were no significant differences in mRS outcomes between the groups after surgery at 30 days (31 $[32.6\%]$ vs. 21 $[22.6\%]$ , RD 10.1, $95\%$ CI − 2.7–22.8, $p{=}0.12$ ) and 90  days (50 $[52.6\%]$ vs. 43 $[43.6\%]$ , RD 8.8, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}-1.6{-}23.8\$ , $p{=}0.21$ ). The distribution of mRS during follow-up is illustrated in Table 3. Additional dichotomous analyses were performed using different cut points of mRS. There was no difference in functional outcome when using $\mathrm{mRS2}$ as the cut-off point, death $\mathrm{(mRS=6)}$ , or severe disability $(\mathrm{mRS}=5\$ ) at each time point of follow-up.

There were no significant differences in complications between the groups (Table  4). The most common complication was non-neurologic infection (20 $[21.2\%]$ vs. 17 $[18.3\%]$ , $p{=}0.63,$ ), such as pneumonia and urinary tract infection. Rebleeding rate was similar between the two groups (3 $[3.2\%]$ vs. 5 $[5.4\%]$ , $p{=}0.50,$ . All rebleeding occurred in participants who underwent surgery in less than $^{6\mathrm{~h~}}$ . Progressive cerebral edema after the surgery occurred in 6 $(6.3\%)$ and 4 $(4.3\%)$ participants in ES and CC groups, respectively $(p=0.75)$ . Among these, 1 participant in each group deteriorated due to severe progressive brain edema that required decompressive craniectomy. None of these required reoperations. Meningitis occurred in 4 $(4.2\%)$ cases in the ES group and 2 $(2.2\%)$ in the CC group $(p=0.68)$ .  

The result of subgroup analyses is shown in Fig. 3. There was a trend toward the positive effect of ES in each stratum of baseline characteristics, but $p$ values for the interaction test were not significant. Among these subgroups, the treatment effect of ES was greater than CC in participants with ages less than 60 years, time to surgery longer than $^{6\mathrm{~h~}}$ , and deep ICH.  

# Discussion  

Recently, MIS techniques, including ES, have been reported to be a feasible technique for ICH removal with the advantages of rapid clot evacuation, improved visualization, and less traumatization of the surrounding brain [29]. In this study, we included patients with supratentorial ICH larger than $30~\mathrm{ml}$ , and the results showed that ES for ICH could increase approximately 13 to $17\%$ of favorable outcomes at 180-day follow-up compared to CC. The treatment effect did not achieve our assumption of a $20\%$ difference in functional outcome between ES and CC because we sought to entirely remove ICH using thorough and well-controlled hemostasis during both endoscopic technique and craniotomy, which may have results to better outcomes, compared to the previous. Although the efficacy of clot volume reduction $(87.6\%$ vs. $81.0\%$ ) and residual ICH (6.5 ml vs. 8.4 ml) were similar in both techniques, ES had advantages in terms of shorter operative time and less blood loss. Previous studies reported a higher evacuation rate, ranging from 70 to $90\%$ [5, 15, 17, 30, 31], and better functional outcomes in ES, compared to the aspiration technique and craniotomy [6, 7, 11, 20, 32].

Data from meta-analyses showed that ES resulted in a 1.3 to 3.3 times higher probability of good functional outcomes than other treatment modalities [11, 20, 33]. In addition, the network meta-analysis reported that ES was the most effective treatment for ICH removal among different surgical interventions, including ES, aspiration plus thrombolysis, and craniotomy [19].  

The functional outcomes of ES in this study were comparable with several authors that reported good functional outcomes, ranging from 40 to $60\%$ [6]. Data suggested that these outcomes were related to the degree of clot evacuation [14]. With $87.4\%$ of participants achieving volume reduction $\ge70\%$ and $83.2\%$ of participants who achieved clot removal to $\leq15~\mathrm{mL}$ in the ES group, we achieved favorable outcomes in $48.4\%$ of participants with a mortality rate of $22.1\%$ . Auer et al. demonstrated a favorable outcome of $40\%$ in patients who underwent ES, of which $44\%$ reached more than $70\%$ of clot evacuation rate [34]. Vespa et al. showed $42.9\%$ of patients with favorable outcomes at 180 days with a median evacuation rate of $71.2\%$ [5]. A multicenter trial of the use of a tubular sheath system also showed a favorable outcome at $52\%$ , and $72\%$ of the patients had a reduction in clot volume of more than $90\%$ [17].  

The major caveat of ES for ICH is intraoperative bleeding control, especially active arterial bleeding. Most of the arterial bleeding usually occurred at the deep and medial part of the hematoma; therefore, meticulous removal of the clot with an outside-in technique can avoid this such event. In the case of active bleeding, it could be effectively controlled with a monopolar suction coagulator, as described in previous studies [1, 7]. In this study, we found that active bleeding required a coagulator only in 30 $(31.6\%)$ of the participants in the ES group. In contrast, low-flow bleeding during ES can be managed with continuous warm saline irrigation or hemostatic agents [21]. With these techniques, we achieved successful hemostasis in all cases without conversion to craniotomy. Although the rate of rebleeding was $3.2\%$ in the ES group, none of them required reoperation, and the rate did not differ from that of the CC group (Fig. 4).

Previous studies suggested that patient selection, particularly clinical severity and timing of surgery, is a key factor of success in surgical management for patients with ICH [35, 36]. STICH and STICH II revealed that despite the lack of advantage of surgery over conservative treatment, subgroups of patients with GCS 10–13 or large ICH were likely to benefit from surgery [3, 4, 27]. The meta-analysis of Zhou et al. found that MIS for ICH was beneficial in subgroups of patients, including both sexes, age of 30 to 80 years, superficial ICH, $\mathrm{{GCS}}\geq9$ , hematoma volume between 25 and $40\mathrm{ml}$ , and surgery within $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ after onset, compared to other treatments [37]. The post hoc subgroup analyses in this study found that ES had a positive effect on subgroups of age less than 60 years, timing to surgery $\geq6\mathrm{{h}}$ , and deep ICH.  

Old age has been identified as a factor in poor outcomes and mortality in patients with ICH [24, 26, 28, 38]. In addition, elderly patients have limited tolerance to surgery and frequently encounter with postoperative complications. Therefore, surgical removal of ICH, either in MIS or in the conventional technique, would yield similar results in older patients. In contrast, previous studies found that younger age was a positive factor for improved outcomes after surgical treatment for ICH [39, 40]. The meta-analysis also indicated a potential benefit of MIS for the removal of ICH between 30 and 80 years of age [37].

The optimal timing of ICH surgery is controversial because early surgery, especially within $^{6\mathrm{~h~}}$ after onset, increases the risk of rebleeding, whereas delayed surgery is often complicated with perihematomal brain edema leading to a worse outcome as a consequence [25, 41]. This is consistent with our study that all rebleeding occurred in patients who underwent surgery less than $^{6\mathrm{~h~}}$ , while brain edema was found in most patients who underwent surgery after $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ from the onset. Furthermore, we found that ES less than $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ was often complicated with active bleeding that required the use of electrocautery, further damaging to the surrounding brain tissue. In contrast, the bleeding control was more comfortable with a stable clot in the ES later than $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ . These results have led to a positive trend toward ES over CC for a time window of more than $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ . Several authors have stated that surgery for ICH within 6 to $^{24\textrm{h}}$ is a reasonable time window, and that the advantages of MIS evacuation still exist for $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ [35, 37]. Hsu et al. reported the experience of more than 400 patients who underwent ES with cauterization in less than $5\%$ of cases and a rebleeding rate of $3.9\%$ , suggesting that surgery within 4 to $^{6\mathrm{~h~}}$ was the most likely to achieve a favorable outcome [1]. Therefore, unless there is an emergent condition, ES with restricted cauterization within 4 to $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ after ictus may ameliorate outcomes by reducing the aforementioned risks.  

The location of ICH has been reported as a factor associated with the outcome [42]. Although most studies did not find an advantage of craniotomy for ICH regardless of its location, some authors demonstrated a potential benefit of surgical treatment for superficial ICH [4, 27, 43]. However, the effect of the location of ICH in MIS remains inconclusive: Several studies found the benefit of MIS in patients with deep ICH [5, 35], while others have shown that patients with superficial ICH may benefit from MIS [34, 37, 44, 45]. This is due to insufficient data and the heterogeneity of patient selection and surgical techniques in each study. However, we found that ES appeared to be more advantageous than CC for patients with deep ICH, as this technique met the concept of keyhole surgery that required a small exposure and less surgical maneuverability to remove the deep-seated hematoma as opposed to the superficial lesion [33].  

There are several limitations to our study. First, despite the significant difference in the primary outcome between ES and CC groups, the clinical implications of this result require further validation because a suitable minimally important difference in surgical treatment for ICH has not been clearly established. In addition, the results of post hoc subgroup analyses in this study require further investigation to ensure robustness. Second, potential biases may be present in this study. RCTs of surgical interventions commonly preclude double blinding, as in this study. In addition, we did not compare the outcomes of surgical groups with those of conservative medical treatment groups as a control group. However, the outcomes were measured by blinded assessors at each follow-up, and we selected participants with ICH who were preferably indicated for surgery. Lastly, these results obtained from a single institutional experience could limit the generalizability. Although we specified the learning curve of the surgeons who were eligible in this study, ES has recently been adopted in our institution and some surgeons did not perform ES for ICH removal. In addition, our ES is different from other emerging techniques that are in the ongoing multicenter RCTs [46–48].  

# Conclusions  

For patients with supratentorial ICH who were surgically indicated, ES was more beneficial in functional outcomes than CC. In addition, ICH removal with ES was performed in a shorter operative time and less blood loss with comparable complications. These findings confirmed the safety and efficacy of clot removal with minimizing brain injury during endoscopic removal of ICH. Further studies are required in multicenter trials to validate these results and to optimize surgical treatment for ICH with emerging endoscopic techniques.
