# ABSTRACT  

Clinical evidence comparing bridging endovascular thrombectomy (bEVT) with intravenous thrombolysis and direct endovascular thrombectomy (dEVT) without thrombolysis for patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) presented directly to an EVT-capable center is overwhelming but inconsistent. This study aimed to analyze the progress and controversies in clinical evidence based on current meta-analyses. Three databases, including MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library, were searched. Relevant data were extracted and reviewed from the pooled studies. The Assessment of Multiple Systematic Review (AMSTAR-2) was used for quality assessment. Twenty-five metaanalyses were finally included. There were $56\%$ (14/25) from Asian countries, $20\%$ (5/25) from North America, and $24\%$ (6/25) from Europe. The majority $(72\%,$ 18/25) of evidence arose in a short period from 2020 to 2022 with the serial publication of four randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Among the 25 meta-analyses, 11 pooled at least three RCTs but there is substantial overlap among seven (five recruited the same four RCTs solely and two recruited the same three RCTs solely). Meanwhile, quality rating based on AMSTAR-2 showed 16 'high' rated studies $(64\%)$ . For functional independence, $40\%$ (10/25) of studies favored bEVT and $60\%$ showed neutral results. For symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage, most studies $(82.6\%,$ 19/23) showed no significant difference. Non-RCT studies contributed to evidence favoring bEVT. Current RCTs provide an update of clinical evidence comparing bEVT and dEVT. However, they simultaneously contribute to an unnecessary overlap among studies. Contemporary observational studies demonstrated different but possibly confounded evidence. Thus, this issue still requires more clinical evidence under standard procedures.  



# INTRODUCTION  

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with large vessel occlusion (LVO) being a major cause.1 Currently, endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) and intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) are two classical therapies used for AIS-LVO. Several randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of EVT after AIS, and EVT has been recommended as the first-line therapy for  

AIS-LVO.2-5 Conversely, although the efficacy of IVT has proven to be limited, with only $10\%{-}20\%$ achieving successful reperfusion, alteplase prior to EVT may have advantages. Besides the possibility of early recanalization before EVT, it may decrease thrombus burden and dissolve smaller distal emboli. Moreover, for patients transferred from a secondary hospital to an EVT-capable hospital, IVT should always be given if patients are eligible, as it would be unethical to withhold a possibly useful treatment over the lengthened duration of the transfer time.6 The research comparing bEVT to dEVT only considers patients presented directly to an EVT-capable center.7 8  

The efficacy and safety of two treatment strategies, bridging EVT (combined treatment of IVT and EVT; bEVT) and direct EVT (EVT alone; dEVT) for patients presenting directly to an EVT-capable center, have been hotly debated. Several RCTs (eg, DIRECT-­MT, DEVT, SKIP, MR CLEAN-­NO IV, SWIFT DIRECT, and DIRECT-SAFE) and observational studies have been performed, greatly updating the clinical evidence available to clinicians.9–14 Subsequently, meta-analyses have attempted to further clarify this issue.15–20 However, these attempts and their source data differ in their methodologies and data reporting systems, limiting the ability to accurately assimilate data from different studies. In addition, the outcomes of prior metaanalyses on bridging therapy versus direct endovascular treatment may be less reliable when based on observational studies. In most observational studies, patients were only withheld alteplase in presence of contraindications. These contraindications include factors like onset to presentation time ${>}4.5$ hours and anticoagulant use, which are in turn associated with poorer outcomes. Thus, the results are confounded by indication.9 Thus, we sought to summarize the current progress and controversies around relevant meta-analyses comparing bEVT and dEVT, with the aim to provide a thorough overview of this important issue and guide future research directions.  



# METHODS Study outcomes and search strategy  

The objective of this study was to compare and discuss the findings of previous meta-analyses comparing the safety and effectiveness of bEVT and dEVT for AIS-LVO. This study was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).21 The search strategy was established using relevant key words, which were “Stroke,” “Thrombectomy,” “Bridging therapy,” “Endovascular,” “Intravenous thrombolysis,” and “Plasminogen.” Three databases, comprising MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library, were searched by two independent reviewers for relevant studies. The detailed search strategy was as given in the online supplementary material (see online supplemental table 1 Search strategy).  



# Eligibility criteria and identification process  

Previously published meta-analyses comparing the safety and effectiveness between bEVT and dEVT for AIS patients were included. There were no restrictions regarding the language of publication or the study design of the included studies. Studies that had data overlap, inaccessible full text, and were not metaanalyses were excluded. The studies identified by our search strategy were screened initially by abstract and, if eligible, by full text to fit inclusion and exclusion criteria. Next, two authors screened all studies against the predefined inclusion criteria, followed by a discussion to reach a consensus when conflicts occurred. Finally, a third senior author arbitrated any unresolved disagreement.  



# Data extraction and quality assessment  

After eligible studies were identified, data were extracted. Extracted data included authors, title, year and month published, journal name, number of included studies, last literature search date, number of patients receiving bEVT, number of patients receiving dEVT, and study design.  

Outcome data were extracted by two authors independently. The primary outcomes included functional independence defined as modified Rankin scale (mRS) 0–2 at 90 days. The secondary outcomes included excellent outcome defined as $\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}1$ at 90 days, any successful recanalization (defined as modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction [mTICl] 2b-3, extended Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction [eTICl] 2b-3, modified Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction [mTIMI] 2b-3, or Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction [TICI]2b-3), successful recanalization with $\leq2$ device passes, and complete reperfusion (defined as mTICI 3, eTICI 3, mTIMI 3, or TICI 3). The safety outcomes included mortality at 90 days, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), and asymptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (aICH). Assessment of Multiple Systematic Review (AMSTAR-2) was used for quality assessment.22 Two authors discussed their respective ratings, and meta-analyses were marked high or moderate based on their quality and estimated overall scores. Bibliometrix R-packages (4.2.0) and Biblioshiny, the app version tool of Bibliometrix,23 were used to obtain the main author’s elaboration among different countries and conduct the Collaboration WorldMap.

# OUTCOME COMPARISON  

The outcomes of included studies were inconsistent, summarized in figure  4. The results of initial subgroup or adjusted analysis in included studies are listed in online supplemental figure 1. Data were classified by outcome supporting a significantly higher rate of bEVT (tagged green), supporting a significantly higher rate of dEVT (tagged orange), without significant difference (tagged blue), and non-reported (tagged yellow). The number of included primary studies that support each outcome are described in figure 4 with elaboration in online supplemental figure 1.  

For functional independence at 90 days, $40\%$ (10/25) of studies favored bEVT and $60\%$ showed no significant difference between bEVT and dEVT. According to AMSTAR-2, the rate of high-quality results in meta-analyses favoring bEVT is $90\%$ (9/10) while the rate in meta-analyses showing no significant difference is only $40\%$ (6/15). In addition, results favoring bEVT correlate with the number of included studies in meta-analyses. Evidence favoring bEVT is drawn from meta-analyses with large numbers of pooled studies $(>13)$ . Regarding the risk of sICH, most studies $(82.6\%$ , 19/23) showed no significant difference between bEVT and dEVT, among which high-quality studies accounted for $68.4\%$ (13/19). Only $17\%$ (4/23) of studies showed a higher rate of sICH in the bEVT group. As for aICH, only $40\%$ (10/25) of studies reported this outcome and $60\%$ $(6/10)$ of them showed a higher rate of aICH in the bEVT group.  

Different subgroup analyses and adjustments were used in the included studies (online supplemental figure 1). Subgroup analyses included RCT or non-RCT, geographic region, IVT eligibility, and onset-to-puncture time. The results of subgroup analyses indicate that bridging therapy tends to be associated with a better prognosis, lower mortality, and higher risk of bleeding.

# DISCUSSION  

Our study summarizes the progress and controversies from 25 meta-analyses comparing the effectiveness and safety between bEVT and dEVT for treating AIS-LVO. Over half of the researches $(56\%)$ were from Asian countries, particularly China $(48\%)$ . The serial publication of four RCTs (DIRECT-­MT, DEVT, SKIP, and MR CLEAN-­NO IV) between 2019 to 2021 considerably advanced clinical evidence over the last 3 years.  

The most recently published two RCTs in various geographical settings (ie, SWIFT DIRECT? and DIRECT-SAFE1l1) have been published, but no related pooled evidence has been found. Among the six published RCTs, two of them (DIRECT-­MT and DEVT) showed similar results between bEVT and dEVT, one (MR CLEAN-­NO IV) showed that dEVT was neither superior nor non-inferior to bEVT, and the remaining RCTs (SWIFT DIRECT, DIRECT-SAFE, and SKIP) did not show the noninferiority of dEVT compared with bEVT. While more studies supported neutral results in terms of functional independence, there is evidence supporting bEVT, especially among nonrandomized data. Further, the trend of evidence favoring bEVT was more conspicuous as the number of non-RCTs increased, which hints at existing differences between RCTs and observational studies.  

Generally, bridging thrombolysis before EVT has both pros and cons. It may have the possibility of early recanalization before EVT, decrease clot burden, and resolve smaller distal emboli. However, it may have low/futile effect on large thrombi, increase hemorrhage risk and costs, lead to allergic reactions, and delay EVT treatment. Also, for patients needing transfer from primary stroke centers to EVT-capable centers, IVT should always be administered when patients are eligible.6 The treatment comparison between bEVT and dEVT is only applicable to patients presenting directly to EVT-capable centers. At the same time, for EVT-capable centers it may be reasonable to choose dEVT for LVO-AIS patients with ICH. In the current study, a limited number of studies analyzed the difference between RCTs and non-RCTs comparing bEVT and dEVT. The non-RCTs (observational studies) seemed to show results favoring bEVT, while the RCTs did not – a difference likely caused by confounding by IVT indication in the observational studies. However, a definitive conclusion could not be reached, since none of the studies took the most recent RCTs (SWIFT DIRECT9 and DIRECT${\mathrm{SAFE}}^{11})$ into account. While considering observational studies may be intrinsically confounded, future meta-analysis may also need to underscore the possible difference between RCT and non-RCT studies. Also, possible confounding factors, such as IVT-eligibility, onset-to-puncture time, and clot burden would hold clinical value when investigating this issue.  

The dramatic pace of new clinical evidence in recent years supports the importance and value of comparing bEVT and dEVT across many studies. However, our present analysis shows there are some drawbacks in the recent meta-analyses that attempted to achieve this objective. The first of these is overlapping. This could be due to the serial publication of RCTs in recent years as most meta-analyses were published between 2020 and March 2022. For example, as many as five meta-analyses analyzed solely the same four RCTs and showed the same results.15 18 32 41 42 A similar overlap could be found in earlier years in another two meta-analyses analyzing the same three RCTs available at that time.17 30 Considering the current status, the two new RCTs (SWIFT DIRECT? and DIRECT-SAFE1l) are very likely to cause further overlap of meta-analysis in this field, a fact that should not be neglected by researchers. Another concern is the overall quality of the existing meta-analyses. High-quality evidence only accounts for $60\%$ (15/25) of observed meta-analyses.

Therefore, it is necessary to avoid the repeated work of pooling evidence comparing bEVT and dEVT in various teams, and several measures would be advisable. First, meta-analysis should only be performed if authors know that the data are likely to be complete for the foreseeable future (ie, meta-analysis of observational data before RCTs are initiated or meta-analysis after all known and registered RCTs are completed). Also, authors may need to obey standard procedures such as registration (eg, PROSPERO) to avoid repetition, and following research assessment principles to lower bias may help guarantee the overall quality.9 11 A dditionally, if possible, the editors of different journals may need to accept any submitted meta-­ analysis that is unlikely to be outdated soon. Moreover, costeffectiveness analyses are also necessary when comparing the two treatment approaches. Furthermore, clinical trials applying new thrombolytics such as tenecteplase for bridging therapy (eg, BRIDGE-TNK, NCT04733742) may show differing results from previous RCTs using alteplase, and further provide unique clinical evidence. There are still problems remaining to be solved. For example, although bEVT increased successful reperfusion observed in some trials, this failed to translate into more favorable functional outcomes. So, if reperfusion increases without improved functional outcomes, would a treatment be worthwhile – essentially increasing futile reperfusion rates?  



# CONCLUSIONS  

The present review provides aggregated evidence from relevant meta-analyses comparing the safety and efficacy between bEVT and dEVT for LVO-AIS. The six RCTs published over the past 2 years have supplied much clinical evidence comparing bEVT and dEVT, but have also given rise to overlapping meta-analyses. The RCTs generally found no major differences between bEVT and dEVT. Observational studies demonstrated different but possibly confounded evidence. Thus, high-quality evidence based on RCTs such as an individual patient data meta-analysis including all published trials is needed. Moreover, measures should be taken to avoid meta-analysis overlap and duplication.
