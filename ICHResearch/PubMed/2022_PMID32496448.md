# Early Goal-directed Therapy During Endovascular Coiling Procedures Following Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: A Pilot Prospective Randomized Controlled Study  

Jason Chui, MBChB, MSc, FANZCA, FHKCA, FHKAM,\* Rosemary Craen, MBBS, FANZCA, FRCPC,\* Christine Dy-Valdez, MD,\* Rizq Alamri, MD,\* Mel Boulton, MD, PhD, FRCSC,† Sachin Pandey, MD, FRCPC,‡ and Ian Herrick, MD, FRCPC\*  

Background: Maintenance of euvolemia and cerebral perfusion are recommended for the prevention of cerebral vasospasm after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). We conducted a pilot randomized controlled study to assess the feasibility and efficacy of goal-directed therapy (GDT) to correct fluid and hemodynamic derangements during endovascular coiling in patients with aSAH.  

Methods: This study was conducted between November 2015 and February 2019 at a single tertiary center in Canada. Adult patients with aSAH within 5 days of aneurysm rupture were randomly assigned to receive either GDT or standard therapy during endovascular coiling. The incidence of dehydration at presentation and the efficacy of GDT were evaluated.  

Results: Forty patients were allocated to receive GDT ${\mathrm{(n}}{\mathrm{=}}21{\mathrm{)}}$ ) or standard therapy $(\mathrm{n}=19)$ ). Sixty percent of all patients were found to have dehydration before the coiling procedure commenced. Compared with standard therapy, GDT reduced the duration of intraoperative hypovolemia (mean difference 37.6 $[95\%$ confidence interval, $6.2{-}37.4]\mathrm{min}$ , $P{=}0.006)$ and low cardiac index (mean difference $30.7[95\%$ confidence interval, 9.5-56.9] min, $P{=}0.035$ ). There were no differences between the 2 treatment groups with respect to the incidence of vasospasm, stroke, death, and other complications up to postoperative day 90.  

Conclusions: A high proportion of aSAH patients presented at the coiling procedure with dehydration and a low cardiac output state; these derangements were more likely to be corrected if the GDT algorithm was used. Compared with standard therapy, use of the GDT algorithm resulted in earlier recognition and more consistent treatment of dehydration and hemodynamic derangement during endovascular coiling.  

Key Words: cerebral vasospasm, delayed cerebral ischemia, cerebral aneurysm, subarachnoid hemorrhage, endovascular coiling  

(J Neurosurg Anesthesiol 2022;34:35–43)  

Approximately one quarter of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) patients suffer from either severe disabilities or death as a result of cerebral vasospasm or delayed cerebral ischemia despite timely intervention to secure the aneurysm.1 Maintenance of euvolemia is reported to be important to prevent such complications, and is recommended in current guidelines for the management of aSAH patients.2,3 The current practice of fluid and hemodynamic assessment is largely based on clinical judgment, which has been shown to be insufficient to guide hemodynamic management after aSAH.4,5 As such, it is important to explore alternative strategies, such as using more objective measurements or intervening at different time-points in the care pathway, that might be more consistently effective at correcting factors that contribute to vasospasm.  

Goal-directed therapy (GDT) is defined as the administration of fluids and inotropes/vasopressors using a predefined treatment algorithm to achieve specific therapeutic endpoints as guided by noninvasive cardiac output monitoring (NICOM) devices. GDT provides a means to accurately assess and manage circulating volume and cardiac output. Application of GDT in non-neurosurgical populations has been reported to improve outcomes, including: (1) lower mortality and reduced surgical complication rates in high-risk surgical patients7; (2) reduced length of hospital stay8 and incidence of pneumonia, urinary tract infections and surgical site infections9,10 in colorectal surgical patients; and (3) lower rates of postoperative complications and reduced length of hospital stay in cardiac surgery patients.11 These promising results in GDT trials have led to radical changes in fluid and hemodynamic practices in colorectal surgery, and also to the routine intraoperative use of GDT worldwide. While colorectal and cardiac surgery differ substantially from aneurysm coiling procedures, the context of the successful application of GDT in these different procedures bears some similarities. These include: (1) a requirement for physiological intervention at a critical, early point in the care pathway (ie, during the surgical procedure when physiological derangement occurs); (2) procedures occurring in an environment where a designated anesthesiologist can promptly apply physiological and pharmacologic interventions; and (3) the use of NICOM to provide a more objectively based algorithm for intervention than traditional clinical measures such as fluid balance and urine output.  

On the basis of the positive experiences using GDT in surgical patients, and the traditional belief that vasospasm that may complicate aSAH is fluid and perfusion pressure sensitive, we hypothesized that the use of GDT to optimize fluid and hemodynamic status early in the patient care pathway (ie, during endovascular coiling) may confer greater benefit on the subsequent development of cerebral vasospasm and related complications. However, we appreciate that application of GDT early in the care pathway for cerebral aneurysm coiling following aSAH is not without challenges: (1) procedures are often performed on an urgent or semiurgent basis at any time of the day or night; (2) notification is often limited and temporally short relative to the start of the procedure; (3) factors such as concomitant cognitive impairment may hamper the consent process; and (4) oversight of compliance is more rigorous as it requires an understanding of clinical behavior and must be done contemporaneously. As there are no prior clinical trials, we took a stepwise approach to investigate the effect of early GDT during endovascular therapy in patients following recent aSAH. This pilot clinical trial was undertaken to evaluate: (1) whether there is an opportunity for earlier intervention using a GDT algorithm (ie, to determine whether some patients arriving at the interventional radiology suite are not in an optimal hemodynamic state); and (2) whether hemodynamic derangements in aSAH patients presenting for coiling can be identified and treated using a GDT algorithm. We also collected outcome data following the coiling procedure to inform the design of a future study, despite the recognition that the sample size of this pilot study was likely inadequate to address this question directly.  



# METHODS  



# Trial Design  

We conducted a pilot, blinded, parallel design, superiority design, randomized controlled study to assess the feasibility of using GDT to optimize hemodynamic management of patients undergoing endovascular aneurysm coiling procedures to determine whether this might promote a reduction in factors that contribute to the risk of postoperative cerebral vasospasm following aSAH. We randomly allocated the study participants into either the GDT or control group (1:1 allocation ratio). The study was conducted at University Hospital, London Health Science Centre, London, Ontario from November 2015 to February 2019 (this includes the postoperative 90-d follow-up period). The study was interrupted for 1 year due to a leave-ofabsence of the principal investigator; the actual study period was, therefore, 27 months. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board at Western University, and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02507999).  



# Participants  

We recruited adult patients (above 18 y of age) who presented with World Federation of Neurologic Surgeons (WFNS) grade I to IV aSAH and who were scheduled for an endovascular aneurysm coiling procedure within 5 days of aneurysm rupture. Participants were excluded if they had: (1) an unruptured aneurysm(s) or did not have aSAH; (2) a previous clipping or coiling procedure for the same aneurysm; (3) undergone a concomitant extracranial-intracranial bypass procedure or other non-neurosurgical procedure, or; (4) a giant aneurysm (size $>25\mathrm{mm}$ ). In addition, pediatric patients (above $18\mathrm{y}$ of age), pregnant women, or those unable to provide informed consent directly or through a substitute decision maker were also excluded.  

The on-call neuroradiologist identified potential study participants when they received a consultation for the endovascular coiling procedure. A research assistant subsequently approached the potential participants to confirm eligibility and obtain written consent either from the participant or substitute decision maker.  



# Interventions  

The study intervention was intraoperative hemodynamic optimization using a NICOM device (FloTrac Edwards EV1000, Edwards Lifesciences, Canada) connected via an arterial catheter. The baseline values of cardiac output, cardiac index, stroke volume and stroke volume variation were obtained after induction of anesthesia and endotracheal intubation.  



# GDT Group  

For patients in the GDT group, the attending anesthesiologist used the information obtained from the NICOM to manage fluid and hemodynamics during the coiling procedure according to a predefined treatment algorithm (Fig. 1). This GDT algorithm was created by the principal author and critically reviewed locally by the neuroanesthesia group at Western University with respect to the appropriateness of treatment goals and to promote compliance with its use before conducting the study. The reason for developing a new GDT algorithm was because the vast majority of those previously published are devised primarily for use in colorectal surgery, septic patients or postcardiac surgery patients. None of these algorithms addresses the considerations of intraoperative aneurysm rupture (ie, step I in the algorithm) or cerebral ischemia due to hypotension (step IV in the algorithm). The new GDT algorithm involves four treatment steps: antihypertensive (step I), fluid (step II), inotropic (step III), and vasopressor (step IV) interventions. The treatment interventions were started after induction of anesthesia and continued until the patient was extubated or transferred to the intensive care unit for postprocedure mechanical ventilation.

# Control Group  

The NICOM device was connected to patients in the control group, but the anesthesiologist and interventionist were blinded to its output. The screen was covered by an opaque plastic bag and the alarms were disabled. The attending anesthesiologist made his/her own clinical decisions as to the management of fluids and hemodynamics that were consistent with routine practice.  

Apart from the aforementioned intervention, anesthesia was conducted in a standard manner in both groups. After transferring the patient to the angiography suite, routine monitoring consisting of pulse oximetry, electrocardiography, noninvasive blood pressure, invasive blood pressure (via the arterial line noted previously), end-tidal $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ and temperature was implemented. General anesthesia was maintained with either sevoflurane or desflurane at around 1 minimum alveolar concentration, and an opioid (fentanyl or remifentanil) was titrated according to surgical stimulation to ensure adequate depth of anesthesia. Volume-controlled ventilation (tidal volume $8\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{kg})$ was used, and respiratory rate adjusted to achieve a $\mathrm{PaCO}_{2}$ of $35\mathrm{mmHg}$ confirmed by arterial blood gas analysis. A forced-air warming blanket and warmed intravenous fluids were used to maintain normothermia. Upon completion of the procedure, the patients were either extubated in the neuroradiology suite and transferred to a neurosurgical observation unit, or remained intubated and ventilated and transferred to the intensive care unit. The study ended when the patient left the neuroangiography suite. Postoperative fluid and hemodynamic management were at the discretion of the attending intensivist and/or neurosurgeon.  



# Outcomes  

Dehydration at baseline was defined as stroke volume variation $>10\%$ on the NICOM device,8 and low baseline cardiac index as ${<}2\mathrm{L}/\mathrm{min}/\mathrm{m}^{211}$ ; both were measured after induction of anesthesia and intubation.11

The duration of hypovolemia was defined as the total duration of stroke volume variation $>10\%$ from anesthesia induction/intubation to end of the coiling procedure. The area-under-threshold (AUT) was calculated by the product of magnitude below threshold and time (ie, the severity-duration product).  

The following outcomes data were collected to inform the larger trial that will be conducted in the future. The primary endpoint for a future study would be the composite outcome of sonographic, radiologic or clinical vasospasm, stroke, and death at 30 days. Sonographic or angiographic cerebral vasospasm was defined as any mean flow velocity $>120\mathrm{cm/s}$ on transcranial Doppler ultrasonography, or transient arterial narrowing on magnetic resonance angiography and/or digital subtraction angiography. Symptomatic vasospasm was defined as clinical deterioration deemed secondary to vasospasm after other causes were eliminated. This composite outcome was used: (1) to improve study efficiency (or power) for detecting potential treatment effects, and (2) because vasospasm, stroke, and death often present as a continuum of disease progression. Ischemic stroke was defined as acute focal neurological dysfunction caused by focal infarction at single or multiple sites of the brain or retina. All study participants were followed up to 90 days after the procedure to obtain time-to-composite outcome data.  

The secondary endpoints for a future study included total hospital length of stay and modified Rankin score (mRS) at 7, 30, and 90-day postprocedure. The time of hospital discharge was defined as the time study participants were discharged either to home or a rehabilitation facility. The mRs is a 6-point scoring system that is commonly used to assess global disability after stroke (0: no symptoms, 5: severe disability, 6: death). Other secondary endpoints included complications that were potentially affected by intraoperative GDT such as acute myocardial infarction, pulmonary edema, chest infection, acute kidney injury, and infectious complications. All outcomes data were obtained by a research assistant blinded to the intervention allocation.  



# Sample Size  

Since this is a pilot study developed in the absence of prior experience using GDT in this patient population or the potential treatment effect of GDT, we used a convenience sample size of 40 patients (20 patients in each study arm). However, this sample size should be sufficient to inform the incidence of fluid and hemodynamic derangements at presentation, as well as to provide sufficient preliminary data to inform the design of a future large-scale study. We initially increased the sample size to 50 patients to compensate for dropout and noncompliance. However, we stopped the study after 40 patients were successfully recruited because there were no dropouts and no loss to follow-up, and also because of a slow accrual rate during the study period.  



# Randomization, Allocation Concealment, Implementation, and Blinding  

The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician using block randomization with a variable block size of 4 to 6. The allocation was placed in sealed opaque envelopes and stored in a locker. The allocation was only disclosed by the research assistant after the participant was transferred into the neuroangiography suite. The attending anesthesiologists were not be blinded to the intervention group because availability, or lack of availability, of NICOM data was obvious. However, the anesthesiologists for patients randomized to the nonintervention group were blinded to the output data from the NICOM as outlined earlier, and interventionists, patients and outcome assessors were blinded as to the intervention group.  



# Analytical Methods  

Patient demographics and surgical and anesthetic characteristics in the 2 intervention groups were summarized. The adequacy of randomization was assessed by inspection for any obvious differences between the 2 groups; no statistical test was performed to assess any baseline imbalance between the 2 groups. The incidence of dehydration and low cardiac index at baseline was determined, as was the number of GDT interventions applied in the GDT group and the requirement for fluid and pressor/anti-hypertensive agents in both groups.  

The hypothesis that GDT significantly reduces the duration of hypovolaemia and low cardiac index was tested using the Mann-Whitney test, and the results reported using both intention-to-treat and as per protocol approaches. The AUTs of hypotension, hypertension, hypovolaemia, and low cardiac index were determined.  

We tested our hypothesis that GDT reduces the composite outcome at day-30 compared with the control group using the Mann-Whitney test on an intention-to-treat approach. The components of the composite outcome (sonographic/radiological/symptomatic cerebral vasospasm, stroke, and death) were also tested individually. We further constructed a Kaplan-Meier curve to display the timeto-composite outcome and assessed the difference using a log rank test. mRS was dichotomized into high (mRs: 4 to 6) and low functional disability (mRs: 0 to 3) for analysis. The time to hospital discharge was presented in a Kaplan-Meier curve and tested for the difference between the 2 groups using a log rank test. For each of the other secondary outcomes, parametric, and nonparametric outcomes were tested with the Student $t$ test and Mann-Whitney test, respectively, and categorical outcomes were tested using the $\chi^{2}$ test.  

A $P$ -value $<0.05$ was considered significant. All analyses were performed using STATA (version 14.0, StataCorp, Texas).  



# RESULTS  

Eighty-eight patients were screened, 48 were excluded and 40 patients were included in the study (Fig. 2). The major reasons for exclusion were inability to obtain consent, and presentation for intervention $>5$ days after aSAH. Of the 40 included patients, 21 were allocated to the GDT group and 19 to the control group. There was no cross-over of interventions between the groups. Follow-up data were obtained on all participants, so 40 patients are included in the analysis.

Baseline characteristics including age, sex, WFNS score, and Fisher grades of aSAH were similar in the 2 groups (Table 1). The majority of patients were middleaged $\left(\mathrm{mean}\pm\mathrm{SD}\colon58\left(\pm11\right)\mathrm{y}\right)$ , female (31/40; $77.5\%)$ , and with normal body mass index (mean ± SD: $27\pm5.3\mathrm{kg/m}^{2}$ ) and no significant pre-existing cardiopulmonary or neurological diseases. All patients except one underwent the coiling procedure within the first two days after symptom onset. The majority of patients (29/40; $73\%$ ) had a low-grade aSAH (WFNS I-II) and the remained (11/40; $28\%$ ) had a high grade sSAH (WFNS III-IV). Only 6 patients required ventilatory support preoperatively. Thirteen patients had preoperative ischemic ECG changes, but only 2 required inotropic support.  

The majority of patients (32/40; $80\%$ ) were transferred to the neuroradiology suite from either a high-dependency or intensive care unit. No patient was hypertensive (ie, systolic blood pressure $>160\mathrm{mmHg}$ or hypotensive (ie systolic blood pressure ${<}90\mathrm{mmHg}$ ) at presentation to the neuroradiology suite. Three patients had low baseline cardiac index $(<2\mathrm{L}/$ $\mathrm{min}/\mathrm{m}^{2})$ . The majority $(60\%)$ of patients were found to have baseline dehydration based on data from the NICOM device (ie, stroke volume variation $>10\%$ ). The mean $(\pm\mathrm{SD})$ time from admission to coiling was 0.9 $(\pm0.1)$ and 0.7 $(\pm0.2)$ days in the GDT and control groups, respectively (Table 1).  

The GDT algorithm was implemented successfully in 19 of 21 patients in the GDT group; the attending anesthesiologists for 2 patients did not implement the GDT algorithm despite being aware of the randomization result. In those 19 patients who had the GDT algorithm implemented, 4 $(21\%)$ required an anti-hypertensive (step I) intervention, 3 $(16\%)$ a fluid (step II) intervention, 3 $(16\%)$ an inotropic (step III) intervention, and 8 $(42\%)$ a vasopressor (step IV) intervention (Table 2). There were no NICOM device technical problems, such as damped arterial line trace, during the study.  



# Outcomes  

The mean $(\pm\mathrm{SD})$ duration of hypovolemia was 20.1 $(\pm4)$ and 51.7 $(\pm9.8)$ minutes in the GDT and control groups, respectively; mean difference (MD), 37.6 $(95\%$ confidence interval [CI]: $6.2{-}37.40\mathrm{min})$ , $P{=}0.006$ . The mean $(\pm\mathrm{SD})$ duration of low cardiac index were $10(\pm5.9)$ and 47.3 $(\pm15.6)$ minutes in the GDT and control groups, respectively; MD: 30.7 $95\%$ CI: 9.5-56.9) minutes, $P{=}0.035$ . Application of the GDT algorithm also reduced the AUT of hypovolaemia; MD 91.7 $95\%$ CI: 17.0-102.2), $P=~0.0024$ .

There was an obvious (but not statistically significant) trend that GDT also reduced the duration and AUT of hypotension and hypertension, suggesting that implementing a GDT algorithm may promote tighter hemodynamic control despite the availability of nonblinded blood pressure measurements in both groups (Table 2).  

Unsurprisingly, there was no significant difference between the GDT and control groups with respect to the composite outcome (proportion of patients with sonographic/ angiographic/clinical vasospasm, stroke or death at $30\mathrm{d},$ ): $57\%$ (12 participants) and $47\%$ (9 participants), respectively; odds ratio (OR) 1.48 $(95\%$ CI: 0.43-5.16), $P=0.536$ (Table 3). The cumulative incidence of the composite outcome up to 90 days was also not significantly different between the GDT and control groups (log rank test: $\chi^{2}=0.04$ , $P{=}0.845,$ ) (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Supplementary Fig. 1, http://links.lww.com/JNA/A286). There was no significant difference between the GDT and control groups with respect to any of the individual components of the composite primary outcome analyzed as part of this pilot study to explore the feasibility of a future investigation, namely sonographic/angiographic and symptomatic vasospasm, stroke, and/or death at 30 days. There was also no evidence of a significant difference between the 2 groups in any of the individual components of the composite primary outcomes at 7, 30, and 90 days.

# DISCUSSION  

This is the first study that demonstrates the feasibility and efficacy of using GDT during interventional coiling procedures to assess and optimize hemodynamic factors such as dehydration and low cardiac output state that may favorably influence vasospasm-related outcomes following aSAH. Most patients in our study underwent endovascular coiling within 1 day of admission. Despite this, we observed that a high proportion presented at their coiling procedure with dehydration. The causes are unknown but likely multifactorial. The high frequency of hypovolemia is likely the result of inadequate resuscitation in the preoperative period compounded by the use of mannitol and/or loop diuretics in some patients, and underscores the potential advantage of early implementation of an objective and systematic (eg, algorithm-based) approach to the correction of these physiological perturbations.  

For this study we developed a new GDT algorithm specifically for aSAH patients, and demonstrated that it can be successfully implemented in the clinical setting. Importantly, when this GDT algorithm was employed, pre-existing fluid and hemodynamic derangements were more likely to be corrected, resulting in shortening of the duration and AUT of these derangements. Overall, compared with standard therapy, the use of GDT during coiling procedures appears to promote earlier and more timely correction of physiological derangements in patients with aSAH.  

Either fluid overload or depletion could be harmful in aSAH patients. Previous studies have demonstrated that hypovolemia was associated with vasospasm and poor outcome following aSAH.12,13 On the other hand, recent cohort studies found that fluid overload (or a positive fluid balance) was also associated with poor outcomes.14–16 Previous studies have also reported that clinical assessment or fluid balance measurements failed to accurately determine circulating volume status in aSAH patients, and that fluid management based on these traditional clinical guides was unable to maintain euvolemia.4,5  

Early experience with the use of GDT in aSAH patients in other settings has been promising. A recent cohort study of 84 patients found that GDT using a thermodilution technique during the first 14 days after aSAH in the intensive care setting was associated with improved cognitive outcomes.17 Another cohort study of 102 patients found that daily blood volume measurement in the intensive care unit using Pulse Dye Densitometry during the first 10 days after aSAH reduced the incidence of severe hypovolemia.18  

There is only 1 randomized controlled study assessing the outcome benefit of GDT on neurological outcome in aSAH patients.19 In that study, 160 aSAH patients in a neurocritical care unit received either GDT guided by a

Pulse Contour Cardiac Output (PiCCO) monitor or standard therapy on days 1 to 4 following a coiling procedure. GDT did not result in any differences in hemodynamic parameters between the 2 groups, and the use of GDT did not reduce vasospasm (up to $21\textup{d}$ after aSAH) nor improve 3-month functional outcomes compared with standard therapy. However, in a subgroup analysis of patients with poor-grade aSAH, GDT was reported to be associated with less vasospasm, better 3-month functional outcomes, and shorter intensive care unit length of stay. On the basis of these results, the authors concluded that short-term fluid and hemodynamic optimization using a NICOM device can reduce vasospasm and improve functional outcomes in patients with poor-grade aSAH.  

Our study differs from this previous study19 in several respects. First, we applied the GDT protocol at an early time-point in the care pathway (during the endovascular coiling procedure rather than 1 day after the procedure), on the premise that early correction of dehydration and cardiac dysfunction may have a greater impact (or pre-emptive effect) on the subsequent development of cerebral vasospasm. Second, the presence of a designated anesthesia provider during coiling procedures may promote the timely implementation of fluid and pharmacological interventions, and compliance with the protocol, once a derangement is identified. This may explain why GDT did not result in any differences in physiological parameters in the earlier study.19 Third, use of the NICOM guided GDT protocol in the postoperative period was not feasible in our study; most of the patients were extubated at the end of procedure and the NICOM device used in this study (FloTrac Edwards EV1000) is only validated in patients during mechanical ventilation at tidal volumes $>8\mathrm{mL/kg}$ .  

Many logistical challenges are associated with performing and confirming compliance with the study protocol during semiurgent procedures, which are often performed at relatively short notice during the day or night. Despite a designated anesthesiologist being present in all cases in this study, 2 providers did not implement any intervention when marked physiological derangements were identified by the NICOM device. This issue would need to be addressed prior to undertaking a large trial, and commitment to the study protocol would need to be secured from all providers.  



# Limitations  

The major limitation of this study was that it was a single center clinical trial. The nature of a single center trial allows for the possibility of inherent practice patterns which may be unique to the institution. Thus, it is unknown whether similar preprocedural physiological derangements are common among aSAH patients who present to coiling procedures in other centers. Since this pilot study was designed to investigate the feasibility of GDT during endovascular treatment in aSAH patients, it was not powered to demonstrate a reduction in vasospasm with GDT. Further studies with adequate power are required to assess the clinical benefit of the GDT. Our findings provide promising evidence that there is an opportunity to intervene earlier to optimize known risk factors that are thought to contribute to cerebral vasospasm. Although the traditional belief that vasospasm following aSAH is fluid and perfusion sensitive, and that volume depletion in these patients is harmful,12,13 the significance of the hypovolemia identified in this study remains uncertain. Finally, this study did not continue the GDT protocol into the postoperative period because of the limitation of the NICOM device. While it seems intuitively sensible to continue the protocol beyond the procedure, this premise has not been a consistent feature of other studies in which application of a GDT protocol only during the intraoperative period has been reported to favorably alter outcomes in colorectal surgery patients.7–10  



# CONCLUSIONS  

Despite its inherent limitations, this pilot study demonstrated the clinical feasibility of using a GDT algorithm to optimize fluid and hemodynamic management during endovascular coiling procedures in patients following recent aSAH. We observed that a large proportion of aSAH patients presented at the coiling procedure with dehydration and a low cardiac output state, and that these derangements were more likely to be corrected if the GDT algorithm was employed. We also confirmed the appropriateness of our study design and analytic methods, and tested the feasibility of confirming compliance with the protocol and of collecting composite outcome and other secondary outcome data as a prelude to a future larger-scale study. The main practical challenge identified in our pilot experience was slow recruitment due to the difficultly of gaining informed consent in neurologically impaired patients. The obvious question that remains is whether intraoperative hemodynamic optimization using GDT can translate into clinical outcome benefits. As dehydration and low cardiac output state are believed to contribute to the development cerebral vasospasm, our results may support the pursuit of a larger-scale study to answer this question.
