# TXA does not affect levels of TBI-related biomarkers in blunt TBI with ICH: A secondary analysis of the Prehospital TXA for TBI Trial  

Lea E Hoefer, MD, University of Chicago Medicine, Department of Surgery  

Andrew J Benjamin, MD, MS, University of Chicago Medicine, Section of Trauma and Acute Care Surgery  

Ann M Polcari, MD, MPH, University of Chicago Medicine, Department of Surgery  

Martin A Schreiber, MD, Oregon Health and Sciences University, Department of Surgery  

Tanya L Zakrison, MD, MPH, University of Chicago Medicine, Section of Trauma and Acute Care Surgery  

Susan E Rowell, MD University of Chicago Medicine, Section of Trauma and Acute Care Surgery  

# Abstract  

Background: Brain specific biomarkers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1), and microtubule-associated protein-2 (MAP-2) have been identified as tools for diagnosis in traumatic brain injury (TBI). Tranexamic acid (TXA) has been shown to decrease mortality in patients with intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). The effect of TXA on these biomarkers is unknown. We investigated whether TXA affects levels of GFAP, UCH-L1, and MAP-2, and whether biomarker levels are associated with mortality in patients receiving TXA.  

Methods: Patients enrolled in the prehospital TXA for TBI trial had GFAP, UCHL-1 and MAP-2 levels drawn at 0 and 24 hours post injury $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n=4}22.$ ). Patients with ICH from blunt trauma with a $\mathrm{GCS}<13$ and $\mathrm{SBP}>90$ were randomized to placebo, $2\mathrm{g}$ TXA bolus, or 1g bolus + 1g/8hrs TXA infusion. Associations of TXA and 24-hour biomarker change were assessed with multivariate  

linear regression. Association of biomarkers with 28-day mortality was assessed with multivariate logistic regression. All models were controlled for age, GCS, ISS, and AIS head.  

Results: Administration of TXA was not associated with a change in biomarkers over 24 hours post-injury. Changes in biomarker levels were associated with AIS head and age. On admission, higher GFAP (OR 1.75, CI 1.31–2.38, $\mathrm{p}{<}0.001$ ) was associated with increased 28-day mortality. At 24 hours post injury, higher levels of GFAP (OR 2.09, CI 1.37–3.30, $\mathrm{p}{<}0.001$ and UCHL-1(OR 2.98, CI 1.77–5.25, $\mathtt{p}{<}0.001$ ) were associated with mortality. A change in UCH levels from 0 to 24 hours post-injury was also associated with increased mortality (OR 1.68, CI 1.15–2.49, $\mathrm{p}{<}0.01\$ ).  

Conclusion: Administration of TXA does not impact change in GFAP, UCHL-1, or MAP-2 during the first 24 hours after blunt TBI with ICH. Higher levels of GFAP and UCH early after injury may help identify patients at high risk for 28-day mortality.  

Level of Evidence: II, therapeutic/care management  

# Keywords  

tranexamic acid; traumatic brain injury; glial fibrillary acidic protein; ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1; microtubule-associated protein-2  

# Background  

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality globally with an estimated 69 million individuals suffering from head injury yearly.(1) In the US alone, the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that TBI led to over 64,000 deaths in 2020 and over 220,000 hospitalizations in 2019.(2) Treatment of TBI has long remained a challenge, with few effective interventions available to mitigate mortality and long-term morbidity for these injuries.  

The potential use of tranexamic acid (TXA) for the treatment of TBI has been under investigation in recent years. TXA inhibits fibrinolysis by blocking binding sites on plasminogen and was shown to reduce mortality when administered early in patients at risk for traumatic hemorrhage. (3) A nested trial of patients with TBI within the CRASH-2 cohort did not reach statistical significance but suggested that further study of the use of TXA in TBI was warranted. Subsequently, the CRASH-3 trial demonstrated reduced mortality in patients with mild to moderate TBI with a 1g TXA bolus $+~1\mathrm{g}$ infusion, and the Prehospital TXA for TBI trial showed reduced mortality in patients with moderate to severe TBI and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) with a prehospital $2\mathrm{g}$ bolus of TXA compared to both the 1g bolus $+1\mathrm{g}$ infusion and placebo groups.(4, 5)  

Prognostication is another major challenge in patients with TBI and remains largely reliant on clinical scales, the most common of which are Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and the Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOS-E).(6),9 While the GCS has helped standardize assessment of patients with acute TBI in both the research and clinical setting, it is subject to inter-rater variability and may not correlate well with long term outcomes.(7, 8) The GOS-E was specifically developed to standardize assessment of long-term disability and injury in patients with TBI, but does not provide guidance in the acute setting.(9)  

Increasing attention has been paid to the potential role of blood-based biomarkers in evaluating acute TBI and their ability to determine need for imaging, likelihood of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), need for neurosurgical intervention and risk for mortality. (10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17) Of the potential biomarker candidates, Glial-fibrillary acid protein (GFAP), a product of astrocyte breakdown, has been one of the most frequently studied. GFAP levels peak at around 20 hours post injury and decrease over 72 hours, with levels typically still detectable at 7 days.(18) GFAP is the biomarker most strongly associated with acute traumatic lesions.(19) Ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1) is a protein found in neurons and has been associated with neuronal injury and disruption of the blood-brain barrier.(20) UCH-L1 has been shown to have a much shorter window of detectability than GFAP, with levels peaking around 6–8 hours and decreasing over 48 hours.(13, 18, 21) Finally, microtubule-associated protein-2 (MAP-2) is a component of the neuronal microtubule system and is associated with ischemic neurologic damage in TBI.(22) Its potential use in TBI has been less extensively studied, with little data available on blood-based kinetics.(23) Studies on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of MAP-2 have demonstrated a CSF peak of 72 hours.(22)  

Serum-based biomarkers of TBI are relatively new to clinical practice and have only been validated for use in clinical decision making for CT scanning. The ALERT-TBI study validated the use of GFAP and UCH-L1 in predicting need for CT scan in patients with mild TBI and led to the first FDA approval of a serum-based TBI biomarker panel. Levels of GFAP less than $22\mathrm{pg/ml}$ and levels of UCH-L1 less than $327\mathrm{pg/ml}$ had a negative predictive value of 0.997 for intracranial injury on head CT.(24) Aside from this, validated cutoff levels or ranges for additional use of these biomarkers in the clinical setting have not been established. A prior secondary analysis of the placebo arm of the Prehospital TXA for TBI trial demonstrated that these three biomarkers significantly improved prediction of ICH and outcomes compared to pre-hospital variables alone, but they have not yet been examined in a group of patients which includes those who received treatment with TXA(10).  

Biomarkers such as GFAP, UCH-L1, and MAP-2 may not only provide a promising method for assessing severity of TBI, but potentially offer a method by which to monitor the efficacy of TBI treatments. In this study, we aim to investigate whether GFAP, UCH-L1 and MAP-2 can be used to measure treatment effect of TXA seen in the $2\mathrm{g}$ bolus arm of the primary trial. We also hypothesize that levels of these biomarkers are associated with outcomes including mortality in a group of patients treated with two different doses of TXA or placebo.  

# Methods  

This is a secondary analysis of data collected during the Prehospital TXA for TBI trial which was a multi-institutional, double-blinded, randomized phase II clinical trial performed across 20 centers and 39 emergency medical systems in the United States and Canada. Patients with suspected TBI, a GCS ${<}13$ prior to administration of any sedatives and at least one reactive pupil, age ${>}15$ years, estimated time from injury to hospital arrival of $\angle2$ hours, and $\mathrm{SBP}{>}90$ were randomized to placebo, treatment with a $2\mathrm{g}$ TXA bolus, or treatment with ${1}\mathrm{g}$ TXA bolus plus $_{1\mathrm{g}}$ TXA infusion over 8 hours. TXA is not currently FDA approved for treatment of traumatic brain injury, and its use in this study is investigational. All treatments were initiated within two hours of injury [ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01990768)]. IRB approval for the parent trial was obtained by the primary study site, and subsequent contingent approval obtained by individual participating sites.  

For this secondary analysis, patients who had ICH due to a blunt mechanism and had biomarker levels drawn were included in the analysis $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n=4}22$ , Figure 1). A power analysis was not conducted for this study as this was a secondary analysis of a previously completed trial and all data was already collected. Primary outcome was mortality at 28 days, and secondary outcomes were levels of GFAP, UCH-L1, and MAP-2 at admission, 24 hours, and the degree of change over 24 hours. GFAP, UCH-L1, and MAP-2 levels were drawn upon arrival to the ED and at 24 hours post injury. 58 patients did not have 24 hour biomarker levels available. Distribution of demographic variables and biomarker levels was assessed using density plots and histograms. Differences in demographic factors between treatment groups were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test for ordinal variables, t-test for continuous variables, and a chi-squared test for categorical variables. Biomarker levels were log-transformed to adjust for a significant right-ward skew. Associations between TXA and biomarker levels at admission, at 24 hours post injury, and in the degree of change over 24 hours were assessed with multivariate linear regression. Patients in the two TXA treatment arms were not combined as the mortality benefit in the $2\mathrm{g}$ bolus group seen in the primary trial was present in comparison both to the placebo and to the $_{1\mathrm{g}}$ bolus $+1\mathrm{g}$ infusion treatment arm. Multivariate logistic regression was used to determine the association between biomarker levels and 28-day mortality at admission, at 24 hours post injury, and based on the degree of change over 24 hours. Biomarker levels were not dichotomized with cutoffs, but rather evaluated as continuous variables. Patients in all three treatment arms were included in all mortality analyses. Patients without 24 hour biomarker levels were only included in admission models. All models were controlled for age, GCS, ISS, and AIS head. All data analysis was done in $\mathrm{R}(2022.02.3\substack{+492}$ “Prairie Trillium” Release).(25) Please see the STROBE checklist attached in the supplemental digital content.  

# Results  

A total of 422 patients with blunt TBI, ICH, and available blood-based biomarkers were identified $\mathrm{\check{n}}\mathrm{=}130$ , placebo; $\scriptstyle\mathtt{n=}130$ , $_{1\mathrm{g}}$ TXA bolus $+1\mathrm{g}$ infusion; and $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{n}}=162$ , $2\mathrm{g}$ TXA bolus). All patients had biomarker levels drawn upon arrival to the Emergency Department (0 hours). There was a minority of patients in each treatment group who did not have 24-hour biomarker levels drawn $\mathrm{\tilde{n}}{=}16$ , $2\mathrm{g}$ bolus; $\mathrm{n}{=}17$ , $1\mathrm{g}+1\mathrm{g}$ infusion; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{n}}=25$ , placebo) and these patients were only included in admission models. The majority of patients were male $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n}=318$ , $75\%$ ) and the median age was 42 (IQR 27–57). Motor vehicle collision was the most common cause of injury $\mathrm{\tilde{n}}{=}148$ , $35\%$ ). Median GCS at presentation was 7 (IQR 4 – 10) with a median AIS head of 4 (IQR 3–5). Median ISS across all treatment groups was 22 (IQR 17–33) (Table 1). ISS, AIS head, and GCS were not statistically different between groups, though there was a significant difference seen in gender distribution between treatment groups (Table 1). There was no significant difference between men in women in average baseline level of GFAP $(\mathtt{p}{=}0.1)$ , UCH-L1 $(\mathrm{p}{=}0.3)$ , or MAP-2 $(\mathrm{p}{=}0.96)$ .  

# TBI biomarker levels over the first 24 hours post-injury  

Unadjusted median MAP, GFAP, and UCH-L1 levels at 0 hours and 24 hours, and the change between 0 and 24 hours are shown in Table 2. All three treatment groups saw a rise in GFAP and MAP-2 levels over the first 24 hours. The majority of patients in all three groups experienced a decline in UCH-L1 levels by 24 hours post-injury consistent with an expected peak of 6–8 hours, though 7 total patients had an increase in their UCH-L1 level from time 0 to 24. Looking at factors associated with a change in biomarker levels over the first 24 hours post-injury, neither the $2\mathrm{g}$ bolus dose nor the $\mathrm{1g~bolus+1g}$ infusion dosing of TXA resulted in a significant difference in multivariate logistic regression (Table 3). The only factors found to have a significant association with a change in all three biomarkers between hours 0 and 24 were AIS head and age. ISS was associated with the change in MAP and UCH-L1 levels during the first 24 hours after injury, (Table 3), but this association was not seen with GFAP levels.  

# Mortality  

Overall 28-day mortality in the study population was $17.0\%$ $\mathrm{\tilde{n}}\mathrm{=}72$ , Table 1). This included 25 patients in the placebo group $(19.2\%)$ , 23 patients in the $\mathrm{1g~bolus+1g}$ infusion group $(17.7\%)$ , and 24 patients in the $2\mathrm{g}$ bolus treatment group $(14.8\%)$ . There was no significant difference in mortality across all three groups (Table 1). As GFAP levels at admission increased (OR 1.75, CI 1.31–2.38, $\mathtt{p}{<}0.001$ ) there was an associated increased risk of 28-day mortality. At 24 hours post injury, increasing levels of GFAP (OR 2.09, CI 1.37–3.30, $\mathrm{p}{<}0.001$ and UCH-L1(OR 2.98, CI 1.77–5.25, $\mathtt{p}{<}0.001$ ) were both associated with mortality. A greater increase in UCH-L1 levels from 0 to 24 hours post-injury was also associated with increased mortality (OR 1.68, CI 1.15–2.49, $\mathsf{p}{<}0.01$ ). No significant association with mortality was seen with the degree of change in MAP-2 or GFAP levels over the first 24 hours. Age and qualifying GCS score were both associated with 28-day mortality in models based on biomarker levels at admission, whereas AIS head only became significant in mortality models incorporating biomarker levels at 24 hours post-injury. (Table 4).  

# Discussion  

In this study, we investigated whether circulating TBI-related biomarkers from patients enrolled in the Prehospital TXA for TBI trial could be used to measure treatment effect and predict outcomes. While we found no difference in levels of three TBI-related biomarkers between TXA treatment groups during the first 24 hours after injury, higher levels of glial-fibrillary acid protein at admission and 24 hours, higher levels of ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase at 24 hours, and increasing UCH-L1 levels during the first twenty-four hours after injury were associated with higher 28-day mortality.  

Although patients with moderate-to-severe TBI and ICH who received a $2\mathrm{g}$ TXA bolus in the Prehospital TXA for TBI trial were shown to have lower mortality compared to both the placebo and $\mathrm{1g~bolus+1g}$ infusion dosing, (5) Neither the $2\mathrm{g}$ TXA bolus dose nor the 1g TXA bolus $+1\mathrm{g}$ infusion were associated with a change in biomarker levels over the first 24 hours. Based on these results, at this time we cannot recommend using GFAP, MAP-2, or  

UCH-L1 as a measure of treatment effect of TXA in patients with TBI and ICH during the first 24 hours after injury.  

There are several reasons why we are not able to identify an association between these three biomarkers and the treatment effect seen with a $2\mathrm{g}$ dose of TXA in the primary trial. First, as mentioned previously, these biomarkers have only been standardized for use in making decisions regarding the need for head CT in patients with mild TBI. The cutoff levels standardized in the ALERT-TBI trial – $22\mathrm{pg/ml}$ for GFAP and $327\mathrm{pg/ml}$ for UCH-L1 – are 10 to 100 times less than the typical GFAP and UCH-L1 seen in our population with ICH. (24) Given that clinical benchmarks have not yet been clearly defined for this range, it may be that these biomarkers are not sensitive at this level to detect the change in mortality seen in the primary trial.  

Secondly, the exact mechanism of TXA in decreasing mortality in TBI is not clearly understood. While TXA has classically thought to decrease mortality through inhibiting fibrinolysis and reducing hemorrhage, laboratory data regarding the effects of TXA on fibrinolysis in TBI patients has been mixed(26). Alternatively, plasmin and plasminogen are known to play a role in inflammation and can act as neurotoxins(27, 28) and it is possible that TXA reduces mortality through these pathways. It may be that GFAP, UCH-L1, and MAP-2 do not specifically reflect the underlying pathway through which TXA works and further study is needed to clarify it’s exact mechanism and identify relevant biomarkers.  

Although GFAP, UCH-L1, and MAP-2 did not demonstrate TXA treatment effect in our study, our results do suggest that circulating brain specific biomarkers can potentially be used to identify risk for outcomes including 28-day mortality in patients with TBI including those who have received TXA. Previous work by our group demonstrated that these three biomarkers improved prediction of ICH and outcomes after TBI compared to pre-hospital variables alone in the placebo group(10), and our study provides evidence that these three biomarkers may be used in prognostication even in patients who have received TXA. Similar to previous studies in which GFAP has been shown to be an excellent predictor of the presence of intracranial lesions specific to the nervous system, in our study GFAP was the only marker to demonstrate a significant association with 28-day mortality in models incorporating admission biomarker levels as well as those with biomarker levels drawn at 24 hours post-injury.(11, 15, 29, 30) This is important as it makes GFAP a potentially useful candidate biomarker in the trauma setting where patients often present with multi-system injuries.  

While no association was found between admission levels of UCH-L1 and mortality, 24- hour UCH-L1 levels as well as change in UCH-L1 over the first 24 hours were both found to be associated with mortality. This makes sense as UCH-L1 has the shortest peak time of all three biomarkers included in this study (6–8 hours post injury). In a patient whose injury does not progress, UCH-L1 levels would be expected to decline by 24 hours post-injury. Higher levels of UCH-L1 at 24 hours after injury, or an increase in the UCH-L1 levels, may be related to active injury progression or ongoing injury, potentially reflected as an increase in mortality as was found in this study. With further study and prospective validation of clinically relevant cutoffs, serial UCH-L1 measurements may be able to be used in real time to detect TBI progression in patients whose physical exam is unreliable or who are too unstable to be taken to the CT scanner.  

The results regarding MAP-2 levels are somewhat difficult to interpret in that the blood base kinetics of its use in TBI have not been well delineated. A prior study on CSF levels in TBI patients found MAP-2 to be an effective marker of injury severity and mortality.(22) While MAP-2 was detected within 6 hours of injury in CSF, it did not reach peak levels until 72 hours post injury. Other studies have found that MAP-2 may be useful in even longer-term prognostication, finding associations between serum levels and functional outcomes at 6 months post injury.(31) Given that our study focused on the first 24 hours post injury, our data may not include the window of time during which MAP-2 is most useful and more long-term studies, especially those clarifying the serum biokinetics of the biomarker, are needed.  

There are several limitations to our study. In general, while serum testing for TBI-related biomarkers provides an advantage over CSF testing in that it is less invasive and more amenable to serial measurements, the use of serum based TBI-related biomarkers is relatively recent and there is extensive work yet to be done on defining serum kinetics, clinical cutoffs, and optimal time sampling and beyond their use in determining the need for CT scan, and especially in patients with moderate or severe TBI (24, 32). Given that not all patients in the original trial had biomarker levels drawn or available at all time points, it may be that our sample size and sample time window limited our ability to identify a relationship between TXA treatment and TBI-related biomarker levels. Future studies should continue to attempt to obtain robust TBI biomarker data over longer time periods to help clarify whether a treatment effect can be measured. Second, inclusion in this trial was not restricted to patients with isolated TBI. While the biomarkers included are thought to be specific to the central nervous system, outcomes may potentially have been impacted by extracranial injuries.  

We specifically excluded patients with penetrating trauma from our analysis which limits the generalizability of our results to blunt injury. From the parent trial, only 19 patients with ICH and available biomarkers sustained penetrating injury which represents a distinct subset of TBI with different outcomes. Similar to previous studies that have generally not included patients with penetrating injury, we feel our dataset was likely underpowered to examine this patient population.(11, 18, 24) Studies with larger cohorts of patients with penetrating TBI are needed to better understand whether TBI-related biomarker kinetics are similar to the blunt TBI population, and to understand their interaction with TXA treatment in this group of patients. Lastly, there is likely some degree of survival bias in our study. In the parent trial, the majority of mortality was observed by 24 hours after injury(5). Given that mortality was higher in the placebo and 1g infusion $+1\mathrm{g}$ bolus treatment arms in patients with ICH, it may be that we are unable to detect a difference in biomarker levels due to the earlier loss of patients in those two arms.  

In conclusion, we found that while treatment with TXA was not found to be significantly associated with changes in TBI-related biomarker levels during the first 24 hours after blunt TBI with ICH, GFAP and UCH- L1 were associated with 28-day mortality within the first 24 hours after injury. Blood-based biomarkers including GFAP and UCH-L1 may be valuable for prognostication and management of patients with blunt TBI and ICH, and further studies are needed to define clinical cutoffs and prospectively validate them for widespread use.
