# Efficacy and Safety of Anticoagulation, Catheter-Directed Thrombolysis, or Systemic ThrombolysisinAcutePulmonaryEmbolis  

Robert S. Zhang, MD,a,\* Muhammad H. Maqsood, MD,b,\* Andrew S.P. Sharp, MD,c Radu Postelnicu, MD,d Sanjum S. Sethi, MD, MPH,e Allison Greco, MD,d Carlos Alviar, MD,a Sripal Bangalore, MD, MHAa  



# ABSTRACT  

BACKGROUND The optimal treatment strategy of patients with pulmonary embolism (PE) (especially those with intermediate risk) continues to evolve and remains controversial.  

OBJECTIVES This study sought to compare the efficacy and safety of anticoagulation (AC) alone, catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT), and systemic thrombolysis (ST) in patients with acute PE.  

METHODS PubMed and EMBASE were searched for randomized controlled trials or observational studies which compared outcomes of AC alone, CDT, and ST in acute PE. Efficacy outcome was all-cause mortality. Safety outcomes were major bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH).  

RESULTS We identified 45 studies (17 randomized controlled trials, 2 prospective nonrandomized trials, and 26 retrospective observational trials), which included 81,705 patients. When compared with AC alone, CDT had lower mortality (OR: 0.55; $95\%$ CI: 0.39-0.80) but higher major bleeding (OR: 1.84; $95\%$ CI: 1.10-3.08) and numerically higher ICH (OR: 1.51; $95\%$ CI: 0.75-3.04). ST was associated with no difference in mortality but higher major bleeding (OR: 2.16; $95\%$ CI: 1.38-3.38) and ICH (OR: 2.26; $95\%$ CI: 1.14-4.48) when compared with AC alone. The risk of mortality (OR: 2.05; $95\%$ CI: 1.46-2.89) and ICH (OR: 1.50; $95\%$ CI: 1.13-1.99) was higher with ST when compared with CDT. Findings were similar when analysis was restricted to trials of intermediate risk PE.  

CONCLUSIONS In patients with acute PE, when compared with AC alone, CDT was associated with a lower mortality but higher risk of bleeding. Moreover, CDT had an enhanced safety profile when compared with ST. (J Am Coll Cardiol Intv 2023;16:2644–2651) $\circleddash$ 2023 by the American College of Cardiology Foundation.  

ulmonary embolism (PE) is the third leading cause of cardiovascular mortality. Early risk stratification is imperative to determine appropriate treatment to reduce morbidity and mortality. Anticoagulation (AC) remains the cornerstone of acute PE management, with rates of mortality in low-risk PE $<1\%$ . However, adverse event rates in patients with intermediate-risk PE with anticoagulation alone has fueled enthusiasm for advanced PE therapies. The benefits of systemic thrombolytics in high-risk patients are well-established but are less clear in the intermediate-risk group. A prior meta-analysis suggested that thrombolytic therapy may reduce mortality in intermediate-risk patients but at the cost of increased major bleeding.1 Because of the limitations of both AC alone and systemic thrombolytics, catheter-based therapies have garnered interest with the hope of providing similar efficacy of thrombus resolution but with an enhanced safety profile. Due to the difficulty of powering randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that evaluate catheter-based therapies for clinically meaningful outcomes, data supporting the use of catheter-based therapies are mostly limited to nonrandomized observational studies, small single-arm prospective studies, and small RCTs that examine surrogate outcomes. Given the paucity of data, we performed a network meta-analysis of both randomized and observational studies to compare the efficacy and safety of AC alone, catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT), and systemic thrombolysis (ST) in patients with acute PE.  



# METHODS  

This network meta-analysis was performed in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines.2 This analysis was not considered for the Institutional Review Board, as no animal or human subject handling was involved. The protocol of this meta-analysis has been registered at PROSPERO (CRD42022341153)  

SEARCH STRATEGY AND INCLUSION CRITERIA. A time limited search until February 15, 2023, was conducted using PubMed and EMBASE databases. The following MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) were used for this search: “anticoagulation,” “CDT,” “systemic thrombolysis,” “pulmonary embolism,” “intermediate-risk pulmonary embolism” and “high-risk pulmonary embolism.” We also reviewed references of the eligible studies to identify further studies. To identify gray literature, ClinicalTrials.gov was searched.  

The inclusion criteria were prospective randomized controlled human trials or observational studies that compared the outcomes of at least 2 of 3 treatment options for PE—AC alone, CDT, and ST. The selected outcomes were mortality, major bleeding risk, and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). Animal-based studies and studies without a comparison arm were not considered. Our exclusion criteria consisted of editorials, single-arm studies, case reports, and review articles. Additionally, we excluded studies that did not compare at least 2 of the 3 treatment interventions, as well as those that did not evaluate outcomes of interest.  

PICO criteria was the following: the population of interest was patients with acute PE; the interventions were CDT and ST; the comparison was AC alone; and outcomes efficacy was mortality and safety was major bleeding and ICH.3  

The studies were screened by 2 independent authors (M.H.M. and R.S.Z.). Nonrelevant studies were first excluded based on title followed by abstract review. Full-text studies were then screened for final selection on basis of previously mentioned, prespecified inclusion criteria.  

DATA EXTRACTION AND QUALITY ASSESSMENT. Two reviewers (M.H.M. and R.S.Z.) independently reviewed the literature and extracted and entered data. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion with another reviewer (S.B.). Data extracted included first author, year of publication, study design, geographical location, inclusion criteria, age, sex, number of patients studied in each intervention arm, follow-up time, and severity of PE (high vs intermediate risk).

Two authors (M.H.M. and R.S.Z.) independently appraised the methodological quality of included RCTs using Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool, which assessed selection, allocation, performance, detection, attrition, and reporting bias—trials with high or unclear risk of bias in sequence generation of allocation, allocation concealment, and blinding of outcome assessors data were considered trials with high bias risk. Six of 16 trials were considered trials with high risk of bias (Supplemental Table 1). The quality of observational studies were assessed using Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale. Studies were considered high quality $(\ge7)$ , intermediate quality (6 points), and low quality $(\leq5)$ on the basis of the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale score (Supplemental Table 2).5 Our analysis had 2 low-quality observational studies, 4 of intermediate quality, and the rest were high-quality observational studies.  

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS. Continuous variables were reported as mean $\pm\mathsf{SD}$ and categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Adverse events using OR, $95\%$ CIs were calculated for binary outcomes analysis.6 Frequentist estimation of network meta-analysis models was used. The primary analysis compared CDT and systemic fibrinolysis with AC alone. Other pairs of comparisons were also performed. Inconsistency between the trials was analyzed at network level7; a $P$ value $<0.05$ was considered for statistically significant heterogeneity. If there was significant inconsistency at the network level, inconsistency was assessed at the loop level using a loop inconsistency plot and inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons. If there was significant heterogeneity, direct meta-analysis was also conducted. Although presence or absence of significant heterogeneity is used to select the random- vs fixedeffects model, we have presented a random-effects model so as to have a conservative interpretation, given the data from largely nonrandomized studies. The effect size of each study and treatment was evaluated using interval plot.7-10 We rank ordered the treatment using surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) scores.7-10 SUCRA provides a hierarchy of treatments, with larger a SUCRA value indicating a better rank of treatment.11 Finally, a network funnel plot was used to estimate publication bias and small study effects.7,8 Relative ranking of interventions on basis of 2 different outcomes was measured using cluster ranking.8,9 Sensitivity analyses were conducted by inclusion of studies with intermediate-risk PE only and separately inclusion of RCTs only. A $P$ value ${<}0.05$ was considered for statistical significance. Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp) with the mvmeta package was used for all the statistical analyses.  



# RESULTS  

LITERATURE SEARCH. The initial search yielded 28,520 reports, which were screened by reviewing the title or abstract. After removing duplications, reviews and case reports, 10,077 studies were selected for further screening. Of these, 140 studies were reviewed in full, and 45 articles that compared at least 2 of the 3 interventions for PE (AC alone, CDT, and ST) were included in the final network meta-analysis (Figure 1).  

BASELINE CHARACTERISTICS. The 45 studies included 81,705 patients with 19,976 who were treated with AC alone, 9,610 who underwent CDT, and 52,119 who were treated with ST. The analysis included a total of 45 studies: 17 RCTs, 2 prospective nonrandomized studies, and 26 retrospective observational reports. There were 34 study arms with AC alone, 28 with CDT, and 31 study arms with ST. The study populations had similar proportions of men $(48.6\%)$ and women, with mean ages ranging from 46.9 to 75.3 years. Out of the 45 studies in our analysis, 20 studies exclusively included patients with intermediate-risk PE, with a total of 11,665 patients (8,873 in the AC-alone group, 1,929 in the CDT group, and 883 in the ST group). The remainder of the studies largely consisted of a mix of intermediateand high-risk populations. There were 17 RCTs, which included a total of 2,210 patients (1,101 AC alone, 78 CDT, and 1,031 ST). The baseline characteristics of the study population are provided in Supplemental Table 3.  

EFFICACY OUTCOME: ALL-CAUSE MORTALITY. Forty-four studies (33 studies with AC alone, 27 studies with CDT, and 31 studies with systemic fibrinolysis) contributed data for all-cause mortality (Supplemental Figures 1A and 1B). When compared with AC alone, CDT was associated with a significantly lower risk of mortality (OR: 0.55; $95\%$ CI: 0.39- 0.80) but ST was not (OR: 1.14; $95\%$ CI: 0.76-1.70) (Central Illustration). ST was associated with a significantly higher risk of mortality compared with CDT (OR: 2.05; $95\%$ CI: 1.46-2.89). CDT was ranked highest, followed by AC alone then ST for the outcome of mortality (Central Illustration). There was no publication bias, but there was significant inconsistency $(P=0.036)$ ) (Supplemental Figure 1C). There was no inconsistency at the loop level using a loop inconsistency plot (Supplemental Figure 1D). The overall inconsistency was driven by the CDT vs ST comparison (Supplemental Table 4). Sensitivity analyses restricted to 30-day mortality, intermediate-risk PE, or RCTs yielded largely similar results (Supplemental Figures 2 to 4).  

Direct meta-analysis was performed to address inconsistency in the network meta-analysis and showed consistent results with the network analysis. In particular, when compared with AC alone, CDT was associated with lower all-cause mortality (fixed-effect model, OR: 0.58; $95\%$ CI: $0.49\substack{-0.69}$ ; $\textit{P}<0.001$ ; random-effects model, OR: 0.65; $95\%$ CI: 0.42-1.00; $\begin{array}{rlr}{P}&{{}=}&{0.05}\end{array}$ ; 16 studies) (Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Figures 5A to 5D). Similarly, risk of allcause mortality with ST was significantly higher compared with AC alone (fixed-effect model, OR: 1.96; $95\%$ CI: 1.64-2.34; 20 studies) (Supplemental Table 6, Supplemental Figures 6 and 7A to 7D). An additional direct meta-analysis, focusing on the outcome of all-cause mortality and limited to RCTs, was performed using various models to compare ST vs AC (Supplemental Table 6, Supplemental Figure 8).

# DISCUSSION  

To our knowledge, this is the first network metaanalysis comparing AC alone, CDT, and systemic thrombolytics for acute PE. Our network metaanalysis has several key findings: first, for the efficacy outcome of all-cause mortality, CDT was associated with a lower risk when compared with AC alone and ST, even when restricting the analysis to the subgroup of patients with intermediate-risk PE. Second, CDT was associated with a higher risk of major bleeding and numerically higher rates of ICH when compared with AC alone, but the risk was lower than that of ST. Third, ST had no mortality benefit when compared with AC alone but had an increased risk of major bleeding and ICH. Fourth, the best treatment (lowest event rate) for efficacy (all-cause mortality) was CDT but for safety (major bleeding) was AC alone.  

Although current guidelines recommend the use of ST for patients with high-risk PE and do not recommend routine use in intermediate-risk patients, a prior meta-analysis of RCTs demonstrated a lower mortality with systemic thrombolytics in patients with intermediate or high-risk PE, but this was offset by increased risk of major bleeding and ICH when compared with AC alone.1 Patients with intermediaterisk PE have a high risk of in-hospital mortality on AC alone $(3\%-15\%)$ ), and as such the lower mortality with thrombolytics observed in this analysis is attractive, but the high risk of major bleeding and ICH precludes the use of systemic thrombolytics as the standard of care for such patients.1  

Catheter-based thrombolytic therapies aim to preserve this potential efficacy of systemic thrombolytics while potentially lowering the risk of major bleeding and ICH. Our analysis of 81,611 patients from 44 studies support the efficacy of CDT and that its use in appropriately selected patients may be associated with lower mortality in intermediate or high-risk acute PE when compared with AC alone. However, the results are driven largely by observational studies and ongoing randomized trials are needed to confirm these findings.  

With regards to bleeding risk, current evidence evaluating CDT in acute PE, which has been limited by single arm studies with surrogate outcomes, observational studies and small RCTs suggest lower but non- negligible rates of ICH $(0.77\%)$ ).12 Our analysis showed that the risk of major bleeding and ICH was lower with CDT when compared with ST. This observation is important given that prior enthusiasm regarding the use of systemic thrombolytics in acute PE has been tempered by the significant bleeding risk, particularly the non-negligible risk of ICH. As a result, research efforts have focused on CDT given the theoretical benefits of local administration of thrombolytic therapy and avoiding thrombolytic agents being shunted away from regions with thrombus toward unobstructed pulmonary arteries. This allows CDT to use a fraction of the total dose of thrombolytics recommended for ST and translates into lower rates of bleeding complications compared with systemic administration. Our findings support the notion that CDT has a better safety profile when compared with systemic thrombolytics. However, CDT was associated with a higher risk of major bleeding and higher risk of ICH when compared with AC alone.  

Prior meta-analysis of RCTs showed lower mortality with systemic thrombolytic use when compared with AC alone.1 However, in our subgroup analysis restricted to RCTs, although there was numerically lower mortality with systemic thrombolytics, this did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy may be explained by 2 factors: First, the analysis by Chatterjee et al1 was performed using the Peto OR methodology. Our analysis yielded similar benefit using the Peto OR and fixed-effect model but not in the more conservative random-effects model (Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Figures 7A to 7D). Given the small sample size of these trials, a random-effects model and conservative interpretation may be more appropriate in this setting. Second, Chatterjee et al combined both systemic thrombolytics and CDT as a common comparator. Ongoing RCTs will provide more data on efficacy and safety of CDT in patients with PE, such as the HI-PEITHO (Ultrasound-facilitated, Catheter-directed, Thrombolysis in Intermediate-high Risk Pulmonary Embolism; NCT04790370), which is expected to include 406 patients randomized in 1:1 ratio of CDT vs AC alone in acute intermediate-high risk PE. The primary outcomes are PE-related mortality, recurrence, and cardiorespiratory decompensation or collapse within 7 days of randomization.  

Despite the reduced dose of thrombolytics with CDT, it is important to note that major bleeding and ICH were still higher with CDT when compared with AC alone. The optimal dose of alteplase remains as a subject of ongoing research. It is important to emphasize that our findings do not suggest that ST has no potential advantages over AC in the high-risk population who are in shock. Rather, our study highlights the lack of hard evidence to support its routine use and the potential risks within the intermediate-risk population. There remains a reasonable amount of skepticism toward catheterbased therapies for acute PE, and the medical community rightly demands large-scale RCTs to support their use. However, the current analysis suggests that these therapies exist in an evidence zone similar to that of ST. In fact, our findings suggest that there may be even less supportive data for the selective use of ST in PE than for CDT. Ongoing studies are evaluating catheter-based thrombectomy as a thrombolytic-free option for the management of large volume PE. This approach may offer a safe and effective alternative to thrombolytic therapy, especially in patients who are at high risk of bleeding or have contraindications to thrombolytic therapy.  

STUDY LIMITATIONS. The results of this analysis should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. First, our analysis included both RCTs and observational studies. However, RCTs contributed $2.7\%$ of total patient cohort, with only 78 patients receiving CDT. As such, the analysis was largely driven by observational studies with associated selection and ascertainment bias. Further RCTs are necessary to validate these findings. Second, individual patientlevel data were not available, and hence individual baseline characteristics were unknown. Finally, our analysis did not include low-risk PE, and these findings do not apply to this category of patients.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

CDT was associated with lower all-cause mortality in patients with acute PE when compared with AC alone. CDT was associated with increased major bleeding and numerically higher ICH when compared with AC alone, but these rates were lower when compared with systemic thrombolytics. Randomized trials are needed to assess the efficacy and safety of these approaches.

# PERSPECTIVES  

WHAT IS KNOWN? Systemic thrombolytic therapy has been shown to reduce mortality in intermediate- and high-risk PE but comes at the expense of increased rates of major bleeding and ICH. The high bleeding risk has sparked interest in catheter-based thrombolytic therapies to optimize the benefit-to-risk ratio of thrombolytic therapy.  

WHAT IS NEW? CDT is associated with a reduction in mortality when compared with anticoagulation alone in patients with acute PE. However, although the major bleeding and ICH rates with CDT were lower than with systemic thrombolytics, they were higher than AC alone. As such, the best treatment for efficacy was CDT but that for safety was AC alone.  

WHAT IS NEXT? RCTs comparing CDT with AC alone, as well as with catheter-based thrombectomy, are warranted to reach more definitive conclusions.
