# A Randomized Controlled Trial of Statins to Reduce Inflammation in Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysms  

![](images/32960fd314d91c88cca5e8643993bcfb3c87b43a9dc7527e2a78ca520cc7a55b.jpg)  

Unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) are relatively common in adults with an estimated prevalence of $3\%$ . Inflammation and atherosclerosis play critical roles in intracranial aneurysms formation and rupture. Statins may reduce aneurysmal inflammation, but previous studies have yielded conflicting results and did not directly quantify aneurysmal inflammation endpoints. Enhancement of the aneurysm wall on vessel-wall magnetic resonance imaging (VW-MRI) is a potential surrogate biomarker for inflammation that has been used to detect unstable UIAs.1 Our study investigates whether statins can decrease UIA wall enhancement, suggesting modulation of inflammation and potential prevention of rupture.  

We performed a randomized placebo-controlled trial with double-blinded assessment of outcome events at a single center (Tiantan Hospital, Beijing, China). This trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (Statin Treatment for UnruptureD Intracranial anEurysms Study [STUDIES]; NCT04149483) and approved by the institutional ethics committee. Our study shows that quantitative WEI and WEVR and circulating inflammatory biomarkers decreased after 6-month atorvastatin treatment, although the change of aneurysm morphologic characteristics, including aneurysm size and shape, was not statistically significant. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first prospective randomized controlled trial investigating the effect of statins on aneurysm wall enhancement. # Imaging of Glycosaminoglycans in Ascending Aortic Aneurysms With Chemical Exchange Saturation Transfer MRI  

Imaging is essential for detecting thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAAs) and gauging dissection risk through diameter assessment. However, data from the International Registry of Aortic Dissection have revealed that many dissections involving the ascending aorta may not be prevented using a recommended diameter intervention threshold.1 Yet lowering the size threshold for intervention would capture many patients for whom aortic replacement would offer no benefit. This challenge highlights the need to ascertain aortic risk with greater precision. Imaging aortic wall composition, not just lumen caliber, might fill this need.  

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are unbranched polysaccharides that accumulate in TAAs. GAGs exist as sidechains of proteoglycans or as hyaluronan, both of which can form large, gel-like aggregates in degenerating ascending aortic media.3 Despite the longstanding recognition of GAGs in TAAs, aortic GAG assessment as a clinical tool has not been explored.  

We asked whether GAGs in the aortic wall could be detected using chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). CEST MRI indirectly detects molecules that have exchangeable protons. When such protons are selectively saturated, their transfer to the bulk water pool produces MRI contrast. Importantly, the amide and hydroxyl protons within GAG disaccharide units can be used to generate endogenous CEST contrast.  

We confirmed that chondroitin sulfate, a major GAG in ascending aortas, and bovine hyaline cartilage yielded a CEST signal at $^{+1}$ parts per million, as evident from the standardized Z-spectrum and magnetization transfer ratio asymmetry plot. We then imaged human ascending aorta samples harvested from individuals undergoing ascending aorta replacement, undertaken in accordance with protocols approved by the Western University Research Ethics Board. Aortic samples were imaged on a Agilent 9.4-T MRI scanner using a volume coil. Anatomic $\mathrm{T}_{2}$ -weighted images were acquired using a fast spin echo pulse sequence and CEST images $(2~\upmu\mathrm{T}.$ , 5s saturation) were acquired using a 2-dimensional Fast Low Angle Shot pulse sequence.
