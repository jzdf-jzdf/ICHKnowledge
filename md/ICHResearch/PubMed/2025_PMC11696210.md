# Abstract  

Background: Yttrium‐90 FF‐21101 $(^{90}\Upsilon-\mathsf{FF}{-}21101)$ is a radiopharmaceutical that targets P‐cadherin as a therapy against solid tumors. A previously reported, first‐in‐ human study determined that a dose of $25\mathrm{mCi}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ was safe, and a patient with clear cell carcinoma of the ovary achieved a complete response. In this article, the authors report the results of $^{90}\curlyvee$ –FF‐21101 treatment in an ovarian carcinoma expansion cohort and in patients with selected solid tumors who had known high P‐ cadherin expression.  

Methods: The trial was conducted as an open‐label study in patients with advanced/ metastatic disease. Radiologic response and safety were evaluated in patients who received $25\mathrm{mCi}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ intravenously once every three cycles of 28 days until they developed progressive disease. Evaluation of the ovarian cohort was conducted in a Simon two‐stage manner to determine further enrollment.  

Results: Fifty‐seven patients (20 with ovarian carcinoma) were enrolled and treated. Patients who had ovarian and solid tumors had received a median of five and three prior therapies, respectively. No complete or partial responses were observed, so the trial was ended. The median progression‐free survival was 118 days for the ovarian cohort and 55 days for the solid‐tumor cohort. The most common treatment‐related adverse events were thrombocytopenia $(40\%)$ and neutropenia $(54\%)$ . One patient each developed fatal veno‐occlusive disease and intracranial hemorrhage. Patients with higher P‐cadherin levels remained on the study longer. Conclusions: $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ –FF‐21101 did not meet the predefined efficacy criteria, and adverse events were consistent with $^{90}\curlyvee$ agents. These data may assist in the development of other P‐cadherin–directed therapies (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02454010).

# INTRODUCTION  

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have been approved for cancer treatment for decades; however, single‐agent mAb monotherapy has been variously successful.1–3 Cytotoxic chemicals and radionuclides have been used to arm mAbs to improve efficacy.3,4 Radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) uses a carrier molecule (typically an antibody) to selectively deliver radionuclides, primarily $\upbeta$ or α emitters, to malignant cells without affecting tissues that lack the target antigen.1–3  

RPT is effective against hematologic tumors. In patients with non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, radiolabeled antibody conjugates at doses ${<}20$ grays (Gy) demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy over naked mAbs and efficacy comparable to that of combination cytotoxic drug regimens alone or combined with naked mAbs.5,6 Solid tumors are less radiosensitive than hematologic tumors, and the best RPT outcomes are in minimal disease settings.1–3 However, the approvals of $\upbeta$ emitters against neuroendocrine cancers and phaeochromocytomas and of an α emitter against bone metastases of prostate cancer have reignited RPT development.  

Potential RPT targets include the cadherin superfamily of transmembrane calcium–binding proteins involved in the formation of cell‐to‐cell adhesion. P‐cadherin is particularly promising because it displays minimal expression in normal adult human tissues.7–9 Accordingly, P‐cadherin–directed therapies have included unmodified mAbs, bispecific T‐cell engagers, and antibody–drug combinations.10–12 Our approach was to develop the radiolabeled antibody yttrium‐90 $^{190}\Upsilon)$ –FF‐21101 as an RPT agent.9,13 Its safety, tolerability, and antitumor activity in advanced solid tumors was investigated in a phase 1, first‐in‐human, dose‐escalation trial (ClinicalTrials. gov identifier NCT02454010).10 As is standard for $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ agents, we used indium‐111 $(^{111}|\mathsf{n})$ imaging to investigate tumor localization and confirm radiation dosimetry to solid organs for safety.14 A favorable safety profile was observed for all tested doses of $5{-}25\mathsf{mCi}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ in the 15 heavily pretreated patients. $^{111}|_{\mathsf{n}}$ dosimetry results indicated that radiation doses were within all organ limits. After seven patients were treated with $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ –FF‐21101, one patient with clear cell ovarian carcinoma developed a complete response (CR). Based on these results (completed in 2018), we began a dose‐expansion phase in patients with ovarian carcinoma along with a separate exploratory cohort in patients with solid tumors known to overexpress P‐ cadherin. Herein, we present the final dose‐expansion phase results for patients treated at the recommended phase 2 dose $(25\mathrm{mCi}/\mathrm{m}^{2})$ .  

# MATERIALS AND METHODS  

# Study design  

The open‐label expansion‐phase study used a Simon 2‐stage design to evaluate the ovarian carcinoma cohort (Figure 1A) with the following parameters: if three or more of 15 patients had responses, the cohort added 13 additional patients (28 total). Enrollment beyond 15 patients was allowed during response evaluation. The exploratory cohort could enroll up to 80 patients with the following diseases: triple‐negative breast cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, biliary cancer (cholangiocarcinoma or gallbladder carcinoma), pancreatic carcinoma, and colorectal cancer. These tumors were selected on the basis of their typically high P‐cadherin expression, as measured by both gene and antigen expression.15,16 Because there are no validated criteria for minimal P‐cadherin expression, it was not an entry criterion for the study. P‐cadherin expression was assessed from archived patient tumors by immunohistochemistry, as described earlier for a planned, correlative clinical response analysis.9 The protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at each site, and all patients provided informed consent.  

FF‐21101 was conjugated with ${}^{90}\Upsilon{\sfCl}_{3}$ for therapeutic treatment by each site, as previously described.9 A $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ –FF‐21101 dose of $25\mathrm{mCi}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ was administered intravenously (without exceeding $60~\mathrm{mCi}$ per administration) every three cycles (one cycle $=28$ days). All doses were infused through a $0.22\cdot\mathrm{mm}$ inline filter for approximately 10 minutes, not exceeding $1~\mathrm{ml}$ per minute. Patients were monitored for infusion reactions 1 hour after each $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ –FF‐21101 dose.  

Patients were monitored weekly during cycle 1, every 2 weeks during cycle 2, and monthly during each subsequent dosing cycle. Computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging was conducted for disease assessment at week 8 and every 8 weeks thereafter until documented progressive disease (PD). Patients were treated until they chose to discontinue or experienced PD, unacceptable adverse events (AEs), or any medical condition that prevented further participation. Because P‐cadherin is expressed in the retina, in addition to standard safety monitoring (physical examination, AEs, and clinical laboratory parameters), ophthalmic safety was monitored within 30 days before each $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐21101 dose and at the end of treatment.9

The ovarian carcinoma cohort included patients who had confirmed platinum‐refractory/resistant epithelial ovarian, peritoneal, or fallopian tube carcinoma. The solid‐tumor cohort consisted of patients who had the tumors listed above. The complete eligibility requirements are listed in Table S1. # End points  

The primary efficacy end point was the proportion of patients with either a CR or a partial response (PR) according to RECIST after $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ –FF‐21101 treatment. The secondary efficacy end points included disease control; the duration of CR, PR, or stable disease (SD) according to RECIST; progression‐free survival (PFS), defined as the time from $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐21101 administration to PD or death; and overall survival (OS). Patients were followed for 12 months after the end of treatment for survival. Other secondary end points included FF‐ 21101 pharmacokinetics (PK) and antidrug–antibody (ADA) formation to evaluate $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐21101 immunogenicity, as previously described.10 PK samples were collected on days 1, 8, and 15 of cycles 1 and 4, and data were analyzed using a noncompartmental approach with Phoenix PK software (Certara). Circulating ADA formation was assessed by measuring serum antihuman/mouse chimeric antibodies on day 1 of cycles 1 (pretreatment), 4, and 5 and at the end of treatment.  

# Statistical analyses  

Response rates are summarized as the number, percentage, and two‐ sided $90\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) for best response (CR, PR, SD, or PD). The Kaplan–Meier product‐limit method was used to estimate SD duration (date of first SD to date of PD), median PFS, and OS. Patients without PD were censored at the last evaluable disease assessment. Patients who did not experience death were censored at the date the patient was last known to be alive. Statistical analysis software (SAS version 9.4 for Windows or higher; SAS Institute, Inc.) was used for all analyses. Safety end points, PK parameters, and serum antihuman/mouse chimeric antibody concentration–time profiles were summarized using descriptive statistics.  

# RESULTS  

# Patient characteristics  

In total, 57 patients (20 in the ovarian cohort, 37 in the solid‐tumor cohort) were enrolled and treated in the dose‐expansion phase from February 2, 2019, to September 30, 2020 (Table 1). The last patient follow‐up occurred on October 21, 2021. The median age for the ovarian cohort was 66 years (range, 47–84 years) and included 17 patients with high‐grade serous ovarian carcinoma, two with clear cell carcinoma, one with fallopian carcinoma, and four described as having “ovarian” or “adenocarcinoma”. All patients with ovarian carcinoma had received prior platinum therapy and had $\mathsf{H}$ ‐scores ranging from 0 to 290 (see Table S2). The median age for the solid‐ tumor cohort was 64 years (range, 36–89 years), $49\%$ were females, and the cohort included 18 patients with colorectal carcinoma, 12 with pancreatic carcinoma, three with bile duct cancer, and two each with breast and duodenal carcinoma. Patients had various tumor burdens based on target lesion sum of the greatest dimension $(19-228\mathrm{mm})$ ). Patients were heavily pretreated, with a median of five prior therapies in the ovarian cohort and three prior therapies in the solid‐tumor cohort (ranging from one prior therapy for patients with bile duct carcinoma to three prior therapies for those with colorectal carcinoma).

One patient (baseline ECOG performance status $=2.$ ) withdrew consent on day 7, complaining of fatigue. The other patient (baseline ECOG performance status $=0$ ) died on day 51 of PD. The most common reason for treatment discontinuation was PD, as measured by RECIST or clinical deterioration (40 patients $[70\%]$ , including patients who died before withdrawing from treatment). The remaining reasons were AEs in nine patients $(16\%)$ , including two fatal, possibly related AEs and seven AEs unrelated to FF‐21101; withdrawal of consent in six patients $(11\%)$ , and physician decision in one patient $(2\%)$ .  

# Pharmacokinetics  

The PK results were consistent with those observed in the dose‐ escalation phase (see Table S3).9 At the recommended phase 2 dose $(25\mathrm{mCi}/\mathrm{m}^{2})$ , the mean half‐life was $62.9\pm14.2$ hours, and the area under the curve was $145,000\pm37,600$ hours per∙ng/mL. The mean clearance was $22.7\pm5.9\mathsf{mL}$ per hour $/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ (range, $11.9\substack{-36.9\mathrm{mL}}$ per $\scriptstyle{\mathsf{hour}}/{\mathsf{m}}^{2}$ . The mean apparent volume of distribution was $2000\pm445\mathfrak{mL}^{2}$ (range, $1140{-}3070\ m/\mathsf{m}^{2})$ . Systemic $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐ 21101 exposure, defined by the mean maximum serum concentration and area under the curve $_{0-\mathrm{t})}$ , was similar between cycle 1 and cycle 4.  

# Efficacy  

Of the 20 patients in the ovarian cohort, 18 were response evaluable by RECIST, and no patients had a CR or PR. A best response of SD was reported for 12 patients $(66\%)$ , and PD was the best response reported for six patients $(33\%)$ . One patient had an $18\%$ decrease in the sum of target lesions (from 22 to $18~\mathrm{mm}$ ) in their first evaluation but met the PD criteria 8 weeks later, developing a new peritoneal nodule. In the solid‐tumor cohort, a best response of SD was reported for nine patients $(29\%)$ , and PD was the best response reported for 22 patients $(71\%)$ . The median SD duration was 169 days $90\%$ CI, 62–268 days; $n=12$ , with six censored) in the ovarian cohort and 66 days ( $90\%$ CI, 56–110 days; $n=9$ , with five censored) in the solid‐tumor cohort.  

PFS and OS were calculated for both cohorts (Figure 2A,B). The median PFS was 118 days $90\%$ CI, 54–222 days) for the ovarian cohort and 55 days $90\%$ CI, 54–857 days) for the solid‐tumor cohort. The median PFS for specific tumor types within the solid‐ tumor cohort were similar to the cohort median: 56 days $90\%$ CI, 52–84 days) for patients with colorectal and duodenal cancer and 55 days ( $90\%$ CI, 48–56 days) for those with pancreatic adenocarcinoma. The median OS was 244 days ( $90\%$ CI, 215–435 days) for the ovarian cohort and 198 days ( $90\%$ CI, 142–235 days) for the solid‐ tumor cohort. These results are consistent with reported durations for late‐stage patients with each cancer type. Cancer antigen 125 progression (a confirmed doubling [or two‐fold increase] or more) was observed in four patients with ovarian carcinoma on study. A cancer antigen 125 progression or response could not be determined in the other 16 patients.

# Safety  

All patients experienced AEs, and all but one patient in each cohort experienced a suspected treatment‐related AE. Of the 10 most common AEs, five were hematologic. Table 3 lists the most common treatment‐emergent AEs in occurred in two or more patients and all treatment‐related AEs.  

Persistent grade ${\ge}3$ thrombocytopenia was present in three patients, all of whom had received multiple prior regimens of carboplatin/paclitaxel or other cytotoxic agents. These findings were clinically distinct from the transient thrombocytopenia observed in the dose‐escalation phase. Therefore, restrictions were placed on repeated dose administration for patients who experienced severe or persistent thrombocytopenia after $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐21101 administration. Specifically, patients who developed grade 4 thrombocytopenia $(<25,000/\upmu\updownarrow)$ or who developed grade 3 thrombocytopenia $(<50,000/\upmu\upL)$ more than 56 days after a $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐21101 infusion could not receive further $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐21101 treatment. Day 15 platelet counts were added for all cycles beginning with cycle 3.  

Five patients required dose reductions to $20\mathrm{mCi}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ . Four patients had fatal AEs while actively on study. Two patients had fatal AEs that may have been caused, at least in part, by $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐ 21101. A patient aged 56 years with ovarian carcinoma who suffered from morbid obesity, hypertension, and upper extremity superficial thrombosis developed portal vein thrombosis and hyperbilirubinemia after approximately 7 months on study and receiving three $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ –FF‐21101 doses. Her prior therapy included multiple lines of cytotoxic therapy and maytansinoid antibody– drug conjugate therapy. A liver biopsy confirmed sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (veno‐occlusive disease). Despite treatment with defibrotide, the patient became hypotensive and increasingly oxygen‐dependent, ultimately dying on comfort care measures. A second patient with pancreatic cancer died of intracranial hemorrhage in a previously unknown cerebral metastases. This event occurred in the setting of grade 3 thrombocytopenia $(74,000/\upmu\mathrm{L})$ and likely was caused by $^{90}\curlyvee\cdot$ –FF‐21101, which may have contributed to cerebral bleeding. Two additional deaths occurred: one in a patient who developed fatal dyspnea/pneumonia and one in a patient who experienced acute respiratory failure while in sepsis. These deaths were considered to be caused by the underlying malignancies. A fifth patient died of PD, which manifested as vaginal bleeding because of tumor encroachment on a uterine artery.  

AEs that met the regulatory definition of being serious AEs (SAEs) occurred in seven patients in the ovarian cohort and 19 patients in the solid‐tumor cohort. Six SAEs in the ovarian cohort were considered possibly related to $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐21101, including grade 3 incidences of anorexia, nausea, fatigue, and thrombocytopenia; grade 4 myelodysplastic syndrome; and grade 5 sinusoidal obstruction syndrome. The only suspected treatment‐related SAE in the solid‐tumor cohort was the intracranial hemorrhage.  

# Evaluation of antidrug antibody formation  

Specific, treatment‐emergent ADA responses were detected in 11 patients but did not appear to alter $^{90}\mathsf{Y}.$ –FF‐21101 clearance. Whether the ADA is neutralizing remains unknown. Two patients who received multiple $^{90}\Upsilon-\mathsf{FF}-21101$ doses and had persistent ADA responses throughout the course of therapy maintained SD for 6 and 12 months.  

# DISCUSSION  

To our knowledge, this is the fourth clinical trial of a P‐cadherin– directed therapy. Each successive clinical trial has tested a different investigational therapy, reflecting technological advances in drug development. The first trial used PF03732010, an unmodified mAb targeting P‐cadherin; despite promising preclinical data, a lack of clinical activity stopped PF03732010 development.18 More recently, Harding et al. reported on PF‐06671008, a modified, bispecific, T‐cell engager.11 However, no patient in their study had a radiologic response, and cytokine release syndrome was a prominent toxicity.11 Duca et al. reported on PCA062, an antibody–drug combination targeting P‐cadherin to deliver maytansinoid.10 Of 47 treated patients in that study, only one patient with head and neck cancer had a PR,10 and the safety profile of PCA062 was consistent with known toxicities associated with maytansin. Development was stopped for both PCA062 and PF‐06671008.  

We stopped enrollment in our trial because none of the 18 evaluable patients in the ovarian cohort had a treatment response. In the solid‐tumor cohort, we planned to enroll patients with high P‐ cadherin–expressing tumors, emphasizing patients with colorectal, pancreatic, and biliary adenocarcinomas. Enrollment occurred concurrently for both cohorts and stopped simultaneously.  

Although we did not meet predefined efficacy criteria, we believe the results of this trial are useful for further development of treatments targeting P‐cadherin–expressing solid tumors.  

It is well established that the principal side effect of $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ is hematopoietic suppression.14,19 Like all $\upbeta$ emitters, the mechanism of action is DNA damage through high‐energy linear transfer, to which bone marrow stem cells are particularly sensitive.20 In various studies using $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ mAb therapeutics at doses ranging from approximately 3 to $50\mathrm{mCi}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ , the incidence of grade ${\ge}3$ thrombocytopenia and neutropenia could be as high as $75\%$ .14,19,21,22 When multiple doses were given over time, the incidence of thrombocytopenia and neutropenia increased over time as well.21 Patients generally recover, but the extent of prior chemotherapy is associated with prolonged or diminished recovery.19 The incidence of hematologic side effects reported here and in the dose‐finding part of the trial were consistent with these reported studies. Apart from a possible case of sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, there were no AEs suggestive of radiation damage to other organs, consistent with the $^{111}|_{\mathsf{n}}$ dosimetric measurements.9 No safety findings were specifically attributable to P‐cadherin targeting in our study; and, despite the retinal expression of P‐cadherin, no visual toxicities were observed.

Four SAEs resulting in death were reported, including two that were considered possibly treatment‐related: thrombocytopenia may have contributed to intracranial hemorrhage; and, although unexpected, veno‐occlusive disease has been associated rarely with radiotherapy and past treatments using antibody–drug conjugates or cytotoxic agents. Hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome shares many features with radiation‐induced liver injury caused by $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ sphere ablation, as described by Sangro et al.23 and reviewed by Kim and Jung.24 Hepatic injury is typically reported several weeks after the delivery of radiation doses ${>}30$ Gy, and patients with prior chemotherapy experience an elevated risk of hepatic injury. An enhanced risk of sinusoidal obstruction has also been reported in patients treated with gemtuzumab ozogamicin.25 In our study, the patient who experienced veno‐occlusive liver disease had received three therapies associated with developing sinusoidal obstruction syndrome: cytotoxic therapy, antibody–drug conjugates, and $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ radiation carried by FF‐21101. Dosimetry experiments indicate that our dosing regimen exposed the liver to a radiation dose much lower than $30~\mathsf{Gy}$ . Therefore, we believe that sinusoidal obstruction syndrome was unlikely to be caused by the radiation dose alone.  

Increasingly sophisticated RPT agents have led to recent successes, as comprehensively reviewed in several valuable articles.1,26 However, RPT for solid tumors remains challenging, perhaps because solid tumors require higher radiation doses than hematologic malignancies.1,27 Other RPTs have been unsuccessful in treating ovarian carcinomas and various gastrointestinal tumors.28,29 The PFS and OS rates observed for the ovarian cohort in our trial were consistent with those reported for heavily pretreated patients in retrospective analyses and for patients with advanced disease in prospective trials.30  

P‐cadherin is overexpressed in many malignancies, with little expression in normal tissues other than the eye. Clinical trials testing various approaches targeting tumor‐associated antigens that are similarly overexpressed in solid tumors, including carcinoembryonic antigen and mucin 1, have also been unsuccessful.27–29,31 Overexpressed antigens may protect them from therapies that otherwise would work against exposed tumor cells. The extensive extracellular matrix in many solid tumors may explain why the radiation dose necessary for antitumor effects is greater than the dose required for hematologic tumors.32 The addition of an α emitter, such as radium‐ 223 or actinium‐225, to FF‐21101 or similar carriers could increase extracellular matrix penetration and improve effectiveness against solid tumors. In addition, solid tumors have hypoxic cores that diminish β‐emitter efficacy but are unlikely to affect α‐emitter activity.2,33  

In pursuit of this approach, it has been demonstrated that actinium‐225 can be conjugated to FF‐21101, and preliminary preclinical results show promise.34 Although this $^{90}\mathsf{Y}$ –FF‐21101 trial and other P‐cadherin–targeting trials have been unsuccessful, P‐cadherin remains an attractive and important target, and valuable lessons have been learned. Further investigation may achieve the therapeutic potential of P‐cadherin–directed RPT.
