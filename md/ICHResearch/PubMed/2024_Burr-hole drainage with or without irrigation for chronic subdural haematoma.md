# Summary  

Background Chronic subdural haematoma is a common surgically treated intracranial emergency. Burr-hole drainage surgery, to evacuate chronic subdural haematoma, involves three elements: creation of a burr hole for access, irrigation of the subdural space, and insertion of a subdural drain. Although the subdural drain has been established as beneficial, the therapeutic effect of subdural irrigation has not been addressed.  

Methods The FINISH trial was an investigator-initiated, pragmatic, multicentre, nationwide, randomised, controlled, parallel-group, non-inferiority trial in five neurosurgical units in Finland that enrolled adults aged 18 years or older with a chronic subdural haematoma requiring burr-hole drainage. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) by computergenerated block randomisation with block sizes of four, six, or eight, stratified by site, to burr-hole drainage either with or without subdural irrigation. All patients and staff were masked to treatment assignment apart from the neurosurgeon and operating room staff. A burr hole was drilled at the site of maximum haematoma thickness in both groups, and the subdural space was either irrigated or not irrigated before inserting a subdural drain, which remained in place for $\mathbf{48~h}$ . Reoperations, functional outcome, mortality, and adverse events were recorded for 6 months after surgery. The primary outcome was the reoperation rate within 6 months. The non-inferiority margin was set at $7\cdot5\%$ . Key secondary outcomes that were also required to conclude non-inferiority were the proportion of participants with unfavourable functional outcomes (ie, modified Rankin Scale score of 4–6, where 0 indicates no symptoms and 6 indicates death) and mortality rate at 6 months. The primary and key secondary analyses were done in both the intention-to-treat and per-protocol populations. The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04203550) and is completed.  

Findings From Jan 1, 2020, to Aug 17, 2022, we assessed 1644 patients for eligibility and 589 $(36\%)$ patients were randomly assigned to a treatment group and treated (294 assigned to drainage with irrigation and 295 assigned to drainage without irrigation; 165 $[28\%]$ women and 424 $[72\%]$ men). The 6-month follow-up period extended until Feb 14, 2023. In the intention-to-treat analysis, 54 $(18\cdot3\%)$ of 295 participants required reoperation in the group assigned to receive no irrigation versus 37 $(12\cdot6\%)$ of 294 in the group assigned to receive irrigation (difference of ${\bf6\cdot0}$ percentage points, $95\%$ CI 0·2–11·7; $\scriptstyle\mathbf{p=0}\cdot30$ ; adjusted for study site). There were no significant between-group differences in the proportion of people with modified Rankin Scale score of 4–6 (37 $[13\cdot1\%]$ of 283 in the no-irrigation group vs 36 $[12\cdot6\%]$ of 285 in the irrigation group; $\mathbf{p=}0\cdot89)$ or mortality rate (18 $[6\cdot1\%]$ of 295 in the no-irrigation group vs 21 $[7\cdot1\%]$ of 294 in the irrigation group; $\scriptstyle\mathbf{p=}\mathbf{0}\cdot58\mathbf{)}$ . The findings of the primary intention-to-treat analysis were not materially altered in the per-protocol analysis. There were no significant between-group differences in the number of adverse events, and the most frequent severe adverse events were systemic infections $(26\left[8\cdot8\%\right]$ of 295 participants who did not receive irrigation vs 22 $[7\cdot5\%]$ of 294 participants who received irrigation), intracranial haemorrhage (13 [4·4%] vs seven $\left[2\cdot4\%\right]\mathrm{\Omega}$ , and epileptic seizures (five $[1\cdot7\%]$ vs nine $\left[3\cdot1\%\right])$ .  

Interpretation We could not conclude non-inferiority of burr-hole drainage without irrigation. The reoperation rate was 6·0 percentage points higher after burr-hole drainage without subdural irrigation than with subdural irrigation. Considering that there were no differences in functional outcome or mortality between the groups, the trial favours the use of subdural irrigation.

# Research in context  

# Evidence before this study  

Surgery to evacuate chronic subdural haematomas includes burr-hole creation for access, followed by irrigation of the subdural space and insertion of a subdural drain. The subdural drain has been established as beneficial, but the therapeutic effect of subdural irrigation has not been addressed. A metaanalysis summarising existing, primarily observational, data on the rates of haematoma recurrence after burr-hole surgery with irrigation compared with surgery without irrigation reported no difference. We conducted a systematic literature review, searching PubMed, MEDLINE Ovid, and Embase Ovid for studies published in English from database inception to Feb 10, 2020, comparing irrigation with no irrigation for chronic subdural haematoma evacuation. Search terms were “chronic subdural hematoma”, “chronic subdural haematoma”, ${}^{\prime\prime}C\mathsf{SDH}^{\prime\prime},$ , “subdural hematoma”, “irrigation”, “no irrigation”, “no-irrigation”, “burrhole drainage”, “recurrence”, “reoperation”, and “outcome”. We found ten studies, of which nine were retrospective by design and one was a prospective study that used a random sampling method. The findings of these ten studies comparing irrigation with no irrigation were highly conflicting: two studies reported a higher incidence of haematoma recurrence with irrigation than with no irrigation, another two studies showed a lower incidence of haematoma recurrence with irrigation than with no irrigation, and the remaining six studies reported no difference.  

# Added value of this study  

The existing evidence on the benefits and downsides of irrigation is highly conflicting. To our knowledge, the FINISH trial is the first multicentre randomised controlled trial to test the therapeutic effect of subdural irrigation as part of burr-hole drainage surgery for chronic subdural haematoma. The FINISH trial showed that the reoperation rate was 6·0 percentage points $95\%\mathrm{Cl}0.2$ –11·7) higher after burr-hole drainage without subdural irrigation than with subdural irrigation. This result translates to needing to treat 17·5 patients (range 8·5–500·0, calculated from the upper and lower limits of the $95\%\subset$ for the difference in reoperation rates) with irrigation to avoid one reoperation. There were no differences in the proportion of patients with unfavourable functional outcomes, mortality, or adverse events between the group assigned to receive burr-hole drainage with irrigation and the group assigned to receive drainage without irrigation.  

# Implications of all the available evidence  

Whereas previous observational evidence has shown conflicting results, the FINISH trial provides evidence favouring the benefits of subdural irrigation. Therefore, in adults with chronic subdural haematoma, subdural irrigation should be considered as part of the burr-hole drainage procedure to reduce the risk of recurrent haematoma requiring reoperation.

# Introduction  

Surgery for people with chronic subdural haematoma is one of the most common intracranial procedures in the USA and Europe.1,2 As populations age and the use of antithrombotic medication increases, incidence of surgery for people with chronic subdural haematoma has been steadily increasing.3,4 Chronic subdural haematoma can cause brain compression, leading to progressive neurological deterioration and even death. Trauma-induced bleeding and the inflammatory cascade have been proposed as key components in the aetiopathogenesis of chronic subdural haematoma.5 A trial comparing dexamethasone with surgery showed the superiority of surgical haematoma evacuation over dexamethasone treatment alone.6  

The most popular surgical method worldwide to treat patients with chronic subdural haematoma,7,8 introduced almost 90 years ago,9 comprised two elements: creation of a burr hole for access and subdural irrigation to evacuate the haematoma. Over subsequent decades, a third element, insertion of a subdural drain, was suggested as a way to improve outcomes following observational evidence.10 Evidence for the efficacy of subdural drain usage as an adjunct to irrigation was delivered about a decade ago in a trial that showed reduced risks of mortality, unfavourable functional outcomes, and haematoma recurrence in patients with chronic subdural haematoma.11 However, the efficacy of subdural irrigation (ie, one of the three core elements of the burr-hole drainage procedure) has still never been tested in a randomised controlled trial.  

The use of subdural irrigation relies on a biological rationale that rapid evacuation of the solubilised haema­ toma relieves brain compression and removes inflam­ matory factors from the subdural space, subsequently reducing the risk of haematoma recurrence.5,12,13 However, subdural irrigation might not be without harm. Reports of harms linked to irrigation include direct cortical injury, intracranial haemorrhage, and pneumocephalus, which is particularly common and concerning because pneumo­ cephalus might prevent brain re-expansion, increasing the risk of haematoma recurrence.14–17  

To test the therapeutic effect of haematoma irrigation, we conducted a study comparing burr-hole drainage with irrigation and burr-hole drainage without irrigation in adult patients with symptomatic chronic subdural haematomas. We hypothesised that burr-hole drainage with no irrigation would be non-inferior to burr-hole drainage with irrigation with respect to efficacy and safety within 6 months of the surgery.  

# Methods  

# Study design  

The FINISH trial was an investigator-initiated, pragmatic, multicentre, nationwide, randomised, controlled, parallel-group, non-inferiority trial conducted in all five neurosurgical units in Finland. The trial compared the effect of burr-hole drainage with and without subdural irrigation on chronic subdural haematoma recurrences.  

We obtained written informed consent from the patients or from their next of kin if the patient was unable to provide consent. The trial was approved by the ethics committee of Helsinki University Hospital (HUS/3035/2019 §238). The trial protocol was designed by the research team, in collaboration with the European Patients’ Academy on Therapeutic Innovation Finland.18 The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04203550.  

# Participants  

Patients were eligible for enrolment if they were aged 18 years or older and admitted to a neurosurgical unit with a symptomatic chronic subdural haematoma requiring haematoma evacuation. We defined symptomatic chronic subdural haematoma as a hypodense or isodense subdural haematoma on head CT imaging or brain MRI, with symptoms attributable to the chronic subdural haematoma as decided by the treating neurosurgeon. We treated people with bilateral haematoma as one patient, providing identical treatment for both sides.  

We excluded patients who required surgical treatment other than burr-hole evacuation, had a cerebrospinal fluid shunt, had previously undergone intracranial surgery, were in a coma, were unable to cooperate for subdural drain use, had received treatment for a haematological malignancy within the past 5 years, had CNS malignancy affecting symptoms or surgery, had an acute infection, or had a high risk of thrombosis (appendix p 98).  

# Randomisation and masking  

We assigned eligible patients randomly in a 1:1 ratio to undergo burr-hole drainage surgery with irrigation or burr-hole drainage surgery without irrigation, using computer-generated block randomisation with block sizes of four, six, or eight, and stratified by study site. The treating neurosurgeon enrolled that participant and conducted the randomisation process immediately before the skin incision. Only the operating neurosurgeon and the operating room staff were aware of the group assignment, which was omitted from all health-care records. Patients and all other medical staff were masked to treatment assignment. The surgeons who carried out the initial surgeries were instructed to refrain whenever possible from participating in further care of the patients, including decisions to reoperate.  

# Procedures  

Surgeries were performed under local anaesthesia, except in cases where general anaesthesia was deemed necessary for patient safety. A routine preoperative antibiotic was given according to local protocols (ie, normally a second-generation cephalosporin $30{-}60\mathrm{min}$ before incision. A standard burr hole (ie, $14~\mathrm{mm}$ ) was drilled at the maximum site of the haematoma, the dura was incised, and a $10~\mathrm{mL}$ exudate sample was obtained. Depending on the assigned treatment group, the subdural space was either irrigated or not. In the group receiving irrigation, saline irrigation fluid at body temperature (ie, $37~^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ was used to rinse the subdural space in all directions until the neurosurgeon deemed the exudate to be clear. The minimum required irrigation volume was $200~\mathrm{mL}$ per side of the brain. Thereafter, a subdural drain was inserted. In the group not receiving irrigation, the drain was inserted immediately after the exudate sample was obtained. In both groups, the drain was connected to a passive drainage bag for $48\textrm{h}(\pm12\textrm{h})$ .11 During the drainage period, we did not use routine prophylactic antibiotics and allowed the patients to move freely.  

6 weeks after surgery, patients underwent a head CT scan as a safeguard to minimise the possibility of missing sizable residual haematomas. We assessed the preoperative and the 6-week postoperative scans centrally (appendix p 99). If the patient underwent reoperation due to chronic subdural haematoma recurrence, we assessed the most recent CT scan obtained before reoperation (ie, a 6-week scan was not obtained after reoperation).  

All patients were followed up for 6 months after the surgery. A clinical follow-up visit was scheduled to coincide with the 6-week head CT scan. At 6 months, the participants or their next of kin were contacted by telephone. Radiological measurements and character­ istics of the haematoma were analysed at baseline and at the 6-week scan, or before reoperation if the second surgery occurred before 6 weeks. Length of hospital stay was recorded at discharge from the neurosurgical unit. The modified Rankin Scale score was assessed at the 6-week and 6-month follow-ups. Haematoma recurrence requiring reoperation was recorded at the time of reoperation. Dates of death were obtained at the 6-month mark through the Finnish population register (which was available for all patients). Data regarding sex were obtained from the Finnish social security number. We did not collect data on ethnicity because Finland has a homogeneous population and Statistics Finland does not produce statistics on ethnicity.19  

# Outcomes  

Our primary outcome for efficacy was reoperation for a symptomatic, recurrent chronic subdural haematoma on the ipsilateral side within 6 months of the initial surgery, measured in both the intention-to-treat and perprotocol population. The decision to proceed to reoperation was made by the neurosurgeon on call and based on radiological findings of chronic subdural haematoma recurrence with symptoms attributable to the chronic subdural haematoma. Reoperations included subdural irrigation and the placement of a subdural drain.  

Secondary outcomes were 6-month functional outcome, assessed by use of the modified Rankin Scale (mRS; where a score of 0 indicates no symptoms and 6 indicates death, dichotomised as favourable functional outcome if mRS score was 0–3 and as unfavourable functional outcome if $\mathrm{mRS}$ score was 4–6); 6-month allcause mortality; duration of surgery; length of hospital stay in the neurosurgical unit; need for further care; postoperative haematoma width at 6 weeks; and minor, severe, and procedure-related adverse events during the 6-month follow-up period. Although haematoma volume at 2 months was included in the protocol as a secondary outcome, we report haematoma width at 6 weeks (time of postoperative head CT) instead to increase comparability with data from previous trials.6,13,20–22 mRS score and allcause mortality were assessed for participants in both the intention-to-treat and the per-protocol populations. Other secondary outcomes were assessed for participants in the intention-to-treat population. Trained research nurses and researchers who were masked to the group assignment assessed the mRS score according to a standardised algorithm.  

# Statistical analysis  

We conducted all statistical analyses and interpreted the data according to a previously registered plan.23 We estimated the sample size assuming a $9\cdot6\%$ reoperation rate (ie, primary outcome) on the basis of six randomised trials with at least 30 patients per treatment group included in a Cochrane review on burr-hole drainage for chronic subdural haematoma.24 This estimate yielded a non-inferiority margin of $9\cdot0\%$ . However, before commencing the study, the trial steering group deemed $9\cdot0\%$ to be an insufficiently stringent non-inferiority margin, lowering it to $7\cdot5\%$ . Accordingly, to achieve $80\%$ power with a one-sided $2\cdot5\%$ significance level to establish non-inferiority while assuming a non-adherence rate of $10\cdot0\%$ and a reoperation rate of $9\cdot6\%$ , we set our original sample size to 540 patients.  

After having recruited $80\%$ of our recruitment target (Jan 23, 2022), a blinded interim analysis restricted to adherence showed a non-adherence rate of $15\cdot7\%$ surpassing our initial estimate by 5·7 percentage points. Consequently, the data and safety monitoring board and steering group decided to increase the recruitment target to 578 patients (appendix pp 48–49).  

The primary analyses, done according to the intentionto-treat and per-protocol principles, estimated the percentage point difference in the reoperation rates between the groups. In the intention-to-treat analysis, the participants were included as they were randomly assigned to their treatment group. The per-protocol analysis included participants who adhered to the assigned treatment protocol with regards to the volume of irrigation used and the duration of subdural drainage. The difference in percentage points was calculated for the effect of no irrigation versus irrigation by use of marginal effects after fitting a logistic regression model adjusting for study site. We considered the primary intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses supportive of the non-inferiority of no irrigation if the upper limit of a two-sided $95\%$ CI (or equivalently, the upper limit of a one-sided $97\cdot5\%$ CI) of the difference in rates was lower than the non-inferiority margin $(7\cdot5\%)$ .  

However, since the goal of chronic subdural haematoma surgery is not only to prevent reoperations but also to improve survival and neurological function, there was a risk that we could have falsely claimed noninferiority if we based the claim solely on reoperation incidence. Accordingly, to guard against this risk, we agreed in advance on the following two interpretative commitments (appendix pp 52, 66–69): non-inferiority of no irrigation versus irrigation could only be claimed if both the intention-to-treat and the per-protocol analyses of the primary outcome supported noninferiority and there were no relevant between-group differences in the proportion of patients with mRS score of $0{-}3\nus4{-}6$ and mortality rate at 6 months.25,26 We used the same statistical method as for the primary analysis, adjusting for study site. The between-group differences were compared by use of two-sided $85\%$ CIs and $95\%$ CIs.

From Jan 1, 2020, to Aug 17, 2022, a total of 1644 patients were screened for eligibility and 589 $(36\%)$ were randomly assigned to a treatment group and treated at five centres in Finland (figure 1; appendix p 101). For the per-protocol analysis, 48 patients in the group receiving irrigation and 51 patients in the group not receiving irrigation were excluded due to non-adherence to the trial protocol. There were no missing data for the primary outcome. The mRS at 6 months was available for 568 $(96\%)$ of 589 patients in the intention-to-treat analysis and for 474 $(97\%)$ of 490 patients in the per-protocol analysis. Mortality data were available for all patients.

# Discussion  

In this nationwide, multicentre, randomised, controlled, non-inferiority trial in adults with symptomatic chronic subdural haematoma, the $95\%$ CI for the difference in the proportion of reoperations within 6 months of the initial surgery between the group that did not receive irrigation versus the group that received irrigation ranged from 0·2 to 11·7 in the intention-to-treat population and from $-0\cdot1$ to $12\cdot1$ in the per-protocol population. Because the upper limit was above our prespecified non-inferiority margin of $7\cdot5\%$ , we could not conclude non-inferiority of burr-hole drainage without irrigation. However, considering that we observed a lower proportion of reoperations in the group that received irrigation than in the group that did not receive irrigation, coupled with no significant between-group differences in functional outcome, mortality, or adverse events at 6 months, the trial findings favour the use of subdural irrigation.  

Although the benefit of the subdural drain in burr-hole surgery for chronic subdural haematomas is established,11,24 and its use has now become commonplace, the question of whether drain usage renders subdural irrigation redundant remained unresolved. In fact, the existing evidence on the benefits and downsides of irrigation, albeit observational by nature, is highly conflicting.27 To resolve the existing uncertainty regarding the use of irrigation, we chose a non-inferiority design to test the following hypothesis: omission of subdural irrigation could not only shorten the surgery but also eliminate the alleged adverse events related to irrigation itself, and could do so without compromising treatment success or safety. Although the omission of irrigation did shorten the median operation time by a mere 4 min, it did not translate into any clinically relevant benefits. On the contrary, the group that did not receive irrigation consistently showed higher reoperation rates than the irrigation group.  

Choosing the most appropriate outcome to determine the therapeutic effect of irrigation in burr-hole drainage surgery posed a challenge. Most trials studying chronic subdural haematoma surgery have used either reoperation rate or functional outcome as the primary outcome.6,11,13,28 We realised that comprehensive assessment of the therapeutic effect of irrigation would require more than one outcome. Therefore, we established resolute interpretative commitments, in advance, for three outcomes: between-group differences in the rates of reoperation (ie, the primary outcome), functional outcome, and mortality.  

In terms of our primary outcome, we identified an overall reoperation rate of $15\cdot4\%$ , which aligns with the rates of reoperations reported in other randomised controlled trials (from $6\cdot0\%$ to $23\cdot0\%)$ .6,11,13,20–22,28,29 Having said that, the Swedish SIC! trial,13 which assessed the influence of irrigation fluid temperature on reoperation rates and was conducted under geographical and demographic conditions similar to those of our trial, reported that 16 $(6\%)$ of 264 patients required reoperation in the group with irrigation fluid at body temperature. In our irrigation group, in which the fluid was also at body temperature, the rate of reoperations was $12\cdot6\%$ in the intention-to-treat group. Although it is possible that our two-fold higher reoperation rate is attributable to the difference in the volume of irrigation fluid used (ie, mean volume of $1717\mathrm{mL}$ [SD 790] in SIC! and $431\mathrm{mL}$ [270] in this trial), we suspect that the difference is related primarily to our routine use of postoperative CT at 6 weeks. Routine use of postoperative CT has been shown to increase the risk of reoperation by $50\%$ , probably because incidental findings of residual haematoma on CT might trigger surgeons to reoperate on patients even with minor symptoms.21  

We identified no significant between-group differences in the rates of unfavourable functional outcome or mortality. Also, the rates of unfavourable functional outcome $(12\cdot9\%)$ and mortality $(6\cdot6\%)$ noted in our trial are in line with the rates reported in other trials (rates of unfavourable functional outcome range from $12\cdot9\%$ to $14\cdot4\%_{,}^{.6,28}$ mortality rates range from $4\cdot0\%$ to $6\cdot9\%$ ).6,13,28  

The trial has some limitations. First, the observed reoperation rate was higher than the reoperation rate used for the sample size calculation ( $15\cdot5\%$ vs $9\cdot6\%$ . Second, since only 589 of the 1644 patients that we screened were assigned to either burr-hole drainage with irrigation or burr-hole drainage without irrigation, we understand that concerns about the generalisability of our findings might arise. However, the age and sex distribution of patients who were assigned to a treatment group were similar to those who were screened but not recruited (appendix p 101).  

In this nationwide, multicentre, randomised, controlled trial in symptomatic patients undergoing burr-hole drainage, we identified that estimates of the rate of reoperations within 6 months after initial surgery in the group that were assigned to receive no irrigation versus the group assigned to receive irrigation ranged from 0·2 to 11·7 percentage points higher (based on the $95\%$ CI). Translating these estimates into relative risks, in the worst-case scenario (ie, upper end of the CI), no irrigation would result in an almost three-fold increased risk of reoperation versus irrigation, and conversely, in the best-case scenario (ie, lower end of the CI), no irrigation would carry a 1·01 times increased risk of reoperation versus irrigation. Given that irrigation seems to be beneficial, a conversion of our estimates indicates that 17·5 patients (range $8\cdot5\mathrm{-}500\cdot0$ , calculated from the upper and lower limits of the $95\%$ CI for the difference in reoperation rates) need to be treated with irrigation to avoid one reoperation. Furthermore, there were no differences in the functional outcome or mortality between patients treated with or without irrigation. Thus, in contrast to our hypothesis of noninferiority of no irrigation versus irrigation, the results of this trial favour the use of subdural irrigation.
