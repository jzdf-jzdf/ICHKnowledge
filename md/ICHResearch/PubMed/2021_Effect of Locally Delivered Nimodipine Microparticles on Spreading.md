# Effect of Locally Delivered Nimodipine Microparticles on Spreading Depolarization in Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage  

Andrew P. Carlson $1^{*}\oplus$ Amal Alchbli1, Daniel Hänggi2, R. Loch Macdonald3,4,5 and C. William Shuttleworth6  

2020 Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature and Neurocritical Care Society  

# Abstract  

Background:  Recurrent spreading depolarizations (SDs) occur in patients after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH), resulting in metabolic stress to brain. These events are closely associated with delayed cerebral ischemia. Preclinical data suggest that the beneficial effect of nimodipine demonstrated in clinical trials may be related to inhibition of SD rather than limitation of large artery vasospasm.  

Methods:  Subjects enrolled in a phase 3 trial of intraventricularly delivered, sustained-release nimodipine (EG-1962) versus standard of care oral nimodipine (NEWTON 2) who required surgical clipping had subdural strip electrodes implanted for monitoring of SD. SD was then scored blinded to NEWTON 2 allocation.  

Results:  Five subjects underwent electrocorticography monitoring of SD. Three of five patients had SD. There were fewer SDs, a lower rate of SD, and shorter depression durations in subjects treated with EG-1962 compared to standard of care. Outcomes were worse in the standard of care group, though there were baseline imbalances.  

Conclusions:  These results are consistent with a beneficial effect of locally delivered nimodipine (EG-1962) on SD after aSAH in more severely injured patients who are at risk of delayed cerebral ischemia related to SD. Larger studies are warranted to test this effect.  

Keywords:  Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, Delayed cerebral ischemia, Nimodipine, Spreading depolarization, Vasospasm  

# Introduction  

The mechanism of delayed injury after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) has been recently recognized to be more complex than explained by large artery “vasospasm” alone, and so the term “delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI)” has gained favor. Evidence for this is that DCI can occur in the absence of large vessel vasospasm [1] and that only a small proportion of patients with angiographic vasospasm develop DCI. Furthermore, clinical trials have yielded seemingly contradictory results, where nimodipine has demonstrated efficacy in improving outcome without significantly affecting angiographic vasospasm [2] and clazosentan has demonstrated improvement in vasospasm without convincing clinical benefit [3].  

Mechanistic data have suggested that these results may be consistent with the hypothesis that massive waves of previously undetectable spreading depolarization (SD) may be one of the central mechanisms of ischemic progression in these patients. SD occurs frequently after aSAH and has been closely associated with time-locked spreading ischemia, DCI, and ischemic neurological deficits [4]. Several converging lines of preclinical investigation have confirmed potentially beneficial roles of the calcium channel antagonist nimodipine on SD [5–7].  

Given the ubiquity of L-type calcium channels across multiple cell types, it is possible that the beneficial effects of nimodipine could be related to synergistic effects from neuronal, glial, and vascular pathways [8].  

Systemic nimodipine dosing is limited by deleterious effects for example systemic hypotension and concentrations delivered to vulnerable brain may be very low [9]. For that reason, an intraventricularly delivered, sustained release formulation of nimodipine was developed and tested in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage [10]. While the phase 1/2a study showed promising improvement in outcome compared to oral nimodipine [10], a phase 3 study did not demonstrate an overall benefit (Carlson, accepted in Stroke). During this phase 3 study (NEWTON 2), we co-enrolled patients who underwent surgical clipping and placement of ECoG electrodes for SD monitoring. The purpose was to determine the effect of locally delivered nimodipine on SD.  

# Methods  

In all patients requiring surgical clipping, prospective consent and enrollment were sought for an observational study of the occurrence of SD after aSAH (UNM HRPO 10-159). At the time of surgery, a $1\times6$ platinum subdural electrode (Auragen, Integra LifeSciences: Plainsboro, NJ) was placed overlying potentially vulnerable brain (for example, frontal lobe after anterior communicating artery rupture and temporal lobe after middle cerebral artery aneurysm rupture) [11, 12]. Electrocorticography (ECoG) was then recorded using a full-spectrum DC amplifier (Moberg component neuromonitoring system: Ambler, PA) [12]. ECoG was reviewed clinically for seizures and full scalp electroencephalogram placed if clinically indicated (such as unexplained neurological deterioration or seizure-like activity). No analysis or intervention for SD was performed during the monitoring period. After 7–10 days, the electrode strip was pulled out at the bedside. ECoG was then exported to LabChart (AD Instruments: Colorado Springs, CO) and scored for SD using standard criteria [11] of slow DC shifts with associated suppression of high-frequency activity.  

After surgery, all subjects were screened for enrollment in a study of the effect of locally delivered, sustainedrelease nimodipine microparticles versus standard of care oral nimodipine (NEWTON 2) [13] (Carlson et al., accepted in Stroke). Briefly, this was a double blind, double dummy randomized controlled trial of the clinical effectiveness of a single dose of intraventricular sustained-release nimodipine (EG-1962, $600~\mathrm{mg}$ nimodipine) (with oral placebo) versus standard of care oral nimodipine (with intraventricular placebo). Subjects were required to receive EG-1962 or placebo intraventricular injection within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ of SAH.  

All SD data were scored and compiled prior to breaking the blinding of allocation to ensure complete blinding of analysis. Overall SD occurrence and frequency per subject were recorded as well as SD frequency, rate (number per overall hours of good recording/artifact free times), and average duration of depression of high frequency ECoG (a measure of the degree of damaging effect and ability to recover from SD [11]) after administration of intraventricular EG-1962 or placebo. Due to small numbers, only descriptive analysis was performed. Data were assessed overall and during the time interval after dosing of intraventricular EG-1962 or placebo and expressed as raw and mean values.  

# Results  

During the NEWTON 2 enrollment period of July 2016– March 2018, 84 subjects were screened and 10 were consented, with 9 ultimately being enrolled at our center. Screen failure patients did not meet the inclusion criteria for the parent study, typically due to inability to find a legally authorized representative, World Federation of Neurosurgeons Scale (WFNS) 1 or 5, delayed presentation, or lack of saccular aneurysm. Four were treated with coiling, and five were ultimately enrolled into both the SD monitoring protocol and NEWTON 2 (all clipped subjects). NEWTON 2 was halted in March of 2018 after the independent data monitoring committee found a low likelihood of achieving the primary outcome. Among the five co-enrolled patients, three received EG-1962 and 2 received standard of care oral nimodipine (Table 1). Baseline WFNS of the EG-1962 subjects were 2, 2, and 3, while the standard of care subjects were 3 and 4. One adverse event of hypotension was reported, which occurred in one of the oral subjects (defined in NEWTON 2 as a decrease in systolic blood pressure $\mathrm{(SBP)}{>}20\mathrm{mmHg}$ or diastolic blood pressure $>10\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}.$ , or a $\mathrm{SBP}\le90\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ , confirmed by 3 consecutive readings and requiring medical treatment). Both oral subjects had SD $\scriptstyle{n=9}$ and 24), while only one out of three EG-1962 subjects had SD $(n=6)$ . Monitoring duration ranged from 116 to $352\mathrm{~h~}$ . Comparing the time after dosing of either EG-1962 or placebo, there were more raw SDs, higher rate of SD, and longer associated ECoG depression in the oral group compared to the EG-1962 group (Fig. 1). Example traces demonstrating these trends are shown in Fig. 2. Ninetyday clinical outcomes were also worse in both oral subjects (eGOS 2[vegetative] and 1[death]) compared to the EG-1962 subjects (eGOS 3[lower severe disability], 3, and 4[upper severe disability]); however, the oral subjects had baseline worse WFNS scores. Two patients had severe angiographic vasospasm requiring intra-arterial therapy, one in each group. The other three patients had no significant vasospasm.

# Discussion  

These data support the hypothesis that nimodipine may exert beneficial effects via SD-related mechanisms. We observed not only a lower rate of SD, but importantly, a shorter duration of ECoG depression in patients receiving EG-1962. These data also are consistent with significant preclinical data showing that nimodipine inhibits SD and improves hemodynamic response, resulting in shorter depression of ECoG due to decreased metabolic stress [5–7]. Dreier et  al. [5] demonstrated that the inverse hemodynamic response (spreading ischemia) that occurs with SD in aSAH-like conditions is effectively reversed into a normal hyperemic response with nimodipine. Dietz et  al. [6] demonstrated a neuronal mechanism of action in a brain slice model of metabolic compromise (ouabain), where SD occurrence was nearly completely suppressed with nimodipine. Finally, Szabo et al. [7] demonstrated both SD attenuation and an augmented local hyperemic response to somatosensory stimulation in an in  vivo global ischemia model (bilateral common carotid occlusion). We recently summarized the myriad roles of nimodipine across various cell types, including possible mechanisms of action [8]. In addition to vascular smooth muscle and neuronal effects, nimodipine may have effects on astrocytes, pericytes, or other cell types as well [8].  

Local delivery of nimodipine or nicardipine has been studied to try to avoid hypotension with systemic administration; however, incomplete understanding of the mechanism of action on the tissue physiology may have limited such investigations. Placement of nicardipine pellets along large vessels exposed at surgery seemed to be very promising in terms of alleviating local vasospasm [14]; however, SD and associated DCI still were found to occur in these subjects [1]. Since the distribution and pharmacokinetics of these implants are poorly understood, the tissue and distal vascular concentrations may be inadequate to target SD and local tissue response to SD. Similarly, intra-arterial infusions may deliver higher concentrations to the arteries, but may suffer from the same lack of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) penetration as oral or intravenous administration. Intraventricular nicardipine has also been proposed, but initial results have been disappointing and pharmacokinetics are not well understood [15].

EG-1962 sought to overcome previous problems of administration and has been found to have dramatically higher CSF concentrations of nimodipine with similar to slightly lower plasma concentrations compared to oral delivery [9]. The phase 1/2a open-label dose-finding study demonstrated a promising benefit on clinical outcomes [10]; however, the phase 3 trial did not demonstrate overall superiority (Carlson et al., Single-Dose Intraventricular Nimodipine Microparticles versus Oral Nimodipine for Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage, Stroke in Press 2020). Several explanations have been proposed, and prespecified subgroup analysis demonstrated clinical benefit in WFNS grade 3 and 4 subjects, arguing that the overall effect was diluted by a neutral or deleterious effect in WFNS 2 subjects (who would be expected to be at low risk for DCI and SD). Given these promising subgroup analyses and our supportive mechanistic data, there is still a role for studies of EG-1962 as an SD targeting therapy, possibly in high-risk subjects or other conditions such as traumatic brain injury or stroke where systemic hypotension may limit effective dosing. These data also emphasize that angiographic vasospasm may not be the most important factor determining outcome given that two of our subjects (one in the standard of care [04] and one in the EG-1962 [01] groups) developed severe angiographic vasospasm requiring intra-arterial therapy.  

In addition to the future study of EG-1962, other exciting delivery strategies are being developed. The use of nimodipine linked pH-dependent nanoparticles [16] represents an exciting, novel approach, where nimodipine could theoretically be delivered only to acidotic tissue in the core and penumbra of ischemia, where the damaging effects of SD are most severe. Such a strategy should be developed and tested further.  

The current study is limited in its scope due to very small numbers and baseline imbalance, so should only be viewed as “proof of concept” that the effect hypothesized from preclinical models on SD is supported by these randomized data. We could not control baseline characteristics in this very small cohort with SD monitoring and outcomes trended similarly to baseline characteristics. Even with these small numbers, it is reassuring that predicted effects on SD and clinical outcome in patients with local nimodipine delivery trend toward a protective effect.  

# Conclusion/Summary  

Patients receiving locally delivered, sustained-release nimodipine had fewer SD and shorter duration of ECoG depression, consistent with preclinical data that demonstrate the mechanism of nimodipine working through SD-dependent mechanisms of ischemia. Larger clinical trials are needed to confirm a clinically relevant effect.
