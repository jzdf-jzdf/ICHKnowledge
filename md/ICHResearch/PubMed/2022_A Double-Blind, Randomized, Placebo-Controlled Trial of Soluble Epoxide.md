# A Double‑Blind, Randomized, Placebo‑Controlled Trial of Soluble Epoxide Hydrolase Inhibition in Patients with Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage  

Ross P Martini1,2, Dominic Siler3, Justin Cetas3, Nabil J. Alkayed2,4, Elyse Allen2,4 and Miriam M Treggiar  

$\circledcirc$ 2021 Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature and Neurocritical Care Society  

# Abstract  

Background:  Epoxyeicosatrienoates (EETs) are endogenous regulators of neuroinflammation and cerebral blood flow. Their metabolism to dihydroxyeicosatrienoates (DHETs) is catalyzed by soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH). After subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), EETs’ pathway amplification may be a therapeutic target for the prevention of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI). We conducted a double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase Ib randomized trial of GSK2256294, a pharmacologic inhibitor of sEH, to evaluate the safety profile and to assess biomarkers of neurovascular inflammation in patients with aneurysmal SAH.  

Methods:  Patients were randomly assigned to receive 10 mg of GSK2256294 or a placebo treatment once daily for 10 days, beginning within 72 hours after aneurysm rupture. The primary study end point was safety. Secondary end points included serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) EETs-to-DHETs ratio, cytokine levels, and serum endothelial injury biomarkers, measured at day 7 and day 10 after SAH. Tertiary end points included neurologic status, disposition, length of stay, incidence of DCI, and mortality; these were assessed at hospital discharge and at 90 days.  

Results:  Ten patients received GSK2256294 and nine patients received a placebo. There were no adverse events related to the study drug. GSK2256294 administration resulted in a significant increase in the EET/DHET ratio at day 7 and day 10 in serum, but not in the CSF. There was a trend for decreased CSF inflammatory cytokines following GSK2256294 administration, but this did not reach statistical significance.  

Conclusions:  GSK2256294 administration was safe and well tolerated in critically ill patients with SAH, producing an increase in serum EETs and the EET-to-DHET ratio. Our findings support future studies in a larger population to evaluate the role of sEH inhibition in the prevention of DCI after SAH and other forms of brain injury and inflammatory conditions.

# Introduction  

Delayed ischemia (DCI) is a disabling and life-threatening complication of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) characterized by blood–brain barrier (BBB) breakdown, neurovascular inflammation, and impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation resulting in abnormal cerebral blood flow. The clinical manifestations of DCI most commonly occur in the first 14 days after aneurysm rupture. DCI often has an insidious presentation and can be detected as an angiographic vessel caliber reduction, an increased transcranial Doppler artery flow velocity, or an acute change in neurologic examination. However, it is also commonly identified radiologically as a new area of cerebral infarction or clinically as a persistent cognitive dysfunction among survivors. There are few effective pharmacologic or therapeutic strategies to prevent or treat DCI. Multiple clinical trials of novel therapeutics for DCI prevention over the past three decades have failed to demonstrate efficacy in improving outcomes [1, 2].  

Epoxyeicosatrienoates (EETs) are arachidonic acid metabolites and endothelial lipid signaling molecules with vasodilator and anti-inflammatory properties. EETs have multiple sites of action, including the endothelium, where they suppress inflammatory responses, and vascular smooth muscle, where they promote vasodilation. More broadly, EETs regulate cell-to-cell communication throughout the neurovascular unit [3, 4]. EETs are synthesized from arachidonic acid by cytochrome P450 epoxygenases and their biological action is primarily terminated via metabolism to dihydroxyeicosatrienoates (DHETs) by the enzyme soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH) [5, 6]. EETs have been implicated in neurovascular inflammation and cerebral blood flow regulation after brain injury, including in SAH [7].  

The inhibition or genetic deletion of sEH increases the bioavailability of EETs, decreases infarct size and neuroinflammation, and improves neurobehavioral performance after experimental SAH and ischemic stroke in animal models [8–10]. Human genetic polymorphisms linked to increased sEH activity and decreased EETs availability are associated with increased mortality and risk of stroke after SAH [11, 12].  

Inhibition of sEH as a potential therapy for DCI has not been evaluated in human patients with SAH. GSK2256294 is a sEH inhibitor previously demonstrated to be safe in healthy patients and to efficiently inhibit EETs metabolism [13, 14]. The drug has not previously been administered to patients with brain injury, and no studies have evaluated in  vivo EET concentrations after drug administration. Furthermore, it is unknown how interventions to inhibit the metabolism of EETs may influence neurovascular and inflammatory mechanisms of injury after SAH.  

We conducted a phase Ib, double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trial of GSK2256294 in patients with aneurysmal SAH. The primary study aim was to demonstrate safety in patients with SAH. Secondary aims were to measure endogenous EETs levels in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), assess biomarkers of neurovascular inflammation, and observe clinical outcomes to inform the design of a larger trial.  

# Methods  

# Study Population and Enrollment  

The study was a phase Ib, double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized trial involving patients admitted to the Neurosciences Intensive Care Unit (NSICU) at Oregon Health and Science University, a comprehensive stroke center certified by the American Heart Association and Joint Commission for Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations, with a catchment area including the state of Oregon, Southwest Washington, and Northern California. Approximately 80–100 patients with aneurysmal SAH are admitted each year. The study protocol was approved by the Oregon Health and Science University Institutional Review Board, and administration of the study drug was conducted under an Investigational New Drug Application approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration. The enrollment period began on 5/1/2018 and concluded on 4/4/2019.  

The inclusion criteria were as follows: age ${>}18$ years, evidence of SAH at head cerebral tomography (CT), digital subtraction cerebral angiography or CT angiogram documenting the presence of a cerebral aneurysm, and presence of an external ventricular drain (EVD). The exclusion criteria were the following: symptom onset compatible with $\mathrm{SAH}>3$ days prior to admission to NSICU, QTc prolongation $>500\mathrm{ms}$ aspartate aminotransferase or alanine aminotransferase ${>}2$ times the normal reference range, history of administration of inducers/inhibitors of CYP3A4 (ritonavir, indinavir, nelfinavir, saquinavir, clarithromycin, telithromycin, chloramphenicol, ketoconazole, itraconazole, nefazodone, cobicistat, or enzalutamide), current or suspected pregnancy, preexisting severe neurologic deficit or condition, chronic renal failure requiring dialysis, severe terminal disease with life expectancy ${<}6$ months, inability to read or understand written or spoken English or Spanish, and refusal of informed consent.  

# End Points  

The primary study end point was safety, assessed objectively by a comparison between the drug and placebo groups of all relevant variables, including vital signs, laboratory values, adverse events, serious adverse events, disease-related events, and mortality.  

Secondary end points included the assessment of the pharmacodynamics of GSK2256294. Multiple eicosanoids isomers are metabolized by sEH, but 14,15-EET is the preferred substrate for sEH and is strongly associated with vasospasm and DCI [8, 15].  

The tertiary end points explored clinical outcomes. Across study drug and placebo groups, we compared incidence of DCI, angiographic vasospasm, and new stroke at hospital discharge, in addition to hospital length of stay, hospital discharge modified Rankin Score (mRS), hospital discharge disposition, 90-day follow-up mRS, and 90-day follow-up Glasgow Outcome Scale extended (GOSE).  

# Randomization and Blinding  

The study team screened patients daily for eligibility. Participants meeting the eligibility criteria and none of the exclusion criteria were randomly assigned to receive 10 mg GSK2256294 orally or a matching placebo daily for 10 days. A computer-generated random sequence based on an unrestricted “fair-coin” randomization procedure was prepared by an independent biostatistician and it was maintained by the investigational pharmacy service, who ensured the integrity of the randomization and the blinding. Unblinding occurred after all patients had completed follow-up and data collection was completed.  

# Study Drug Administration  

In prior phase Ib trials in which repeat dosing of GSK2256294 was used, enzyme inhibition was sustained at greater than $95\%$ for 24 hours at a dose of $10~\mathrm{mg}$ [13]. Patients assigned to the GSK2256294 group received $10\mathrm{-mg}$ capsules of the study drug and patients enrolled in the placebo group received identical placebo capsules daily. Patients with intact swallowing received the capsule formulation orally. Patients not able to swallow received the contents of the capsule suspended in normal saline, administered via orogastric or nasogastric enteral access by the bedside nurse. A 10-day administration schedule was chosen because this represents the highest risk time period for development of neuroinflammatory changes related to DCI.  

The first dose of study drug or placebo was administered after informed consent, randomization, and collection of baseline laboratory samples. Subsequent doses were administered daily for a total treatment course of 10 days. Patients who were able to swallow received the capsule formulation orally. Patients unable to swallow received the contents of the capsule suspended in normal saline, administered via orogastric or nasogastric enteral access. Study drug administration was recorded in the electronic medical record and the administration was verified daily.  

# Data Collection Baseline  

Baseline patient evaluation and testing was performed in conjunction with routine NSICU care and monitoring. In addition to demographic data, we collected the Charlson Comorbidity Index [16], characteristics of SAH severity and neurological impairment, admission labs and vital signs, baseline electrocardiogram and liver function tests, and home medications.  

# Daily NSICU Data Collection  

Patients were continuously monitored in the NSICU during the 10-day period of study drug administration. Vital signs were recorded at 4-hour intervals. We assessed the QTc interval and monitored the presence of any cardiac arrhythmias daily. Total fluid balance was recorded daily, as well as select laboratory data (serum sodium, blood glucose, BUN, creatinine, hematocrit, and peripheral white blood cell count). We also determined the daily Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score, transcranial Doppler (TCD) data, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). If the patients were alert and cooperative, headache severity was also obtained.  

# Adverse Event Monitoring and Reporting  

Adverse events were monitored on an ongoing basis during the period of study drug administration, up to completion of the 90-day follow-up. We monitored for any adverse events, as well as disease-specific adverse events related to SAH. Seizures, new onset myocardial dysfunction, NSTEMI/STEMI, hospital acquired infections, and thromboembolic events were noted, along with findings of cerebral angiography and interventions performed during the procedure. Adverse events were identified and reviewed for seriousness, severity, and relatedness to the study intervention by a qualified independent safety monitor. The study protocol included standard mandatory reporting of serious and study drug–related adverse events to the Food and Drug Administration, but none of these events occurred.  

# Clinical Outcomes  

Clinical end points were assessed at hospital discharge or 28 days (whichever occurred first) and at 90 days after randomization. We defined DCI as a change in clinical examination (the development of new focal neurological signs or decreased level of consciousness that resulted in a decrease in the GCS score of 2 points, lasting for at least 1 hour, and was not attributable to infection, changes in sedation, or nonischemic cerebral pathology such as acute hydrocephalus), accompanied by evidence of decreased cerebral blood flow (TCD elevation, angiographic vasospasm, or new stroke at discharge) [17]. The last head CT prior to hospital discharge was reviewed blindly by the principal investigator for any new areas of new cerebral infarction. We defined a new cerebral infarction as one that was not present on imaging between 24 and 48 hours after aneurysm repair and not attributable to other causes such as surgical clipping or endovascular treatment. Hypodensities resulting from extraventricular drains or residual intraparencyhmal hematomas were not considered new strokes. The discharge disposition and mRS were also recorded at discharge. The GOSE score, mRS score, and mortality were collected from a patient or surrogate phone call at 90 days following randomization by a member of the research team using a standardized questionnaire. We determined angiographic vasospasm based on the procedure report for cerebral angiograms as the presence of mild, moderate, or severe vasospasm.  

# Pharmacodynamics and Biomarker Data Collection and Analysis  

Baseline blood and CSF samples were collected prior to study drug administration and repeated on study days 7 and 10. Blood samples were collected by the bedside nurse and CSF samples were collected by medical staff, from proximal EVD access. If the EVD was planned to be removed as part of clinical decision making prior to a scheduled sample collection, CSF was obtained prior to removal of the drain. Blood and CSF samples were processed and batched for subsequent determination of eicosanoids, inflammatory cytokines, and endothelial injury biomarkers.  

# Eicosanoid Profile Assay  

CSF collected from the EVD was placed immediately on ice and spun in a chilled centrifuge at 10,000 RPM for 10 minutes. Whole blood was collected in serum separator tubes, placed immediately on ice, and spun in a chilled centrifuge at 2,200 RPM for 15 minutes. The supernatant CSF and serum were collected and $10\upmu\mathrm{l/mL}$ of $1\%$ butylated hydroxytoluene was added as an antioxidant agent to prevent EETs oxidation. Samples were stored at $-80^{\circ}C$ in amber glass vials until analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry.  

# Inflammatory Cytokine and Endothelial Injury Biomarker Analysis  

Serum and CSF samples for cytokine and endothelial injury biomarker analysis were stored at $-80~^{\circ}C$ in polypropylene vials and processed as a single batch using commercially available Luminex multiplex immunoassay kits, according to the manufacturer recommended protocol (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). The endothelial injury biomarkers of interest were the following: Intercellular adhesion molecule 1, vascular cell adhesion molecule, platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1, E-selectin, and P-selectin. The CSF cytokines of interest included interferon- $\upgamma$ (IFN-γ), Interleukin $1\upbeta$ (IL-1β),  

Interleukin 6 (IL-6), Interleukin 8 (IL-8), and Tumor Necrosis Factor- $\upalpha$ (TNF- $\cdot\alpha$ ).  

# Statistical Methods  

Data are presented descriptively stratified by group as mean and standard deviation or frequency distribution. Safety was assessed descriptively by using summary tables of all relevant parameters, including vital signs, laboratory values, and listings of adverse events, serious adverse events, disease-related events, and mortality, and reported with $95\%$ confidence interval. In bivariate analyses, we compared groups using two-sample Student’s $t$ -test for continuous variables and $\chi^{2}$ statistics for categorical variables. We compared serum and CSF concentrations of 14,15-EET and 14,15-DHET at baseline and on study days 7 and 10. Additionally, we compared inflammatory cytokines in the CSF and endothelial injury biomarkers in serum at baseline and on study days 7 and 10.  

A two-sided α level of 0.05 was required for statistical significance. The total sample size was selected a priori based on the need to evaluate and describe the safety profile of the study drug in this patient population. All analyses were performed using the software Stata version 16.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX).  

# Results  

During the enrollment period, 47 patients were screened, 33 met eligibility criteria, and 20 underwent random assignment (Fig.  1). One patient who was randomly assigned to the placebo group withdrew consent prior to receiving the first dose of study drug. One patient in the placebo group had a severe rebleed of an unsecured aneurysm, and the family elected for transition to comfort care on study day 9, resulting in 9/10 doses of placebo received.  

# Demographics  

There were no differences in baseline demographic characteristics, liver function tests, QTc interval, or the Charlson Comorbidity Index between the GSK2256294 group and the placebo group (Table 1 and Supplementary Material Table  S1). Fewer patients in the GSK2256294 group were receiving antihypertensives and anticoagulants prior to admission compared with the placebo group. The modified Fisher grade, Hunt & Hess scale, and location and treatment of aneurysms were similar between the groups. Fewer patients in the GSK2256294  group were intubated prior to intensive care unit (ICU) admission compared with those in the placebo group $(20\%$ GSK2256294 vs. $44.4\%$ placebo).

# Biomarkers of Endothelial Injury and Neuroinflammation  

Serum biomarkers of endothelial injury were similar between the GSK2256294 group and placebo (Supplementary Material Table S2). CSF inflammatory cytokines levels comparing the GSK2256294 group and placebo are shown in Table 4. There were no significant differences in the inflammatory cytokine profile. However, there was a trend toward reduction of IFN- $\cdot\gamma,$ IL- $\boldsymbol{\cdot}1\upbeta$ , IL-8 and TNF- $\upalpha$ in the GSK2256294 group compared to placebo group.  

# Clinical Outcomes  

Clinical outcomes data are summarized in Table 5. There were no differences between the GSK2256294 and placebo groups in multiple measurement modalities for DCI, including maximum TCD Lindegaard ratio, incidence of angiographic vasospasm, or new stroke on follow-up imaging. The ICU length of stay and hospital length of stay were shorter in the GSK2256294 group compared with placebo. There were no differences between the groups in the functional outcome measures, including discharge mRS and GCS, or 90-day follow-up mRS, GOSE, or proportion of patients with GOSE ${>}4$ . However, there was a trend for higher discharge disposition to home in the GSK2256294 group compared with the placebo group.

# Discussion  

The objectives of this double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized trial were as follows: (1) to assess the safety of GSK2256294 administration in patients with SAH, (2) to characterize the pharmacodynamics of drug administration on EETs levels and biomarkers of neurovascular inflammation, and (3) to estimate clinical end points.  

# Safety  

Our results suggest that GSK2256294 administration is safe in patients with SAH. We did not record any serious adverse events that were unexpected or related to study drug administration. We also did not observe any differences in vital signs or laboratory values measured throughout the treatment course as part of safety monitoring (Supplementary Material), including the QTc interval or liver function tests. Healthy patients in prior phase I trials occasionally reported headache; we did not observe a significant difference in the worst headache score between groups, but our assessment was limited because many patients were not able to respond to questions. Contact dermatitis was also previously reported as an adverse event. One patient in the GSK2256294 group was noted to have lower extremity cellulitis on the first day after drug administration, but upon further investigation, the patient had symptoms of cellulitis prior to arrival at the hospital.  

Patients with SAH are at risk for a number of diseaserelated adverse events related to severe brain injury, neuroinflammatory consequences on other organ systems, dysregulation of immune responses, prolonged requirement for intensive care, and immobility. We observed a number of these disease-related events with similar frequency between the GSK2256294 group and the placebo group. In particular, there was a number of hospital-acquired infections in both groups, but there was no increased risk of infection in patients who received GSK2256294 vs. placebo. A similar proportion of patients in both groups required a ventriculoperitoneal shunt for persistent hydrocephalus.  

There were two patients in the GSK2256294 group who developed deep vein thrombosis compared with none in the placebo group. Whether this represents an actual increase in the risk of deep vein thrombosis rather than a random difference in this very small sample is unknown. No prior clinical evaluations of GSK2256294 have demonstrated an increase in thrombotic events, and no laboratory studies exist to suggest that sEH inhibition results in promotion of thrombotic pathways. In fact, EETs have been demonstrated to inhibit microvascular thrombosis and platelet aggregation and promote fibrinolysis [18, 19]. Taken together with prior evaluations in healthy patients, the safety profile of GSK2256294 is very reassuring.

# Pharmacodynamic Effects  

The second aim of the trial was to demonstrate the pharmacodynamic effects of GSK2256294 on inhibiting sEH and increasing bioavailable EETs. Prior efficacy studies of GSK2256294 relied upon combining serum samples of drug treated patients with exogenous deuterated EETs and measuring metabolism to byproduct DHETs. This method was previously chosen because epoxyeicosanoids are unstable lipid molecules that undergo both enzymatic and nonenzymatic metabolism, especially under oxidative and inflammatory conditions such as those observed in SAH. We chose to directly measure EETs and DHETs in the serum and CSF to characterize the endogenous action and differentiate between serum and CSF effects. We also specifically focused on the 14,15-EET regioisomer, because 14,15-EET has been most closely linked with the pathogenesis of DCI after SAH and is the most efficiently metabolized by sEH [3].  

GSK2256294 administration resulted in a significant and measurable increase in the serum concentration and bioavailability of 14,15-EET via decreased metabolism to 14,15-DHET. GSK2256294 does not cross the BBB, and in the CSF we measured no differences in the 14,15-EET/14,15-DHET ratios at any timepoint between the GSK2256294 and placebo groups. BBB disruption [20], which would allow GSK2256294 to exert CSF effects, but we found no evidence of this. Additionally, EETs are paracrine mediators of cell-cell communications, and as such exert their effects at the tissue level. Although we were able to measure differences in serum EETs between the GSK2256294 group and placebo group, the relevance of a serum measurement of a paracrine mediator that reaches its critical levels in the pericellular microenvironment is not clear.  

As part of the pharmacodynamic effects, we also explored downstream neuroinflammatory biomarkers and endothelial inflammation. Via paracrine actions, eicosanoids regulate vasomotor tone, inflammatory cell chemotaxis, BBB function, and neurovascular coupling, all of which are important factors in the pathogenesis of DCI [21]. We measured biomarkers of endothelial and vascular smooth muscle inflammatory signaling known to be regulated by EETs, to assess whether sEH inhibition could modify these pathways, and to provide estimates for planning a larger trial. We did not detect any differences in the serum biomarkers (Supplementary Material).  

We also measured downstream biomarkers of neuroinflammation, including expression of inflammatory cytokines in blood and CSF (most notably CSF TNF- $\upalpha$ , IL-6, and IL-8), which have been associated with vasospasm and DCI [22–24]. Although there was a trend for reduced serum neuroinflammatory biomarkers, we did not observe differences in CSF cytokine levels when comparing GSK2256294 with placebo administration. However, due to the early removal of the EVD in some patients, the sample for the CSF analysis was incomplete, preventing to draw definitive conclusions on the role of GSK2256294 on these biomarkers. There may also be important central nervous system (CNS) actions of EETs for which a drug that crosses the BBB to produce CNScompartment amplification would be clinically beneficial. Other inhibitors of sEH that may cross the BBB have been shown in animal models to decrease neuroinflammation [25]. Future investigation of this pathway, and future drug development, should focus on molecules that cross the BBB.  

# Clinical End Points  

We measured several clinical end points during hospital stay, at hospital discharge, and at 90 days after enrollment in the trial. The interpretation of any differences we observe in this sample is limited by the sample size; the primary use of the clinical data is for the planning and for the estimation of the power needed for a larger trial. For example, we observed that patients in the GSK2256294 group had significantly shorter ICU and hospital lengths of stay, and the GSK2256294 group was more likely to be discharged to home compared to placebo. At 90-day follow-up, there was an increased proportion of patients in the GSK2256294 group living at home when compared with placebo. One possible explanation for the apparent improved outcomes in the GSK2256294 group may be the imbalance in the severity of brain injury between the groups. Despite similar Hunt and Hess or modified Fisher grade, patients in the GSK2256294 group had higher GCS score at randomization when compared with the placebo, which may indicate that this group was less severely neurologically injured, and more likely to have a good recovery.  

# Limitations  

This trial was designed as a pilot study to evaluate safety, and therefore the interpretation of the results is limited in scope due its small sample size. A future phase II trial that will use a composite score of change from baseline to day 7 in CSF biomarkers of inflammation $(\mathrm{IFN-}\gamma,$ $\mathrm{IL}{-}1\upbeta$ , IL-6, IL-8, and TNF- $\upalpha$ ) as surrogate endpoint, will require a total sample of 120 patients, assuming a twosided $\upalpha$ level of 0.05 and at least $90\%$ power, using the rank-sum-type global statistical test to summarize treatment’s effect, as described by O’Brien [26]. There were additional relevant sample considerations based on the availability of the study drug. Although the compound remains available, the primary manufacturer is no longer supporting its development.  

# Conclusions  

We provide the first evidence to support the safety of sEH inhibition to modulate EETs-mediated neurovascular function and inflammation after SAH in humans. GSK2256294 is safe to administer in critically ill patients with SAH and increases serum EETs via effectively inhibiting sEH. GSK2256294 does not cross the BBB and does not increase CSF EETs concentration. Drug development of sEH inhibitors for SAH and other forms of vascular/ inflammatory brain injury should possibly focus on a molecule with more favorable CNS penetration. The downstream cerebrovascular and clinical effects of sEH inhibition on patients with SAH remain speculative.
