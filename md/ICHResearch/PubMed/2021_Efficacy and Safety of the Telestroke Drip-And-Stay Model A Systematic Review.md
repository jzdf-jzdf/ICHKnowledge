# Efficacy and Safety of the Telestroke Drip-And-Stay Model: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis  

Hena Waseem, MD,† Yasir A. Salih, $\mathrm{MPH,\ddagger}$ Charles P. Burney, MD,† Mark A. Abel, MD,† Natalie Riblet, ${\mathrm{MD},\ddagger}$ Anthony Kim, MD,§ and Nathaniel Robbins, MDk  

Objectives: To compare outcomes between two models of acute ischemic stroke care. Namely 1) “drip-and-stay”, i.e. IV tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) administered at a spoke hospital in a telestroke network, with the patient remaining at the spoke, versus 2) “drip-and-ship”, i.e. tPA administered at a spoke hospital with subsequent patient transfer to a hub hospital, and 3) “hub”, i.e. tPA and subsequent treatment at a hub hospital. Materials and Methods: We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis according to PRISMA guidelines. Literature searches of MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane from inception-October 2019 included randomized control trials and observational cohort studies comparing the drip-and-stay model to hub and drip-and-ship models. Outcomes of interest were functional independence (modified Rankin Scale $\leq1$ ), symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), mortality, and length of stay. Pooled effect estimates were calculated using a fixedeffects meta-analysis and random-effects Bayesian meta-analysis. Non-inferiority was calculated using a fixed-margin method. Results: Of 2806 unique records identified, 10 studies, totaling 4,164 patients, fulfilled the eligibility criteria. Meta-analysis found no significant difference in functional outcomes (mRS0-1) (6 studies, $\mathrm{RR}=1.09$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.981.22, $p=0.123)$ , sICH (8 studies, $\mathrm{RR}=0.98$ , $95\%C1$ 0.641.51, $p=0.942^{\cdot}$ ), or 90-day mortality (5 studies, $\mathrm{RR}=0.98\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.73\substack{-1.32,}$ , $p=0.911$ , respectively) between patients treated in a drip-and-stay model compared to patients treated in drip-and-ship or hub models. There was no significant heterogeneity in these outcomes. Drip-and-stay outcomes (mRS 01, sICH) were non-inferior when compared to the combined group. Conclusions: Our findings indicate that drip-and-stay is non-inferior to current models of drip-and-ship or hub stroke care, and may be as safe and as effective as either.

# Introduction  

Stroke is both a leading cause of disability and the fifth most common cause of death in the United States.1  

Prompt administration of IV tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) can prevent major disability in acute ischemic stroke (AIS), but the relative scarcity of comprehensive care facilities and neurologists with expertise in AIS care makes this challenging. By connecting multiple facilities in a “telestroke network,” patients presenting at a facility without a stroke specialist (a “spoke”) can be evaluated remotely for IV tPA by a specialist at a stroke center (a “hub”), receive IV tPA on-site, then be transferred to the hub (i.e. “drip-and-ship”).3,4 Door-to-needle time is reduced, saving neurons, and patients receive hub-level care after the intervention. There is enough evidence suggesting that drip-and-ship care produces outcomes equivalent to hub care5 that the American Heart Association now supports telestroke as a level 1 recommendation for stroke care.6  

A third model, dubbed “drip-and-stay”, has been proposed, whereby patients presenting at a spoke facility complete their entire hospital stay at the spoke. This model has the same advantages as drip-and-ship in lowering door-to-needle time, but also relieves stress on hub facilities with limited beds and overworked neurologists, better manages healthcare costs, improves finances of critical access hospitals (by keeping patients in-house), and allows patients and their families to remain near their homes.79 Despite these potential benefits, the drip-andstay model has not yet been widely adopted, and relatively little is known about outcomes compared to hub or drip-and-ship models.  

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to examine this question in detail, focusing on whether adults treated with IV-tPA for AIS in a drip-and-stay model experience non-inferior functional and safety outcomes when compared to the same population treated in drip-and-ship or hub models.  

# Methods  

# Review protocol  

Prior to initiation of the systematic review, a study protocol was drafted to define study eligibility criteria and data that would be collected for meta-analysis. The full protocol is available upon request. We adopted the standard methodology outlined by Cochrane10 for study selection. Methods and findings were reported according to PRISMA guidelines.11  

# Literature search  

The following electronic databases were searched: MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. Key words and exploded MeSH terms were used to create search strings of the topics “telemedicine,” and “stroke.” Search results were combined using Boolean operators $\mathrm{^{\prime\prime}AND^{\prime\prime}}$ and $\mathit{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}\mathrm{OR}^{\prime\prime}$ . Search was not limited based on publication date, study location, or language. Last search date was October 24, 2019. In addition to electronic databases, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov, The Stroke Trials Registry, The World Health Organization  

International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP), Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, and EU Clinical Trials Register to locate additional studies. Last search date of these other sources was October 28, 2019.  

# Eligibility criteria  

A study identified by our literature search was eligible for inclusion if it 1) was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) or non-randomized observational cohort study; 2) evaluated the efficacy and safety of AIS care in a telestroke network; 3) included one group of patients diagnosed with AIS and treated with IV-tPA at a spoke hospital without subsequent transfer to a hub hospital (drip-and-stay); and 4) compared the drip-and-stay group to a group of patients treated with IV-tPA at a hub hospital (hub) and/or patients treated with IV-tPA at a spoke hospital with completion of treatment at a hub hospital (drip-and-ship). Otherwise-eligible studies which did not distinguish between patients receiving IV-tPA alone versus some combination of IV-tPA and mechanical thrombectomy were excluded if $>5\%$ of the cohort received mechanical thrombectomy. Additional eligibility criteria included 5) reporting of at least one of two primary outcomes: a functional outcome or a safety outcome. The functional outcome recorded whether a patient was assigned a Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score $\leq1$ at 90 days and the safety outcome recorded the number of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhages (sICH). Secondary outcomes (90-day mortality, hospital length of stay (LOS), hospitalization complications, discharge conditions, and discharge prescription medications) did not influence selection.  

# Study selection  

After compiling results of the literature search, four non-blinded reviewers (MA, CB, YS, HW) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility using the software Rayyan.12 The reviewers were then divided into two teams of two to review, each team independently and in duplicate, full text articles of those studies that had passed the screening stage to determine final eligibility for inclusion. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion-based consensus amongst all four reviewers.  

# Data collection  

Four non-blinded reviewers (MA, CB, YS, HW) were divided into two teams of two to extract, each team independently and in duplicate, relevant data from the selected studies. Data extraction was accomplished using a standardized data collection form which included fields related to the methodology (study design, study type), study population (cohort size), patient demographics (age, sex), comorbidities, NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at presentation, hospitalization complications, discharge prescription medications, and outcomes (primary and secondary). Where possible, we attempted to obtain missing data by contacting corresponding authors. Discrepancies in data collection were resolved by discussion-based consensus amongst all four reviewers.  

# Assessment of risk of bias  

The Newcastle-Ottawa Tool for Cohort Studies was used to assess all included studies for risk of bias.13,14 Four non-blinded reviewers (MA, CB, YS, HW) were divided into two teams of two and used the instrument to assess, each team independently and in duplicate, the methodological quality of each included study. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion-based consensus amongst all four reviewers.  

# Meta-analysis  

For each included study, the number of patients exhibiting each outcome value was determined for both the exposure and comparison groups. The exposure group was always drip-and-stay, while the comparison group was either drip-and-ship or hub, depending on the study. A per-study risk ratio (RR) and corresponding $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) was computed for each dichotomous outcome, and a per-study standardized mean score difference (SMD) was computed for each continuous outcome. The fixed-effects inverse variance model was used to compute a pooled RR (resp. SMD) per outcome.15,16 A $z\cdot$ -test was computed for each pooled ${\mathrm{RR}},$ with a 2-tailed $p$ -value $<0.05$ considered statistically significant. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s $Q$ test, with $p$ -values $<0.1$ considered significant heterogeneity. Variation across studies attributable to heterogeneity was computed with the $I^{\wedge}2$ statistic, with $I^{\wedge}2>50\%$ considered substantial heterogeneity.17 During meta-analysis, dripand-ship and hub groups were treated as a single comparison group, allowing us to compute pooled RR and SMD values across all the included studies, which was not possible for any other comparison. This was secondary to prior research suggesting that drip-and-ship outcomes are not inferior to hub outcomes.5 Sensitivity analysis was performed for each outcome, using hub (which represents the presumed ideal scenario) as the comparison group instead of the combined drip-and-ship or hub group. Additional sensitivity analysis was performed by varying the definition of the functional primary outcome (functional independence defined as an mRS score $\leq2$ and an mRS score $\leq3$ ) and the 90-day mortality secondary outcome (in-hospital mortality, total mortality). A fixed-margin method was used to evaluate for non-inferiority of the drip-and-stay model, using a preserved fraction of $50\%$ . The non-inferiority margin was determined based on a prior, large scale systematic review and meta-analysis.18 Bayesian meta-analysis was performed as a confirmatory measure, with a random-effects model using the halfnormal distribution as a weakly-informative prior.19 Agreement between both meta-analyses was taken to strengthen evidence, while disagreement weakened it. We also intended to perform a meta-regression of NIHSS values to test for patient group selection bias and assess per-outcome publication bias using a funnel plot technique and Egger’s linear regression.20 All non-Bayesian statistical analyses were performed with the STATA SE software (version 15.1; StataCorp. 2017. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC), using the “metan” package.21 Bayesian meta-analysis was performed with R (R Core Team, 2020). R: a language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. URL https://www.R-project.org]) and RStudio (RStudio Team, 2020). RStudio: Integrated Development for R. RStudio, Inc., Boston, MA URL http://www.rstudio. com/) using the “bayesmeta” package.19  

# Results  

Description of studies  

# Results of search  

Electronic database search identified 3977 records, with an additional 372 records identified from other sources. After removing duplicates and ongoing studies, 2806 remaining records were reviewed for eligibility. Of these, 2564 were excluded through title and abstract screening, and an additional 232 were excluded on full text screening. The 10 remaining studies were included in the final analysis.2231 Complete search results and details of study selection and the search strategy are illustrated in Fig. 1.  

# Included study characteristics  

Characteristics of each included study are summarized in Table 1. All included studies were non-randomized observational cohort studies with a control group. Three studies were conducted outside of the United States. Four studies compared drip-and-stay patients to drip-and-ship patients (22, 26, 30, 31). Seven studies compared dripand-stay patients to hub patients.2329 In total, 4164 patients were identified. Mean age per cohort did not differ statistically between groups, ranging from 64.8 to 76.2 years in drip-and-stay; 63.1 to 70.8 years in drip-andship; and 67.6 to 72.1 years in hub groups.2231 The proportion of female patients ranged from $38.6\%$ to $60\%$ in drip-and-stay groups; $45.6\%$ to $56.2\%$ in drip-and-ship groups; and 36.4% to 55.5% in hub groups.2224,2631 Eight studies reported NIHSS across comparison groups .2224,2630 Door-to-needle time was reported as a mean in four studies,23,24,26,28 and as a median in three studies.22,24,30 Prevalence of hypertension ranged from $66.2\%$ to $81.3\%$ in drip-and-stay groups (6 studies reporting) $^{24,26,27,29-31},$ , from $72.4\%$ to $80.6\%$ in drip-andship groups (2 studies reporting),30,31 and from $73.4\%$ to $79.5\%$ in hub groups (3 studies reporting).24,27,29 Prevalence of dyslipidemia ranged from $36.8\%$ to $55.2\%$ in dripand-stay groups (5 studies reporting),24,26,27,30,31 from $35.3\%$ to $40\%$ in drip-and-ship groups (2 studies reporting) (30, 31), and from $44.5\%$ to $55.5\%$ in hub groups (2 studies reporting).24,27 Prevalence of diabetes mellitus ranged from $19.1\%$ to $31.8\%$ in drip-and-stay groups (6 studies reporting), $^{24,26,27,29-31}$ from $26.9\%$ to $33.6\%$ in drip-and-ship groups (2 studies reporting),30,31 and from $22.7\%$ to $24.2\%$ in hub groups (3 studies reporting).24,27,29 A single study reported smoking prevalence, recording $71\%$ for its drip-and-stay group, and $39.8\%$ for its dripand-ship group.30

# Primary outcomes  

Eight studies reported some form of mRS score. Three reported absolute mRS scores.22,23,26 Five reported mRS score as a dichotomous functional outcome, with various score ranges defining functionality, three studies used mRS $0{-}1,\dot{2}8{-}30$ two studies used mRS $0{-}2^{28,31}$ and one study used $\mathrm{mRS}0{-3.}^{27}$ Seven of the eight studies scored mRS at 90-day follow-up.22,23,26,2831 One scored mRS again at 6 months(29), and one scored only at discharge.27 Nine studies reported sICH rates.2226,2831  

# Secondary outcomes  

Nine studies reported some form of mortality,2224,2631 with three reporting in-hospital mortality (24, 26, 27), and five reporting 90-day mortality (22, 23, 28, 29, 31). Four studies reported hospital LOS.24,26,27,30 Hospitalization complications were reported in only one study. No other secondary outcomes were reported by any study.  

# Methodological quality of included studies  

Newcastle Ottowa assessed a majority of the included studies to have a low risk of bias in the “population selection”, “comparability”, and “outcomes” dimensions. Four studies were assessed to have an elevated risk for bias in the “comparability” dimension, which we defined as a score of 0.24,25,28,31 Three studies were assessed to have an elevated risk for bias in the “outcomes” dimension due to inadequate follow-up, which we defined as a score of 0.22,23,30 Full risk of bias assessment is given in Table 2.  

# Results of meta-analysis  

# Functional outcome  

Functional outcomes (mRS score) in the drip-and-stay group were non-inferior to functional outcomes in the combined drip-and-ship or hub group (margin for noninferiority: 0.91). Meta-analysis found no significant difference in good functional outcome (mRS score 01) between patients in the drip-and-stay group compared to the combined drip-and-ship or hub group (6 studies, $\mathrm{RR}=1.09$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.98\substack{-1.22,}$ $p=0.123)$ , with no evidence of heterogeneity $(I\wedge2=0\%$ , $p=0.769$ for Cochran’s Q test) (Fig. 2A). Bayesian meta-analysis found no significant difference in this outcome between the two groups (Fig. 2C).

# Safety outcome  

Safety outcomes (rate of sICH) in the drip-and-stay group were non-inferior to safety outcomes in the combined drip-and-ship or hub group (margin for non-inferiority: 1.69). Meta-analysis found no significant difference in safety outcomes between patients in the drip-and-stay group compared to the combined drip-and-ship or hub group (8 studies, $\mathrm{RR}=0.98$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI~}0.64{-}1.51,$ $p=0.942)$ with no evidence of heterogeneity $I^{\wedge}226.2\%$ , $p=0.219$ for Cochran’s $Q$ test) (Fig. 3A). Bayesian meta-analysis also found no significant difference in sICH rates between the two groups (Fig. 3C). Sensitivity analysis using hub as the comparison group rather than the combined drip-andship or hub group did not find any significant difference in safety outcomes (Fig. 3B).  

# Secondary outcomes  

Meta-analysis found no significant difference in 90-day mortality between patients in the drip-and-stay group compared to the combined drip-and-ship or hub group [(5 studies, $\mathrm{RR}=0.98_{.}$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.731.32, $p=0.911,$ ); no evidence of heterogeneity $\mathit{I}^{\wedge}2=0\%,$ , $p=0.991$ for Cochran’s Q test)] (Fig. 4A), no was there a difference in total mortality [(9 studies, $\mathrm{RR}=0.99$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.781.27, $p=0.948)$ ; no evidence of heterogeneity $\begin{array}{l}{{I\wedge2=0\%,}}\end{array}$ $p=0.987$ for Cochran’s Q test)], or in-hospital mortality [(4 studies, $\mathrm{RR}=1.01$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.65\substack{-1.57,}$ , $p=0.959)$ ; no evidence of heterogeneity $(I^{\wedge}2~=~0\%,~p=0.680$ for Cochran’s Q test)]. Bayesian meta-analysis found no significant difference in 90-day mortality between the two groups (Fig. 4C). Sensitivity analysis using a hub comparison group rather than the combined drip-and-ship or hub group did not find any significant difference in any mortality outcome (Fig. 4B). Non-inferiority could not be evaluated as there did not exist any documented, statistically significant mortality difference between tPA treatment and placebo in the literature.18

# Publication bias  

Per-outcome publication bias could not be assessed with a funnel plot or Egger’s linear regression because no outcome was reported across 10 or more studies.20  

# Discussion  

The primary finding is that the drip-and-stay model of AIS care is non-inferior to models that involve a hub center (i.e. drip-and-ship or hub). This is the first meta-analysis of telestroke care to evaluate the relative efficacy of the drip-and-stay model. No significant difference in mRS score, sICH rates, or mortality rates could be identified between patients treated in the drip-and-stay model compared to patients treated in the drip-and-ship or hub models. Drip-and-stay may be associated with increased hospital LOS, but the results were mixed dependent on the analysis used and was based on results for 3 studies.  

This study has several limitations. No RCTs could be identified that met eligibility criteria for inclusion in this study. However, the available non-randomized observational cohort studies all took place in formalized telestroke networks, and all but three had a total study population greater than 300.24,27,31 All included telestroke networks were located within the United States and Europe, limiting generalizability outside these regions. In addition, the precise capabilities and protocols of each study’s telestroke network was not always adequately described, further limiting generalizability. Transfer eligibility criteria and transfer protocols between hub and spoke centers would be especially informative. In this meta-analysis, no spoke hospital had 24-7 access to a stroke neurologist. Six studies specified that telestroke consultation was performed only after the administration of IV tPA, but the level of involvement from the hub facility, such as whether the consultation was a one-time event or regular and ongoing, was not reported.23,24,26,2830 Future trials should report detailed characteristics of the spoke hospital (e.g. critical access hospital vs. metropolitan center) and hospital capabilities (e.g. ICU care, specialized stroke nurses, weekend physiotherapy).  

Most studies did not report relatively common outcome measures in the wider stroke literature, such as rehabilitation needs, prescription of stroke-related medications (lipid-lowering medications, anti-coagulants, anti-platelet therapy), quality of patient experience, and hospitalization complications, nor did they report pre-morbid characteristics and stroke risk factors such as smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia  all of which affect outcomes. Door-to-needle time was also not reported. Without this information, the possibility of treatment allocation bias remains. We attempted to mitigate potential bias by comparing NIHSS scores between groups, with the hypothesis that patients with higher NIHSS would be more likely to be transferred to a hub. However, because of the variability between papers in how NIHSS scores were aggregated before reporting, it was impossible to evaluate NIHSS scores as a potential confounder.  

Although this analysis supports “drip-and-stay” models of care, most individual studies had some limitations, as discussed above. While a formal RCT could be considered, we would advocate for additional high-quality observational cohort studies in order to justify an RCT that potentially withholds hub level care. To this end, enhanced reporting guidelines for observational cohort studies and quality improvement registries should be developed, and we recommend that future observational cohort studies increase efforts to report their patient selection protocols and provide detailed patient-level information used to allocate treatment.  

While the majority of hubs in this meta-analysis are academic centers at the core of telestroke networks, another practice model exists where non-academic hospitals consult private teleneurologist groups for treatment decisions. Though these non-academic hospitals are not part of a “telestroke network”, per se, and our results are not necessarily generalizable to this model, use of remote telestroke services is essentially a drip-and-stay model. The growth of telestroke usage therefore presents an opportunity to study drip-and-stay-like outcomes in comparison to more traditional models involving academic hubs.  

Despite the limitations of this study, the central finding that drip-and-stay care is noninferior to transfer models has several important ramifications. Transfers for ischemic strokes have more than doubled within the last decade  mostly in rural settings.32 Telestroke models have several advantages in rural areas where transfers are time-consuming and logistically challenging. Long ambulance rides can lead to complications during transport (e. g. hemorrhagic transformation, uncontrolled hypertension). Hospitalization far from home means family and friends have a harder time visiting the patient, which can impose significant psychological, medical, and logistical challenges. Assuming a relatively equivalent hospital LOS can be achieved, the reduction in hospital transfers could save the healthcare system millions of dollars every year in ambulance rides and other costs.32,33  

Another advantage of a drip-and-stay model is more efficient allocation of neurologists. Trained neurologists are in short supply, and rural areas suffer disproportionately from coverage issues; many critical-access hospitals do not have a neurologist on staff, and even some regional hospitals do not have full-time coverage.7 This maldistribution causes academic centers to be overwhelmed by ever-increasing patient volume and transfer demand. Limiting transfers of patients which can be managed safely at a spoke hospital can allow hubs to allocate resources to high-acuity patients. Profitability is increasingly difficult for rural hospitals. Stroke remains unfortunately prevalent, and retaining stroke patients in-house could help alleviate financial stresses at critical-access hospitals. Finally, logistics involving transfers can sometimes be uniquely dangerous or taxing for healthcare system, as we have seen recently as result of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic.  

# Conclusions  

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that, based on currently available evidence, a drip-andstay model for treatment of acute ischemic stroke is noninferior to drip-and-ship or hub models, both of which include some level of direct patient care at an academic center hub. Given the many advantages of avoiding hospital transfers, the growing popularity of telestroke, and the growing financial stress on both academic centers and rural hospitals alike, drip-and-stay models of telestroke care are promising, and future studies should further evaluate this alternative system for providing high-quality care for patients with AIS.
