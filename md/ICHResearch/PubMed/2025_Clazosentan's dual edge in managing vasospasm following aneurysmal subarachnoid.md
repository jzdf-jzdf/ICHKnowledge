# Clazosentan’s dual edge in managing vasospasm following aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage: an updated systematic review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression of clinical outcomes and safety profiles

Received: 29 October 2024 / Revised: 14 December 2024 / Accepted: 27 December 2024

# Abstract  

Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (aSAH), resulting from ruptured aneurysms, is a major contributor to strokerelated mortality and morbidity. Despite advances in healthcare, aSAH remains severe and often leads to complications such as cerebral vasospasm (CV), cerebral infarction, and delayed ischemic neurological deficits (DIND). Clazosentan, an endothelin receptor antagonist, has demonstrated potential in alleviating vasospasm and its associated outcomes, although evidence of its efficacy remains unclear. A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that compared clazosentan with placebo in adult patients with aSAH. Primary outcomes were vasospasm-related cerebral infarction and DIND. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of vasospasm, mortality, and adverse effects. Data were analyzed using Review Manager 5.3, and meta-regression was employed to assess moderators, including aneurysm treatment modality. Eight RCTs, involving 3,186 participants, were included in the analysis. Clazosentan significantly reduced vasospasm-related cerebral infarction ( $\mathrm{RR}{=}0.56$ , $95\%$ CI: $0.42–0.76$ , $p{=}0.0002)$ and DIND $\mathrm{{RR}}=0.67$ , $95\%$ CI: 0.55–0.80, $p<0.0001$ ). Dosages of $10~\mathrm{mg}$ and $15~\mathrm{mg}$ were found to be the most effective. However, clazosentan had no effect on the overall mortality $(p=0.48)$ and was associated with an increased risk of hypotension, pulmonary edema, and pleural effusion. Clazosentan significantly reduced vasospasmrelated cerebral infarction and DIND in patients with aSAH, particularly at higher doses. Nonetheless, its use is linked to notable adverse effects, highlighting the need for careful assessment of its risk-benefit profile in clinical practice.

# Introduction  

Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (aSAH), defined as bleeding in the subarachnoid space due to aneurysmal rupture, accounts for $5\%$ of all strokes and has an incidence rate of 9 per 100,000 cases, although this rate varies significantly with age and sex. aSAH has a very high disease burden, with half of its patients being under the age of 55, and one-third passing away in the days or weeks following bleeding [1]. Despite advances in healthcare, such as timely diagnosis and improved treatment modalities, mortality and morbidity rates associated with aSAH remain high. $45\%$ of patients died within a month of hemorrhage, with $15\%$ dying at the time of bleeding. Individuals who survive often face residual deficits in functional status, cognition, and mental health [2]. aSAH can lead to cerebral vasospasm (CV), which reduces blood flow to the brain and causes delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI). This complication contributes significantly to the high mortality and morbidity rates associated with DCI [3].  

CV can present in various ways, from having no symptoms to causing serious conditions such as DCI and cerebral infarction. These severe outcomes can lead to delayed ischemic neurological deficits (DIND) and significant clinical deterioration [4]. After aSAH, DCI is a major and treatable cause of death and disability, contributing to mortality in $23\%$ of patients and lasting deficits in $37\%$ [3]. Research has shown that elevated levels of Endothelin-1 in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and increased expression of Endothelin A receptors in the blood vessels of the brain play a role in vasoconstriction [5]. To address this issue, clazosentan have been developed. It works as a competitive antagonist of Endothelin A receptors, blocking the effects of Endothelin-1 and thereby improving blood flow to the brain [3].  

Pontes et al. conducted a meta-analysis that indicated a decreased risk of DCI and DIND. Additionally, no change in functional outcomes and an increased risk of adverse effects with clazosentan have been reported [6]. Although clazosentan has been tested in multiple clinical studies, the results have been mixed. Some studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in reducing vasospasm-related mortality [7, 8]; however, more recent evidence suggests that it may not offer significant benefits [9].  

Therefore, this updated meta-analysis aimed to provide robust and collective evidence regarding the efficacy of clazosentan in decreasing morbidity defined by the incidence of vasospasm-related cerebral infarction and vasospasm-related DIND and mortality. Furthermore, this article offers an insightful analysis of the safety considerations associated with clazosentan, including the frequency of adverse effects. We also evaluated the effectiveness of various dosages of clazosentan and defined the optimal dose to achieve maximum efficacy with minimal risk of side effects.  

# Methods  

The PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews and metaanalyses were strictly followed in the conduct of this meta-analysis, and it was in conformity with the Cochrane Collaboration’s methodological approach [10, 11].  

# Literature search  

To identify relevant studies evaluating the effect of clazosentan on patients with aSAH, a thorough literature search was conducted using the PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases, covering all records from their inception to August 2024. There were no restrictions on the search based on sample size, language, or time constraints. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and pertinent keywords were employed in the strategy, such as (“endothelin receptor antagonists” OR “Clazosentan”) AND (“subarachnoid hemorrhage” OR “aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage” OR “intracranial aneurysm”) AND (“vasospasm” OR “related outcomes”). The title, abstract, full text, and bibliography of every recognized study were carefully examined. Furthermore, references from applicable literature were thoroughly searched to find relevant studies, without regard to the publishing language, location of the world, or ethnicity.  

# Study inclusion Criteria  

The research study adhered to stringent eligibility criteria for inclusion, which encompassed the following: (a) studies providing relevant outcome data, (b) adult patients $(\geq18$ years) diagnosed with aSAH, and (c) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with at least one intervention group receiving clazosentan compared to a placebo control group, with relevant outcomes. Studies involving intravenous doses of 1, 5, 10, or $15~\mathrm{mg}$ of clazosentan were pooled. Studies involving animal models, improper designs (such as non-randomized trials), deficiency of relevant data, or publications categorized as case reports, editorials, reviews, conference abstracts, or duplicate publications were excluded.  

# Data extraction  

After conducting the systematic search, numerous articles were identified and imported into the EndNote Reference Manager (Version X7.5; Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia,  

Pennsylvania). Duplicate entries were carefully identified and removed using EndNote software. Two reviewers independently reviewed the full texts of the publications that met the inclusion criteria. Data from the selected trials were then extracted and systematically compiled into an informationextraction table. Essential data included the first author’s name, year of publication, sample size, participant demographics (age and sex), baseline characteristics, clazosentan dosage, and aneurysmal treatment modality. Any disagreements regarding article selection or data extraction were resolved by discussion or by a third reviewer.  

# Outcomes of interest  

The primary outcomes of this study were vasospasm-related cerebral infarction, identified as clinically significant cerebral infarctions occurring after the initiation of the study drug, as observed on CT scans, and vasospasm-related DIND. DIND was defined as a reduction of $\geq2$ points on the modified Glasgow Coma Scale (mGCS) or an increase of $\geq2$ points on the abbreviated National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (aNIHSS) lasting at least $^{2\textrm{h}}$ . In cases where neurological scores were not assessable, DIND was determined based on the presence of CV on digital subtraction angiography (DSA), initiation of rescue therapy, or clinical signs suggestive of vasospasm, such as unexplained fever or new neurological deficits, confirmed by imaging or Doppler studies.  

Secondary outcomes included poor outcomes, defined as a Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended (GOSE) score of $\leq4$ , with GOSE scores ranging from 1 (dead) to 8 (upper good recovery), incidence of vasospasm (moderate to severe), characterized by an inner artery reduction of $\geq34\%$ , and mortality. Rescue therapy, employed in response to moderate or severe vasospasm or clinical symptoms of vasospasm, consists of hypervolemia, hypertension, hemodilution (HHH) therapy, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty, and intra-arterial administration of antiplatelet drugs or vasodilators.  

Safety was evaluated by assessing the incidence of mortality, pulmonary edema, pleural effusion, pneumonia, hypotension, anemia, and cerebral hemorrhage.  

# Risk of bias assessment  

The RoB 2 approach was used to thoroughly evaluate the risk of bias for each RCT. This tool assesses seven variables: the creation of random sequences, concealment of allocation, blinding of result evaluations and participants, incompleteness of outcome data, selective reporting, and other possible prejudice sources. Each criterion was classified as “low risk of bias,” “high risk of bias,” or “unclear risk of bias,” meaning that there was either insufficient information available or uncertainty about the possibility of bias. These classifications were in line with the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias assessment methodology [12].  

# Statistical analysis  

Review Manager 5.3 (RevMan 5.3) was used for all the statistical analyses. The Mantel-Haenszel method was used to evaluate dichotomous outcomes. The results were reported as risk ratios (RRs) with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs). To account for potential heterogeneity among the trials, a random-effects model was used. Higgins I² and Q statistics from Cochrane were used to measure heterogeneity. Low heterogeneity was indicated by values less than $50\%$ , moderate heterogeneity was indicated by values $>50\%$ , and significant heterogeneity was indicated by values greater than $75\%$ . The I² statistic measures the percentage of variation between studies, which may be attributed to heterogeneity rather than chance [13]. Analyses were conducted with stratification across key subgroups, categorized by clazosentan dosages of 1, 5, 10, and $15~\mathrm{mg/h}$ , as well as by aneurysm treatment methods of surgical clipping (SC) and endovascular coiling (EC). A meta-regression analysis was conducted using the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software to examine the relationship between the primary outcomes (vasospasm-related cerebral infarction and DIND) and several moderators. The moderators assessed included age, female sex, drug duration, aneurysmal surgery type (SC or EC), and drug onset. This analysis enabled us to explore the potential impacts of these variables on the observed outcomes across the included studies. A strict P value criterion of less than 0.05 was applied when assessing statistical significance.  

# Results  

# Study screening and selection  

An initial search across the five databases yielded 1,270 studies. A systematic review was conducted to remove duplicates, eliminate 292 entries, and leave 978 unique studies. The titles and abstracts were meticulously screened, resulting in the exclusion of 956 irrelevant studies. Full-text assessments were performed on 22 studies to evaluate the safety and efficacy data, leading to the exclusion of 15 studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, seven studies comparing different dosages of clazosentan with placebo were included in the final analysis. The PRISMA flow diagram details the study-selection process (Fig. 1).

# Baseline and study characteristics  

This pooled analysis included eight RCTs published across seven manuscripts that met the inclusion criteria, with a total of 3186 participants [7–9, 14–17]. Of these, 2,015 patients received intravenous clazosentan for 14–15 days, while 1,171 were assigned to the placebo group. The average age of participants ranged from 45 to 59.6 years, drug duration was 14 to 15 days, and drug onset was between $48\mathrm{~h~}$ and $96\mathrm{~h~}$ . The participant pool consisted of 1,019 men and 2,167 women.  

The eight pooled RCTs comprised two phase 2 studies [7, 14] and six phase 3 trials [8, 9, 15–17], conducted between 2005 and 2024, with results published across seven studies. One study comprised two RCTs [17]: one involving patients who underwent SC and the other involving patients who underwent EC for ruptured SAH before being assigned to either clazosentan or placebo. These studies were treated as separate studies, facilitating subgroup analysis based on the type of aneurysmal intervention.  

The administered dosages were as follows: $1\mathrm{mg/h}$ in one study, $5~\mathrm{mg/h}$ across four studies, $10~\mathrm{mg/h}$ in three studies, $15~\mathrm{mg/h}$ in three studies; and $0.2~\mathrm{{mg/kg/h}}$ in one study (equivalent to $10.6~\mathrm{{mg/h}}$ for a $53~\mathrm{kg}$ adult). Although the mean weight was not provided in this study, a mean weight of approximately $53\mathrm{kg}$ was assumed, based on data from a comparable study [9] (Table 1).  

# Quality assessment  

Assessment of the risk of bias across the included RCTs indicated a generally low risk of bias. Most studies employed robust methods for random sequence generation and allocation concealment, ensuring that selection bias was minimized. Double-blinding was consistently applied, effectively reducing the risk of performance bias, while outcome assessment was performed by blinded committees, limiting the detection bias. Incomplete outcome data were managed appropriately in most cases, often using intention-to-treat analysis. Selective reporting was not evident as predefined outcomes were typically reported as planned. Although a few studies presented concerns related to small sample sizes or funding sources, the overall risk of bias was low, with only minor uncertainties in some cases. # Vasospasm-related cerebral infarction  

In seven studies, Clazosentan reduced the incidence of cerebral infarction compared to placebo (212/1988 vs. 266/1598), with a RR of 0.56 ( $95\%$ CI: 0.42, 0.76; $p{=}0.0002)$ and moderate heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}56\%)$ ).  

For the different dosages, significant effects were observed with $10~\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle{p<0.0001}$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and $15~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.002$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=11\%}$ ). No significant effects were observed with 1 mg $\scriptstyle(p=0.27)$ or $5\mathrm{mg}\left(p{=}0.26;\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}65\%\right)$ .  

Regarding aneurysmal procedures, clazosentan showed a significant reduction in infarcts for SC $\boldsymbol{\Tilde{p}}{=}0.01$ ; $1^{2}{=}72\%$ ) but not for EC $\mathrm{\Delta}p=0.28$ ; $1^{2}{=}64\%$ ) (Fig. 3).  

# Vasospasm-related DIND  

In six studies, clazosentan significantly reduced DIND compared with placebo (285/1978 vs. 332/1582), with an RR of 0.67 ( $95\%$ CI: 0.55, 0.80; $p<0.0001$ and moderate heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}29\%$ ).  

Significant reductions were observed with $5\operatorname*{mg}\left(p=0.03\right.$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%}$ ), $10~\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle{p<0.0001}$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ), and $15~\mathrm{mg}$ ( $\scriptstyle{p=0.04}$ ; $1^{2}=54\%$ ). No significant effect was observed with $1~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.36)$ .  

Clazosentan was effective in reducing deficits in both SC $(p=0.01\$ ; $1^{2}{=}50\%$ ) and EC $\scriptstyle{p=0.02}$ ; $1^{2}{=}59\%$ ) (Fig. 4).  

# Vasospasm  

In four studies, clazosentan reduced vasospasm compared to placebo (228/649 vs. 411/637), with an RR of 0.54 $95\%$ CI: 0.47, 0.62; $p<0.00001$ ) and moderate heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}25\%)$ .  

Significant effects were observed across dosages of $1~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.007)$ , $5~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p<0.00001$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=10^{0}/0}$ ), $10~\mathrm{mg}$ $\left(p<0.00001;1^{2}=0\%\right)$ , and $15\mathrm{mg}\left(p<0.00001\right)$ .  

Vasospasm rates were significantly lower in SC $(p<0.00001$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%}$ ) and EC $(p=0.002)$ (Fig. 5).

# GOSE ≤ 4  

In four studies, no significant difference was found in $\mathrm{GOSE}\le4$ outcomes between clazosentan (374/1454) and placebo (254/1080), with an RR of 1.04 ( $95\%$ CI: 0.88, 1.22; $p{=}0.68,$ ) and low heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}13\%$ ).  

No significant effects were observed across dosages of $5~\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle(p=0.19$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ), $10~\mathrm{mg}$ $\overset{\v}{\boldsymbol{p}}=\boldsymbol{0}.09$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%}$ ), and $15~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.33)$ .  

For aneurysmal procedures, there were no significant differences between Clazosentan and placebo for either SC $(p=0.75$ ; $1^{2}{=}35\%$ ) or EC $\overset{\cdot}{p}=0.71$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=12\%}$ ) (Fig. 6).  

# Rescue therapy  

In six studies, clazosentan significantly reduced the need for rescue therapy compared to placebo (246/1982 vs. 332/1585), with an RR of 0.61 ( $95\%$ CI: 0.48, 0.77; $p<0.0001)$ and moderate heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}52\%)$ .  

![](images/cac881c760b881360532c3bfefc7356b828597249414b29246e2fbc0a6ee8c96.jpg)  
Fig. 6  Forest plot for GOSE ≤ 4  

Significant reductions were observed with $5~\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle(p=0.0006$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ), $10~\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle{p=0.0002}$ ; $1^{2}{=}11\%$ ), and $15~\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle{p=0.01}$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=57\%}$ ). No significant effect was observed with 1 mg $(p=0.61)$ ).  

For aneurysmal procedures, clazosentan was effective in reducing rescue therapy for SC $\scriptstyle{p=0.02}$ ; $1^{2}{=}49\%$ ), but not for EC $\scriptstyle{p=0.07}$ ; $1^{2}{=}77\%$ ) (Fig. 7).  

# Mortality  

Across seven studies, mortality rates were similar between the clazosentan (91/1976) and placebo (62/1577) groups, with an RR of 1.12 $95\%$ CI: 0.81, 1.55; $p{=}0.48,$ ) and no heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%)$ ).  

No significant differences in mortality were observed for clazosentan across different dosages (1 mg, $p{=}0.88$ ; $5~\mathrm{mg}$ , $p{=}0.94$ ; $10~\mathrm{mg}$ , $p{=}0.93$ ; $15~\mathrm{mg}$ , $p{=}0.17\$ ), with $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%}$ for all dosages.  

Mortality rates also did not differ significantly between clazosentan and placebo for either SC $(p=0.93)$ or EC $(p{=}0.73)$ , with $1\%$ for both (Fig. 8).  

# Anemia  

In six studies, Clazosentan was associated with a higher incidence of anemia compared to placebo (370/2004 vs. 186/1588), with a RR of 1.49 ( $95\%$ CI: 1.27, 1.76; $p<0.00001;$ and no heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%)$ ).  

Significant effects were observed with the $5\mathrm{mg}$ dose $(p=0.0001$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and $10~\mathrm{mg}$ dose $\scriptstyle{p=0.03}$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ . No significant effects were observed for $1~\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle{p=0.14)}$ or $15~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.17)$ .  

Regarding aneurysmal procedures, clazosentan significantly increased anemia in SC $\scriptstyle{p=0.001}$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%}$ ) but not in EC ( $\scriptstyle{p=0.15}$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ) (Fig. 9).  

# Hypotension  

In six studies, clazosentan significantly increased the incidence of hypotension compared with placebo (204/1797 vs. 62/1389), with an RR of 2.42 ( $95\%$ CI: 1.83, 3.20; $p<0.00001,$ ) and no heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%)$ ).  

Significant effects were observed at $5~\mathrm{mg}$ $p<0.00001$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and $15~\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle{p<0.0001}$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ). No significant effects were observed with $1~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.40)$ or $10~\mathrm{mg}$ $\left(p=0.68;1^{2}{=}45\%\right)$ .  

Clazosentan significantly increased hypotension in both the SC $(p<0.0001$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and EC $(p=0.004\$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ) groups (Fig. 10).  

# Pulmonary edema  

In five studies, clazosentan significantly increased the incidence of pulmonary edema compared to placebo (209/1884 vs. 65/1470), with an RR of 2.60 $95\%$ CI: 1.82, 3.72; $p<0.00001.$ ) and moderate heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}29\%$ .  

Significant effects were noted across all doses: $1~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.03)$ , $5\mathrm{mg}$ $\scriptstyle{p=0.003}$ ; $\scriptstyle\mathrm{I}^{2}=40\%$ ), $10~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.007)$ , and $15\:\mathrm{mg}\:(p{=}0.01;\:\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}5\:\mathrm{l}\%)$ .  

Pulmonary edema rates were also significantly higher with clazosentan for both SC $(p=0.02)$ and EC $(p=0.0002$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%}$ ) (Fig. 11).  

# Pleural effusion  

Across four studies, clazosentan increased pleural effusion rates compared to placebo (172/1502 vs. 37/1092), with an RR of 3.40 ( $95\%$ CI: 2.40, 4.83; $p<0.00001$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ).  

Significant effects were found at $5~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p<0.00001$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%}$ ), $10\mathrm{mg}$ $p<0.0001)$ , and $15\:\mathrm{mg}\:(p=0.003;\:\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ), whereas the $1~\mathrm{mg}$ dose showed no significant effect $(p{=}0.08)$ (Fig. 12).  

# Pneumonia  

In three studies, clazosentan increased the incidence of pneumonia compared to placebo (168/1284 vs. 81/870), with an RR of 1.42 ( $95\%$ CI: 1.10, 1.83; $p{=}0.006)$ and no heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%)$ ).  

Significant effects were observed at $5~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.008\$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ), while no significant effects were observed at $1~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.39)$ or $15\mathrm{mg}$ $\overset{\vartriangle}{\boldsymbol{p}}=0.60$ ; $\scriptstyle\mathrm{I}^{2}=64\%$ ) (Fig. 13).  

# Cerebral hemorrhage  

In three studies, clazosentan showed no significant difference in cerebral hemorrhage rates compared to placebo $(47/1353~\mathrm{vs.}~27/960)$ , with an RR of 1.19 ( $95\%$ CI: 0.74, 1.92; $p{=}0.48,$ and no heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%)$ .  

No significant effects were observed at $5~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p=0.39\$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ) or $15~\mathrm{mg}$ $(p{=}0.99\$ ; $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ).  

Clazosentan also showed no significant differences in SC $(p=0.50)$ or EC ( $\overset{\_}{p}=0.42$ ; $1\%$ ) (Fig. 14).  

# Meta-regression  

Meta-regression analysis for vasospasm-related cerebral infarction revealed that drug duration (-0.317, $p{=}0.329,$ and age (-0.05, $p{=}0.338,$ ) did not significantly affect the outcome. The drug onset also showed no significant effect (0.006, $p{=}0.569)$ . A higher proportion of female patients was significantly associated with an increased incidence of infarcts (7.539, $p{=}0.0227.$ ). Regarding aneurysmal surgery type, the use of SC was associated with a non-significant trend towards fewer infarcts (coefficient: -0.363, $p{=}0.070$ ), whereas EC also showed a non-significant trend towards fewer infarcts (coefficient: -0.675, $p{=}0.102$ ) (Figure S1-S5).

# Discussion  

This meta-analysis, encompassing eight reports from seven studies with 3,186 patients, evaluated clazosentan’s effectiveness and safety in managing complications from vasospasm after aSAH. Clazosentan significantly reduced the incidence of cerebral infarction, DIND, and vasospasm compared to placebo and lowered the need for rescue therapy, although it was associated with increased rates of anemia, hypotension, pleural effusion, and pulmonary edema, without significantly affecting mortality or cerebral hemorrhage. A prior meta-analysis [18], which included both cohort studies and RCTs, reported similar outcomes, showing significant reductions in vasospasm, DCI, and the need for rescue therapy with clazosentan, along with increased rates of anemia, hypotension, and pulmonary complications. However, there were methodological constraints, including the inclusion of cohort studies that primarily compared clazosentan with fasudil hydrochloride and verapamil, with no segregation between patients undergoing SC and EC to perform subgroup analyses, while RCTs compared it with placebo, potentially introducing bias. Additionally, the lack of subgroup analyses based on dosage and aneurysmal procedure type in the previous meta-analysis underscored the need for an updated, more rigorous, RCT-focused approach. Our subgroup analysis revealed that the $10~\mathrm{mg}$ and $15~\mathrm{mg}$ doses were particularly effective, with the $10~\mathrm{mg}$ dose demonstrating broad efficacy and the $15~\mathrm{mg}$ dose significantly reducing vasospasm and delayed ischemic deficits. Clazosentan was more effective in reducing cerebral infarction and neurological deficits in SC than in EC, suggesting that its efficacy may vary with the type of aneurysmal procedure, while the $1~\mathrm{mg}$ and $5~\mathrm{mg}$ doses were ineffective in reducing cerebral infarction or DIND.

In managing vasospasm following aSAH, several treatment modalities are used to address complications, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Nimodipine, a calcium channel blocker, is the primary treatment recommended by the 2023 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) guidelines for preventing DCI in aSAH patients [19]. Nimodipine is well established to improve outcomes by reducing the DCI and enhancing cerebral perfusion [20]. However, despite its effectiveness, it is associated with side effects such as headache, rash, vertigo, flushing, nausea, diarrhea, palpitations, and, hypotension, which can negatively impact cerebral perfusion [21–23]. Hypervolemic hemodilution, which increases the blood volume and decreases the viscosity to improve cerebral blood flow, is less commonly used. This approach has risks, such as fluid overload, leading to complications, such as pulmonary edema or heart failure [24]. Induced hypertension involves raising the blood pressure to enhance perfusion and relieve vasospasm. While this can be effective, it requires careful monitoring to prevent adverse effects, such as myocardial strain or worsening of pre-existing cardiovascular conditions [25]. Endovascular therapies, such as intra-arterial vasodilator infusion and balloon angioplasty, offer a direct approach to managing vasospasm and are typically used when medical management is insufficient. Although these interventions demonstrated a significant reduction in inhospital mortality, their impact on long-term outcomes and functional recovery was comparable to that of the standard treatment. Additionally, they are associated with prolonged ICU stay and hospitalization, indicating that they may not be the best option for all patients [26]. Other therapies such as intravenous magnesium, statins, and cilostazol have been attempted with little success [27–30]. Clazosentan, an endothelin receptor antagonist, has come into the spotlight by targeting endothelin-1, a potent vasoconstrictor implicated in vasospasms. By providing a novel mechanism of action, clazosentan may enhance the management outcomes. This approach holds promise for addressing some limitations of current treatments by directly targeting the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of vasospasm [5].  

Several meta-analyses have evaluated the efficacy of clazosentan in treating aSAH, showing that higher doses $(10{-}15~\mathrm{mg/h})$ can significantly improve angiographic vasospasm, vasospasm-related DINDs, and DCI [31–33]. The exact reason for the ineffectiveness of lower clazosentan doses $(1{-}5~\mathrm{mg/h})$ in reducing cerebral infarction in patients with aSAH remains unknown. However, this could be due to insufficient inhibition of endothelin-1, inadequate cerebrovascular effects, or a failure to reach the pharmacodynamic threshold for efficacy. Other contributing factors may include individual variability, limited tissue penetration, and a reduced impact on non-vasospasm mechanisms, such as inflammation and microcirculatory dysfunction. Furthermore, many of these studies were limited by the small number of included trials and narrow range of reported outcomes, constraining their ability to thoroughly investigate the effects of different dosages or procedural types. A recent meta-analysis by Pontez et al. examined the effects of clazosentan dosage, categorizing them simply as low $(\le5~\mathrm{mg/h})$ or high $(>5~\mathrm{mg/h})$ [6]. This meta-analysis builds on previous findings by offering a detailed examination of clazosentan’s effects, focusing on specific dosages $\mathrm{(1~mg,5~mg,10~mg}$ , and $15~\mathrm{mg}$ ) and procedural types (SC vs. EC). This approach enables identification of the optimal dose that maximizes therapeutic benefits while minimizing side effects, which is a crucial balance in vasospasm management.

Notably, Jie Song et al. found that high-dose clazosentan effectively enhanced angiographic vasospasm and reduced DINDs and DCI [31]. A recent meta-analysis by Pontez et al. also confirmed that Clazosentan significantly reduced vasospasm-related DCI, moderate-to-severe angiographic vasospasm, and DIND, although it was associated with higher rates of pulmonary adverse events, hypotension, and anemia [6]. These findings align with those of our study, which further underscores the impact of clazosentan across varying dosages and procedural types.  

SC and EC are the two primary interventions for aSAH. SC involves placing a clip at the base of the aneurysm to stop blood flow and prevent rebleeding, whereas EC entails filling the aneurysm with coils to induce clot formation and seal it off [34]. Comparative analysis suggests that EC results in fewer poor functional outcomes at 1-year, lower vasospasm rates, and similar mortality and rebleeding rates compared with SC. However, SC is associated with a lower rebleeding rate at discharge and higher aneurysmal occlusion rate at 1 year. Therefore, the choice of intervention should consider the patient’s condition, clinical factors, and available resources to optimize outcomes [35]. Supporting these procedural distinctions, Ping Hu et al. found that SC is associated with a higher incidence of DCI compared to EC for aSAH, which supports a preference for coiling when both options are feasible [34]. Given that Clazosentan’s effects may vary between these procedures due to their differing impacts on the blood vessels, assessing both approaches is essential for developing a more tailored treatment strategy. Notably, our study observed differences in the efficacy of clazosentan between SC and EC. Our analysis showed that clazosentan significantly reduced the incidence of vasospasm-related cerebral infarction and DINDs in patients undergoing SC, indicating that clazosentan is particularly effective in managing the higher risk of complications associated with clipping. In contrast, clazosentan demonstrated less pronounced effects in reducing vasospasm-related cerebral infarctions among patients undergoing coiling, potentially due to the lower baseline risk of such complications associated with coiling compared to clipping, although other outcomes, such as DIND, showed significant improvement. These findings may be attributed to the lower baseline risk associated with coiling, as demonstrated in the study conducted by Ping Hu et al. [34]. Nonetheless, Clazosentan offers substantial protective benefits in the higher risk setting of SC, emphasizing the importance of tailored treatment strategies that take procedural risks into account.  

In addition, meta-regression analysis found that longer clazosentan duration and older age significantly reduced vasospasm-related delayed neurological deficits, while a higher proportion of women was associated with increased cerebral infarction. The prolonged effect of clazosentan likely reflects its cumulative benefits in preventing vasospasm and maintaining the vascular tone. The association between older age and fewer deficits suggests a need for further research. The increased risk of cerebral infarction in females may be related to sex-specific biological factors, such as hormonal influences or higher rates of aneurysmal rupture [36]. These results underscore the importance of personalized treatment approaches, considering age and sex, and optimizing clazosentan duration.  

While this meta-analysis has several noteworthy strengths, it also has important limitations, including a small sample size, limited number of studies, and absence of a comparator group. Additionally, functional outcomes such as GOSE have not been consistently reported across RCTs. When reported, there was a lack of standardization in outcome scales, definitions of favorable outcomes, and follow-up durations. The excessive subgrouping in our analysis further reduced the statistical power of our findings. Moderate heterogeneity in outcomes related to vasospasminduced cerebral infarction was observed, likely owing to variability in dosing and aneurysmal procedures. Furthermore, differences in standard-of-care practices across centers significantly impacted the consistency of the outcomes. Future RCTs should prioritize the collection of standardized functional outcomes and long-term follow-up data to better evaluate patient recovery trajectories. Additionally, consideration should be given to how these findings can inform practice in resource-limited settings where access to endovascular procedures may be restricted.  

# Conclusion  

This meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy and safety of clazosentan compared with placebo across various doses and aneurysmal procedures. Overall, Clazosentan appears to be an effective treatment for mitigating vasospasmrelated complications, although its use is associated with adverse effects. These findings should be considered when determining treatment strategies and patient management. Future studies should focus on large-scale randomized trials with long-term follow-up to confirm these results, optimize dosing regimens, and identify the patient subgroups that benefit the most.
