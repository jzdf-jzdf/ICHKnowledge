# Predicting Gastrostomy Tube Placement After Intracerebral Hemorrhage: External Validation of the GRAVo Score  

David Lin1, Michael Minyetty1, Magdy Selim1,2, Sarah Marchina1,2, Filipa Carvalho1, Elizabeth Heistand1, Gogia Bhanu1,2, Saad Hasan1,2 and Sandeep Kumar1,2\*

# Abstract  

Background:  Dysphagia is a common consequence of intracerebral hemorrhages (ICH). It can lead to enduring impairments of dietary intake and the requirement for feeding via percutaneous gastrostomy (PEG) tubes. However, variabilities in the course of swallowing recovery after ICH make it difficult to anticipate the need for PEG placement in an individual patient. A new tool called the GRAVo score was recently developed to predict PEG tube placement after an ICH but has not been externally validated. Our study aims were to externally validate the GRAVo score in a multicenter cohort and reexamine the role of race in predicting PEG placement, given the uncertain biological plausibility for this relationship observed in the derivation cohort.  

Methods:  Patients for this analysis were selected from a previously completed multicenter, randomized, doubleblind futility design clinical trial, the Intracerebral Hemorrhage Deferoxamine trial, and underwent a retrospective review of prospectively collected data. The GRAVo scores were computed by using previously established methods using the following variables: Glasgow Coma Scale $\leq12$ (2 points), race (1 point for Black), $\mathsf{age}>50$ years (2 points), and ICH volume $>30~\mathsf{mL}$ (1 point). Association of GRAVo scores with PEG placement were examined by using logistic regression analysis after adjustment for exposure to deferoxamine. Model performance was estimated by using area under the receiving operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Subsequently, a second model was created by excluding scores for race, and the AUROC of both models were compared.  

Results:  A total of 291 patients with complete data points served as the study cohort; 38 ( $13\%)$ underwent PEG placement. The median GRAVo score for patients in the PEG and non-PEG groups were 4 (interquartile range 3–4) versus 2 (interquartile range 2–3), respectively $(p<0.0001$ ). External validation of the GRAVo score yielded an AUROC of 0.7008 $95\%$ confidence interval 0.6036–0.78); the model obtained without assignment of scores for the variable race yielded an AUROC of 0.6958 ( $95\%$ confidence interval 0.6124–0.7891). The receiver operating characteristic curves from both models demonstrated close overlap.  

Conclusions:  The results of our external validation demonstrate the validity of GRAVo scores for predicting PEG tube placement after an ICH. However, its performance was more modest compared with that of the derivation cohort.  

Inclusion of the race variable had no measurable effect on model performance. Differences in patient characteristics between these cohorts may have influenced our results. These findings should be taken into consideration when using the GRAVo score to assist clinical decision making on PEG placement after an ICH.  

Keywords:  Gravo score, ICH, Intracerebral hemorrhage, Gastronomy tube, ${\mathsf{PEG}},$ External validation  



# Introduction  

Dysphagia is one of the most common consequences of stroke and independently increases the risk of pneumonia, institutionalization, and deaths [1, 2]. Recent studies primarily focused on the ischemic stroke population have highlighted the major risks of poststroke dysphagia, its overall prognosis, and the time course of swallowing recovery [1, 3, 4]. Dysphagia that occurs in the aftermath of an intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), on the other hand, remains less well studied and estimating prognosis for its recovery is more uncertain. Smaller studies indicate that the risk of dysphagia is higher after ICH, and patients with ICH are less likely to be screened for swallowing impairment and are more likely to fail dysphagia screening [5–7]. Patients with persistent dysphagia who have an inadequate oral intake can require nutritional support via percutaneous gastrostomy (PEG) tubes. Decisions about PEG placement can be challenging in patients hospitalized with ICH, as the time course of swallowing recovery in this patient population remains poorly charted, and predictions of recovery for individual patients are even more difficult. A lack of clarity on this issue fuels management uncertainties, variabilities in clinical practice, and use of resources.  

Recently, a scoring system, the GRAVo score, was derived to predict PEG placement after an ICH [8]. The GRAVo score, which uses a few clinical variables (Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS], Black race, age) and one radiological variable (ICH volume) is easy to compute and was found to accurately predict PEG placement in a cohort of patients with ICH from a single center. This model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs) of 0.8410 ( $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 0.7504–0.9316) and 0.7911 $95\%$ CI 0.6931–0.8892) in the derivation and interval validation models, respectively, showing good accuracy [8]. A subsequent validation with a partial data set from the original cohort and another center showed a more moderate accuracy with an AUROC of 0.66 [9]. Another interesting finding from this investigation was the independent effect of Black race on PEG placement after accounting for the effects of ICH severity (GCS and ICH volume) and age. However, it was unclear whether the inclusion of race in the original model cohort was done because of its differential biological effects on swallowing recovery or whether it occurred from statistical confounding or from other unmeasured effects of race on treatment decisions.  

It is vital that models such as these that affect clinical decision making are developed with adequate rigor and its performance is validated in diverse patient populations to generate confidence in its prognostication abilities prior to its incorporation in clinical practice. The aim of our study was to externally validate the GRAVo score in an independent multicenter cohort of patients derived from the Intracerebral Hemorrhage Deferoxamine (i-DEF) trial, a randomized controlled clinical trial conducted across 40 centers in North America, which consecutively enrolled patients with spontaneous ICH [10]. Furthermore, we wanted to investigate whether the inclusion of race in the predictive model affected its overall performance in this more diverse cohort.  



# Methods  



# Study Design and Participants  

This study was performed at an academic university hospital. Patients were selected for retrospective review from a previously completed prospective, multicenter, futility design, randomized, placebo-controlled, doubleblind, phase II clinical trial, the i-DEF trial, conducted at 40 hospitals across Canada and the United States (NCT02175225). Details about trial enrollment criteria and patient characteristics have already been published [10]. Briefly, participants enrolled in the trial were adults aged 18–80  years with primary, spontaneous, or supratentorial ICH and were randomly assigned to receive deferoxamine mesylate (DFO) or placebo (saline) infusions for 3 consecutive days within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of hemorrhage onset. Patients with secondary causes of ICH and infratentorial bleeds were excluded. Participants were evaluated in person daily until the day following last study infusion, on day 7 or discharge (whichever was earlier), and at day 30 and day 90.  



# Procedures  

Between November 23, 2014, and November 10, 2017, 9,070 patients were assessed for eligibility for participation in the i-DEF trial and 294 were recruited. A flow diagram displaying trial recruitment, screen failures, and postrandomization exclusion has been published previously [10]. Briefly, all 294 patients enrolled in the i-DEF trial were screened for enrollment. Demographic and all relevant clinical characteristics of the participants were recorded at trial enrollment (within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of ICH onset). Patient data were reviewed for missing information on PEG placement or incomplete information on relevant variables for computing the GRAVo score (below) that would lead to their study exclusion. Hemorrhage severity was measured with the GCS, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, and ICH score. All computed tomography scans were analyzed in the i-DEF core-imaging laboratory by experienced raters using imaging analysis software (Analyze 11.0 Visualization and Analysis Software for Medical Imaging; AnalyzeDirect, Overland Park, KS) with the use of density thresholds on each slice, followed by manual correction to calculate ICH volumes. Patients underwent swallowing assessments according to their institutional practices. The treating team considering the patient’s swallowing and overall clinical status referred patients for PEG placement. This trial did not require prespecified protocols for swallowing assessment, even though data on placement of PEG tubes were collected prospectively. Therefore, the PEG placement in this cohort represented routine clinical practice. Assessors were anonymized to the trial group assignment for this outcome but not for this retrospective analysis.  

GRAVo scores were tabulated retrospectively using prospectively collected data by calculating a composite score for each patient using the following score assignments: ${\mathrm{GCS}}\leq12$ (2 points), race (1 point for Black race), $\mathrm{age}>50$ years (2 points), and ICH volume $>30~\mathrm{cm}^{3}~\mathrm{mL}$ (1 point), on the basis of previously described methods [8]. Subsequently, total scores were recalculated for all patients by excluding score assignments for race but by using all other aforementioned variables.  



# Statistical Analysis  

Out of 294 patients enrolled in the i-DEF trial, 3 patients were classified for postrandomization exclusion [10]. Therefore, a final cohort of 291 with complete data on all relevant variables and study outcomes was assembled and served as the study sample for this investigation. All univariate analyses were conducted using by Student’s $t.$ -test for continuous variables that were normally distributed, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for nonnormally distributed data, and $\chi^{2}$ tests for categorical variables, when appropriate. A $p$ value $<0.05$ was considered for statistical significance. A logistic regression model was built to assess the association of GRAVo scores (main variable of interest) with PEG placement (main binary outcome), after adjusting for any intervention effect from exposure to DFO treatment. We computed the sensitivity and specificity for all GRAVo scores in predicting PEG placement. We also derived the probability of PEG placement for each score and constructed corresponding marginal plots. We calculated AUROC to assess the overall accuracy and performance of this model. We recalculated PEG prediction model scores for each patient excluding scores for Black race. The AUROC for the two GRAVo scores models, with and without scores for Black race, were then compared. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 13.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).  



# Results  

The median age of the entire cohort was 60.3  years (standard deviation 11.98), 112 $(38\%)$ patients were women, and there were 68 $(22\%)$ Black patients. The baseline GCS score and ICH volume was 14 (interquartile range [IQR] 12–15) and $15~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ (IQR 7–30), respectively. A baseline modified Rankin Scale score of 0 was observed in 260 $(89\%)$ patients; the remainder $(11\%)$ had a modified Rankin Scale score of 1. The average GRAVo score for the entire cohort was 2 (IQR 1–4). A total of 38 $(13\%)$ patients underwent PEG placement. The baseline characteristics of patients with ICH who underwent PEG placement versus those who did not is summarized in the Table 1. Differences in important variables in the original derivation and external validation cohorts are summarized in Table 2.  

Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that GRAVo score was significantly associated with PEG placement (odds ratio 1.62, $95\%$ CI 1.25–2.11; $p<0.001,$ ), after adjustment for exposure to DFO; treatment with DFO did not have an independent effect on the odds of PEG placement (odds ratio 0.56, $95\%$ CI 0.27–1.18; $p{=}0.132^{\cdot}$ . The probability of PEG placement for each score was computed along with its $95\%$ CI and is displayed in Fig. 1. A GRAVo score $\mathsf{of}\geq4$ correctly classified $74.06\%$ of PEG placement with a sensitivity of $50\%$ and a specificity of $77.65\%$ . A score $\mathrm{of}\ge5$ correctly classified $78.84\%$ of PEG placement with a sensitivity of $23.68\%$ and a specificity of $87.06\%$ , whereas a score of 6 correctly classified $87.03\%$ of all PEG placement with a sensitivity of $5.26\%$ and a specificity of $99.22\%$ . External validation of the GRAVo score was conducted using the entire cohort and yielded an AUROC of 0.7008 $(95\%\mathrm{CI}0.6036{-}0.78)$ for the entire model. The AUROC of GRAVo scores without the inclusion of scores for Black race was 0.6958 $(95\%\mathrm{CI}0.6124-$ 0.7891). The receiver operating characteristic curves from both models show close overlap (Fig. 2).  



# Discussion  

The recently derived GRAVo score is an important effort toward improving prediction for PEG placement in a hospitalized patient with spontaneous ICH. We conducted an external validation of this instrument in a larger sample derived from a multicenter cohort using prospectively collected data, which adds to the robustness of our findings. Our results confirm the significant association of GRAVo scores with PEG placement after ICH. However, it exhibited a more modest performance in our validation cohort with an AUROC of 0.7008 $95\%$ CI 0.6082–0.7822). The discrepancies in estimates between derivation and external validation models merit further scrutiny and optimization prior to its adoption in clinical practice.

GRAVo scores, like other clinical scoring systems, offer a promise of providing better estimates of the probability of an event at an individual level and thereby aid in personalized decision making for patient care. Validation in an independent cohort, separate from the one used for its derivation, is necessary to ascertain its reproducibility and generalizability. A systemic review of stroke prediction models shows that most have had a suboptimal performance on external validation with a C statistic ranging from 0.49 to 0.66, whereas a value $_\mathrm{of}\ge0.8$ is traditionally considered as a threshold for a good predictive model [11]. Several noteworthy differences between the two cohorts may have contributed to the differences in model estimates. The rates of PEG placement were more than double in the derivation compared with the i-DEF cohort, even though the baseline distribution of age, GCS, and ICH volumes appear comparable; however, a striking difference in the race composition of Black participants between the two cohorts was observed $54\%$ in original cohort compared with $22\%$ in i-DEF) (Table  2) [8, 10]. Differences in the race mix, access to home care and rehabilitation, or presence of other unmeasured confounder may have influenced decision making for PEG placement. Severity of dysphagia, endotracheal intubation or other patient attributes may have had a significant influence on PEG placement, which the model did not account for.  

Clinicians often rely on the patient’s neurological and swallowing status, nutritional needs, and the anticipated trajectory of dysphagia recovery in pursuing decision about PEG placement. The trajectory of swallowing recovery can be difficult to anticipate after ICH, as established predictive models have been lacking. Furthermore, a large multicenter clinical trial has raised concerns for the potential of harm in early PEG tube placement after stroke [12]. These considerations fuel uncertainties regarding the need and timing for PEG tube placement after an ICH. The GRAVo score, in this respect, is an elegant predictive tool that uses a few variables to compute a risk score and thus the probability for PEG tube placement after ICH. However, clinicians may want to consider its relatively modest performance on external validation when relying on it for their decision making.

Patient preferences, access to long-term care, efforts to expedite hospital discharge, and provider perceptions of outcomes (cognitive biases) are equally important factors that may influence placement of PEG tubes. These considerations are not fully captured by such predictive instruments. In fact, there is a risk that an incomplete understanding of the process of development and validation of these predictive instruments, or undue reliance on such tools, in making clinical decisions may fuel self-fulfilling prophecies and/or erode patient choice. The procedure for PEG tube placement itself is not technically challenging in most patients, although it carries risks, including that of bowel perforation, peritonitis, bleeding, and infections [13]. However, being fed via a PEG tube is an important quality of life issue. Patient representatives often report dissatisfaction with the adequacy of information and alternative options presented to them prior to PEG placement [14]. Observations from the large Nationwide Inpatient Sample show that PEG placement rates after stroke are significantly higher in racial minorities, Medicaid recipients, and patients with low household incomes [15, 16]. It is unclear whether these differences are due to underlying illness severity or patterns of clinical practice, or whether this reflects a lack of choice and opportunities for patients or their representatives for making informed decisions. The incorporation of race as a predictor for PEG placement, in this context, may carry a risk of furthering disparities in quality care. It is worth noting that the performance of the GRAVo score in our multicenter cohort with or without inclusion of this variable did not improve the prediction of PEG placement.

Despite some of these limitations, GRAVo scores can be helpful for patient counseling and informing decision making on PEG placement in survivors of ICH. Uncertainties in the accuracy of prediction by this instrument should be shared with patients or their representatives. Other overarching issues that pertain to this discussion are the prospects of PEG tube removal in these individuals and their overall outcomes. Statistical models that predict PEG tube removal after stroke are lacking and should be an area of further investigation. Data from large observational cohorts show that patients with stroke who undergo PEG tube placement have high rates of institutionalization, medical complications, and long-term mortality [17]. Information on palliative care options in this context should therefore be incorporated in a shared decision-making process [18].  

Our study methods carry some limitations. We performed model validation using a clinical trial cohort, which may have differences from the patient population encountered in real-life practice. For example, the i-DEF trial did not enroll patients older than 80 years or patients with infratentorial ICH, renal failure, coagulopathy, deep coma with a GCS score $\leq6$ planed hematoma evacuation, or high risk of acute respiratory distress syndrome; therefore, our model estimates may not apply to patients with ICH with these characteristics. Differences in key variables between the derivation and validation cohorts may explain some of the differences in model performance between these two cohorts. Patients in both cohorts were similar in measures of stroke severity (GCS and ICH volumes) but had major differences in the race composition and rates of PEG placement. The race distribution and rates of PEG placement of our multicenter cohort align more closely with the population of hospitalized patients with ICH in the United States [15, 16]. Our cohort did not include any infratentorial ICH, unlike the derivation cohort $(19\%)$ . However, infratentorial location was not found to be a significant predictor of PEG placement in the model development. Although our patients were enrolled at stroke centers across North America, we could not ascertain whether they underwent standardized protocol-based swallowing assessment. Although this may have been desirable, it also reflects real-life practice. The retrospective nature of our study design and analysis is a limitation; on the other hand, all patients in our cohort underwent prospective collection of important clinical variables, such as the computation of ICH volumes, which adds to the reliability of our estimates. Data from the National Inpatient Sample demonstrate that practice settings are another source of variability in PEG placement after ICH; rates of PEG placement differ by geographical regions in the United States, hospital size, and location [15, 16]. The composition and sample size of our validation cohort was not equipped to address these sources of variability. It is therefore important that prediction models such as the GRAVo score undergo validation in diverse practice settings. Finally, exposure to deferoxamine may have affected our model estimates. Although we conducted statistical adjustment for the effects of deferoxamine in our cohort, we cannot rule out the possibility of some residual intervention effect. We, however, expect that this effect would be minor and not alter our estimates significantly.  



# Conclusions  

Our findings demonstrate that GRAVo scores provide modest but statistically significant estimates for PEG placement after acute or subacute ICH. This tool can be useful for patient counseling and informing decision making on this issue. However, our findings urge caution against its use as a primary basis for determining the need for PEG tubes, as this may place the patient’s autonomy and preferences at risk. Future prospective investigations should assess the impact of this instrument in assisting clinical decision making, use of resources, and patient outcomes.
