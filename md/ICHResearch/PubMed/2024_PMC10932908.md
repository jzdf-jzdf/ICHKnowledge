# Abstract  

Background: Non-traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is independently associated with long-term increased risk of major arterial ischemic events. While the relationship between ICH location and ischemic risk has been studied, whether hematoma volume influences this risk is poorly understood.  

Methods: We pooled individual patient data from the MISTIE III (Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus Alteplase for Intracerebral Hemorrhage Evacuation Phase 3) and the ATACH-2 (Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage-2) trials. The exposure was hematoma volume, treated as a continuous measure in the primary analysis, and dichotomized by the median in secondary analyses. The outcome was a symptomatic clinically overt ischemic stroke, adjudicated centrally within each trial. We evaluated the association between hematoma volume and the risk of an ischemic stroke using Cox regression analyses after adjustment for demographics, vascular comorbidities, and ICH characteristics.  

Results: Of $1{,}470\mathrm{ICH}$ patients, the mean age was 61.7 (SD, 12.8) years and 574 $(38.3\%)$ were female. The median hematoma volume was $17.3\mathrm{mL}$ (IQR, 7.2-35.7). During a median follow up of 107 days (IQR, 91-140), a total of 30 ischemic strokes occurred of which 22 were in patients with median ICH volume $\geq17.3~\mathrm{mL}$ with a cumulative incidence of $4.6\%$ ( $95\%$ CI, 3.1-7.1). Among patients with median ICH volume $<17.3\mathrm{mL}$ , there were 8 ischemic strokes with a cumulative incidence of $3.1\%$ $95\%$ CI, 1.7-6.0). In primary analyses, using adjusted Cox regression models, ICH volume was associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke (HR, 1.02 per mL increase; $95\%$ CI, 1.01-1.04). In secondary analyses, ICH volume ${\geq}17.3~\mathrm{mL}$ was associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke (HR, 2.5; $95\%$ CI, 1.1-7.2), compared to those with an ICH volume $<17.3\mathrm{mL}$ .  

Conclusions: In a heterogeneous cohort of ICH patients, initial hematoma volume was associated with a heightened short-term risk of ischemic stroke.

# Introduction  

Survivors of non-traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) face a 2-3-fold increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), including ischemic stroke.1-4 The risk of ischemic stroke is elevated regardless of vascular comorbidities and

antithrombotic medication use.3 In fact, the 1-year cumulative incidence of MACCE is about $8\%$ even in the absence of atrial fibrillation.5 An ischemic stroke adversely affects recovery after ICH and lowers the odds of favorable functional outcomes by more than $50\%$ .6 Therefore, factors that increase stroke risk after ICH have garnered interest. Recent data suggest that Black ICH patients and those of Hispanic ethnicity have a higher ischemic stroke risk, compared to White ICH patients. Furthermore, hematoma location may play a role in that the risk of MACCE is higher after a lobar ICH mainly due to a heightened risk of recurrent ICH, while there appears to be a trend toward more ischemic events among patients with a deep ICH.5,8,9 It is, however, unknown whether other hematoma characteristics such as hematoma volume are also associated with ischemic stroke risk. We therefore sought to evaluate the association between admission hematoma volume and subsequent risk of an ischemic stroke in a pooled, prospective cohort of ICH patients.  

# Methods  

# Data Availability  

De-identified data from the MISTIE III (Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus Alteplase for Intracerebral Hemorrhage Evacuation Phase 3) and the ATACH-2 (Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage-2) trials are available at the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke data archive. To gain access, requesters will need to submit a proposal and sign a data-access agreement.  

# Study Design  

This was a retrospective cohort study using pooled individual patient-level data from patients with ICH enrolled in two clinical trials, the ATACH-2 trial,10,11 and the MISTIE III trial.12 The trial protocols were approved by an ethics committee at each enrolling site, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant or the legal surrogate. The institutional review board of Weill Cornell Medicine approved this study. This study was performed in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational studies (Online Only Supplemental Files).13  

# Study Setting  

For this study, we pooled data from 2 ICH clinical trials. ATACH-2 was a randomized, multicenter, open-label trial that compared the efficacy of intensive versus standard blood pressure management initiated within 4.5 hours of ICH onset in patients with spontaneous supratentorial ICH, and showed no difference in the primary endpoint of death or major disability at 90 days.10 The inclusion criteria for ATACH-2 were adult patients 18 years of age or older with a Glasgow Coma Scale score $\geq5$ at presentation, and initial hematoma volume $<60\mathrm{mL}$ .10  

MISTIE III was an open-label, blinded endpoint trial, where patients with a moderate to large, non-traumatic supratentorial ICH were randomized to stereotactic minimally invasive surgery with concurrent fibrinolysis, as compared to conservative medical management.12 Although there was a mortality benefit to stereotactic surgery with fibrinolysis, the trial was neutral on the 6-month primary composite endpoint of major disability or death. Inclusion criteria for MISTIE III were age $\geq18$ years, ICH $\ge30\mathrm{mL}$ , Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14 or less or National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score $\geq6$ , and demonstration of ICH volume stability for at least 6 hours.12 Both trials excluded secondary causes of ICH and patients with planned withdrawal of care.  

# Measurements  

We included all ICH patients enrolled in the ATACH-2 and MISTIE III trials. The exposure was hematoma volume, ascertained on the computed tomography scan at the time of admission, using semi-automated planimetry. In each study, a neuroimaging core of board-certified neurologists and radiologists blinded to treatment assignment and outcome evaluated the computed tomography scans of the head. Hematoma location was classified as lobar ICH if there was selective involvement of cerebral cortex, underlying white matter, or both, and as deep ICH if there was selective involvement of thalami, basal ganglia, or both. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were obtained at the discretion of the treating physicians in the ATACH-2 trial. 14 In the MISTIE III trial, one of two protocolized MRI scans were obtained during hospitalization- on the day of randomization, and between days 7 and 10 after randomization, with the exception of patient instability, withdrawal of care, death, or contraindications for MRI.15  

The main outcome was an ischemic stroke. Clinical criteria for cerebral infarction were based on the presence of focal and/or global neurologic deficits (sensory, motor, cranial nerve, or speech). Radiologic criteria (CT or MRI) for cerebral infarction were based on the presence of focal and/or global abnormalities in brain images that indicated regions which had lost viability. Intensity was graded as either asymptomatic or symptomatic. For the present study, we only included symptomatic events. All ischemic strokes were reported by site investigators, independently reviewed by the coordinating center, and adjudicated by the safety events committee within each trial. MISTIE III had follow-up data at 3-months, 6-months, and 1-year, while ATACH-2 only had 90-day outcomes. To maintain uniform follow-up times, we only counted the first 120 days after discharge, including a 30-day grace period for the ascertainment of 3-month outcomes.  

# Statistical Analysis  

We used standard descriptive statistics to describe patient characteristics. Pearson Chisquared and Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Kaplan-Meier survival statistics were used to estimate the incidence of stroke among participants based on the median hematoma volume.  

We evaluated the association between hematoma volume and the risk of an ischemic stroke using Cox regression analyses after adjustment for demographics, vascular comorbidities, and ICH characteristics. In the primary analysis, hematoma volume was modeled as a continuous exposure, while in secondary analyses the hematoma volume was dichotomized by the median ( $<17.3~\mathrm{mL}$ vs. $\geq17.3~\mathrm{mL}$ ). Since there were a total of 30 ischemic stroke outcomes, we built 4 different models adjusting for 3 covariates at a time, chosen a priori, to prevent overfitting of the model. The first model was unadjusted; the second was adjusted for universal confounders, in particular, age, sex, and race; the third included age and vascular comorbidities (atrial fibrillation, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack [TIA]); the fourth included ICH characteristics (hematoma location, presence of intraventricular hemorrhage [IVH], treatment randomization arm); and the fifth model adjusted for presence of IVH, hematoma location, and the $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -Vasc score ( $\scriptstyle\geq{2}$ vs. <2). Our rationale for using the $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -Vasc score was that the individual components of the score are independent stroke risk factors even in the absence of atrial fibrillation. Patients were censored at the time of death, occurrence of outcome, or at the last available follow-up (day 100). We assessed the overall fit of the model using Cox-Snell residuals, and constructed log-log plots to ensure that the proportional hazard assumption was not violated. To account for clustering of data from trials, we chose the vce (cluster) option to account for standard errors as a function of between-trials variation.  

In three separate sensitivity analyses, we treated hematoma volume as a continuous measure after natural logarithmic transformation to approximate linearity, excluded patients in the MISTIE III trial who underwent surgical hematoma evacuation, and excluded patients in the ATACH-2 trial who received intensive blood pressure reduction. All sensitivity analyses entailed the 5 Cox regression models described above. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (StataCorp. Statistical Software: Release 16. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP; 2019). All analyses were 2-tailed and the threshold for statistical significance was $\mathsf{p}{<}0.05$ .  

# Results  

# Study Population  

Among the 1,499 ICH patients, we excluded 29 who were lost to follow up and therefore had missing data on ischemic stroke (Figure 1). Of the $1{,}470\mathrm{ICH}$ patients included in the analysis, the mean age was 61.7 (SD, 12.8) years and 574 $(38.3\%)$ were female. Baseline characteristics of ICH patients in the ATACH-2 and MISTIE III trials are shown in Table 1 and Supplemental Tables S1 and S2. The median hematoma volume was 17.3 mL (IQR, 7.2-35.7). When stratified by the median hematoma volume, patients with volume $\geq17.3~\mathrm{mL}$ had higher proportions of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and prior stroke/TIA. Furthermore, the proportion of patients with IVH and lobar ICH were higher in the group with hematoma volumes over the median cut-off (Table 2).  

# Primary Analysis  

During a median follow up of 107 days (IQR, 91-140), a total of 30 ischemic strokes occurred of which 22 were in patients with median ICH volume ${\geq}17.3~\mathrm{mL}$ with a cumulative incidence of $4.6\%$ $95\%$ CI, 3.1-7.1). Among patients with median ICH volume $<17.3~\mathrm{mL}$ , there were 8 ischemic strokes with a cumulative incidence of $3.1\%$ $95\%$ CI, 1.7-6.0) (Figure 2). In 5 different Cox regression models, hematoma volume assessed as a continuous exposure was associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke (Table 3 and Supplemental Table S3). The first model was unadjusted (hazard ratio [HR], 1.03 per mL increase; $95\%$ CI, 1.01-1.05). The second model adjusted for age, sex and race (HR, 1.02 per mL increase; $95\%$ CI, 1.01-1.04) suggesting that the risk of an ischemic stroke increased by $2\%$ , for every 1mL increase in hematoma volume. In the third model, we controlled for age, atrial fibrillation, and prior stroke/TIA (HR, 1.03 per mL increase; $95\%$ CI, 1.01-1.07) i.e., $3\%$ increased risk of ischemic stroke with $1\mathrm{mL}$ increase in hematoma volume. The fourth model included hematoma location, presence of IVH, and treatment randomization arm as covariates (HR, 1.02 per mL; $95\%$ CI, 1.01-1.04). Lastly, in the fifth model, the covariates included presence of IVH, hematoma location, and the $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -Vasc score (HR, 1.01 per mL increase; $95\%$ CI, 1.01-1.03).  

# Secondary Analysis  

In multivariate Cox regression models using hematoma volume dichotomized by the median, ICH volume $\mathrm{217.3~mL}$ was associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke in the unadjusted model 1 (HR, 3.0; $95\%$ CI, 1.3-7.0); in model 2 after adjusting for demographics (HR, 3.1; $95\%$ CI, 1.3-7.6); in model 3 after accounting for age, atrial fibrillation, and prior stroke/TIA (HR, 2.9; $95\%$ CI, 1.3-7.0); in model 4 controlling for hematoma location, presence of IVH, and treatment randomization arm (HR, $3.595\%$ CI, 1.5-7.3); and finally, in model 5 that controlled for the presence of IVH, hematoma location, and $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -Vasc score (HR, 2.5; $95\%$ CI, 1.1-7.2) (Table 3 and Supplemental Table S3).  

# Sensitivity Analysis  

In sensitivity analyses treating log-transformed hematoma volume, after excluding 250 patients randomized to surgical hematoma evacuation in the MISTIE III trial, and after the exclusion of 500 patients in the ATACH-2 trial who received intensive blood pressure reduction, we continued to observe a significant association between admission hematoma volume and an increased risk of ischemic stroke (Supplemental Tables S4 and S5).  

# Discussion  

In a pooled heterogeneous clinical trial cohort of ICH patients, the admission hematoma volume was independently associated with a heightened short-term risk of ischemic stroke. The increased stroke risk was observed when the hematoma volume was treated as a continuous variable and subsequently, dichotomized by the median. The risk of an ischemic stroke increased by about $2\%$ , for every 1mL increase in hematoma volume.  

Several recent studies have shown a link between ICH and subsequent risk of MACCE.1-4 This increased risk of MACCE is influenced by demographic factors, mainly race and ethnicity7, and ICH-related factors, particularly hematoma location.8,9 However, since prior studies mainly examined large population cohorts, they lacked important ICH characteristics such as admission hematoma volume and presence of intraventricular hemorrhage. In this context, our findings from 2 large prospective ICH cohorts, with protocolized, blinded ascertainment of neuroimaging markers, and blinded outcome assessment, offers novel insight into the relationship between hematoma volume and risk of subsequent ischemic stroke.  

One plausible explanation for the relationship between hematoma volume and short-term stroke risk is the withholding of antithrombotic therapy, both short- and long-term. For instance, rates of resumption of antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications at the time of discharge from ICH hospitalization are as low as $16.6\%$ and $11.1\%$ , respectively. At  

1-year follow up, the proportion of ICH patients with concurrent atrial fibrillation who have antiplatelet medication prescription is $43.6\%$ , while those with anticoagulation therapy prescribed is $11\%$ , reflecting the hesitancy in the use of antithrombotic therapy after ICH.17 Another reason for the elevated stroke risk could be the severity of underlying cerebral small vessel disease. Acute punctate infarcts on diffusion-weighted imaging in the acute period of ICH are believed to be precipitated by cerebral small vessel disease with superimposed blood pressure changes.18 In fact, diffusion-weighted imaging lesions have been correlated with admission hematoma volume.19 Furthermore, these lesions, in turn, lead to a 3-fold increased risk of recurrent stroke20, shedding light on the impact of admission hematoma volumes on subsequent ischemic cerebrovascular events. Impaired access to healthcare and the resulting poor risk factor management could also contribute to the stroke risk, since patients with larger hematomas are more likely to have major disability. Lastly, activation of thrombo-inflammatory cascades by the hematoma products, centrally and peripherally, may also play a role in thrombotic events after ICH.22,23  

This study has some notable limitations. First, our findings may not be generalizable to the ICH population at large, given that our pooled cohort was subject to the inclusion criteria of the ATACH-2 and MISTIE III trials. Second, data were not available on antithrombotic medication use after ICH, a proven therapy for secondary stroke prevention.24 Presumably, clinicians may have been less enthusiastic about prescribing these medications with larger ICH volumes due to the perceived higher risk of ICH recurrence, which could have affected our results. Third, only a subset of patients had magnetic resonance imaging of the brain which precluded assessment of the severity of cerebral small vessel disease in the entire cohort. Fourth, we lacked clinical data to ascertain the etiological subtype of stroke. Fifth, given the short follow up of 100 days in our study, the ascertainment rates may have differed over time with missed events at later time points. It is also possible that the variability in the timing of imaging (CT and/or MRI scans) during follow up across the two cohorts may have resulted in the underreporting of the frequency of cerebral infarcts. However, since we considered only symptomatic events which presumably would have warranted further neuroimaging as part of standard medical care, we believe the lack of scheduled follow up imaging likely did not play a significant role in influencing our results. Furthermore, ischemic stroke events may have been undercounted in patients with large ICH, where changes in clinical examination may have been difficult to discern given the severity of ICH. While the low number of ischemic stroke events limited the number of covariates in the Cox regression models, it was reassuring that regardless of the combination of covariates, the HR remained persistently elevated. Finally, although we had information on ICH location, we did not have data on the etiology of ICH, a factor which may have impacted the risk of recurrent stroke in our ICH cohort.9  

# Conclusions  

In a large prospective cohort of ICH survivors, admission hematoma volume was associated with an increased short-term risk of ischemic stroke. Given that incident ischemic stroke events adversely affect functional recovery after ICH, further study of the link between ICH characteristics and subsequent ischemic stroke may yield new strategies for improving long-term outcomes after ICH.
