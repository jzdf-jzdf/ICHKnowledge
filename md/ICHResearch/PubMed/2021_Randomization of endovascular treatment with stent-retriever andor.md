# Randomization of endovascular treatment with stent-retriever and/or thromboaspiration versus best medical therapy in acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion trial: Rationale and design  

Raul G Nogueira', Fabricio O Lima2,3, Octavio M Pontes-Neto4, Gisele S Silva5.6, Francisco Jose Mont'Alverne’, Daniel G Abud, Michel Frudit9, Paulo Passos10, Diogo C Haussen1, Guilherme Dabus11, Gabriel R de Freitas12, Jamary Oliveira-Filho13, Daniel C Bezerra14, David S Liebeskind15, Mario B Wagner'6, Jose EF Passos'7, Carlos A Molina'8 Joseph Broderick19, Jeffrey L Saver15 and Sheila O Martins10,20 iD  

# Abstract  

Background: RESILIENT is a prospective, multicenter, randomized phase III trial to test the safety, efficacy, and costeffectiveness of mechanical thrombectomy as compared to medical treatment alone in patients treated under the less than ideal conditions typically found in the public healthcare system of a developing country.  

Methods: Subjects must fulfill the following main inclusion criteria: symptom onset $\le8\mathrm{h}$ , $\mathsf{age\geql8}$ years, baseline NIHSS ${\ge}8$ , evidence of intracranial ICA or proximal MCA (M1 segment) occlusion, ASPECTS ${\ge}6$ on CTor ${>}5$ on DWI-MRI and be either ineligible for or unresponsive to intravenous alteplase. The primary end-point is the distribution of disability levels (on the modified Rankin Scale, mRS) at 90 days under the intention-to-treat principle.  

Randomization: Randomization is performed under a minimization process using age, baseline NIHSS, intravenous alteplase use, occlusion site and center.  

Design: The trial is designed with an expectation of a $10\%$ difference in the proportion of favorable outcome (mRS 0–2 at 90 days) common odds ratio of 1.615.  

Primary outcome: Projected sample size is 690 subjects with pre-planned interim analyses at 174, 346, and 518 subjects.  

Secondary outcomes: Secondary end-points include: 90-day functional independence $(\mathsf{mRS}\leq2)$ , $\mathsf{mRS}$ shift stratified for treatment with IV rt-PA at 90 days, infarct volume on $24\mathsf{h}$ CT or MRI, early dramatic response (NIHSS 0–2 or improvement ${\ge}8$ points) at $24\mathsf{h}$ , vessel recanalization evaluated by CTA or MRA at $24\mathsf{h}$ , and the post-procedure rate of successful reperfusion (defined as a modified Treatment in Cerebral Infarction 2b or greater). Safety variables are mortality at 90 days, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage at $24\mathsf{h}$ and procedure-related complications.

# Background  

Intravenous (IV) recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA, alteplase) has had a major impact on the outcomes of acute ischemic stroke patients (AIS).1–3 In large part due to its ease of administration, relatively simple treatment logistics, and highly favorable cost-effectiveness profile, IV alteplase has had an increasingly broad global adoption remaining the only approved treatment for AIS until 2014.4 However, it has a narrow therapeutic window, strong time dependence, and a limited benefit in strokes caused by large vessel occlusion (LVO), which are the most disabling type of AIS and account for as many as 30 to $40\%$ of all cases. Indeed, the rates of recanalization with IV alteplase range from only $10\%$ in cases of occlusion of the internal carotid artery (ICA) to $30\%$ in cases of occlusion of the middle cerebral artery (MCA).5  

For LVO strokes, mechanical thrombectomy (MT) adds the possibility of treatment beyond the conventional therapeutic window, with lower chances of hemorrhagic complications, greater chance of reperfusion and better functional outcomes. Between 2014 and 2015, five randomized controlled trials were published comparing MT associated with the best medical treatment versus best medical treatment alone. These studies demonstrated an overwhelming superiority to endovascular treatment with odds ratio ranging from 1.8 to 4.2 and a number need to treat ranging between 3.2 and 7 to achieve favorable outcome.6–10  

However, all these studies were exclusively carried out within the wealthy systems of high-income countries and it remains unknown whether hospitals in the public healthcare system of developing countries have enough organizational infrastructure and resources to quickly triage, evaluate, and treat these patients with similar safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness profile. Several thrombectomy devices are currently approved by the Brazilian National Agency of Sanitary Surveillance (ANVISA). However, given the lack of a national healthcare policy to cover thrombectomy in the public system, endovascular reperfusion is only available to the small minority of Brazilians who can afford private healthcare. In the present study, we aim to assess whether MT with stent-retriever and/or thromboaspiration is superior to standard medical treatment alone in achieving more favorable outcomes in AIS patients treated within the public system of Brazil, a middle-income country. In addition, we plan to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of thrombectomy in our healthcare reality.  

# Methods  

# Study design  

RESILIENT is a randomized, prospective, multicenter, controlled, open-label clinical trial with blinded outcome assessment with sequential design. Randomization will be 1:1 for MT with stent-retriever and/or thromboaspiration versus medical treatment alone and performed under a minimization process via a centralized website. For the primary outcome, subjects will be followed for 90 days post randomization. Approximately 20 comprehensive stroke centers in Brazil will participate.  

# Patient population  

Subjects presenting with AIS within $^\mathrm{8h}$ from symptom onset and attributable to an occlusion of the intracranial ICA and/or proximal segment of the MCA (M1). Subjects are either ineligible for alteplase IV or have received alteplase IV without clinical improvement. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table 1.

# Baseline measures  

All patients will have standard assessments for demographics, past medical history, current medications, baseline laboratory tests, and stroke severity (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, NIHSS). Baseline neurological impairment (e.g. NIHSS) and functional (e.g. modified Rankin scale, mRS) scores will be performed by a certified physician.  

# Randomization  

An interactive web response system randomization procedure is implemented via the RESILIENT Trial website. After eligibility status is confirmed, the subject will be randomized into equal numbers (1:1) for one of the two arms of the study (stent-retriever thrombectomy and/or thromboaspiration plus standard medical treatment [intervention group] vs. standard medical treatment alone [control group]). Randomization will use a minimization process to balance the numbers of patients across the two groups with respect to age $\leq70$ or $>70$ years), baseline NIHSS score (8–16 or $\geq17,$ ), intravenous alteplase use, occlusion site (intracranial ICA or MCA-M1), and participating center. This minimization procedure will lower the chances of any significant discrepancies in important baseline prognostic factors that may occur by chance and confound the primary outcome while also preserving a greater degree of randomness.  

# Treatment  

For the purposes of the RESILIENT study, only vascular neurologists and neurointerventionists in comprehensive stroke centers that have proper training in acute stroke therapy will treat patients. In addition, thrombectomy procedures may only be conducted by neurointerventionists who have performed at least five prior MT procedures in AIS patients. The prior MT procedures may be accomplished in the private or public sectors. In order to ensure proper qualification of the entire team and system, participating sites that have no prior experience with thrombectomy will be required to perform at least three to five MTs in a roll-in phase regardless of the individual level of experience of the local operators. Neuroimaging interpretation, time metrics, and angiographic and clinical outcomes of these roll-in cases will be reviewed by a quality assurance committee in order to decide whether additional roll-in cases are required or the center is approved to start the randomization process.  

Patients will be admitted to acute stroke or intensive care units and both treatment groups will receive the same medical care following the Brazilian Stroke Guidelines and the American Heart Association (AHA) Guidelines.11,12 Any intraarterial or intravenous administration of thrombolytics occurring after randomization is forbidden. Moreover, in order to allow an intention to treat interpretation, no crossover is permitted.  

Endovascular therapy in RESILENT should strictly adhere to the following principles:  

1. Only MT with pre-approved devices (stent retrievers and/or thromboaspiration) will be allowed in the RESILENT study.   
2. If MT fails after a maximum of six passages per vessel, no other pharmacological or mechanical rescue therapy is allowed. The maximum number of passes with the same device will follow the manufacturer specific instructions (IFU).   
3. Balloon angioplasty and/or extracranial ICA stent implantation in cases with tandem ICA/M1 occlusions will be allowed according to specific local protocols.   
4. Angioplasty and/or intracranial vessel stent implantation beyond the ICA petrous segment will not be allowed.   
5. The use of a balloon guide catheter in proximal ICA and/or local aspiration with a large-caliber aspiration catheter is strongly encouraged.   
6. All interventional treatments must be started (defined as the time of arterial puncture) no later than $^\mathrm{8h}$ relative to the time the subject was last seen well. Arterial access should be obtained within $60\mathrm{min}$ of randomization, not exceeding $90\mathrm{min}$ (ideally $60\mathrm{{min})}$ following CT or MRI imaging evaluation. Termination of the endovascular treatment will occur if: (1) neurological deterioration is detected, leading to suspected intracranial hemorrhage; (2) the duration of endovascular treatment reaches $^{3\mathrm{h}}$ ; (3) an mTICI flow rate 2b or 3 is restored; (4) occlusion is refractory to six attempts at thrombectomy with stent-retrievers and/or thrombus aspiration in the same vessel.  

# Primary outcome  

The primary outcome will be the distribution of 90-day levels of disability on the mRS (shift analysis) as assessed by central adjudication of video recordings of structured patient and/or family interviews.13–15 Central mRS scores will be assigned by two blinded certified neurologists, with any differences resolved by consensus discussion.  

# Secondary and safety outcomes  

All secondary and safety endpoints will be assessed by blinded local staff to the treatment arm (for clinical scales) or by the independent Clinical Events Committee (CEC) (for medical and procedural complications) and the Imaging Core Lab (for parenchymal and vascular imaging variables). Secondary efficacy and the safety endpoints are shown in Tables 2 and 3.  

# Blinding, masking, and outcome adjudication  

Each site will designate one or more trained physicians to perform the follow-up evaluation at 24 $(-2/+12)$ h and, 5 $(\pm\nobreakspace2)$ days or prior to discharge if discharge occurs before five-days. At the three month-follow-up visits, a locally designated independent, blinded neurologist not involved in the trial patient management, will record the mRS score in a face-to-face structured clinical interview16,17 and record the interview using a portable camera. For the primary end-point, the video recording will be evaluated by two blinded independent mRS-certified neurologists who will centrally adjudicate the mRS score. Disagreements will be resolved by consensus. Data entry forms will not reveal the study arm assignment and patients will be instructed not to reveal any relevant information that can potentially lead to disclosing their treatment group to the evaluators. In cases in which the video recording is unavailable, outcomes will be determined by blinded local investigators using the same structured interviews. Cases missing 90- day follow-up will be assigned to the mRS at discharge unless their living status is unknown in which case, they will be assigned the worst outcome (e.g. death, mRS 6). All neuroimaging secondary end-points including recanalization at $24\mathrm{{h}}$ , infarct volume, and hemorrhage will be determined by the Imaging Core Lab, which will be also blinded to treatment allocation. Successful recanalization in the post-procedure angiography in the thrombectomy group will also be classified by the Imaging Core-Lab. Serious adverse events (SAEs) and procedural related complications will be adjudicated by two members of the independent Clinical Events Committee that will be unblinded to treatment arm.

# DSMB/safety monitoring  

An independent Data Safety Monitoring Board will be established (DSMB). The DSMB will be composed of three stroke neurologists who are not affiliated with the sponsor nor are participating in the trial and a statistician. The DSMB will meet on a regular basis and its role will be to: (1) make recommendations to the Executive Committee regarding stopping or extending the trial based on the pre-planned efficacy interim analysis; and (2) review the occurrence of adverse events and make recommendations to the Executive Committee regarding safety of the study.  

# Imaging core laboratory  

A centralized Imaging Core Lab will be used in this study to provide independent and systematic, expert evaluation of images. The imaging data will be evaluated for completeness and reviewed for compliance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria as well as for safety and protocol deviations. The core lab will review all CT and MRI images independently from the angiographic images. The baseline CT/MR images will be reviewed for the confirmation of inclusion criteria (occlusion of the intracranial ICA and/or MCA-M1 with or without concomitant cervical carotid occlusion or stenosis; ASPECTS score), presence/absence of hemorrhage and collateral status. If CT/MRI perfusion is performed, the qualifying inclusion parameters will be also reviewed. The follow-up CT/MR images at 2 $4(-2/+12)$ h will be analyzed for vessel patency and infarct volume as well as any hemorrhagic transformation. MRI will be the preferred imaging method for infarct volume measurement, but follow-up MRI is unlikely to be available in most patients. Bleeding will be classified according to the method used in the ECASS trials.18,19 Symptomatic ICH will be defined as per the SITS-MOST definition: local or remote parenchymal hemorrhage type 2 on follow-up imaging, combined with a neurological deterioration of $\geq4$ points on the NIHSS from baseline, or from the lowest NIHSS value between baseline and $24\mathrm{{h}}$ , or leading to death. In addition, non-parenchymal and subarachnoid hemorrhages will be described. Pretreatment conventional angiography images will be analyzed to determine clot location, pre-treatment perfusion status (e.g. baseline modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction [mTICI] scale score), and collateral status (according to the ASITN angiographic collateral flow classification). Post-treatment conventional angiography images will be analyzed for reperfusion status according to the mTICI scale. Angiographic images will also be evaluated for evidence of any procedural complications including arterial perforation, arterial dissection, and embolization to a previously uninvolved vascular territory. The core lab will not provide any information to the investigators other than data about any deviations from the neuroimaging inclusion criteria.  

# Statistical analysis plan and sample size  

The primary objective of the RESILIENT study is to demonstrate that MT can be safely applied at the level of the public system of a developing country and its efficacy and cost-effectiveness is maintained despite the many limitations existent in such healthcare environment. The statistical analysis plan followed the assumptions generated by the REVASCAT investigators.20 For the treatment arm (thrombectomy), the good outcome rate $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq2)$ at 90 days was estimated at $35\%$ for the population with a median baseline NIHSS score of 17, based on the SONIIA registry of patients treated with endovascular therapy in the centers of stroke in Catalonia (data from 2011, updated in April 2012), which met the main inclusion criteria of the RESILIENT study. For the control group (clinical treatment only), the good outcome rate was estimated at $25\%$ based on the relevant literature. These estimates generated an expected difference in the proportions of 0.10 for good evolution, leading to an odds ratio of $(0.35/(1-0.35))/(0.25/(1-0.25))=1.615$ . This odds ratio is therefore chosen as an alternative hypothesis for improvement over any possible cut-off point over the 90-day mRS with analysis by means of ordinal logistic regression for subjects treated with the endovascular approach compared with those treated with standard clinical therapy alone. Unadjusted and adjusted estimates allowing for the potential confounding effects of all minimization factors will be presented for primary and secondary endpoints. The impact of effect modification (interaction) will also be explored and reported. A computer simulation found the total sample size required with these expected proportions and assuming unilateral type I and type II error probabilities of 0.025 and 0.10, respectively, to be 690. For the planned interim analyses in the sequential design, the Lan-DeMets alpha spending approach based on a power function with $\mathrm{phi}=1$ was used to derive stopping boundaries.21 The number of patients and the theoretical probabilities of stopping the study by efficacy or futility at each interim analysis, established through computer simulation for the null and alternative hypotheses are shown in Table 4.

# Historical context and relationship with the other mechanical thrombectomy trials  

The RESILIENT trial was designed in close resemblance with the REVASCAT trial.20 There were two main reasons behind this decision: (1) as there is very limited experience with endovascular stroke treatment in the Brazilian Public Healthcare System, there was not enough data to generate reliable hypotheses for sample size calculations, and (2) given the many other ongoing thrombectomy trials, a pre-specified plan for an eventual pooled analysis of two similar trials would increase the likelihood of generating more impactful data. As such, only few modifications were made in terms of the inclusion and exclusion criteria and the overall assumptions were similar across the two trials despite an expectation for worse overall outcomes in RESILIENT given the socio-economic differences across the two populations and the significant lower healthcare resources in the Brazilian public healthcare system as compared to Catalunya. Amongst the modifications are the absence of an upper age requirement in  

RESILIENT versus a $\le80$ -year cut-off in REVASCAT, the requirement for a higher baseline NIHSS score in RESILIENT vs. REVASCAT ( $\geq8$ vs. ${\geq}6,$ ), the lack of a requirement in RESILIENT to wait a minimum of $30\mathrm{min}$ after the initiation of the IV alteplase infusion for the performance of the vessel imaging (CTA or MRA) to confirm persistent intracranial occlusion, and the inclusion of ASPECTS cutoffs that were 1- point lower in RESILIENT versus REVASCAT (e.g. exclusion: NCCT ASPECTS ${<}6$ vs. $^{<7}$ or DWI-MRI ASPECTS ${<}5$ vs. ${<}6$ ). In addition, REVASCAT mandated that ASPECTS had to be evaluated by CBV maps of CT perfusion, CTA source imaging (CTASI), or DWI-MR in patients whose vascular occlusion study (CTA/MR) confirming qualifying occlusion was performed beyond $4.5\mathrm{h}$ of last seen well, a requirement that does not exist in RESILIENT. Finally, there is a stronger encouragement for the use of a balloon guide catheter and/or local aspiration in RESILIENT.  

The RESILIENT trial was originally submitted for national approval in March 2013. On 22 January 2014, the trial received an unrestricted grant from the Department of Science and Technology of the Brazilian Ministry of Health. The trial also received unrestricted grants from Medtronic (Irvine, CA) and Penumbra (Alameda, CA) for the donation of the thrombectomy devices, pending regulatory approval. The approval by the National Ethics Committee occurred on 13 November 2014. In the meantime, the results of the MR CLEAN trial were presented in October 2014, followed by the presentations of the ESCAPE, SWIFT Prime, EXTEND-IA trials in February 2015. Few weeks later, members from the RESILIENT trial steering committee met with representatives from the Brazilian Ministry of Health to discuss the strongly positive new evidence about endovascular stroke treatment and inquire whether the RESILIENT study could be converted from a randomized controlled trial into a prospective mechanical thrombectomy registry that would allow for the inclusion of all qualifying patients.  

After carefully analyzing the new evidence, the Ministry of Health concluded that all the aforementioned trials had taken place in healthcare environments that were far from the reality of the Brazilian public system and, therefore, there was not enough data to support the feasibility, safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness of thrombectomy in the national public system. As such, the government continued to expect and support a randomized clinical trial to assess whether thrombectomy should be incorporated into the Brazilian Universal Public Healthcare System.

# Discussion  

There are many reasons why the results of the published thrombectomy trials cannot be directly applied to low- and middle-income countries. Due to their lower socio-economic and nutritional status, the population assisted by the public healthcare system in lowand middle-income countries is, in general, much more vulnerable and will likely have worse outcomes despite technically successful treatments. Given the more limited resources and the lower educational level of the population, emergency medical service (EMS) transportation is much less frequently utilized in lowand middle-income countries. This can negatively impact several steps of the pre-hospital care, including the proper patient triage to a stroke center (as opposed to non-stroke ready hospitals) and the pre-notification process, potentially resulting in delays and less effective treatment. Brazil has a public free EMS with a single national call number (SAMU 192) covering $70\%$ of its population. However, even in Brazilian metropolitan areas, less than $20\%$ of the stroke patients arrive to the hospital via EMS despite the fact that EMS arrival has been associated with over two-fold increase in intravenous thrombolysis utilization as compared to other means of hospital arrival.22 In North America and Europe, most of the neuroendovascular interventions are performed in bi-plane angiography suites. This can theoretically improve the speed, safety, and efficacy of the procedures. In contrast, nearly all neuroendovascular procedures performed in under-resourced countries are done with single-plane equipment. Furthermore, the angiography suite is often shared with cardiologists, vascular surgeons, and peripheral interventional radiologists, which may hinder the rapid access of acute stroke patients to endovascular treatment. The impact of post-discharge neurorehabilitation on stroke outcomes is well established,23,24 yet access to outpatient rehabilitation in the public health system of developing countries is extremely restricted and often completely inexistent.  

The importance of properly addressing the applicability of MT in under-resourced countries cannot be underestimated. Endovascular treatment of stroke is one of the highest efficacy treatments in medicine. Yet, due to its difficult implementation and high costs, MT is routinely available in few countries which limits its global effectiveness. The incidence of stroke is declining worldwide, largely as a result of better control of hypertension and other vascular risk factors including smoking. However, the absolute number of strokes continues to climb, as a consequence of population growth and ageing. The overall increase in the burden of stroke is expected to be even higher in low- and middle-income countries as they undergo the epidemiological transition from infectious to non-communicable diseases, such as stroke and cardiovascular disease, as the predominant cause of morbidity and mortality. Moreover, the fact that stroke tends to affect patients at younger age in low- and middle-income countries further potentiates the higher socio-economic impact of the disease in these regions. Even within the same country, socio-economic status is important in explaining disparities in the stroke burden amongst the wealthy and the poor. In Sao Paulo, Brazil, for example, a clear regional clustering leading to a geographical pattern of high stroke mortality related to socio-economic differences has been described.25  

Paradoxically, the regions that are most likely to benefit from thrombectomy are exactly the ones that are currently deprived of it. From a political and economic perspective, low- and middle-income countries not only have lower per capita gross national income but also tend to invest less of their budget in public healthcare. Taking as an example, the government health expenditures for Brazil, Mexico, and Colombia (the three most populated countries of Latin America) account for only $2.9\%$ to $3.9\%$ of their gross domestic product and $10\%$ to $13\%$ of their general government expenditures. In contrast, Chile’s government health expenditures accounts for $5.0\%$ and $20\%$ of its gross domestic product and general government expenditure, respectively.26 As high-income countries like Chile not only have more funds but also have policies for greater health prioritization, they are exactly the ones that have an existent infrastructure to more easily incorporate thrombectomy and optimize stroke care.27  

Brazil is the only country with more than 100 million inhabitants that accepted the challenge of a universal public healthcare system (SUS). Indeed, SUS is the largest nondiscriminatory government run public healthcare system in the world. The system is entirely free of any cost, for any person, including foreigners, virtually covering $100\%$ of the over 210 million Brazilians through a network of over 50,000 affiliated sites distributed across the country’s 3.2 million square miles. Currently, over three-quarters of the population exclusively depend on SUS for health services. Since its inception in 1990, great progress has been made to reduce disparities in access to medical care and improve healthcare outcomes. However, there are many challenges to SUS. There is a high degree of complexity associated with the country’s size and regional variations that promotes the perpetuation of inequalities in terms of access and quality of care. Undoubtedly, one of the main challenges is related to its fragile funding structure which has been further aggravated by the recent economic and political crises. Moreover, given its tropical location and incomplete sanitation infra-structure, Brazil frequently suffers from epidemics such as the current crisis with dengue fever, chikungunya, yellow fever, and Zika infection, which consume large amounts of human and financial resources. In this context, it seems quite logical why governmental healthcare authorities would question the local validity of data obtained in resource-rich environments that are completely dissonant from the capabilities of their public healthcare systems and do not share their same challenges and priorities.  

In conclusion, despite the enormous health and socio-economic impact of stroke in low- and middleincome countries, limited attention has been paid by their governments to the growing opportunities to reduce the burden of stroke on patients, their families, and society as a whole. Future studies should therefore properly acknowledge and address the many challenges to the implementation of stroke care in these underresourced regions. The RESILIENT trial hopes to fill some of the gaps in the current evidence by providing unequivocal proof about the feasibility, safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness of mechanical thrombectomy in the public healthcare system of a developing country.
