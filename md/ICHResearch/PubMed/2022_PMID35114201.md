Background: In recent randomized controlled studies, the prevention of stroke and cognitive function of ticagrelor has been controversial. We conducted a meta-analysis to compare ticagrelor with other antiplatelet treatment in patients with vascular high-risk factors disease, defined as acute coronary syndrome, stroke or transient ischemic attack, coronary artery disease or peripheral artery disease.  

Methods: We searched the PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane libraries for published randomized controlled trials and additional available data from ClinicalTrials.gov. The primary outcome was related adverse stroke events and the secondary outcome was cognitive function related adverse events. The outcomes were statistically analyzed using Peto odds ratio.  

Results: 12 RCTs with 105,654 patients were included in meta-analysis. Primary outcomes: all stroke (OR 0.84, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.78–0.90, $P<0.001$ ); Secondary outcomes: ischemic stroke (OR 0.83, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.77–0.90, $\mathbf{P}<0.001,$ ), transient ischemic attack (OR 0.78, $95\%\mathrm{CI~}0.62–0.97$ , $P=0.029;$ ), intracranial hemorrhage (OR 1.33, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 1.09–1.61, $P=0.005^{\cdot}$ ), Parkinson's disease (OR 0.30, $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.12–0.72$ , $P=0.007.$ ), dementia (OR 0.31, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.13–0.77, $P=0.012$ ), dizziness (OR: 1.39, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 1.03–1.87, $P=0.032;$ ), insomnia (OR 1.45, $95\%\mathrm{CI}1.05–2.00$ , $P=0.026^{\circ}$ .  

Conclusions: Ticagrelor may provide more favorable outcomes for all stroke, ischemic stroke, and transient ischemic attack prevention in patients with vascular high-risk factors. However, this benefit may come with the cost of intracranial hemorrhage, dizziness and insomnia. Ticagrelor may reduce the risk of dementia and Par­ kinson's disease, although available data are limited.  



# 1. Introduction  

Stroke is the second most common cause of death in the world, and it has become the main cause of affecting the quality of human life [1]. In addition, stroke-related sequelae also have an important influence on patients' health. For example, stroke may cause cognitive impairment, dementia and other cognitive related adverse events, resulting in a huge social burden [2]. In recent years, with the improvement of stroke units standard (including the use of antiplatelet drugs), the mortality rate of stroke patients has decreased [3]. Related anti-stroke drugs play an important role in the current treatment, especially antiplatelet drugs. For example, aspirin, clopidogrel and ticagrelor can inhibit platelet aggregation and have proved to be effective in the secondary prevention of stroke.  

Ticagrelor is a non-precursor antiplatelet drug, which can take effect directly without liver metabolic activation, and can reversibly bind with P2Y12 receptor on platelets to inhibit antiplatelet effect [4]. Due to the widespread deletion of CYP2C19 allele in Asian population, the standard oral dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel, DAPT) has its limitations [5]. Fortunately, compared with DAPT, the platelet response rate of ticagrelor is lower, which is more beneficial in patients with highrisk vascular diseases [6,7]. At present, the completed randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have proved that ticagrelor is more effective than the conventional antiplatelet therapy in inhibiting platelet activity. THALES showed that in patients with mild to moderate acute noncardiac ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) who did not receive thrombolytic therapy, compared with aspirin alone, ticagrelor combined with aspirin could reduce the risk of stroke or death within 30 days [8]. Based on positive results from clinical trials, ticagrelor was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2011 and 2020, respectively, for the treatment of patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) and stroke [9,10]. However, in other randomized controlled studies, among the patients with vascular high risk factors, ticagrelor did not show any superiority over other antiplatelet drugs [11,12].  

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of the protec­ tive effects of the antiplatelet agent ticagrelor on stroke and cognitive function in patients with vascular high-risk factors. We included cognitive and other adverse events potentially related to stroke and additional data were collected from Clinicaltrials.gov. Based on previous studies, we defined high-risk vascular disease as CAD, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), stroke or TIA, or peripheral artery disease (PAD) [7].  



# 2. Methods  

We searched randomized controlled trials to compare the risk of adverse events between the ticagrelor and the control group. We con­ ducted this systematic review and meta-analysis study according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) criteria [13].  



# 2.1. Data sources and searches  

We conducted a web search of three databases (PubMed, Embase and Cochrane Library) on March 10, 2021, without language restrictions. The search strategy consists of two interlinked concepts: P2Y12 in­ hibitors (ticagrelor，prasugrel and cangrelor) and RCTs. Literature retrieval adopts the combination of subject words and free words. In addition, we manually retrieved citations based on systematic reviews of relevant research to avoid omissions. Specific retrieval strategies are given in Supplement Table I - III. We combined all the searched articles into a single bibliographic management tool (EndNote) for further filtering.  



# 2.2. Study selection  

Our inclusion criteria include: (1) Comparison of ticagrelor treated with P2Y12 inhibitor versus control (other antiplatelet treatment); (2) Clinical randomized controlled trial design; (3) The sample size of experimental group and control group was greater than 100. Literature exclusion criteria: (1) The duration of ticagrelor treatment was less than one month; (2) Overlapping studies and subgroup analysis with the same registration number; (3) Tests that are in progress or terminated prematurely; (4) Case reports, reviews, meta-analyses, non-human tri­ als; (5) Repeated studies and failure to extract available data.  



# 2.3. Data extraction  

Two reviewers independently retrieved and screened available studies. If the data obtained are inconsistent, the data shall be determined after consultation. The extracted content includes: (1) The basic information of patients (first author, time of publication, age, sex, name of trails, statistical time point, the number of patients, disease types); (2) The primary outcome was all-cause stroke, and the secondary outcomes were ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, intracranial hem­ orrhage, TIA, headache, dizziness, epilepsy, and death (all-cause), cognitive disorder, dementia, anxiety, depression, insomnia, Parkinson's disease. First, we use data from publications. If the original article was not reported, we searched the clinical trial website (ClinicalTrials.gov) for complete adverse event data. Data from different ticagrelor dose groups in the same study were extracted and reported separately.  



# 2.4. Assessment of study quality  

We used the Cochrane risk of bias tool to evaluate the quality of the included studies [14]. It was conducted independently by two re­ searchers, mainly based on the following aspects: (1) random sequence; (2) allocation concealment; (3) whether the researcher and the subjects are blinded; (4) the study results were evaluated by blind method; (5) the ending data is complete or not; (6) Whether the research results are selectively reported; (7) other biases. If there is any disagreement, reach an agreement after joint discussion.  



# 2.5. Statistical analysis  

The number of adverse events in each group was divided by the overall number of people to calculate the event rate. We reviewed the total number of all relevant adverse events to assess the usefulness of the statistical analysis. For all included studies, odds ratios (OR) and $95\%$ confidence intervals (CI) were calculated based on the number of events and the total number of patients in the experimental and control groups. We used Peto OR for statistical analysis, which is the preferred method for small probability events [15]. In the absence of significant hetero­ geneity $(P>0.1$ and $I^{2}<50\%)$ , OR and CI were combined using a fixedeffect model. If significant heterogeneity was present $(P\leq0.1\$ and $I^{2}\geq$ $50\%$ ), the random-effects model (Mantel-Haenszel) was used to combine OR and $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ . Funnel plots were used to evaluate publication bias and Egger's test calculated the quantitative results. The sources of hetero­ geneity were explored using inverse variance estimates. Statistical analysis was performed using Review Manager (version 5.3), R Software (Version 4.0.5) and STATA (version 15.1).  



# 3. Results  

All databases were searched for 2306 articles (PubMed: 803, Embase: 207, Cochrane Library: 1296). By browsing the titles and ab­ stracts, 2180 studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria, such as repetition, review and non-human, were excluded, and 126 studies were retained for full-text reading screening. Finally, 12 studies were included in this meta-analysis. A total of 105,654 patients were evalu­ ated across the 12 studies with 56,543 in ticagrelor group (including different doses) and 49,111 in control arms.  



# 3.1. Study characteristics  

All studies were randomized controlled with sample sizes ranging from 200 to 9619 in the intervention group. There were three studies on stroke or TIA [6,8,12], and one on peripheral artery disease [11]. Including two studies of stable coronary artery disease [16,17]. The other six ACS studiy populations were characterized as follows [4,18–22]. Cannon et al. enrolled patients with non-ST-segment eleva­ tion acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS) and divided them into two groups according to the dose of ticagrelor [18]. There were two studies of populations for ACS patients, with ST-segment elevation or NSTE-ACS [19,20]. Bonaca et al. conducted a study in patients more than 1 year after myocardial infarction [4]. In tang and Berwanger et al. ‘s study, all patients were diagnosed as ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [21,22]. Bonaca et al. administered $90~\mathrm{mg}$ and $60~\mathrm{mg}$ of tica­ grelor in two experimental groups [4]. The dosages of ticagrelor in other studies were $90~\mathrm{mg}$ . The final data point for all studies ranged from one month to 36 months. Ten of the studies have been registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, and data on adverse events not reported in the orig­ inal text is available there. The characteristics and results of all studies are summarized in Table 1.  



# 3.2. Quality of included studies  

The 12 included studies were all randomized controlled studies, and two of them were open-label studies, so there was a moderate risk of hidden bias. Since most of the included studies were published in top journals and the risk of pooled bias evaluation was low, only a few RCTs were noted for the risk of uncertain outcome evaluation. The results of literature quality evaluation and the evidence of article quality evalu­ ation is shown in Supplement Table IV and Table V.  



# 3.2.1. Primary outcome  

Table 2 summarizes all primary and secondary outcomes. As shown in Fig. 1A, patients on ticagrelor had a lower risk of all stroke compared to the control group (12 RCTs, OR 0.84, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.78–0.90, $P<0.001$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=~0.384$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=6.2\%)$ ). Two studies involving different doses were included.  



# 3.2.2. Secondary outcomes  

The risk of ischemic stroke was lower in the ticagrelor group than in the control group (9 RCTs, OR: 0.83, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.77–0.90, $\mathrm{~P~}<0.001$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=0.761$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0.0\%$ , Fig. 1B). There was no sig­ nificant difference in hemorrhagic stroke adverse events between the experimental group and the control group (8 RCTs, OR 1.14, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.81–1.59, $P=0.448$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=0.182$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=29.6\%$ , Supplement Fig. I). There was a statistically significant difference in the incidence of intracranial hemorrhage and TIA adverse events in the ticagrelor group (8 RCTs, OR 1.33, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 1.09–1.61, $P=0.005$ , het­ erogeneity $P$ value $=0.229$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=24.2\%$ , Fig. 1C and 8 RCTs OR 0.78, $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.62–0.97$ , $P=0.029$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=0.227$ , $\Gamma^{2}=24.4\%$ , Fig. 1D). However, there was no statistically significant difference in allcause deaths between the experimental group and the control group (12 RCTs, OR: 0.95, $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.91\substack{-1.00}$ , $P=0.157$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=$ 0.761, $\mathrm{I}^{2}=27.9\%$ , Supplement Fig. II).  

In the following results, no statistical difference was found between the experimental group and the control group: headache (8 RCTs, OR: 1.02, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.92–1.14, $P=0.655$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=0.021$ , $\operatorname{I}^{2}=$ $54.1\%$ , Supplement Fig. III), epilepsy (6 RCTs, OR: 1.28, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.69–2.35, $P=0.435$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=0.169$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=33.9\%$ , Supplement Fig. IV), cognitive disorder (4 RCTs, OR: 1.00, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$  

0.25–4.00, $P=0.998$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=0.404$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0.4\%$ , Sup­ plement Fig. V), anxiety (7 RCTs, OR: 1.25, $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.65-2.40,P=0.507$ , heterogeneity $P$ value $=~0.232$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=24.8\%$ , Supplement Fig. VI), depression (5 RCTs, OR: 1.07, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.71–1.59, $P=0.754$ , heteroge­ neity P value $=0.688$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0.0\%$ , Supplement Fig. VII).  

The ticagrelor can reduce the risk of Parkinson's disease (6 RCTs, OR: 0.30, 95%CI 0.12–0.72, $P=0.007$ , heterogeneity P value $=0.668$ , $\operatorname{I}^{2}=$ $0.0\%$ , Fig. 2A) and dementia (6 RCTs, OR: 0.31, $95\%\mathrm{CI}~0.13–0.77$ , $P=$ 0.012, heterogeneity P value $=0.296$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=17.6\%$ , Fig. 2B). But increases the risk of dizziness (8 RCTs, OR: 1.39, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 1.03–1.87, $P=0.032$ , heterogeneity P value $=0.529\$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0.0\%$ , Fig. 2C) and insomnia (5 RCTs, OR: 1.45, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 1.05–2.00, $P=0.026$ , heterogeneity $\mathrm{\bfP}$ value $=$ 0.639, $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0.0\%$ , Fig. 2D).  



# 3.3. Publication bias  

No significant publication bias was found in funnel plots of all studies. (Fig. VII - XXII in the Supplement). No publication bias was observed by egger's statistical test in the primary results of ticagrelor for all-cause stroke protection $\textstyle(p=0.8987)$ ).  



# 3.4. Sensitivity analyses  

Further sensitivity analysis was performed for studies with greater than $50\%$ heterogeneity. Headache was significantly heterogeneous across all studies $(I^{2}=54.1\%)$ $P=0.021$ ), the heterogeneity of other adverse events was not significant (Supplement Fig. III). To further explore the heterogeneity of headache events, we used a random-effects model (OR: 0.81, $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.58–1.13$ , $I^{2}=47.5\%$ , Supplement Fig. XXIII). The results obtained after recalculation were the same as before, and there was no statistical difference in headache between the experimental group and the control group.  



# 3.5. Subgroup analyses  

In subgroup analyses of the control group using antiplatelet (aspirin and clopidogrel) and placebo, ticagrelor was protective against all-cause stroke (10 antiplatelet-RCTs, OR: 0.86, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.79–0.94, heterogeneity $P$ value $=0.24$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=21\%$ , Supplement Fig. XXIV; 2 RCTs, OR: 0.80, $95\%$ CI 0.69–0.91, heterogeneity $\mathrm{\bfP}$ value $=~0.38$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ , Supplement Fig. XXIV).  



# 4. Discussion  

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of the protec­ tive effects of ticagrelor on stroke and cognitive function in patients with vascular risk factors. We statistically analyzed data from 12 randomized controlled clinical trials involving 56,543 patients using ticagrelor and obtained additional data from ClinicalTrials.gov. Our meta-analysis showed that ticagrelor reduced the risk of all-cause stroke compared to controls. The secondary results of this study suggest that ticagrelor reduces the risk of ischemic stroke, TIA, dementia, and Parkinson's disease, but may increase adverse events such as intracranial hemorrhage, dizziness, and insomnia. In addition, our meta-analysis showed that ticagrelor did not increase the risk of hemorrhagic stroke, death, headache, epilepsy, cognitive function, anxiety, or depressive events.

Ticagrelor was significantly better than the control group in prevent­ ing all-cause stroke, ischemic stroke, and TIA adverse events. Effects of Ticagrelor and Clopidogrel in Patients with Peripheral Artery Disease (EUCLID) results showed that the ticagrelor group had a significantly lower risk of ischemic stroke than clopidogrel group in PAD patients (HR 0.78, $95\%\mathrm{CI}~0.62–0.98$ , $P=0.03\$ ) [11]. Unfortunately, although three studies involving the pre­ vention of mild stroke and TIA were included in this study [6,8,12], further stratification of stroke severity needs to be implemented in future studies due to data limitations. Ticagrelor is the only drug that can reversibly block ADP receptors and regulate adenosine levels to produce biological effects [23]. Previous statistical evaluations of antithrombotic agents (including clopidogrel or prasugrel) have shown a lower incidence of platelet reactivity with ticagrelor [24,25]. Ticagrelor has more advantages than traditional P2Y12 inhibitors, it should be considered with greater caution in patients at high risk for intracranial hemorrhage.  

Our results are consistent with those of several large randomized trials in which ticagrelor is more protective against stroke than the control group, providing clinicians with a better choice of antiplatelet agents. With the increasing use of newer antiplatelet drugs, clinicians will be more and more required to pay attention to functional disease adverse events that affect the quality of life in addition to the disease itself. Our study not only evaluated the common stroke related events, but also calculated the risk of neurocognitive function related diseases. Currently, the effect of antiplatelet therapy on cognitive function is controversial. Clinicians often ignore the impact of neurocognitive function on the quality of life of patients with coronary artery disease or stroke after their professional treatment. This model of care may not be suitable for patients with cognitive disorders such as post-stroke depression or dementia, and they may still have cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases under control, but cognitive-related adverse events may threaten their health and quality of life. In a secondary analysis of the Prevention of Cardiovascular events in ischemic stroke patients with high risk of cerebral hemorrhage (PICASSO) trial, Kwon et al. concluded that cognitive impairment was associated with recur­ rence of ischemic stroke in high-risk patients [26]. Cognitive impair­ ment after stroke has become a major global public health problem [27]. Focusing on the disease itself and the mental health of patients may better serve the patients, and preventing the occurrence of adverse events in advance is an important area to be considered in the future.  

We still need more reliable data to determine the effect of ticagrelor on stroke and cognitive-related diseases. At present, the biological mechanism of antiplatelet drugs in human body still needs to be further studied, in addition to the treatment of target diseases, whether it can help to prevent the progression of brain injury and protect neural function. Studies have shown that ticagrelor has neuroprotective effects in addition to its antiplatelet properties in a mouse animal model [28]. The antiplatelet drug cilostazol has previously been reported to have a preventive effect on central nervous system diseases including Parkin­ son's disease, but the physiological mechanism of the protective effect is still unclear and has not yet been reported [29]. Parkinson's disease affects $2{-}3\%$ of people over 65 years of age and is currently considered to be closely related to aging, genetic and environmental factors [30]. Unfortunately, studies of the same antiplatelet drug ticagrelor and the risk of disease have rarely been reported. At present, the biggest chal­ lenge may be that the mechanism of ticagrelor is not completely clear, and the impact on the prevalence of Parkinson's disease should be paid attention to by clinical neurologists in the future. Some experts have noted and conducted preliminary analyses of the effects of antiplatelet drugs on cognitive function. A secondary analysis of the SPS3 trial by Pearce et al. found that dual antiplatelet had no effect on cognition in the short term, but a longer period of observation was needed [31]. How­ ever, some studies have suggested that anti-platelet therapy is associated with an increased risk of dementia, but the mechanisms remain unclear [32]. In recent years, NLRP3 targets have been found to play an important role in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease and dementia [33,34]. More importantly, Huang et al. found that ticagrelor inhibited the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, suggesting that ticagrelor might be a potential therapeutic agent for NLRP3 associated diseases (such as Alzheimer's dementia), however further research is needed [35]. Although we found that the prevalence of dementia (OR 0.31, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.13–0.77, ${P}=0.012,$ and Parkinson's disease (OR 0.30, 95CI: 0.12–0.72, $P=0.007)$ ) in ticagrelor treatment group was lower than that in control group, there were still more confounding factors, and more data were needed to guide the routine use in the future.  

Limitations of the study relate to the available data. First of all, the patients with different types of diseases are included, and it is suggested that researchers classify the patients with different diseases in the future. And it may lead to potential uncertainties in the quality of enrolled patients that need to be further addressed in future studies. Second, there are different definitions for the diagnosis of cognitive diseases. For example, depression should be diagnosed and followed up according to the history, clinical symptoms, and diagnostic scales (ICD-10 and DSMIV), and it is affected by the subjective judgment of different evaluators. In addition, a limitation of this analysis is that the original texts and ClinicalTrials.gov of some studies cannot collect available data, so we have to exclude these studies in the analysis. Fourth, in all the included studies, although the publication bias is not large, the number of adverse event reports is very small, which is also a problem to be avoided in future studies. Except using the random effect model to calculate a result with large heterogeneity, we use the Peto which is more suitable for small probability events to evaluate the heterogeneity, and the results show that the heterogeneity seems to be very low. Finally, the follow-up time of less than one year in some included studies may be a limitation. The incidence of Parkinson's disease or Alzheimer's disease increases with age, so long-term follow-up studies are needed in the future. We also need to conduct independent experimental analysis for specific adverse events in the future.  



# 5. Conclusions  

The results of our meta-analysis show that the primary outcome of this study suggests a protective effect of ticagrelor against all-cause stroke. Secondary outcomes suggest that ticagrelor has a protective ef­ fect on ischemic stroke and TIA, but the incidence of intracranial hem­ orrhage, dizziness, and insomnia should not be ignored. Ticagrelor may reduce the risk of dementia and Parkinson's disease, although data are limited.
