# ABSTRACT  

INTRODUCTION: Evidence about predictors of poor outcomes such as cerebral infarction (CI) after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) has not been fully elucidated.   
EVIDENCE ACQUISITION: We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis on studies with adults with aSAH considering RCT and nonRCT, prospective, and retrospective cohort studies describing clinical, imaging as well as angiographic studies in patients with aSAH. EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS: After reviewing the complete text, 11 studies were considered eligible, out of which four were ruled out. Degree of clinical severity was the most predictive factor with a higher degree at the presentation on different severity scales being associated with a statistically significant increasing the risk of suffering a CI following aSAH (OR 2.49 $[95\%$ CI 1.38-4.49] $\mathrm{P}{=}0.\dot{0}003)$ . Aneurysm size increased the risk of CI (OR 1.49 $[95\%$ CI 1.20-1.85] $\scriptstyle\mathrm{{P=}}0.000{\overline{{3}}}$ ; $12\%$ ). In six studies analyzed, it was found that an important factor for the subsequent development of CI is vasospasm (OR 7.62 [2.19, 26.54], $\mathrm{P=0.0001}$ ).   
CONCLUSIONS: The development of vasospasm is a risk factor for CI development after aSAH. In our review, three factors were associated with an increased risk of CI: clinical severity at presentation, vasospasm, and aneurysm size. The major limitation of this meta-analysis is that included studies were conducted retrospectively or were post hoc analyses of a prospective trial.

# Introduction  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a disease that remains with high morbidity and mortality. Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) constitutes from $2\%$ to $6\%$ of all causes of strokes.1-3 Among patients presenting with SAH, $75\%$ to $85\%$ are secondary to a ruptured cerebral aneurysm (e.g., aSAH).4, 5 After the acute phase of an aSAH, the neurological status of patients might worsen; this is ascribed to ischemia in the nervous tissue, which occurs in approximately $30\%$ of cases.6 Once ischemia endures in time, it leads to a loss of oxygen and a brain tissue damage known as cerebral infarction (CI), which strongly contributes to the development of transient or permanent neurological deficits or poorer outcomes, such as immediate death in $12\%$ of patients.7, 8 CI is the objectively identified presence of infarction in a determined vascular territory on cranial computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within 6 weeks after a SAH or proven at autopsy, and not attributable to other causes such as surgical clipping or endovascular treatment.9 It is critically important to acknowledge whether CI diagnosis was made by angiography, CT, MRI, clinically, or at autopsy because of its influence on outcomes in different scenarios.8, 10, 11 One of the ultimate outcomes shown in aSAH is CI, which is better defined on CT or MRI rather than angiography or clinically. CI is commonly classified as early and delayed. Early CI (ECI) usually occurs within 3 days after aSAH and is attributed to a tissue injury related to the rupture of the initial aneurysm. Delayed CI (DCI) usually occurs within 28 days or even 6 weeks after the aSAH and is frequently attributed to an arterial vasospasm.12, 13 This concept is actually misused in several studies, given that vasospasm treatment does not always lead to improvements in clinical nor functional outcomes related to CI 14. Some authors also suggest using “vasospasm” only to describe an imaging test and not as a DCI manifestation.9  

Previous studies indicate that CT reports ischemic findings (hypodensities) in $30\%$ to $65\%$ of aSAH survivors in the long term.14-17 However, less than $30\%$ develop neurological deficits.18 DCI occurs in $28\%$ of patients between 4 to 10 days after the acute phase.19 ECI occurs in $27\%$ and is associated as a predictor of DCI and unfavorable outcomes.20 However, Ayling et al. showed a lower frequency of CI but almost no difference between ECI or DCI $18\%$ and $19\%$ , respectively).12 Prevention of this complication has always been one of the main goals of the aSAH treatment, followed by aneurysm occlusion. Since there is no way to revert ischemia, prevention seems to be the most plausible option. Despite the current body of evidence, to our knowledge, there are very few studies or meta-analyses collecting data regarding predictors of CI.21, 22 Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to establish predictor factors of CI after aSAH.  

# Evidence acquisition  

# Search strategy  

A systematic search for RCT, not RCT, prospective and retrospective cohort studies was conducted by two authors through PUBMED, Specialized Registry Cochrane Injuries Group, Central Cochrane Registry of Controlled Trials (The Cochrane Library), MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), and PubMed [http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/ entrez]; with no time restriction until august 30 of 2019. In addition to the reference list of included studies and other relevant data in addition to potentially eligible studies. The search string applied to each database is shown in Supplementary Digital Material 1 (Supplementary Text File 1).  

# Selection criteria  

Studies with the following selection criteria were enrolled in the meta-analysis. First, adults $(>18$ years of age) with aSAH; secondly, randomized controlled trials (RCT) and no RCT, prospective and retrospective cohort studies describing clinical and imaging, e.g., angiographic outcomes in patients with SAH, written in English or Spanish.  

# Data extraction  

The following data were extracted: age, sex, aneurysm size, clinical severity of SAH using the scales Hund and Hess (HH) and World Federation of Neurological Surgery (WFNS), and tomographic assessment (Fisher Scale), presence of vasospasm (clinical, angiographic or by transcranial doppler), location of the aneurysm (anterior or posterior circulation) and treatment (clipping vs. coils). We chose an age cut-off as a result of an estimated average of 50 years due to heterogeneity in the studies. Large aneurysms were characterized by $10\mathrm{mm}$ of diameter or more.21 Authors were contacted for missing data. The doubts were clarified by consulting an expert in research methodology.  

Quality assessment and risk of bias  

The modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for assessing the quality and risk of bias of the included studies.22 Two reviewers (WF, LRMS) assessed the risk of bias of each study using this scale, and high, moderate, and low risk of bias were defined as $\mathrm{NOS}{<}4$ , between 4 and 6, and ${>}6$ , respectively, as in the original NOS.  

# Statistical analysis  

The statistical analysis was carried out through the relative risk with the generic inverse methodology of the variance (IV) to combine odds ratio (OR) demonstrated in each study in case it is not explicitly in the text and the authors of the same do not contribute given OR was calculated from data provided in the text. Significant results were considered if CI $95\%$ was ${>}1$ and a $\mathrm{P}$ value less than 0.05 was statistically significant. A random-effects analysis model calculated using the Review Manager software (RevMan 5.3, Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Center, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014) was used. Forest plot pooling all predictors was generated using the DistillerSR Forest Plot Generator from Evidence Partners. Heterogeneity was assessed by calculating I2, with the high heterogeneity of the studies included in the analysis being above $60\%$ . The risk of publication bias was further assessed using and comparing the Egger’s test23 and Begg’s test.24 P-value less than 0,1 for Egger’s test was considered statistically significant.  

# IRB/Ethics Committee Approval  

Meta-analyses do not involve human subjects. Meta-analyses collect and synthesize data from previous clinical trials in which the trial investigators have already obtained informed consent do not require IRB review or ethics committee approval.  

# Evidence synthesis  

# Study selection  

After conducting the systematic search of the information following our strategy, 100 bibliographic citations were identified (PUBMED 50, EMBASE 20, MEDLINE 10, Central Cochrane 10 LILACS 10) 20 were considered potentially eligible based on the title or abstract, or both, and full texts were obtained. After reviewing the complete text, 11 studies were considered eligible, 4 were ruled out because they did not meet the inclusion criteria and did not answer the research question, and 7 met the inclusion criteria for the review (Figure 1, Supplementary Digital Material 2: Supplementary Table I).8, 12, 19, 25-28 All crosssectional observational studies were found.12, 25-27  

# Overview of included studies  

Among 7 included studies in the analysis, 4 obtained the maximum score of 7,8, 12, 25, 27 1 obtained 6 points28 and the remaining 2 of 5 points26 on the scale used. $71.43\%$ of the studies were reported as high quality and $28.57\%$ as moderate quality according to the modified Newcastle-Ottawa scale (Table I),8, 12, 19, 25-28 and the statistical analysis was performed according to the protocol.  

# Meta-analysis  

No statistically significant differences were found in patients over 50 years old compared to patients under this age (OR 1.15 [0.99- 1.33], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.07$ ) (Supplementary Digital Material 3: Supplementary Figure 1). Also, the analysis did not find statistically significant differences between both genders for the outcome evaluated (OR 0.89 [0.66- 1.13], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.29\$ ) (Supplementary Digital Material 4: Supplementary Figure 2). For both outcomes, the evaluation of heterogeneity resulted in low heterogeneity (for age and sex $12\%$ and $47\%$ , respectively). This meta-analysis found that there is no difference between the high grade on the Fisher scale (grade 3 or 4) compared with patients with low grade (Fisher 1 or 2) (OR 2.67 [0.71- 10.06], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.15$ ) for the development of CI after an aSAH. However, high heterogeneity was addressed $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}96\%)$ (Supplementary Digital Material 5: Supplementary Figure 3).

# Discussion

Our study has demonstrated which patients with aSAH were at a high risk of CI. We identified 7 retrospective studies that were included in the study. aSAH is associated with severe complications that occur in the course of the disease. The most important are bleeding, intracerebral hematoma, intraventricular hemorrhage, vasospasm, CI, and hydrocephalus.29-31 It was surprising that high-grade Fisher was not a predictor of CI, although previously, it has been clearly demonstrated its association with a higher risk of cerebral vasospasm. Tomography is an excellent tool to measure severity and prognosis in patients with aSAH. In 1980, the Fisher scale was conceived to predict the risk of vasospasm but not clinical outcome.32 However, some shortcomings in particular cases substantially limited its performance. This is the case of Jabbarli et al.25 and Kanamaru et al.19 that demonstrated the pitfalls discussed for this scale. These shortcomings include patients with thick cisternal blood and concomitant intraventricular or intraparenchymal blood. Later in 2001, Claassen et al.33 revisited and modified the fisher scale to improve its accuracy in predicting vasospasm and indirectly CI. This modified Fisher scale has been validated,34 and several studies have been benefited conferring more reliability to their results. This is the case of Rabinstein et al.8 and Mijiti et al.27

In that order, the results of this meta-analysis could be influenced by the high heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}96\%)$ ) of the studies included for this analysis due to scale misusage in literature. This encourages researchers to conduct well-designed studies that correctly assess each variable by using the most accurate scales. However, the study by Henk et al. showed that the Fisher Scale, Modified Fisher Scale, and Hijdra Sum Score are all associated with clinical DCI. This review showed that patients with Fisher 3 also have the highest risk of clinical DCI compared to Fisher 1 and 2, even with Fisher 4, this is consistent with what is shown by Fisher et al., whose patients with Fisher 3 had the highest risk of vasospasm, a well-known cause of DCI. Modified Fisher scale shows an increased risk of DCI per grade increase on the scale, whereas the Fisher scale does not, showing that patients with modified Fisher 4 had the highest risk of DCI.35 As for pre-existing diseases in patients with SAH, not much has been found to be related to the development of DCI. However, habits such as smoking showed to be a strong predictor of DCI.36  

The rupture of a cerebral aneurysm and subsequent blood leaking into the subarachnoid space is associated with several complications. The hemoglobin is converted to methemoglobin, heme, and hemin. These substances might bind and activate the TLR4 causing a cascade of inflammation that will damage white and subcortical gray matter by the activation of microglia after phagocytosis and recognition of these substances. The immune response prompts the leukocyte migration into the subarachnoid space of macrophages and neutrophils that will clarify hemoglobin and its metabolites in this space.37 Acute vasoconstriction following aSAH occurs after the intracranial pressure increases; subsequently, cerebral blood flow decreases, and thus, cerebral perfusion pressure decreases, either resulting in diffuse cerebral ischemia.2 Persistent hemorrhage despite these mechanisms produces a vicious circle in which intracranial pressure rises lethally, resulting in the progressive reduction of perfusion and prolongation of ischemia. Thus, arterial vasospasm is usually the cause of ischemia, and endured ischemia results in CI.7  

Predictors of CI after aSAH  

# Aneurysm size  

The main evidence regarding aneurysm size has been demonstrated by the CONSCIOUS-I trial,38 analyzed by Crowley et al.28 This analysis showed that 413 patients were randomized, and data from 381 cases were analyzed. In this study, when performing the logistic regression analysis for the characteristic associated with CI, it was found that an aneurysm size of $6{\cdot}15\mathrm{mm}$ was associated with CI, OR 0.38 ( $95\%$ CI 0.16-0.85; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.019$ ). Similarly, Ferguson et al. performed a multivariable logistic regression and showed a dose-dependent relationship analysis between larger aneurysm $({>}12\mathrm{mm})$ and a higher risk of CI.39 These results also fit with the poorer outcome of patients with larger aneurysms40 since CI is also associated with poorer outcomes.8 Several studies have shown that the risk of perforator infarction,41 postoperative CI,42 and neurologic worsening43 increased with aneurysm size. The explanation of this is that since a larger aneurysm might include several perforators and even branches, it may entail inadvertent occlusion when treating the aneurysm by clipping or coiling. Also, a larger aneurysm might lead to a mass effect on the surrounding structures and respective clinical deficits, which would logically be ameliorated immediately by clipping rather than coiling. Thus, it is necessary to consider this management in this subset of cases.44  

# Clipping vs. coiling  

Our analysis showed a statistically significant increased risk of CI in clipping; this may be due to vessels manipulated during surgery would prompt higher rates of vasospasm. Similar to our results, Yousef et al. did not find a statistically significant difference between clipping and coiling treatment to develop CI.32 On the other hand, clipping allows cleaning and clearance of basal cisterns, reducing the rate of vasospasm.45 The CONSCIOUS-1 trial38 reported a higher rate of vasospasm detected by angiography in endovascular coiling than in clipping cases $51\%$ vs. $49\%$ ). Kanamaru et al.19 were, in contrast, dissenting in their observations. They showed a higher rate of vasospasm in clipping than in coiling $(54.9\%$ vs. $45.1\%$ . It could suggest that previous retrospective studies may have skewed the data since results could not be replicated in a prospective study.  

Surprisingly new data from our analysis was that among all other factors such as age, sex, tomographic findings (Fisher Scale), and location of the aneurysm (anterior or posterior circulation), none were significantly associated with CI development. Another factor challenging to measure was the surgeon’s experience in clipping and coiling procedures. Maybe this factor results in biased results in many of the included trials, such as the ISAT trial (e.g., this was not assessed for microsurgery neurosurgeons).46 This fact clearly jeopardized the clipping outcomes and generated fewer quality results. Similarly, it was addressed in the BRAT trial, where the outcome differed significantly, even in the long-term follow-up.47 There could be many factors contributing to this: Fewer cases included in the study as aforementioned, heterogeneous data and reports, and the statement that these factors may not have a bearing. More extensive and better-designed studies such as Brami et al.29 may adjust the outcome, but these factors were not found significant in this analysis.  

# Clinical severity at presentation  

Clinical scales significantly influence the outcomes, as Dengler et al. showed in their study. They showed that both HH and WFNS predicted the occurrence of CI and patient outcomes with no significant differences.48 In contrast, Konczalla et al.49 showed that a HH grade V was more strongly associated with a higher risk of CI, unlike WFNS that showed no significant association. Rabinstien et al.8 conducted a study of 143 cases of SAH and concluded that a single unit increase in the WFNS grading prompted increased rates of CI. These results were significant by multivariate logistic regression model with statistically significant variables on univariate analysis. In addition, Foreman et al.32 demonstrated that clinical-grade on admission is a strong predictor of clinical vasospasm and CI. Yousef et al.30 showed no association between HH grade and outcomes, e.g., CI thought also acknowledged the possible bias in this result due to missing data. Unlike this, when tested in this meta-analysis, it showed clearly a relationship, but we found it is heterogeneous and may need further testing.  

# Vasospasm  

Almost all studies have supported this outcome. The CONSCIOUS-1 Trial data represents the highest significance, with multivariate logistic regression analysis showing a significant and robust association between angiographically-confirmed vasospasm and new CI $(\mathrm{P}{<}0.0001)$ ).38 There have been studies correlating transcranial Doppler and angiography findings with CI development as follows. Brami et al. recently showed a dosedependent relationship between the severity of arterial narrowing and delayed CI. They also concluded that cerebral vasospasm of large arteries is necessary to develop CI.31 It is necessary to add that Baldwin et al.50 and AlMufti et al.51 demonstrated that arterial vasospasm (both by angiography) is a predictor of CI not exclusive of late stages, but that arterial narrowing at the time of admission was associated with CI.  

On the other hand, different studies are not able to fully correlate arterial narrowing with CI.8, 10, 11 Since vasospasm diagnosis may be influenced by the technique used for diagnosis (CT, transcranial Doppler, or clinic) and thus, its prediction factor, further studies will be required to corroborate or dismiss this correlation. Miller et al. failed to demonstrate an accurate prediction of CI using transcranial Doppler to characterize cerebral vasospasm.52 Albeit Crowley et al. demonstrated a strong association between angiographic vasospasm and CI, which persisted beyond adjusted analysis for admission neurological grade and aneurysm size (OR 9.31; $95\%$ CI 3.70 –23.4; $\mathrm{P}{<}0.0001$ ).28 However, Added to this, the volume of blood in the subarachnoid space correlates with the severity of the vasospasm.29-31 None of these can estimate the association of vasospasm if asymptomatic infarction in comatose or sedated patients is developed, and it may remain undetected and result in a lower association.49  

# Limitations of the study  

Despite the detailed review, several limitations apply to our study. Even though the eligibility criteria for this study were strict, several limitations were observed. Firstly, all of the included studies were conducted retrospectively or were post hoc analyses of a prospective trial. This increased the risk of bias and, therefore, the credibility of our results. The recently published study by Brami et al.,29 which was not included in this analysis and which will be included further as an update of this study, established that the presence of vasospasm in the large artery is a significant determinant of delayed CI. Another potential limitation for the publication bias analysis was the low number of studies included in this meta-analysis. According to Cochrane Manual,53 Funnel plot analysis decreases its validity for detecting publication bias if less than 10 studies are included, even more, if heterogeneous results are included.  

# Conclusions  

Vasospasm is associated as a predictor of an increased risk of CI after aSAH. The extra benefit of the early categorization of patients at risk should be further studied. Clinical severity at presentation determinates outcome, and larger aneurysms at presentation are associated with a poorer prognosis. Surgical clipping has been shown to trend to a higher risk of CI formation when compared to coiling. Considering the aforementioned factors combined, more aggressive management of aSAH can lead to better outcomes.
