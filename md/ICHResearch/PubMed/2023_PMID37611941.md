# ABSTRACT  

Background  Surgical treatment of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is unproven, although meta-analyses suggest that both early conventional surgery with craniotomy and minimally invasive surgery (MIS) may be beneficial. We aimed to demonstrate the safety, feasibility, and promise of efficacy of early MIS for ICH using the Aurora Surgiscope and Evacuator.  

Methods  We performed a prospective, single arm, phase IIa Simon’s two stage design study at two stroke centers (10 patients with supratentorial ICH volumes ${\ge}20$ mL and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of ${\ge}6,$ and surgery commencing $<12$ hours after onset). Positive outcome was defined as $250\%$ 24 hour ICH volume reduction, with the safety outcome lack of significant ICH reaccumulation.  

Results  From December 2019 to July 2020, we enrolled 10 patients at two Australian Comprehensive Stroke Centers, median age 70 years (IQR 65–74), NIHSS score 19 (IQR 19–29), ICH volume 59 mL (IQR 25–77), at a median of 227 min (IQR 175-377) post-onset. MIS was commenced at a median time of 531 min (IQR 437–628) post-onset, had a median duration of 98 min (IQR 77- 110), with a median immediate postoperative hematoma evacuation of $70\%$ ( $10867{-}80\%)$ . A positive outcome was achieved in 5/5 first stage patients and in 4/5 second stage patients. One patient developed significant 24 hour ICH reaccumulation; otherwise, 24 hour stability was observed (median reduction $71\%$ (IQR 61–80), 5/9 patients $<15\mathsf{mL}$ residual). Three patients died, unrelated to surgery. There were no surgical safety concerns. At 6 months, the median modified Rankin Scale score was 4 (IQR 3–6) with $30\%$ achieving a score of 0–3.  

Conclusion  In this study, early ICH MIS using the Aurora Surgiscope and Evacuator appeared to be feasible and safe, warranting further exploration.  

Trial registration number  ACTRN12619001748101.  



# INTRODUCTION  

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) contributes disproportionately to stroke related morbidity and mortality due to severity and the lack of highly effective treatments. Surgical ICH treatment is of uncertain clinical benefit. Conventional craniotomy based approaches are unproven, although individual patient meta-analysis suggests surgery within 8hours post-onset may be beneficial.2 Following inconclusive randomised controlled trials, alternative approaches have been developed. The catheter based thrombolysis enhanced clot removal MISTIE-III (Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus Rt-PA for ICH Evacuation Phase IIl) trial was neutral, although a mortality benefit was seen, with possible functional benefit in patients with smaller final post-evacuation hematoma volumes due to substantial hematoma evacuation.3  

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) under endoscopic guidance may minimise trauma to surrounding brain, permitting direct visually guided hemostasis and better remove deeper hemorrhage. Surgical efficacy rates with newer techniques and devices are high, with minimal ICH reaccumulation risk.4 Recent American Heart Association guidelines endorsed MIS to reduce mortality ; whether patients survive with acceptable functional impairment remains uncertain.  

ICH has time dependent treatment targets, especially hematoma expansion and blood derived thrombin and iron cytotoxicity. Although most expansion occurs early, a proportion experience later expansion, decreasing probabilistically over time.7 It is likely, analogous to reperfusion for ischemic stroke, that treatment for ICH is also time dependent. Following rapid technological device advances, early MIS is therefore an attractive ICH treatment approach. Accordingly, we performed a pilot study of early ICH MIS using the Aurora Surgiscope and Evacuator (Integra LifeSciences, Princeton, New Jersey, USA), examining feasibility, safety and surgical efficacy.  



# METHODS Study design and participants  

We performed an investigator initiated, prospective, single arm, open label, phase IIa Simon’s two stage design study enrolling patients with acute supratentorial ICH at two Australian Comprehensive stroke centers, following ethical board approval $(\mathrm{CONSORT^{8}}$ screening flowchart; online supplemental figure S1).  

Key inclusion criteria were acute ICH with intraparenchymal volume $\ge20\mathrm{mL}$ , National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score $\geq6$ , and surgery deemed to be feasible $<12$ hours after onset. We excluded patients with non-reversible anticoagulation (for full inclusion and exclusion criteria, see online supplemental methods). Either consent or emergency treatment enrollment with follow-up consent was permitted by state regulations.

Following enrolment, expedited stereotactic MIS using the Aurora Surgiscope and evacuator (figure 1) occurred, as described by Troiani and colleagues.? A burr hole or mini-craniotomy facilitated long axis hematoma evacuation via the shortest and safest route, commencing at the mid-posterior margin, proceeding proximally. Clot was removed via a Fukushimatype suction device or Evacuator at surgical discretion. Active bleeding was treated with irrigation, direct pressure, via the Aquamantis device, sucker diathermy, or Evacuator diathermy at the surgeon’s discretion. Thrombin application was discouraged. Brain CT was performed immediately postoperatively; theatre return was permitted if ICH removal was inadequate. CT was repeated at 24 hours. Follow-up (modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score) was performed at 3 and 6 months. Study data utilised REDCap electronic data capture tools (Yale University).  

A positive outcome was predefined as $\geq50\%$ ICH planimetric volume reduction at 24 hours. The predefined safety outcome was lack of significant ICH reaccumulation at the 24 hour follow-up scan (defined as either an increase in hematoma volume between the postoperative and 24 hour scans of $>100\%$ and $>10\mathrm{mL}$ , or hematoma volume on the follow-up scan which exceeded the admission volume). Other prospectively defined endpoints see reported in the online supplemental methods. The trial was prospectively registered (ACTRN12619001748101) and overseen by a data monitoring committee. One of the authors (TJK) had full access and responsibility for study data (which can be provided on reasonable written request).  



# Statistical analysis and sample size  

This study adopted Simon’s two stage design. The null hypothesis that the true response rate is 0.2 was tested against a one sided alternative. In the first stage, five patients were to be accrued. If there were one or fewer responses in these five patients, the study was to be stopped. Otherwise, five more patients were to be accrued, totaling 10. The null hypothesis was to be rejected if five or more positive responses were observed. This design yields a type I error rate of 0.03 and power of 0.8 when the true response rate is 0.6. Outcomes are presented as counts (proportions) or medians (IQRs).

# RESULTS  

From December 2019 to July 2020, 10 patients were enrolled (baseline variables in table  1 and online supplemental table) of median age 70 years (IQR 65–74), Glasgow Coma Scale 13 (IQR 11–14), and NIHSS score 19 (IQR 19–25). Most ICH cases were putaminal in location (7/10); two thalamic and one lobar (temporoparietal) cases were also enrolled. Most were of arteriolosclerotic (or hypertensive arteriopathy) etiology (9/10), whereas the one lobar case was due to probable cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Intraventricular hemorrhage (6/10) was common. Median ICH volume was $59\mathrm{mL}$ (IQR 25–77).  

Patients were enrolled (table  2) at a median of $227\mathrm{min}$ post-onset (IQR 175-377), surgery commencing 531 (IQR $437{-}628)\operatorname*{min}$ post-onset (353 (IQR250-469) minutes postarrival). The median procedure duration was $98\mathrm{min}$ (IQR 77–110); active bleeding was encountered and controlled in all patients. In all 10 patients, irrigation was used in obtaining hemostasis. Additional methods included local pressure $(\mathrm{n}{=}1)$ ), local pressure and diathermy to the surgical sucker and Evacuator device $(\mathrm{n}{=}3)$ , Aquamantis device $(\mathrm{n}{=}1)$ ), Aquamantis and device diathermy $(\mathrm{n}{=}1)$ ), and all methods combined $(\mathrm{n}{=}1)$ ). Return to theater following inadequate evacuation occurred once. Median postoperative hematoma evacuation was $70\%$ (IQR $67{-}80\%)$ .  

All five stage 1 patients achieved a positive outcome and therefore stage 2 proceeded. In total, $9/10$ patients achieved a positive outcome. One patient developed significant non-symptomatic ICH reaccumulation at 24 hours; volume reductions in the remaining nine were stable (median $71\%$ (IQR $61{-}80\%)$ ). Representative images are shown in figure 2. Median ICH volume at 24 hours was $12\mathrm{mL}$ (IQR 5–25); $5/10$ patients achieved both $<15\mathrm{mL}$ and $<10\mathrm{mL}$ residual volume.  

The one patient with significant ICH reaccumulation had suboptimally controlled blood pressure (systolic pressure $140{-}160\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg},$ for several hours following the immediate postoperative CT, which had demonstrated good initial evacuation. Return to theatre for further hematoma evacuation did not occur. They received a tracheostomy on day 11 of admission following failed extubation and died on day 15 due to transtentorial herniation, for which the family declined intervention.

# DISCUSSION  

Our pilot study of early ICH MIS using the Aurora Surgiscope demonstrated promising surgical efficacy. Early surgery appeared safe and was feasible, commencing usually $<8$ hours post-onset; significant ICH reaccumulation was rare. The single instance of reaccumulation was associated with suboptimal control of acutely elevated postoperative blood pressure; postoperative management guidance protocols were tightened for subsequent patients.  

ICH MIS is commonly performed in Asian countries; endoscopic approaches can be surgically effective in an early time frame,11 but technical complexity and uncertainty of benefit has limited uptake in western countries. Recently a large western consecutive patient series demonstrated highly effective evacuation, low rebleeding rates, and a trend to improved survival and functional outcome with earlier versus later surgery,4 mirroring a broader MIS meta-analysis.12 A benefit trend with earlier intervention was also suggested in a fibrinolytic intraventricular hemorrhage removal meta-analysis.13  

MIS may disrupt less surrounding normal tissue that conventional craniotomy based surgery, especially for deep ICH, and may lessen the risk of early hematoma reaccumulation. Contrary to the MISTIE-II trial, which excluded many patients due to an ICH stability requirement in their protocol, an early stereotatic MIS pathway could leverage prehospital and hospital ischemic stroke protocols to channel patients rapidly post-onset to theater, for surgical control of active bleeding.  

Although most ICH expansion occurs early, a proportion experience hematoma expansion up to 24 hours after onset. Whereas use of a éstability scan’ in MISTIE-III was appropriate for an intervention employing stereotactic thrombolysis, this paradigm excludes patients with a pathophysiologically relevant target for intervention. Early MIS explicitly targets this population and, analogous to ischemic stroke, we postulate that that increasingly earlier intervention may lead to ever greater benefit. Advances in device technology to improve surgical visibility and better facilitate hematoma removal will make this treatment paradigm feasible at most neurosurgery capable stroke centers.  

The promising results of this study have led to the phase 2B/3 seamless design EVACUATE randomised controlled trial (UltraEarly, Minimally inVAsive intraCerebral hemorrhage evacUATion vs Standard treatment, Clinicaltrials.gov NCT03342664), with surgery occurring within 8 hours of onset. The two participating centers in the currently reported study have commenced enrolment; new centres without prior experience will perform a minimum of five virtually proctored ‘run-in’ cases before being able to enrol patients into the randomised controlled trial. A similar MIS approach, aiming to ameliorate hematoma expansion and early blood related cytotoxicity, is being tested in several other randomised trials using various devices, with early variably defined (Clinicaltrials.g0v NCT05460793, NCT04434807 and NCT02880878).  

Study limitations include the small numbers of patients and sites involved. Our cohort comprised generally very large and clinically severe ICH. Arrival to surgery times require abridging; urgent surgery of this nature is difficult to achieve in a trial context without proven benefit, as other cases may take precedence for emergency theater access. Only $5/10$ patients achieved $<15\mathrm{mL}$ ICH residual at 24 hours. However, increasing operator experience and device refinement will likely foster faster and more complete ICH removal.  



# CONCLUSION  

In this study, early minimally invasive ICH removal using the Aurora Surgiscope and Evacuator was feasible, appeared to be safe, and warrants further evaluation.
