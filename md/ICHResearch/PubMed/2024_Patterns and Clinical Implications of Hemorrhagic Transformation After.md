# Patterns and Clinical Implications of Hemorrhagic Transformation After Thrombolysis in Acute Ischemic Stroke  

Results From the ENCHANTED Study  

Yanan Wang, MD, PhD, Toshiki Maeda, MD, PhD, MPH, Shoujiang You, MD, PhD, Chen Chen, MD, PhD, Leibo Liu, PhD, Zien Zhou, MD, PhD, Thompson G. Robinson, BMedSci, MD, FRCP, FESO, et al.  

Neurology® 2024;103:e210020. doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000210020  

# Abstract  

# Background and Objectives  

Hemorrhagic transformation may be a potentially devastating complication of IV thrombolysis (IVT) in acute ischemic stroke, but what degree of hemorrhage indicates the greatest negative effect is not known. We aimed to define the associations between hemorrhagic transformation patterns, classified according to clinical and imaging categories, and clinical outcomes after IVT.  

# Methods  

We conducted a post hoc analysis from the international Enhanced Control of Hypertension and Thrombolysis Stroke Study. Symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) was defined based on established criteria, such as the Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke-Monitoring Study (SITS-MOST) criteria. Asymptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage (aICH) was defined as any intracerebral hemorrhage that did not meet the criteria for sICH. Imaging subtypes of hemorrhagic transformation were assessed using the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification system. The primary outcome was death or major disability, defined by modified Rankin scale (mRS) scores 3–6 at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included death, death or disability $(\mathrm{mRS}2{-}6)$ , and poor health-related quality of life (HRQoL), defined as an overall heath utility score $\leq0.7$ (mean).  

# Results  

Of the 4,370 participants, 779 $(17.8\%)$ developed any intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), with a median time from randomization to hemorrhage of 23.5 hours (interquartile range 18.92–26.07). According to the SITS-MOST criteria, 62 patients ( $1.4\%$ of 4,370) were classified as sICH, and 717 patients ( $16.4\%$ of 4,370) were classified as aICH. sICH per SITS-MOST criteria was associated with death or major disability (odds ratio [OR] 23.05, $95\%$ CI 8.97–59.23), death (OR 20.14, $95\%$ CI 11.32–35.84), death or disability (OR 61.36, $95\%$ CI 8.40–448.01), and poor HRQoL (OR 17.87, $95\%$ CI 7.47–42.71). Similarly, aICH per SITS-MOST criteria was also associated with death or major disability (OR 2.23, $95\%$ CI 1.84–2.70), death (OR 1.82, $95\%$ CI 1.39–2.38), death or disability (OR 2.29, $95\%$ CI 1.87–2.80), and poor HRQoL (OR 1.81, $95\%$ CI 1.50–2.18). Comparable associations were observed for sICH and aICH defined by other criteria, as well as for imaging subtypes based on Heidelberg Bleeding Classification system.  

# Discussion  

All forms of post-IVT hemorrhagic transformation in acute ischemic stroke are associated with increased odds of poor clinical outcomes. Of note, aICH after IVT should not be considered clinically innocuous.

# Introduction  

Hemorrhagic transformation is a potentially devastating complication of IV thrombolysis (IVT) after acute ischemic stroke, defined as hemorrhage within the infarcted area or parenchyma hemorrhage outside the infarct zone.1 According to the presence of worsening neurologic deficits or the development of new symptoms, hemorrhagic transformation can be classified as either symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) or asymptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage (aICH).2 Although studies are consistent in showing that sICH predicts a poor prognosis after IVT,3-6 the evidence is mixed regarding the effect of aICH, with contradictory results in different studies investigating functional outcome.6-11 Based on its imaging appearance alone (Heidelberg Bleeding Classification system), hemorrhagic transformation can be categorized into hemorrhagic infarction (HI), parenchymal hemorrhage (PH), and other subtypes. HI is further subdivided into small petechiae along the infarct margins (HI1) or more confluent petechiae within the infarcted area without a space-occupying effect (HI2). PH is characterized by a hematoma occupying $30\%$ of the infarcted area with a slight space-occupying effect (PH1) or a hematoma exceeding $30\%$ of the infarcted area with significant mass effect (PH2). Other subtypes include hemorrhage in areas outside the cerebral infarction, subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, and subdural hematoma.2 Although PH2 after IVT is clearly a stronger predictor of poor prognosis,12-14 the associations of the other imaging subtypes of hemorrhagic transformation with adverse outcome are unclear. Some studies suggest HI represents successful reperfusion and is therefore a good surrogate measure of clinical outcome,14,15 whereas other studies have found no such relationship.13 Again, positive13 and neutral12,14 associations have been shown across studies between HI2 or PH1 and poor postIVT outcome. Besides functional outcome, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is also an especially important outcome that reflects the overall well-being and daily functioning of patients.16,17 However, there is a lack of studies investigating the effects of hemorrhagic transformation on quality of life in patients with acute ischemic stroke after thrombolysis.  

We aimed to determine the relations of the patterns of hemorrhagic transformation (by clinical and imaging categories) and clinical outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke after IVT in the Enhanced Control of Hypertension and Thrombolysis Stroke Study (ENCHANTED).  

# Methods  

# Study Design and Data Sources  

The data used in this study were obtained from the ENCHANTED trial, a prospective, randomized, multicenter, open-label, blinded-end point trial with a $2\times2$ partial factorial design.18-20 The trial included 2 treatment arms in patients eligible for IVT for acute ischemic stroke: arm A,21 which compared the efficacy and safety of low-dose $(0.6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ body weight) IV alteplase with standard-dose $\mathrm{0.9~mg/kg}$ body weight) IV alteplase and arm ${\mathrm{B},}^{22}$ which compared the efficacy and safety of early intensive blood pressure (BP) lowering (systolic target $130{-}140\mathrm{mmHg}$ ) with guideline-recommended BP control (systolic target $<180~\mathrm{mm}$ $\operatorname{Hg})$ . Arm A was conducted between March 1, 2012, and August 31, 2015, and included a total of 3,310 patients. Arm B was conducted between March 3, 2012, and April 30, 2018, and included a total of 2,227 patients.  

# Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Participant Consents  

The study protocol was approved by the appropriate ethics committee at each participating center, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient or an appropriate surrogate. The study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01422616).  

# Measurement of Baseline Variables  

Various baseline data included demographics (age, sex, and ethnicity), clinical features of systolic and diastolic BP, and severity of neurologic impairment according to scores on the NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Medical history includes hypertension, previous stroke, coronary artery disease, other heart disease, diabetes mellitus, and hypercholesterolemia. Other data were smoking status and medication (use of antihypertensive agents, warfarin anticoagulation, aspirin/ other antiplatelet agents, glucose-lowering agents, and statin/ other lipid-lowering agents). All ENCHANTED investigators were trained and certified in the use of the NIHSS.23 The etiologic subgroup of acute ischemic stroke according to the investigator’s opinion was classified as large artery occlusion because of significant atheroma, small vessel disease, cardioembolism, and other or undetermined etiology.  

# Imaging Data  

All patients underwent a brain CT or MRI scan before randomization, with ad hoc follow-up scans performed at 24–36 hours. Further brain CT or MRI scans were undertaken as part of usual clinical practice. Brain images were centrally adjudicated for any intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) by an imaging adjudication panel that was blind to clinical information.24 Any ICH was defined as the presence of hemorrhage within the infarct territory or parenchymal hematoma outside the infarct zone that was not seen on the initial imaging but detected on follow-up scans. sICH was defined according to criteria of the Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke-Monitoring Study (SITS-MOST),25 National Institutes of Neurological Diseases and Stroke (NINDS) study,26 European-Australian Cooperative Acute Stroke Study 2 (ECASS2),27 ECASS3,28 and the Third International Stroke Trial (IST-3)29 (eTable 1). aICH was defined as any ICH not meeting criteria for sICH. Imaging subtypes of hemorrhagic transformation were assessed using the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification, which includes HI1, HI2, PH1, PH2, and other subtypes (eTable 2).30 The times and imaging modalities used for the assessment of sICH are outlined in eTable 3.  

# Outcome Measures  

The primary outcome of this study was death or major disability, defined as scores 3–6 on the modified Rankin scale (mRS) at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included death at 90 days; death or disability, defined as scores 2–6 on the mRS at 90 days; and poor HRQoL at 90 days. HRQoL was assessed using 3-level version of the 5-Dimensional European Quality of Life Scale (EQ-5D), with poor HRQoL defined as an overall heath utility score ${\le}0.7$ (mean). Scores from each EQ-5D subscale were combined into a single utility score using UK population-based preference weights. Details about HRQoL and EQ-5D in the ENCHANTED study have been published previously.31 Outcome assessments were carried out by investigators who were not involved in the patient’s clinical management and were blinded to treatment allocation. Evaluations at $90~\pm~7$ days were conducted through structured telephone interviews or face-to-face consultations with surviving patients or their caregivers. All ENCHANTED investigators were trained and certified in the use of mRS.32  

# Statistical Analysis  

Data were reported as mean (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentage for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the Student t test or the Mann-Whitney $U$ test, and categorical variables were compared between groups using the $\chi^{2}$ test or Fisher exact test. Univariate analyses were performed to compare baseline factors associated with any ICH and outcomes, with significant variables $\left(p<0.1\right)$ or considered clinically relevant to the outcomes included in multivariable logistic regression models. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the association between patterns of hemorrhagic transformation (clinical and imaging categories) and outcomes. The results were reported as odds ratio (OR) and $95\%$ CI. ORs were adjusted for time from symptom onset to hospital; age; male sex; Asian ethnicity; NIHSS score; history of hypertension, previous stroke, coronary artery disease, other heart disease, diabetes mellitus, and hypercholesterolemia; use of antihypertensive agents, warfarin anticoagulation, aspirin, or other antiplatelet agents, glucoselowering agents, and statin or other lipid-lowering agents; final diagnosis at the time of hospital separation; allocation to lowdose alteplase (arm A); and allocation to early intensive blood pressure management (arm B). Sensitivity analyses were performed separately for arm A and arm B. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX) and $p$ -values $_{<0.05}$ (2-sided) were considered significant.

# Results  

# Baseline Characteristics  

A total of 4,370 ENCHANTED participants were included in this study. The mean (SD) age of the patients was 66.94 (12.65) years, $62.4\%$ were male, and $65.9\%$ were from Asia. Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Of these participants, 779 $(17.8\%)$ developed any ICH, with the median time from randomization to detection being 23.5 hours (IQR 18.92–26.07). Among these 779 participants with any ICH, 574 cases were identified by CT scans, 204 by MRI scans, and 1 participant had no reported imaging modality (eTable 3). The proportion of sICH and aICH varied according to different criteria (Figure 1A). The proportion of sICH was the highest at $6.4\%$ (278/4,370) per the NINDS criteria and the lowest at $1.4\%$ (62/4,370) per the SITS-MOST criteria. Conversely, the proportion of aICH was the highest at $16.4\%$ (717/4,370) per the SITS-MOST criteria and the lowest at $11.5\%$ (501/4,370) per the NINDS criteria. The proportion of Heidelberg Bleeding Classification categories were $5.5\%$ for HI1 (240/4,350), $4.1\%$ for HI2 (177/4,350), $3.2\%$ for PH1 (139/4,350), $2.8\%$ for PH2 (121/4,350), and $1.9\%$ (82/4,350) for other (Figure 1B). Among the 4,370 patients followed up after 90 days, 1,530 participants $(35\%)$ experienced death or major disability $(\mathrm{mRS}3-6)$ , 364 $\left(8.3\%\right)$ died, 2,159 $\left(49.4\%\right)$ experienced death or disability $(\mathrm{mRS}2{-}6)$ , and 1,500 $(34.3\%)$ had poor HRQoL.  

Compared with patients without any ICH, those with any ICH were significantly older (69.3 vs 66.4 years, $p<0.001$ ) and had higher NIHSS scores on admission (12 vs 7, $p<0.001,$ . The any ICH group comprised a higher proportion of coronary artery disease ( $18.4\%$ vs $13.2\%$ , $p<0.001\$ ), other heart diseases ( $8.1\%$ vs $5.8\%$ , $p=0.019,$ ), and hypercholesterolemia ( $18.5\%$ vs $14.8\%$ , $p=0.01$ ). In addition, patients with any ICH were more likely to receive antihypertensive agents $49.7\%$ vs $44.9\%$ , $p=0.016$ ), warfarin anticoagulation ( $3.6\%$ vs $2.0\%_{;}$ , $p=0.006,$ ), aspirin or other antiplatelet agents ( $28.5\%$ vs $20.2\%$ , $p<0.001,$ ), and statin or other lipid lowering agents $22.4\%$ vs $17.6\%_{;}$ , $p=0.002,$ ), but they were less like to receive early intensive BP management ( $18.6\%$ vs $25.2\%,p<0.001)$ ) (Table 1).

# Association of Hemorrhagic Transformation With Death at 90 Days  

Any ICH was associated with death at 90 days (OR 2.42, $95\%$ CI 1.88–3.11). Compared with patients without any ICH, patients with sICH per SITS-MOST criteria had a significantly increased risk of death at 90 days (OR 20.14, $95\%$ CI 11.32–35.84). Similar results were obtained for sICH per NINDS, ECASS2, ECASS3, or IST-3 criteria. Patients with aICH per SITS-MOST, ECASS3, or IST-3 criteria showed similar results, with an increased OR for death at 90 days (SITS-MOST: OR 1.82, $95\%$ CI 1.39–2.38; ECASS3: OR 1.76, $95\%$ CI 1.34–2.31; IST-3: OR 1.75, $95\%$ CI 1.33–2.31). Regarding imaging subtypes, HI2 (OR 1.83, $95\%$ CI 1.15–2.90), PH2 (OR 7.47, $95\%$ CI 4.83–11.57), and other subtypes (OR 3.24, $95\%$ CI 1.67–6.28) were associated with death at 90 days, whereas HI1 (OR 1.33, $95\%$ CI 0.86–2.06) and PH1 (OR 1.46, $95\%$ CI 0.88–2.41) showed no significant difference (Figure 3). Similar results were found in sensitivity analyses (eTable 6).  

# Association of Hemorrhagic Transformation With Death or Disability at 90 Days  

Any ICH was associated with death or disability $(\mathrm{mRS}2{-}6)$ at 90 days (OR 2.60, $95\%$ CI 2.14–3.18). Compared with patients without any ICH, patients with sICH or aICH per SITSMOST criteria had a significantly increased risk of death or disability at 90 days (sICH: OR 61.36, $95\%$ CI 8.40–448.01; aICH: OR 2.29, $95\%$ CI 1.87–2.80). Similar results were obtained for sICH and aICH per NINDS, ECASS2, ECASS3, or IST-3 criteria. Regarding imaging subtypes, HI1 (OR 2.65, $95\%$ CI 1.89–3.72), HI2 (OR 1.68, $95\%$ CI 1.17–2.42), PH1 (OR 2.68, $95\%$ CI 1.68–4.28), PH2 (OR 5.32, $95\%$ CI 3.10–9.13), and other subtypes (OR 2.14, $95\%$ CI 1.31–3.52) were associated with death or disability at 90 days (Figure 4). Similar results were found in sensitivity analyses (eTable 7).  

# Association of Hemorrhagic Transformation With Poor HRQoL at 90 Days  

Any ICH was associated with poor HRQoL at 90 days (OR 2.10, $95\%$ CI 1.74–2.51). Compared with patients without any ICH, patients with sICH or aICH per SITSMOST criteria had a significantly increased risk of poor HRQoL at 90 days (sICH: OR 17.87, $95\%$ CI 7.47–42.71; aICH: OR 1.81, $95\%$ CI 1.50–2.18). Similar results were obtained for sICH and aICH per NINDS, ECASS2, ECASS3, or IST-3 criteria. Regarding imaging subtypes, HI1 (OR 2.06, $95\%$ CI 1.53–2.79), HI2 (OR 1.44, $95\%$ CI 1.01–2.04), PH2 (OR 3.65, $95\%$ CI 2.38–5.59), and other subtypes (OR 2.55, $95\%$ CI 1.56–4.18) were associated with poor HRQoL at 90 days. There was also a trend of association between PH1 and poor HRQoL at 90 days (OR 1.46, $95\%$ CI 0.99–2.14) (Figure 5). Similar results were found in sensitivity analyses (eTable 8).

# Discussion  

In this post hoc analysis of the ENCHANTED trial database, we have defined the relationship of different categories of hemorrhagic transformation (clinical or imaging) and clinical outcomes after IVT for acute ischemic stroke. Our findings revealed that all forms of hemorrhagic transformation in relation to IVT are associated with poor functional outcome and reduced HRQoL.  

In this study, the incidence of any ICH after thrombolysis was $17.8\%$ , which falls within the reported range of $0\%-85\%$ . This wide variation can be attributed to factors such as the time frame, imaging modality, and treatment type used. For instance, ECASS2 investigators reported over $50\%$ of hemorrhagic transformation in patients using serial CT scans within 1 week,27 whereas RHAPSODY investigators found evidence of hemorrhage in $80\%$ of patients at 30 days using MRI, although most of these were microbleeds.35 The incidence of any ICH is reported to be $10\%{-}48\%$ after thrombolysis and $31\%-$ $80\%$ after thrombectomy .35-37 These variations highlight the importance of standardized protocols and time frames in assessing HT frequency across studies.  

Our study confirms previous research3-6 indicating that patients with any ICH or sICH after IVT have an increased likelihood of subsequent death and disability after acute ischemic stroke. In addition, our findings regarding post-IVT aICH align with a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which demonstrated that aICH was associated with poor functional outcome regardless of IVT or thrombectomy use.38 Notably, aICH per SITS-MOST, ECASS3, and IST-3 criteria was associated with increased 90-day mortality, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed with NINDS or ECASS2 criteria. These inconsistencies might be due to the different criteria of aICH. In our study, aICH was defined relative to sICH. Given a fixed total number of hemorrhagic transformation cases, some cases may be classified as sICH according to certain criteria, but may be classified as aICH according to other criteria. Specifically, the ECASS3 and SITS-MOST criteria have more stringent definitions for sICH, requiring an increase of 4 points in the NIHSS score or hemorrhage leading to patient death for classification. By contrast, the NINDS and ECASS2 criteria have more lenient definitions, classifying any decrease in neurologic function as sICH.2 Consequently, patients experiencing hemorrhagic transformation with less than a 4-point increase in the NIHSS score may be classified as aICH according to ECASS3 and SITS-MOST criteria but as sICH according to NINDS and ECASS2 criteria. Furthermore, imaging subtypes are another important factor to clarify sICH, beyond just neurologic deterioration. Although both NINDS and ECASS criteria define sICH as neurologic deterioration plus any hemorrhage, their definitions of “any hemorrhage” differ. In NINDS criteria, “any hemorrhage” includes hemorrhagic infarction, hematoma within or remote from the infarcted area, intraventricular hemorrhage, and subarachnoid hemorrhage.39 By contrast, ECASS criteria define “any hemorrhage” as including only hemorrhagic infarction and parenchymal hematoma.27,28,40 Therefore, a hematoma remote from the infarcted area plus neurologic deterioration would be classified as sICH under the NINDS definition but not under the ECASS definition. This discrepancy leads to different distributions of sICH and aICH proportions under different definitions. In this context, it is important to recognize the impact of aICH on patient outcomes after IVT, given its higher incidence $(10\%-$ $\bar{3}0\%\Bigg)^{38}$ compared with sICH $(2\%-7\%)$ .2

In terms of imaging subtypes of hemorrhagic transformation, we observed that patients with PH2 after IVT were at high risk of poor functional outcome, consistent with findings from other studies.12-14 Previous studies have consistently identified PH as a marker of delayed recanalization and a predictor of poor outcome after stroke.41-45 Notably, this trend extended to various subtypes of hemorrhagic transformation, including HI1, HI2, PH1, and others subtypes, contrary to previous reports. 13-15 Although HI has traditionally been recognized as a marker of early recanalization and favorable outcome,41 some studies have cast doubt on this belief, with HI found to be related with poor functional outcome in the general stroke population,43,46 and in stroke patients with atrial fibrillation. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 51 studies involving 100,510 patients revealed that all imaging forms of hemorrhagic transformation, except HI1, are associated with poor functional outcome at 90 days, regardless of IVT or thrombectomy.48 These research findings prompt a reevaluation of the influence of different subtypes of hemorrhagic transformation on prognosis, suggesting the need for further investigation to customize suitable treatment strategies.34  

To date, no studies have reported the impact of hemorrhagic transformation after IVT on HRQoL, which comprehensively reflects patients’ mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/ discomfort, and anxiety/depression.49 Our findings suggest that any ICH, aICH, and sICH were associated with an increased risk of poor HRQoL. Moreover, all forms of hemorrhagic transformation based on imaging subtypes were associated with poor HRQoL. These findings fill a knowledge gap in this field and highlight the importance of considering the potential long-term impact of hemorrhagic transformation on quality of life in the management of ischemic stroke.  

This study highlights the importance of considering all types of hemorrhagic transformation in stroke patients, not just symptomatic forms. Both symptomatic and asymptomatic hemorrhages can significantly affect functional outcome and quality of life. Clinicians should use more personalized management strategies for patients at high risk of hemorrhagic transformation. Although there are several risk factors of hemorrhagic transformation, such as age, stroke severity, infarct size, and atrial fibrillation, only a few can be modified.2 Treatments targeting free radicals and inflammation have shown promising results in animal studies50 but have not shown efficacy in clinical trials.51,52 Future research should focus on finding better ways to prevent hemorrhagic transformation and on developing more effective treatment strategies for stroke patients.  

Strengths of our study include the use of a large data set from an international, multicenter, prospective study with standardized and near complete data collection, where imaging assessment were performed blind to clinical data. This allowed us to provide fresh insights into the impact of aICH on post-IVT outcomes in acute ischemic stroke outcomes and emphasize the need for further research to better understand this entity. Our study has also highlighted the importance of differentiating between imaging subtypes of hemorrhagic transformation in predicting functional outcome, including HRQoL. However, our study had some limitations. First, our study’s sample size for other imaging subtypes (hemorrhage in areas outside the cerebral infarction, subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, and subdural hematoma) remained relatively small $\mathrm{~\boldmath~\omega~}_{\mathrm{{n}}}=82\mathrm{~\boldmath~\omega~}_{\mathrm{{}}}$ ), precluding a comprehensive examination of their individual effects on outcomes. Further research is needed to investigate the relationship between these subtypes and outcomes. Second, because of insufficient data, our study could not explore the impact of hemorrhagic transformation on long-term outcomes (i.e., 12 months) or provide information on the management of sICH or aICH, which may have influenced the outcomes. Addressing these gaps should be a priority for future research. Third, although we adjusted for potential confounders that could affect the outcome measures in the multivariable analysis, residual confounding may still be present, which can lead to underreporting of the association of aICH with poor outcomes after thrombolysis.  

In summary, all forms of hemorrhagic transformation in relation to IVT after acute ischemic stroke are associated with increased odds of poor functional outcome, death, and poor HRQoL at 90 days. The likelihood of these outcomes is particularly high for patients with sICH and aICH per various criteria. All imaging subtypes of hemorrhagic transformation, including HI1, HI2, PH1, PH2, and other subtypes, were also associated with adverse clinical outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring for hemorrhagic transformation after IVT and suggest that efforts need to be made to prevent and manage hemorrhagic transformation so that patients have the best opportunity for favorable recovery and HRQoL.
