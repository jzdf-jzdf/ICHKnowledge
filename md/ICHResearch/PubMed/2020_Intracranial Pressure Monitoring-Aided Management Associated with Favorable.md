# Intracranial Pressure Monitoring-Aided Management Associated with Favorable Outcomes in Patients with Hypertension-Related Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage  

Junwei Ren1 $\cdot$ Xing Wu1 & Jiongwei Huang1 & Xudong Cao2 & Qiang Yuan1 $\cdot$ Dalong Zhang3 & Zhuoying Du1 & Ping Zhong1 & Jin Hu1  

Received: 28 August 2019 / Revised: 23 February 2020 / Accepted: 26 February 2020

# Abstract  

To investigate the effect of intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring on the functional outcome of patients with hypertension-related spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). We included 196 patients with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores of 3–12 in this observational study, of which 103 underwent ICP monitors. Binary and ordinal regression analyses were used to estimate the effect of ICP monitoring on the functional outcome. The rate of adverse events, blood pressure control, and length of hospitalization were compared between the two groups. ICP monitoring had a significant impact on the clinical outcome of patients by shifting the Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS-E) scores in a favorable direction $(p=0.027)$ and reducing mortality at discharge $(p=0.004)$ and 6 months later $(p=0.02)$ . The rate of favorable outcome at 6 months was higher in the ICP-monitored group $(p=0.03)$ . However, subgroup analysis showed that no relationship between ICP monitoring and clinical outcome was found for patients with GCS scores of 3–8. For patients with GCS scores of 9–12, the distribution of GOS-E scores at 6 months shifted in a favorable direction in the ICP-monitored group $(p=0.001\$ ). The rate of favorable outcome at 6 months was higher in the ICP-monitored group $(p=0.01\$ ). The mortality at discharge and 6 months later was also lower in the ICP-monitored group. Thus, our study supports the value of ICP monitoring in hypertension-related ICH patients with GCS scores of 3–12, especially those with GCS scores of 9–12.

# Introduction  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), which is the least treatable form of stroke, affects more than 1 million people annually worldwide [1, 2]. Nearly one-third of stroke cases are caused by ICH [3], and $60{-}70\%$ are due to hypertension-related ICH [4, 5]. However, no interventions have been demonstrated to improve outcomes of ICH patients [2, 6, 7]. Elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) is a common complication of spontaneous ICH, mainly caused by the mass effect of hematoma, peripheral cerebral edema, and obstructive hydrocephalus [8–11]. The main cause of death in the acute phase of ICH is elevated ICP due to an extending hematoma [12–14]. Early discovery and promptly treatment of elevated ICP are supposed to decrease the morbidity and mortality of ICH patients. Since there is limited data on the role of ICP monitoring in the management of spontaneous ICH patients, current ICP management for ICH is mainly derived from the guideline for traumatic brain injury [15]. The role of ICP monitoring in ICH and its effect on outcomes of ICH patients are largely unknown. In this study, we retrospectively investigated ICP monitoring in patients with hypertensionrelated ICH and compared the outcomes with those of patients with hypertension-related ICH, who underwent ICP monitoring and those who did not receive ICP monitoring.  

# Methods  

# Study Design and Participants  

Patients with spontaneous ICH admitted to our neurological intensive care unit between May 2010 and May 2017 were retrospectively included in our monitoring database. Inclusion criteria were spontaneous supratentorial ICH confirmed on computed tomography (CT) scan, age $>14$ and $\leq70$ years, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score $\leq12$ , known time of ICH onset, more than one CT scan available, and admission within $^{24\mathrm{~h~}}$ of symptom onset. Exclusion criteria were infratentorial hemorrhage, GCS score $>12$ , definite evidence that the ICH was secondary to a brain structural abnormality (e.g., arteriovenous malformation, intracranial aneurysm, tumor, or trauma), cerebral infarction within the last 30 days or recent use of thrombolysis for ischemic stroke (or other vascular condition), primary intraventricular hemorrhage, antiplatelet medicationrelated ICH, vitamin K antagonists-related ICH, new anticoagulant medication-related ICH, subsequent surgery or ventriculostomy placement, or the patient died within the next $^{24\mathrm{h}}$ . All patients were treated according to the guidelines for managing spontaneous ICH [15, 16]. The implantation of the ICP monitor was based on the neurosurgeon’s experience and the choice of patients or their legal surrogates. The evacuation of intracranial hematoma and/or decompressive craniectomy was performed according to an individual consensus of the neurosurgeon. This retrospective observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal surrogates according to national regulations.  

# Clinical Management  

Patients were divided into ICP-monitored and non-ICPmonitored groups. Patients in the ICP-monitored group underwent implantation of an ICP probe (Codman; Johnson & Johnson, Raynham, MA, USA). The ICP sensor was inserted into the anterior horn of the lateral ventricle contralateral to the hematoma, allowing for drainage of cerebral spinal fluid (CSF). All patients were treated according to a standardized management protocol. An ICP goal of $<$ $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ was maintained using a stepwise management strategy [17]. Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) was targeted at $\geq$ $60\mathrm{mmHg}$ at all times. The non-ICP-monitored group was managed based on imaging or clinical examinations. Clinical neurological status, such as GCS score, pupil size, and reactivity, were monitored every hour in both groups. Head CT scans were performed at admission; preoperatively; immediately after surgery; at 1, 2, 5, and 7 days after admission; and at any other time of clinical indication in all patients. If the pressure exceeded $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ , CSF was drained through the intraventricular catheter. Drainage of CSF was intermittent; efforts were made to remove the minimum volume of fluid required to control ICP. Mild hyperventilation $({\mathrm{PCO}}_{2}$ , $30-$ $33\mathrm{mmHg})$ was applied as necessary. If the ICP rose to $>$ $30\mathrm{mmHg}$ or the CPP dropped to $<60\mathrm{mmHg}$ over $30~\mathrm{min}$ and failed to respond to the abovementioned medical treatment, decompressive craniectomy was performed. In addition, for each case, we administrated urapidil or nicardipine intravenously to control blood pressure according to the ASA guidelines.  

# Data Collection  

Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, mean BP, admission GCS score, National Institute of Health stroke scale (NIHSS), modified Rankin scale (mRS), ICH site, change in pupillary reactions (one reacting or none reacting), and radiographic parameters (such as hematoma volume and midline shift) at admission, were recorded. The following parameters were recorded in hospital: ICP, BP, CPP (measured hourly on postoperative days 1–7), surgery (hematoma evacuation, decompressive craniotomy, and ICP monitoring), adverse events, and functional outcome. Adverse events were defined as catheter-related events (catheter-related infection, malfunction, unplanned ICP catheter removal, or catheterrelated hemorrhage), hydrocephalus, pneumonia, sepsis, nervous system infection, cardiac shock/arrest, renal failure, GCS score decreased by 2, or a new loss in pupil reactivity. Furthermore, we represented blood pressure variability (BPV) as the coefficient of variation, i.e., the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean BP, which indicates BP dispersion independent of mmHg. All patients were followed up by telephone by the same physician after 6 months.  

# Outcome  

The primary outcome measure was the score on the Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS-E) [18], which assesses the effect of ICH on patients’ function 6 months after discharge and the proportion of participants with a favorable outcome, defined as moderate disability and good recovery. A GOS-E score of 1 indicates death, 2 indicates a vegetative state, 3–4 indicates severe disability, 5–6 indicates moderate disability, and 7–8 indicates good recovery. Also, we assessed the mRS at 6 months after discharge, mortality at discharge and 6 months after discharge, duration of hospitalization, the incidence of adverse events during hospitalization, mannitol usage, glycerin fructose usage, hypertonic saline usage, and proportion of systolic arterial pressure control during hospitalization.  

# Statistical Analysis  

The data distributions of the two groups (ICP monitored vs. not monitored) were assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The baseline characteristics were compared using the chisquare or Fisher’s exact test for dichotomous variables and the $t$ test or Mann–Whitney $U$ test for continuous variables. Following the univariate analyses, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed at a significance level of $p<0.20$ . Multivariate ordinal logistic regression was used for the primary analysis to compare the distributions of the 6-month GOS-E scores (collapsed to six categories by pooling death with vegetative state and lower severe disability) between the two groups while controlling for baseline covariates [19]. The primary outcome was also analyzed using multivariate binary logistic regression by dichotomizing the GOS-E scores into binary outcomes (unfavorable outcome, score of 1–4; favorable outcome, score of 5–8). The resulting odds ratios (ORs) and $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Other continuous variables were tested with the $t$ test, and other outcomes were tested with the chi-square test or logistic regression. All statistical analyses were performed by using the SPSS for Windows version 22.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and the threshold for statistical significance was a two-sided $\alpha=0.05$ .  

# Results  

# Demographic and Clinical Characteristics  

In total, 196 patients were included in this study from May 2010 to May 2017. A flow diagram outlining patients’ selection process was shown in Fig. 1. Of all patients, 87 $(44.4\%)$ had GCS scores of 3–8 on admission, and 109 $(55.6\%)$ had GCS scores of 9–12. The mean age was 54 years; 142 $(72.4\%)$ patients were male, and the median GCS score on arrival was 9 (3–12). The median NIHSS and mRS were 25 (12–38) and 5 (3–5), respectively. And, 152 $(77.6\%)$ had a basal ganglia hemorrhage, and 79 $(40.3\%)$ had broken ventricles. The mean hematoma volume was $36.3\mathrm{~ml}$ , and 121 $(61.7\%)$ patients had a midline shift $>5~\mathrm{mm}$ ; 99 $(50.5\%)$ underwent evacuation of a hematoma by craniotomy (82 in the ICP-monitored group and 17 in the non-ICP-monitored group), and 28 $(14.3\%)$ underwent a decompressive craniectomy (23 in the ICP-monitored group and 5 in the non-ICP-monitored group). An ICP monitor was inserted in 103 $(52.3\%)$ patients, and 93 $(47.7\%)$ cases received no monitor. The mean duration of ICP monitoring was $4.0\pm1.4$ days, and an $\mathrm{ICP}\geq20\mathrm{mmHg}$ at any point was observed in $65.0\%$ patients. # Clinical Outcomes  

Our study found that ICP monitoring was not associated with clinical outcome at 6 months in the unadjusted (OR 0.86 ( $95\%$ $\operatorname{CI}0.53\substack{-1.42})$ , $p=0.56$ for ordinal analysis; OR 0.81 ( $95\%$ CI 0.46–1.42), $p{=}0.46$ for binary analysis) model. ICP monitoring was also not associated with mortality at 6 months in the unadjusted (OR 1.59 ( $95\%$ CI 0.77–3.26), $p=0.21\$ ) model. However, after adjusting age, GCS, NIHSS, decompressive craniectomy, hematoma site, intraventricular hemorrhage, midline shift, and hematoma volume, it demonstrated that the distribution of GOS-E scores shifted in a favorable direction in the ICP-monitored group in the adjusted (adjusted OR (aOR) 0.54 $95\%$ CI 0.31–0.93), $p=0.027.$ ) model (Table 2 and Fig. 2). The rate of favorable outcome at 6 months was higher in the ICP-monitored group in the adjusted ( $50.5\%$ vs. $45.2\%$ ; aOR 0.47 $(95\%\mathrm{CI}0.24-0.93)$ ), $p=0.03,$ model. The prognosis-based mRS in the adjusted model also showed a better outcome in the ICP-monitored group than the nonICP-monitored group (aOR 2.05 ( $95\%$ CI 1.19–3.55), $p=$ 0.01) (Table 2). The rates of mortality both during hospitalization ( $3.9\%$ vs. $14.0\%$ ; aOR 6.56 ( $95\%$ CI 1.80–23.92), $p=$ 0.004) and at 6 months ( $15.5\%$ vs $22.6\%$ ; aOR 2.73 ( $95\%$ CI 1.15–6.48), $p=0.02,$ were lower in the ICP-monitored group in the adjusted model. There was no significant difference in the length of hospitalization between ICP-monitored and nonICP-monitored groups ${17.7\pm7.3}$ vs. $21.0\pm15.2$ , $p=0.06,$ ). In addition, there was no significant difference between the two groups in the use of medication to control elevated ICP ( $88.3\%$ vs. $90.3\%$ ; OR 1.23 (0.49–3.07), $p=0.66)$ , such as mannitol usage ( $80.6\%$ vs. $88.2\%$ ; OR 1.80 (0.81–3.98), $p=$ 0.15) and hypertonic saline usage ( $12.6\%$ vs. $11.8\%$ ; OR 0.93 (0.39–2.19), $p=0.87$ ). # BP Control and Intracranial Hypertension During Hospitalization  

Since BP is important for the extension of hematoma in ICH patients [20], BP control rate and variability were compared between the ICP-monitoring group and the non-ICPmonitoring group. BP was controlled better in the ICPmonitored group than in the non-ICP-monitored group $(50.1\%$ vs. $41.2\%$ , $p=0.003,$ ) (Table 2). BPV in the ICPmonitored group was significantly lower $9.9\pm2.2$ vs. 10.7 $\pm2.9$ , $p=0.04,$ than that in the non-ICP-monitored group, particularly for patients with GCS scores of 9–12 ( $9.2\pm2.0$ vs. $10.2\pm2.4$ , $p=0.048)$ (Tables 2, 3 and 4).  

About $66\%$ (67/103) of patients had an episode of intracranial hypertension $\left(\geq20\mathrm{mmHg}\right)$ during hospitalization, among whom $38.8\%$ (26/67) had an episode of $\mathrm{ICP}{\geq}30\mathrm{mmHg}$ . The median CPP was $87.0\mathrm{mmHg}$ . There was no significant correlation between CPP and GCS at admission $(r=0.13$ , $p=$ 0.24). Ordinal regression analysis showed that the CPP was significantly associated with ICH outcome at 6 months after discharge in mRS (aOR 0.94 (0.89–0.99), $p=0.025,$ ), but CPP was not significantly associated with the outcome in GOS-E (aOR 1.06 (0.997–1.12), $p=0.06)$ . Also, the CPP was significantly associated with 6-month mortality (aOR 0.91 $\left(0.84-\right.$ 0.99), $p=0.022;$ . Furthermore, a subtype analysis showed that $72.9\%$ (35/48) of patients with GCS scores of 3–8 had an episode of intracranial hypertension $\mathrm{(\geq20~mmHg)}$ ), among whom $45.7\%$ (16/35) had an episode with $\mathrm{ICP}\geq30\mathrm{mmHg}$ . The median CPP was $86.8\mathrm{mmHg}$ . About $58\%$ (32/55) of patients with GCS scores of 9–12 had an episode of intracranial hypertension $(\geq20\mathrm{mmHg})$ ), among whom $31.3\%$ (10/32) had an episode with $\mathrm{ICP}\geq30\mathrm{mmHg}$ . Median CPP was $87.5\mathrm{mmHg}$ .  

We further divided those patients with ICP monitoring into two sub-groups $(\mathrm{ICP}\geq20\mathrm{mmHg}$ and $\mathrm{ICP}<20\mathrm{mmHg}$ ). The number of patients in the two groups was 67 and 36 respectively. Except for a slight difference in age between the two groups $\phantom{0}{52.5\pm10.3}$ vs. $57.3\pm10.2$ , $p=0.028\$ ), there was no significant difference in other baseline data. Further prognostic analysis revealed that there was no significant difference in the 6-month outcome (GOS-E: aOR 2.04 (0.91–4.58), $p=$  

0.09; mRS: aOR 0.58 (0.26–1.28), $p=0.08,$ ) and 6-month mortality (aOR 0.89 (0.24–3.30), $p=0.86,$ between the two groups.  

However, the incidence of adverse events during hospitalization was higher in the ICP-monitored group than in the non-ICP-monitored group $90.3\%$ vs. $83.6\%$ , $83.6\%$ vs. $74.1\%$ for all patients and those with GCS scores of 9–12 respectively) (Tables 2, 3 and 4), although no difference in the frequency of coma was found between the two groups (GCS scores $3-8\colon97.9\%$ vs. $89.7\%$ , $p{=}0.17,$ (Table 3).  

# Discussion  

In this study, we retrospectively investigated the effect of ICP monitoring on the outcome of hypertension-related ICH patients with ${\mathrm{GCS}}\leq12$ . First, it showed that ICP elevation (ICP $>20\mathrm{mmHg}$ ) was found in over $65\%$ of ICH patients. Second, we found that ICP monitoring was associated with a better clinical outcome and lower mortality at 6 months after discharge. Finally, the stratification analysis of ICH patients who were managed with ICP monitoring demonstrated favorable outcomes in the subgroup with GCS scores of 9–12, but not in the subgroup with GCS scores of 3–8.  

The mess effect of hematoma and obstructive hydrocephalus secondary to IVH in ICH patients can raise the patient’s ICP. But the rates of ICP elevation and extent of increase are still controversial in existed studies [21]. Recently, a systematic review showed that the pooled prevalence rate for any episode of intracranial hypertension $(>20\mathrm{mmHg})$ after ICH was $67\%$ $(95\%\mathrm{CI}51{-}84\%)$ [22]. Our study found that $65.9\%$ (67/103) of ICH patients had an episode of intracranial hypertension, which was consistent with the systematic review. Moreover, it demonstrated that $25.2\%$ (26/103) of ICH patients had an episode with $\mathrm{ICP}{\geq}30\mathrm{mmHg}$ in our study, which was not surprising because the mean hematoma volume in our study was $36.3~\mathrm{ml}$ . Most of recent studies showed that mean ICP and the frequency of occurrences with $\mathrm{ICP}\geq20\mathrm{mmHg}$ were independently associated with the mortality and a poor outcome after ICH, particularly, $\mathrm{ICP}>30\mathrm{mmHg}$ predicted higher short-term mortality [23–25]. This strongly suggests that elevated ICP complicates the clinical condition of the majority of ICH patients, and it is necessary to perform ICP monitoring in ICH patients with GCS scores $\leq12$ , since ICP greater than $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ is considered as a threshold for clinical management in neurocritical care.  

Up to now, the evidence to support clinical decisionmaking based on ICP monitoring is lacking, and the benefit of invasive ICP monitoring in the management of ICH is still controversial. Recently, a retrospective study compared the outcomes of ICH in patients with ICP monitoring to outcomes in those without ICP monitoring. Based on the matched analysis, this study demonstrated that ICP monitoring was not associated with reduced mortality rates in ICH patients at 90 days or during hospitalization. Moreover, functional outcomes at 90 days appeared to be worse in ICH patients who underwent ICP monitoring [21]. However, the lack of standardized protocol for ICP monitoring in this study, such as parenchymal monitor vs ventriculostomy and monitor placement site, may lead to under- or over-treatment of ICH patients. Instead, with a standardized protocol for ICH management in our study, ICP monitors were inserted in the lateral ventricular contralateral to the hematoma, which allows CSF drainage at the same time ICP monitoring. Another main confounding factor in this study was nearly $50\%$ ICH patients with ICP monitoring received antiplatelet therapy since it increased the rebleeding risk for any surgical procedure. Although we excluded those ICH patients who received antiplatelet or antithrombotic therapy, our finding still showed that ICP monitoring was not associated with the long-term outcomes in the unadjusted model. However, after adjusting the confounding factors, our study revealed that ICP monitoring was associated with both long-term outcomes and lower mortality. In addition, a retrospective observational multicenter study conducted by our center to evaluate the efficacy of traumatic brain injury management guided by ICP monitoring found that ICP monitoring was significantly associated with lower 6-month mortality for patients with TBI who had a GCS score of 9–12 at admission [26]. Thus, we present the first study to explore the specific sub-groups based on GCS scores, for which ICP monitoring might be significantly associated with improved outcomes. After stratifying our patients into the moderate group (GCS 9–12) and severe group (GCS 3– 8) according to GCS score, our study showed that ICP monitoring did not have a significant therapeutic benefit for comatose patients with ICH (GCS scores of 3–8). This may be attributed to the status of the patients with ICP monitoring was relatively poorer than those without ICP monitoring in our study. The patients with ICP monitoring seemly have more pupillary abnormalities $57.3\%$ vs $45.2\%$ , $p=0.06,$ , larger hematoma $38.3\pm22.7\mathrm{ml}$ vs $34.0\pm21.9\mathrm{~m}$ , $p=$ 0.17), more midline shifting $(67.0\%$ vs $55.9\%$ , $p=0.11$ ), and more proportion of intraventricular hemorrhage $(50.5\%$ vs. $29.0\%$ , $p=0.002,$ . But more importantly, it revealed that although ICP monitoring guides clinical treatment by surveillance of dynamic pressure changes in the cranial cavity, it does not change the primary brain injury at onset after ICH.  

In addition, our study demonstrated that ICP monitoring was associated with both lower mortality and favorable outcomes in the subgroup with GCS scores of 9–12 during a long-term follow-up. An ordinal regression analysis revealed a significant favorable shift of the distribution of GOS-E scores and mRS in the ICP-monitored group at 6 months after discharge. There were several reasons for the benefits of ICP monitoring in this subpopulation. First, secondary brain injury caused by intracranial hypertension is one of the main causes of a poor prognosis, particularly for ICH patients with GCS scores of 9–12, who have a less primary injury but may further deteriorate. ICP monitoring can detect adverse events, such as secondary hemorrhage or spiking pressure, leading to timely intervention [27]. During clinical practice, if ICP is above $30\mathrm{mmHg}$ and lasts over $30\mathrm{min}$ , the patient will be underwent immediate cerebrospinal fluid drainage, followed by an urgent CT scan to detect the enlarged hematoma [17]. In our study, three patients with continuously elevated ICP of $>30\mathrm{mmHg}$ showed hematoma enlargement by following CT scan and underwent a secondary operation for hematoma evacuation. Second, a rightward shift in the cerebral autoregulatory curve in ICH patients with chronic hypertension and loss of autoregulation in the insulted brain of ICH patients increases the risk of cerebral hypoperfusion during BP control management. Thus, maintaining optimal CPP at the of time BP controlling for ICH patients is vital for treating reversible damage and preventing secondary injury. Simply BP controlling without ICP monitoring may decrease CPP, which may increase the risk of cerebral ischemia. Our study showed that the CPP was significantly associated with ICH outcome at 6 months after discharge in modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and 6-month mortality. The median CPP was $87\mathrm{mmHg}$ in ICP monitoring group, which was approximate to the median optimal CPP $83\mathrm{mmHg}$ , as evaluated by the pressure reactivity index [28]. Third, the aggravation of vasogenic edema caused by BP fluctuates may affect the outcome [29–31]. In our study, the rate of BP control in the ICP monitoring group was significantly better than that in the non-ICP monitoring group. Also, the variation of BP over time in the ICP monitoring was significantly narrower than that of non-ICP monitoring.  

The present study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, which risks recall and selection bias. Second, our study was observational, and then its results are likely to be confounded by some bias. Specifically, patients with ICP monitoring seemly have received more surgical intervention, such as surgical hematoma evacuation and decompressive craniectomy. Although we have minimized this confounding factor by statistical analysis, we still cannot confidently exclude that surgical intervention has no effect on the clinical outcome. Third, the sample size was relatively small, and thus, some of our analyses might be limited by small statistical power. Fourth, since the implantation of the ICP monitor was based on the neurosurgeon’s experience and the choice of patients or their legal surrogates, this study may have some selection biases. A further multicenter prospective randomized clinical trial is needed. More importantly, rather than using the one-size-fits-all approach, incorporating physiological and radiological information such as ICP, cerebral autoregulation, and CT angiography spot sign scores in future trials may be of help in finding a pathway to individualizing treatment for each patient.  

# Conclusions  

ICP monitoring was associated with favorable outcomes and a significant decrease in mortality at discharge and 6 months later in patients with GCS scores of 3–12, especially those with GCS scores of 9–12. Unfortunately, no significant association was observed between ICP monitoring and the outcome of patients with GCS scores of 3–8.
