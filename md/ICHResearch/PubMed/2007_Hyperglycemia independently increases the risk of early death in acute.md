# Hyperglycemia independently increases the risk of early death in acute spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage  

Kazumi Kimura⁎, Yasuyuki Iguchi, Takeshi Inoue, Kensaku Shibazaki, Noriko Matsumoto, Kazuto Kobayashi, Shinji Yamashita  

Department of Stroke Medicine, Kawasaki Medical School, 577 Matsushima, Kurashiki-city, Okayama, 701-0192, Japan  

Received 18 November 2006; received in revised form 3 February 2007; accepted 6 February 2007  

# Abstract  

Background: It is unclear whether hyperglycemia on admission in patients with acute intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) increases the risk of early death.  

Methods: 100 consecutive patients (median age, 67.8 years) with acute supratentorial ICH within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of onset were prospectively enrolled. Clinical characteristics and plasma glucose were assessed in all patients. ICH volume was measured on admission CT $(<24~\mathrm{h})$ and follow-up CT $(<48\mathrm{~h~})$ scans. Patients were divided into two groups: the death group, who died within 14 days of onset, and the survival group. The association between early death and clinical characteristics were investigated by multivariate logistic regression analysis.  

Results: The death group consisted of 11 patients (median age, 77 years), while the survival group consisted of 89 patients (median age, 67 years). The admission plasma glucose level and the ICH volume were higher in the death group than in the survival group (glucose: death, $205~\mathrm{mg/dl}$ vs. survival, $131\mathrm{mg/dl}$ , $p^{<0.0001}$ ; and ICH volume: survival, $13.6\pm15.3\mathrm{~ml}$ vs. death $101.1\pm48.7\mathrm{ml}$ , $p{<}0.0001$ . Using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, cut-off values that predicted early death were $150\mathrm{mg/dl}$ for the glucose level and $>20~\mathrm{ml}$ for the initial IVH volume. On multivariate logistic regression analysis, admission plasma glucose level $>150\mathrm{mg/dl}$ (OR 37.5, CI 1.4–992.7, $p\mathrm{=}0.03)$ and IVH volume $>20\mathrm{ml}$ (OR 64.6, CI 1.3–3173.5, $p{=}0.04,$ were independent factors associated with early death.  

Conclusion: Admission hyperglycemia may independently increase the risk of early death in acute spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage.

# 1. Introduction  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) accounts for $10\%$ to $15\%$ of stroke; the mortality rate is high, and it is associated with a poor functional outcome [1]. A large initial ICH volume and early hematoma growth are associated with neurological deterioration and poor outcome [1–3]. The following factors have been identified as being predictors of early hematoma growth: heavy drinking; low fibrinogen level; consciousness disturbance; poorly controlled diabetes; use of an antithrombotic agent; high fever; and high blood pressure [1–3].  

On the other hand, in patients with acute ischemic stroke and subarachnoid hemorrhage, hyperglycemia has been reported to be a predictor of poor outcome [4–8]. Hyperglycemia after acute stroke may be due to several mechanisms, such as acute stress, autonomic hormonal changes, and metabolic alteration as a result of brain injury [9]. Therefore, we hypothesized that hyperglycemia at admission was associated with patients' poor outcome in acute spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage as well as ischemic stroke. We studied that hyperglycemia independently increased the risk of early death in acute spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage.  

# 2. Methods  

Consecutive patients with spontaneous supratentorial ICH who were admitted to our stroke center within the first $^{24\mathrm{~h~}}$ of onset from April 2004 to June 2006 were enrolled. A detailed history of vascular risk factors, drug abuse, and concomitant medication was obtained from each patient. Patients with ICH related to vascular malformation were excluded from this study. NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores were recorded to assess the level of consciousness and the neurological status on admission $(<24~\mathrm{h})$ and on followup $24\mathrm{h}$ , $48\mathrm{{h}}$ , 7th day, and discharge day or 28th day). Casefatality within 14 days of onset was used as a measure indicative of poor outcome. Patients were divided into two groups: the death group, who died within 14 days of onset, and the survival group.  

Two cranial CT scans were carried out: at the baseline visit $(<24~\mathrm{h})$ and on follow-up (24 to $48\mathrm{~h~}$ of onset). All cranial CT scans were done according to the Radiology Department protocol, with an image matrix of $340\times340$ and a slice width of $8{-}10~\mathrm{mm}$ .  

Investigators who read the CT scans were blinded to the clinical information. ICH volume was measured on baseline and follow-up cranial CTs, according to the formula $A\timesB\timesC\times0.5$ , [10] where $A$ and $B$ represent the largest perpendicular diameters through the hyperdense area on CT scan, and $C$ represents the thickness of the ICH (the number of $8{-}10{-}\mathrm{mm}$ slices with hemorrhage).  

The location of the ICH was classified as being in the putamen, thalamus, lobar, and other locations. Hematoma enlargement was defined as a hematoma that grew more than $33\%$ of its initial volume [11].  

Arterial blood pressure and temperature values were obtained immediately prior to CT scanning. All patients had baseline blood samples drawn in the emergency room before CT scan $(n=100)$ ). The main hemostatic variables (leukocyte count, erythrocyte count, platelet count, and fibrinogen), $\mathrm{HbAl_{C}},$ total-cholesterol, triglyceride, CRP, albumin, glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (GOT), glutamic pyruvic transaminase (GPT), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), sodium, potassium, and glucose levels were determined.  

Statistical analysis was done using the Statview (version 5) statistical package. Significance for inter-group differences was assessed by Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney $U$ test and the Kruskal–Wallis $U$ test for continuous variables. To study the correlations between continuous variables, the Spearman correlation coefficient was used. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was done to determine the factors that could be considered as independent predictors of early death. Variables showing a $p{<}0.1$ on univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. The cut-off points of each continuous variable for predicting early death were obtained using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. $\mathrm{A}p$ value of $<0.05$ was considered significant.  

The study protocol followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.  

# 3. Results  

A total of 100 patients $(68.7\pm12.7\$ years old, 43 female) was enrolled in the present study. The mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ of the  

NIHSS score on admission for all patients was $12.4\pm7.8$ . The locations of the hematoma were: lobar in 17 patients, the putamen in 33, the thalamus in 44, and other locations in 6. Eleven patients $(11\%)$ died within 14 days of onset because of brain herniation. The interval from stroke onset to death was $4.1\pm3.5$ days; in these patients, the location of the hematoma was in the putamen in 4 patients, in the thalamus in 2 in thalamus, and lobar in 4.

# 4. Discussion  

In the present study, admission hyperglycemia was identified as an independent predictor of early death. Patients with an ischemic stroke or subarachnoid hemorrhage who were hyperglycemic tend to have a worse neurological outcome [4–8]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia was associated with the increased rate of hemorrhagic transformation with thrombolytic therapy [12–16]. In animal experiments of cerebral ischemia, the deleterious effects of hyperglycemia appear to be due to acidosis, release of excitatory amino acids, exaggeration of edema formation, blood–brain barrier (BBB) injury, and hemorrhagic transformation of the infarct [17,18]. However, few reports have dealt with the relationship between hyperglycemia and outcome in patients with acute brain hemorrhage [19–21]. In acute ICH patients, brain edema is also usually associated with deterioration. Gebel reported that relative edema, which is the edema volume divided by the hematoma volume, was strongly predictive of functional outcome in patients with hyperacute supratentorial spontaneous ICH and brain edema volume increases by approximately $75\%$ during the first $^{24\mathrm{~h~}}$ after hyperacute spontaneous ICH [22,23]. Subsequently, brain edema increased gradually over time. In an experimental ICH model, Song et al. reported that hyperglycemia caused more profound brain edema than a normal glucose level [24]. Therefore, it is possible that the presence of hyperglycemia may increase brain edema, which then results in a poor outcome.  

Hyperglycemia in the setting of acute neurological injury is attributed to a catecholamine surge and the generalized stress response [4]. Tracey and Murros concluded that hyperglycemia was simply a stress response to an acute stroke, and a poor clinical outcome was quite evident in hyperglycemic patients [25,26]. An additional cause of an increased glucose level was a history or the presence of the potential for having diabetes mellitus. In fact, the present study showed that the glucose level was significantly correlated with $\mathrm{HbAl_{C}}$ , however the admission glucose level had no significant correlation with the ICH volume. On subgroup analysis, the glucose level was higher in patients with an ICH volume $>15.0\mathrm{ml}$ than in patients with an ICH volume $<6.0~\mathrm{~ml~}$ or $6.1{-}15.0~\mathrm{ml}$ . The location of the hematoma was not associated with the glucose level. Therefore, the glucose level was mainly associated with brain damage when the ICH volume was over $15~\mathrm{ml}$ .  

In the present study, hyperglycemia was associated with hematoma enlargement, which resulted in death. The association between hyperglycemia and hematoma enlargement has been previously noted [2]. Hyperglycemia also predisposes patients to hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic infarcts, both spontaneously [27,28] and after tPA administration [29]. However, the mechanism by which hyperglycemia contributes to hematoma enlargement remains unclear.  

This study had some limitations. First, blood samples were obtained in the emergency room immediately after patients arrived. Thus, in a few patients, the blood glucose level may have been increased due to a postprandial increase. Second, it has recently been shown that increased D-dimer levels after acute brain hemorrhage were associated with early deterioration and poor outcome [30]. Unfortunately, 15 patients did not have D-dimer data because D-dimer is not measured in our hospital on Sundays and holidays. Therefore, D-dimer data were excluded from the analysis.  

In conclusion, admission hyperglycemia and large ICH volume independently increased the risk of early death in acute spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage. In critically ill neurological patients, strict glucose control has been linked to reductions in intracranial pressure, duration of mechanical ventilation, and seizures [31]. This study suggests that control of hyperglycemia in the acute stage of brain hemorrhage may decrease brain edema and hematoma enlargement, which may improve patient outcomes.
