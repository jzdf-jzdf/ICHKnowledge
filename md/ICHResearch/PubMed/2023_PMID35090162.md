# Comparison of Effects of Propofol and Sevoflurane on the Cerebral Vasculature Assessed by Digital Subtraction Angiographic Parameters in Patients Treated for Ruptured Cerebral Aneurysm: A Preliminary Study  

Steve Joys, MD,\* Nidhi B. Panda, MD,\* Chirag K. Ahuja, DM,† Ankur Luthra, DM,\* Manjul Tripathi, MCh,‡ Shalvi Mahajan, DM,\* Narender Kaloria, DM,\* Chirag Jain, $DM,\dagger$ Nidhi Singh, MD,\* Sabina Regmi, $MD,^{*}$ Kiran Jangra, $DM,^{*}$ Rajeev Chauhan, DM,\* Shiv L. Soni, $DM,^{*}$ and Hemant Bhagat, $DM^{*}$  

Background: Studies have evaluated the effects of volatile and intravenous anesthetic agents on the cerebral vasculature with inconsistent results. We used digital subtraction angiography to compare the effects of propofol and sevoflurane on the luminal diameter of cerebral vessels and on cerebral transit time in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH).  

Methods: This prospective preliminary study included adult patients with good-grade aSAH scheduled for endovascular coil embolization; patients were randomized to receive propofol or sevoflurane anesthesia during endovascular coiling. The primary outcome was the luminal diameter of 7 cerebral vessel segments measured on the diseased and nondiseased sides of the brain at 3 time points: awake, postinduction of anesthesia, and postcoiling. Cerebral transit time was also measured as a surrogate for cerebral blood flow.  

Results: Eighteen patients were included in the analysis (9 per group). Baseline and intraoperative parameters were similar between the groups. Propofol increased the diameter of 1 vessel segment at postinduction and postcoiling on the diseased side and in 1 segment at postcoiling on the nondiseased side of the brain $(P<0.05)$ ). Sevoflurane increased vessel diameter in 3 segments at postinduction and in 2 segments at postcoiling on the diseased side, and in 4 segments at postcoiling on the nondiseased side $(P<0.05)$ . Cerebral transit time did not change compared with baseline awake state in either group and was not different between the groups.  

Conclusions: Sevoflurane has cerebral vasodilating properties compared with propofol in patients with good-grade aSAH. However, sevoflurane affects cerebral transit time comparably to propofol.  

Key Words: cerebral vasculature, cerebral blood flow, digital subtraction angiography, propofol, sevoflurane, subarachoid hemorrhage  

(J Neurosurg Anesthesiol 2023;35:327–332)  

tudies investigating the effects of volatile and intravenous anesthetic agents on cerebral blood flow (CBF) have reported varying results. Propofol is considered to have an indirect cerebral vasoconstrictive action reducing global and regional CBF,1,2 whereas sevoflurane has been shown to cause a decrease, no change, or an increase in CBF at different minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) values, with differences in global and regional CBF.1,3–5 Human studies have used positron emission tomography or transcranial Doppler ultrasonography to provide indirect evidence of cerebral vasoconstriction or vasodilation1–3; however, neither modality allows direct visualization and assessment of the effects of anesthetic agents on the cerebral vasculature. Moreover, most studies have been conducted in nonpathologic brains.  

In this study, we used digital subtraction angiography (DSA) to compare the effects of propofol and sevoflurane on the cerebral vasculature in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). The primary outcome of the study was the effect of propofol and sevoflurane on the luminal diameter of 7 cerebral vessel segments at similar depths of anesthesia. The secondary outcome was cerebral transit time (CTT) as a surrogate measure of $\mathrm{CBF^{6,7}}$ ; CBF is inversely related to CTT, that is, CTT increases when CBF decreases, and vice versa.6 We hypothesized that, compared with sevoflurane, propofol would decrease the luminal diameter of the cerebral vessels and increase CTT in patients with aSAH.  



# METHODS  

We conducted a prospective randomized open-label, blinded endpoint preliminary study in patients with ruptured cerebral aneurysm scheduled for endovascular coil embolization. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (NK/5206//DM/683, dated 06/04/2019) and registered with Clinical Trials Registry, India (CTRI/ 2019/04/018637, dated 16/04/2019). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or legal representatives in those unable to give consent. Patients aged 18 years and above with good-grade aSAH (ie, World Federation of Neurological Surgeons’ grades 1 or 2, modified Hunt and Hess grades 1 or 2, or modified Fisher grade ${<}2$ ) were included in the study. Patients who were hemodynamically unstable, intubated, or sedated, or those with vasculitis, hydrocephalus, cerebral vasospasm (diagnosed clinically), or serum hemoglobin concentration ${<}10$ or $>15\:\mathrm{g/dL}$ were excluded from the study. The patients were randomized using a computer-generated random number sequence to receive either propofol (group P) or sevoflurane (group S) for induction and maintenance of anesthesia; allocation was performed using a sealed envelope technique. The data analyzer and treating neuroradiologist were blinded to group allocation.  



# Anesthesia Protocol  

Diagnostic DSA was performed in conscious patients under local anesthesia. Patients in whom severe vasospasm $(>50\%$ reduction in arterial caliber compared with unaffected contralateral cerebral hemisphere vessels) was confirmed during diagnostic DSA were excluded from further study. The remaining patients were then anesthetized for the coiling procedure; standard monitoring included state entropy, end-tidal carbon-dioxide concentration and esophageal temperature. Anesthesia was induced with intravenous propofol (1.0 to $2.5\mathrm{mg/kg)}$ in patients in group $\mathrm{\bfP}$ and with inhaled sevoflurane in group S to achieve a state entropy of 50; analgesia was provided with intravenous fentanyl $2\upmu\mathrm{g/kg}$ . The clinical endpoints of induction were loss of consciousness and loss of verbal response in group P and loss of eye-lash reflex in group S. Following the loss of consciousness, vecuronium $(0.1\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{kg})$ was administered to facilitate endotracheal intubation. Anesthesia was maintained with either intravenous propofol (50 to $150\upmu\mathrm{g/kg/min)}$ ) or inhaled sevoflurane (0.8 to $1.2\mathrm{MAC})$ per group allocation and ventilated with a 50:50 air-oxygen mixture; state entropy was maintained between 40 and 60. The entropy sensor was removed if it interfered with image acquisition during the coiling procedure. Fluid replacement consisted of intravenous normal saline. Mean arterial pressure was maintained within $20\%$ of baseline during induction and maintenance of anesthesia. Hypotension (mean pressure $<20\%$ of baseline) was treated with $0.1\mathrm{mg}$ boluses of phenylephrine; if ${>}3$ boluses of phenylephrine were required, dopamine infusion was initiated. Ventilation was titrated to maintain partial pressure of carbon dioxide at 35 to 40 $\mathrm{mmHg}$ , and the temperature was maintained between 35 and $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . The patients were extubated postprocedure at the discretion of the treating anesthesiologist.  



# Coiling Procedure  

All coiling procedures were performed by a single neuroradiologist. Femoral artery access was used for the introduction of the catheter into the arterial territory of the cerebral aneurysm. Nimodipine $(1\mathrm{mg})$ was added to $1\mathrm{L}$ of $0.9\%$ saline for the guiding catheter flush to assist the passage of coils; the flush was run at ${\sim}11$ over 90 minutes. Appropriate-sized platinum coils were placed within the aneurysm sac to obliterate the aneurysm completely.  



# Data Collection  

Demographic and clinical data were recorded. Arterial blood gas analysis was performed before making vascular measurements at the start of the diagnostic DSA, postinduction of anesthesia, and after the coiling procedure. The cerebral vascular measurements included the luminal diameters of the supraclinoid internal carotid artery, the M1, M2, and M3 segments of the middle cerebral artery, and the A1, A2, and A3 segments of the anterior cerebral artery, and CTT. These measurements were made in the awake state before induction of anesthesia (baseline) on the diseased (ipsilateral to the ruptured aneurysm) and nondiseased sides of the brain, at 60 minutes postinduction of general anesthesia on the diseased side of the brain, and postcoiling on the diseased and nondiseased sides of the brain. CTT is the time difference between arterial inflow and venous outflow and was calculated from the first appearance of the contrast medium in the carotid siphon to the first appearance of the contrast in the parietal vein.6 All cerebral vascular measurements were made using the same bi-plane flat detector angiographic system (Philips Allura Xper FD20/10) and with the consensus of 2 neuroradiologists. Details of the cerebral vascular measurements are shown in the Supplementary Material (Supplemental Digital Content 1: Figure illustrating measurements of vessel diameter and CTT, http://links.lww.com/JNA/A485).  



# Statistical Analysis  

The study was conducted as a preliminary investigation. Based on feasibility and study time frame, we planned to recruit 20 patients with 10 patients in each group. Data were described as mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ , or frequency and percentage as appropriate. Quantitative variables were compared between study groups using the Student $t$ test for parametric data and Mann-Whitney $U$ test for nonparametric data. Comparison of quantitative variables between different time periods within and between groups (group×time interaction) for diseased and nondiseased sides of the brain was performed using mixed method repeated-measures analysis of variance with adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. Thirty-three patients were assessed, and 20 patients were eligible for randomization. Two patients (1 per group) were excluded after randomization because severe vasospasm was confirmed during the diagnostic DSA, leaving 18 patients available for analysis (Supplemental Digital Content 2: Study flow chart, http://links.lww.com/JNA/A486). Demographic, preoperative, and intraoperative data are shown in Table 1. There were no differences in the incidence of intraprocedural hypotension or hypertension, or in heart rate, between the 2 groups.

# DISCUSSION  

Propofol is considered to have an indirect cerebral vasoconstrictive effect secondary to a reduction in cerebral metabolic rate.2,8 However, in our study, propofol caused no change in diameter in the majority of vessel segments except being associated with vasodilation in 1 arterial segment on both the diseased and nondiseased side of the brain. Sevoflurane caused an increase in diameter in more arterial segments than propofol on both sides of the brain. These findings are consistent with previous studies which demonstrated the cerebral vasodilatory property of sevoflurane, including in large vessels.3,9 However, the vessel diameters in both groups in this study may have been influenced by the effects of the underlying disease state on the ability of the vasculature to respond normally to anesthetic agents as compared with their effects in healthy individuals. Early after aSAH, local insults to neurovascular coupling, microangiopathy, and oxidative damage lead to vasospasm and necrosis of the distal vasculature, forming focal and patchy ischemic zones even in the absence of large vessel vasospasm.10–12 Moreover, the diseased side of the brain is associated with increased susceptibility to focal ischemia.13 Although autoregulation is often preserved in good-grade aSAH, the regional response of the cerebral vasculature may vary based on regional differences in autoregulatory impairment depending on the severity of vasospasm in the supplying arterial tree and on the severity of focal ischemia.10,14–16

We observed no difference in CTT compared with baseline at postinduction and postcoiling with both propofol and sevoflurane, suggesting that neither agent affects CBF significantly compared with awake states or with duration of exposure. The findings are consistent with previous studies which showed no change in global CBF or middle cerebral artery flow velocity with sevoflurane.5,17 Transcranial Doppler studies have shown that sevoflurane produces a decrease or no change in CBF at MAC 0.5 to 1.5, but an increase in CBF at higher concentrations.9,18 These findings have been explained by a direct vasodilatory effect of sevoflurane which opposes the decrease in CBF associated with cerebral vasoconstriction as a consequence of the reduction in cerebral metabolic rate. Studies conducted on nonpathologic brains have shown that propofol decreases CBF compared with the awake state and also causes a greater decrease in CBF compared with sevoflurane.1,2 Ishibashi et $\mathrm{al}^{8}$ reported that propofol increases cerebral circulation times compared with sevoflurane in patients undergoing unruptured aneurysm coiling. However, CTT did not increase during propofol anesthesia in our study, and there was no difference in CTT between the 2 anesthetic agents. Aneurysmal SAH is associated with decreased CBF and cerebral metabolic rate in both cerebral hemispheres, an effect attributed to the toxic effects of blood in subarachnoid space.19 However, cerebral blood volume increases secondary to peripheral vasodilation in response to vasospasm in large conductance vessels.15,19 The underlying disease state may have influenced CTT in our study, despite a sevoflurane-induced increase in vessel diameter. However, this finding needs further clarification.

This study has several limitations. Being a preliminary study, the sample size was likely too small to arrive at definitive conclusions. Moreover, the impact of aSAH on the study findings cannot be validated without comparison with a healthy control group; the nondiseased side of the brain used as the control in the current study may have been affected by the global effects of SAH. The adjuvant drugs used during the study may also have influenced our findings. Nimodipine is a cerebral vasodilator, so, the inclusion of nimodipine in the catheter flush solution might have affected postcoiling vessel diameters. Similarly, phenylephrine can increase CBF and cerebral blood volume while impairing cerebral microcirculation.20 Furthermore, measurements were not made in the nondiseased side of the brain at the postinduction time as this step is not performed as part of our routine aneurysm coiling procedure. Finally, only patients with goodgrade aSAH were included; hence, our results cannot be extrapolated to patients with poor-grade aSAH.  

In conclusion, sevoflurane has vasodilating properties compared with propofol in patients with good-grade aSAH; however, sevoflurane affected CTT comparably to propofol. Our findings emphasize the need for further studies with larger sample sizes and inclusion of patients with poorer grade aSAH to fully understand the effects of propofol and sevoflurane on the cerebral vasculature after aSAH.
