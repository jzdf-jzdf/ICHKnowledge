# Early Deterioration, Hematoma Expansion, and Outcomes in Deep Versus Lobar Intracerebral Hemorrhage: The FAST Trial  

Lindsey R. Kuohn , BA\*; Jens Witsch , MD\*; Thorsten Steiner , MD; Kevin N. Sheth , MD; Hooman Kamel , MD, MS; Babak B. Navi , MD, MS; Alexander E. Merkler , MD, MS; Santosh B. Murthy , MD, MPH; Stephan A. Mayer , MD  

BACKGROUND: In patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), it is unclear whether early neurological deterioration, hematoma expansion (HE), and outcome vary by supratentorial ICH location (deep versus lobar). Herein, we assessed these relationships in a clinical trial cohort that underwent brain imaging early after symptom onset. We hypothesized that HE would occur more frequently, and outcome would be worse in patients with deep ICH.  

METHODS: We performed a post hoc analysis of the FAST (Factor-VII-for-Acute-Hemorrhagic-Stroke-Treatment) trial including all patients with supratentorial hemorrhage. Enrolled patients underwent brain imaging within 3 hours of symptom onset and 24 hours after randomization. Multivariable regression was used to test the association between ICH location and 3 outcomes: HE (increase of ${\ge}33\%$ or 6mL), early neurological deterioration (decrease in Glasgow Coma Scale score ${}^{\ge2}$ points or increase in National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale ${\ge}4$ points within 24 hours of admission), and 90-day outcome (modified Rankin Scale).  

RESULTS: Of 841 FAST trial patients, we included 728 (mean age 64 years, $38\%$ women) with supratentorial hemorrhages (deep $\scriptstyle\mathsf{n=}\mathsf{G}23$ , lobar $\ttn=105$ ). HE (44 versus $27\%$ , $P{=}0.001$ ) and early neurological deterioration (31 versus $17\%$ , $P=0.001\$ ) were more common in lobar hemorrhages. Deep hemorrhages were smaller than lobar hemorrhages at baseline (12 versus $35\mathsf{mL}$ , $P{<}0.001$ ) and 24 hours (14 versus $38mL$ , $P{<}0.001$ ). Unadjusted 90-day outcome was worse in lobar compared with deep ICH (median modified Rankin Scale score 5 versus 4, $_{P=0.03}$ ). However, when adjusting for variables included in the ICH score including ICH volume, deep location was associated with worse and lobar location with better outcome (odds ratio lobar location, 0.58 $[95\%$ CI, 0.38–0.89]; $\widetilde{P{=}0.01}$ ).  

CONCLUSIONS: In this secondary analysis of randomized trial patients, lobar ICH location was associated with larger ICH volume, more HE and early neurological deterioration, and worse outcome than deep ICH. After adjustment for prognostic variables, however, deep ICH was associated with worse outcome, likely due to their proximity to eloquent brain structures.

As a result of the incomplete identification of hemorrhage characteristics by ICH location, acute treatment and monitoring in patients with deep versus lobar hemorrhage locations do not fundamentally differ.12 Herein, we used data from a large randomized trial with serial neuroimaging data, including time from symptom onset to administration of the investigational product, and blinded volume measurements, to examine whether deep versus lobar supratentorial hemorrhage correlated with differing rates of HE, END, and poor functional outcome. We hypothesized that HE would occur more frequently, and functional outcome would be worse in patients with deep ICH. In alignment with prior studies, we anticipated that END would be more common in patients with lobar hemorrhages.  

# METHODS  

Anonymized data from the FAST trial (Factor VII for Acute Hemorrhagic Stroke) can be requested through the Virtual International Stroke Trials Archive (VISTA, www.virtualtrialsarchive.org).  

# Study Design and Participants  

We performed a secondary analysis of the FAST trial, a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of rFVIIa (recombinant activated factor VIIa) for the treatment of spontaneous ICH.6 Patients were eligible for enrollment in the FAST trial if they were 18 years of age or older and had a spontaneous ICH documented on a brain computed tomography (CT) within 3 hours of symptom onset. Patients were excluded from trial participation if any of the following characteristics was present: admission GCS of 5 or less, surgical ICH evacuation planned within 24 hours of admission, secondary ICH due to vascular malformation or trauma; known anticoagulant use, thrombocytopenia, or history of coagulopathy, sepsis, crush injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation, pregnancy, prior disability (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score ${>}2.$ ), known thrombotic or vaso-occlusive disease (includes deep vein thrombosis, myocardial and cerebral infarction) within 30 days before ICH onset. A total of 841 patients were enrolled between May 2005 and February 2007 at 122 sites across 22 countries. Enrolled patients were randomly assigned to receive placebo, $20~\upmu{\mathfrak{g}}$ of rFVIIa per kilogram of body weight, or $80~\upmu{\mathfrak{g}}$ of rFVIIa per kilogram of body weight within 4 hours of stroke onset. In this analysis, we excluded patients with infratentorial hemorrhages $(\mathsf{n}{=}40)$ ) and patients without data on ICH location $\left(\mathfrak{n}{=}48\right)$ ).  

Written informed consent for trial participation was provided by each patient or their legally authorized representative. Institutional review board approval was obtained at each enrolling site of the FAST trial. Given that only deidentified, publicly available data were used, this retrospective study was exempt from additional, local IRB approval. Results were reported in adherence to the STROBE statement guidelines.13  

# Neuroimaging  

Patients enrolled in the FAST trial underwent head CT imaging upon admission (within 3 hours of symptom onset) and at 24 and 72 hours after administration of the investigational product. CT images were analyzed using Analyze software (Mayo Clinic) and individually read by 2 neuroradiologists who were blinded to patient randomization groups.14 Parenchymal and intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) volumes and perihematomal edema volume were calculated semi-automatically using computerized planimetric methods.14 Midline shift was measured at the septum pellucidum. For the purpose of our study, HE was defined in 2 ways: (1) growth in hematoma volume by ${\ge}33\%$ or $6~\mathsf{mL}$ from baseline to 24 hours and (2) growth in hematoma volume by ${\ge}33\%$ only, as was used in the original FAST trial.4 We used the first, more inclusive definition of HE in univariable and multivariable analyses based on recently published recommendations.15 Patients without available CT scan at 24 hours were excluded from the analysis $(\mathsf{n}{=}25\$ ).  

# Clinical Assessments  

Clinical assessments were performed at the time of FAST trial enrollment; 24 hours after administration of the investigational product; hospitalization day 2, day 3, and day 15 or at discharge; and on day 90 after the index ICH. Neurological assessments included the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale. Functional outcome was assessed at 90 days after ICH using the mRS.  

# Outcomes  

We investigated both HE and END as short-term, in-hospital outcomes, and used the mRS at 90 days after ICH as the longterm outcome. All outcome variables were assessed and collected prospectively in the FAST trial. END was defined as a decrease in the GCS score by at least 2 points or an increase in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale by at least 4 points within 24 hours of admission.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.0.0). Descriptive statistics are presented as counts (percent) and median (interquartile range) or mean (SD) for discrete and continuous variables, respectively. For unadjusted comparisons of discrete variables, we used $\chi^{2}$ testing, and for comparisons of continuous variables, we used unpaired t tests. Unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression was used to model the association between patient characteristics, neuroimaging variables, including HE, and END. Where indicated, analyses were adjusted for (1) time from symptom onset to administration of investigational product, which we refer to as onset-to-needle time and (2) FAST trial randomization group. The FAST trial randomization group variable included 3 levels in accordance with the original FAST trial protocol: placebo, $20~{\upmu\g}$ of rFVIIa per kilogram of body weight, or 80 μg of rFVIIa per kilogram of body weight. We used an interaction analysis to determine whether ICH location modified the relationship between FAST trial randomization group and HE. Non-normally distributed covariates, including onset-to-needle time, ICH volume, and IVH volume were log transformed to approximate normality. We modeled functional outcome at 90 days (mRS) using multivariable ordinal logistic regression, which evaluated mRS scores across the full scale without dichotomization. Multivariable models were built by selecting covariates with $P\mathrm{<}0.1$ in univariable analyses, backwards eliminating covariates with $P>0.1$ , and removing colinear variables. To test the association between deep versus lobar ICH location and functional outcome, we adjusted for the variables of the ICH score, including age, baseline $\mathsf{GCS},$ and ICH volume at 24 hours. Rather than including dichotomous presence of IVH at 24 hours, we adjusted for continuous IVH volume at 24 hours due to the demonstrated impact of IVH volume on functional outcomes after ICH. Infratentorial location was not included in this analysis as patients with infratentorial ICH were excluded from the analytical cohort.  

# RESULTS  

A total of 728 patients (mean [SD] age 64 [13] years, 278 $[38\%]$ female) with supratentorial hemorrhages and 24-hour neuroimaging were enrolled in the FAST trial and included in our analysis. Baseline characteristics of these patients stratified by deep versus lobar hemorrhage location are shown in Table  1. The location of the ICH was deep in 623 patients $(86\%)$ and lobar in 105 patients $(14\%)$ . The median onset-to-needle time was 167 minutes in patients with lobar hemorrhages compared with 158 minutes in those with deep hemorrhages $\mathrm{\Delta}^{\prime}P{=}0.05$ ). Differences in baseline and 24-hour neuroimaging measurements between lobar and deep hemorrhages are summarized in Table 2. Lobar hemorrhages compared with deep hemorrhages were substantially larger at both baseline (median ICH volume 35 versus $12\mathsf{mL},$ , $P{<}0.001$ ) and 24 hours (38 versus 14 mL, $P\mathrm{<}0.001$ ). The perihematomal edema/ICH ratio was not significantly different between ICH locations (at baseline $\widetilde{\calF}\widetilde{=}0.3,$ , at 24 hours $\widetilde{P{=}0.7}$ ).

# Functional Outcome at 90 Days  

All but 2 patients included in our analysis had available 90-day follow-up. At 90 days, 37 patients $(33\%)$ with lobar hemorrhages and 104 patients $16\%)$ with deep hemorrhages had died. Among survivors, comparing those with lobar and deep ICH, $7\%$ versus $4\%$ had an mRS score of 0, $11\%$ versus $15\%$ had an mRS score of 1, $14\%$ versus $16\%$ had an mRS score of 2, $14\%$ versus $18\%$ had an mRS score of 3, $17\%$ versus $23\%$ had an mRS score of 4, and $5\%$ versus $7\%$ had an mRS score of 5. In univariable analysis (Table 5), lobar ICH location was associated with worse (higher mRS scores) 90-day functional outcome (OR, 1.56 $[95\%$ CI, 1.08–2.27]; $_{P=0.02}$ ), as were the components of the ICH score (age: OR, 1.04 $[95\%$ CI, 1.03–1.05]; baseline GCS: OR, 0.70 $[95\%$ CI, 0.66–0.75]; ICH volume at 24 hours: OR, 2.77 $[95\%$ CI, 2.42–3.18]); and IVH volume at 24 hours (OR, 1.19 $[95\%$ CI, 1.15–1.23]). In a multivariable analysis accounting for the ICH score covariables and IVH volume at 24 hours, the relationship between lobar ICH location and functional outcome was reversed from the univariable analysis; the adjusted model showed that lobar hemorrhages were inversely associated with poor 90-day functional outcome (OR, 0.58 $[95\%$ CI, 0.38–0.89]; $\widetilde{P}\widetilde{=}0.01$ ). The relationship between age, baseline GCS, ICH volume at 24 hours, and IVH volume at 24 hours and functional outcome did not change. In multivariable analyses separated by FAST trial arm, we found similar results (Table S4).  

# DISCUSSION  

In this post hoc analysis of a large, randomized controlled trial, we found that deep ICH location, when adjusting for covariates known to be associated with long-term functional outcome, was associated with worse outcome compared with lobar ICH location. However, we also made the seemingly paradoxical finding that patients with lobar ICH in this cohort had larger hemorrhages at baseline and generally worse outcomes than those with deep ICH in the unadjusted analysis. Deep ICH location was not associated with increased risk for HE, and in fact, both HE and END occurred more frequently in patients with lobar ICH. These findings highlight a group of patients, those with lobar ICH that are admitted in an early time window, whose hemorrhage location is generally favorable but deteriorate because of dynamic hemorrhage characteristics. Early interventions in this group may be promising.

# Hematoma Expansion  

In our sample, HE occurred more frequently in lobar compared with deep ICH. This finding contradicts our hypothesis, as well as it contradicts prior studies that had suggested that HE is more frequent in patients with deep ICH.5,11 Discrepancies across prior studies are possibly explained by variable definitions of HE, and perhaps the inconsistent adjustment for symptom onset to imaging time.16 Moreover, the use of antiplatelet therapy before the index ICH has been associated with HE in lobar but not in deep ICH, which may also have confounded prior studies as well as ours, as this information was not available in FAST.17 Given that lobar hemorrhages tend to be larger than deep hemorrhages at baseline, they may also be more likely to expand and thus meet criteria for HE when including absolute expansion ${>}6$ mL.18 However, lobar ICH location has not previously been associated with increased risk of HE despite this fact. We hypothesize that patients with lobar hemorrhages, a seemingly benign hemorrhage location compared with deep hemorrhages, who present early after symptom onset may be optimum candidates for acute interventions aimed at reducing the risk of HE. However, interventional trials, such as the SPOTLIGHT (“Spot Sign” Selection of Intracerebral Hemorrhage to Guide Hemostatic Therapy) and STOP-IT (The Spot Sign for Predicting and Treating ICH Growth Study) trials, that have aimed to reduce HE in at-risk patients have not been successful in limiting expansion or improving functional outcome.19 But like the FAST trial, SPOTLIGHT and STOP-IT relied on rFVIIa intervention and did not examine deep and lobar hemorrhage separately. These factors may have contributed to the failure of these trials. Further studies are needed to investigate the feasibility and benefit of limiting HE in lobar ICH.

# Functional Outcome  

We identified a complex relationship between lobar ICH location and 90-day functional outcome. Patients with lobar hemorrhages experienced overall worse outcomes but, adjusted models found lobar ICH location to be protective against poor outcome. In line with this result, lobar ICH location was also associated with better functional outcomes in a recent study in patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery.20 Our findings suggest that lobar hemorrhage is intrinsically less detrimental than hemorrhage in deep regions, possibly due to lower odds of developing IVH and subsequent hydrocephalus or due to on average less injury to the pyramidal tract. However, lobar hemorrhages are also prone to larger hematomas at baseline and, importantly, early expansion. Given that HE occurs during the hospital admission in patients who present early after symptom onset, targeting HE in patients with lobar hemorrhage may represent an opportunity for improved outcomes. So far, this has proven difficult, however, as the drivers of HE in lobar locations are not as well understood as the hypertension-mediated expansion linked to deep ICH locations.21,22 The STICH 2 trial failed to show an improvement in outcome in patients with superficial ICH undergoing early hematoma evacuation.23 However, minimally invasive surgical techniques have shown promise and are being tested in ongoing trials.24 In the context of such novel techniques a better understanding of hematoma characteristics may facilitate optimal selection of patients to undergo surgery. Our study highlights novel aspects. In patients with lobar ICH that are admitted in an early time window after symptom onset, outcome seems to be driven by larger baseline hemorrhage volumes and a greater frequency of HE, which may be targeted through implementation of early interventions.25,26 END is not closely associated with HE and might thus not be useful as a marker of HE.27

# Limitations  

Our study has several limitations. First, our analyses adjust for the time from symptom onset to administration of the investigational product (onset-to-needle time), which is a proxy for the more commonly used measure of time from symptom onset to first head CT that was unavailable in the FAST trial data. Given that all patients were enrolled within 4 hours of symptom onset, we do not anticipate that there was a significant difference between onset-to-needle and onset-to-CT time. Additionally, assessment of HE, especially in large hemorrhages, may have been subject to error based on inherent variability in CT slice positioning and angle during serial imaging. Likewise, very small hemorrhages, compared with larger ones, were more likely to meet criteria for expansion by ${>}33\%$ even if the expansion was clinically negligible. Second, associations reported here, may represent false positives caused by segmentation of the original study population. However, baseline covariates remained largely balanced between ICH location comparison groups, indicating that this source of error is likely small. Third, the FAST trial systematically tried to manipulate HE by administering rFVIIa to patients in the intervention groups. Throughout our study, we adjusted our analyses for the study randomization group as appropriate. We also tested for an interaction between ICH location, HE, and study treatment. Given that the rFVIIa intervention did not affect outcomes in the FAST trial, it is unlikely that it affected outcomes in the present analysis. Additionally, trial data may be prone to healthy participant bias and may not reflect the broader ICH population. Lastly, we lacked antiplatelet and ICH mechanism data and could not adjust for these factors in multivariable models. Independent follow-up studies are needed to ascertain that our findings can be reproduced in cohorts outside of clinical trials.  

# Conclusions  

We found that in patients with ICH selected for the FAST trial and undergoing brain CT imaging within 3 hours of symptom onset, HE and END were more common in those with lobar compared with deep ICH. After adjustment for variables that influence outcome patients with deep ICH paradoxically had worse outcomes at 90 days, despite the fact that lobar ICH was associated with larger hemorrhage volumes and worse outcomes in the unadjusted analysis. Our findings suggest that it might be promising to test therapeutic interventions aimed at hematoma reduction or removal in patients with lobar ICH that present early after ictus.
