# ABSTRACT  

Objectives: Several direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have been approved by the European Medicines Agency since 2008. The aim of the present cost-effectiveness-analysis was to analyze apixaban com­ pared to other DOACs and vitamin K antagonists (warfarin) in Austria.  

Methods: A cost-utility-model was developed to simulate lifetime-costs and quality-adjusted-life-years of DOACs and warfarin, based on a published Markov-Model and 23 randomized trials with 94,656 atrial-fibrillation (AF) patients. Each year, a patient has a probability of suffering a clinically relevant (extracranial) bleed, an intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), an ischemic stroke or a myocardial infarction (MI), remaining healthy, or deceasing. Direct-costs (2018€) were derived from published sources from the payer’s perspective.  

Results: In the base-case, warfarin had the lowest cost of $12,968\in$ $\langle95\%-C1{\pm}593\in\rangle$ followed by apixaban $15,269\in\pm661\in)$ , edoxaban $15,534\in\pm641\in)$ , dabigatran $15,687\in{\pm667\in})_{i}$ , and rivaroxaban $(17,522\in$ $\pm764\in)$ . Apixaban had the highest quality-adjusted-life-years estimate at 5.45 (SD, 0.06). In a MonteCarlo probabilistic sensitivity analysis, apixaban was cost-effective vs. edoxaban, dabigatran, warfarin, and rivaroxaban in $85.6\%$ , $79.0\%,$ , $76.4\%,$ , and $61.2\%$ of the simulations, respectively.  

Conclusion: In patients with AF and an increased risk of stroke, prophylaxis with apixaban was highly cost-effective from the perspective of the Austrian health-care system.

# 1. Introduction  

Atrial fibrillation (AF) constitutes a significant public health problem. In Europe, estimated 15 million people suffer from an AF. It is the most common cardiac arrhythmia [1]. It was observed that in the industrialized nations over the past few decades, the prevalence of AF has markedly increased [2]. Based on the expected demographic changes in the German population, the number of patients with AF is likely to increase from 1,793,277 in 2009 to 2,126,656 in 2020 [2]. Converting these data to Austria, 234,300 individuals are affected. Considering the underreported not diagnosed cases to the extent of $1.2\%$ [3], 340,000 patients with AF could be esti­ mated for Austria. Based on IQVIA sales data, a patient share of $64\%$ or 119,700 diagnosed patients had received prescriptions of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs).  

In patients with AF, the associated increased risks of ischemic stroke – first or recurrent – and systemic embolism (SE) present a major challenge. Approximately $20-30\%$ of the patients experiencing ischemic stroke have been diagnosed with AF before, during, or after the stroke event. Embolic events, such as ischemic stroke, lead to high hospitalization costs and long-term maintenance costs which contribute to the high economic burden to health-care systems [4].  

In patients with AF, anticoagulant (AC) therapy with vita­ min K antagonists (VKAs) has been the gold standard for the prevention of stroke over the last decades. Several DOACs have been approved by the European Medicines Agency since 2008. Unlike VKAs, these drugs do not require regular blood monitoring of patients by international normalized ratio (INR) testing due to a wider therapeutic window allows once (rivaroxaban, edoxaban) or twice (dabigatran, apixaban) daily intake and is associated with fewer drug–drug interactions to date [5]. These medications directly inhibit the blood’s ability to form blood clots. The rapid onset and relatively short halflife of anticoagulant action offers advantages for DOACs over warfarin in some clinical settings [6]. DOACs prevent strokes at least as good as VKAs (some studies have even shown superior efficacy or at least non-inferior) in patients with non-valvular AF (NVAF), and these findings have been implemented in the 2016 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines that recommended DOACs over VKA therapy in this indication [7]. Moreover, antiplatelet therapy is no longer recommended in these patients [7,8].  

Apixaban is one of the DOACs that is a direct factor Xa inhibitor with rapid absorption and an approx. 12-h half-life. Approximately $27\%$ is eliminated through renal excretion [9]. Apixaban demonstrated its superior efficacy and safety versus warfarin in preventing stroke in patients with NVAF in the ARISTOTLE study [10] and versus aspirin in the AVERROES study [11], respectively.  

The minority of cost-effectiveness analyses were performed to compare apixaban with VKAs. The objective of this healtheconomic study was to assess the incremental cost and costeffectiveness of apixaban compared to other DOACs and VKAs (warfarin) to determine the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio among the treatment alternatives. Resource use and associated costs were analyzed in a more detailed manner for the Austrian health-care system.  

# 2. Methods  

Several cost-effectiveness models of DOACs for stroke preven­ tion have been developed for different comparators and dif­ ferent countries. The majority of the models included 8–14 health states, representing the main clinical outcomes in antic­ oagulated patients with NVAF (ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic attack, systemic embolism, myocar­ dial infarction, intracranial hemorrhages, other major hemor­ rhages, and minor bleeding) [12].  

To examine the potential economic impact of apixaban, we constructed a cost-effectiveness model that computed costs and health benefits associated with each treatment. The model used a Markov process to project the long-term costs and health effects. The present probabilistic Markov simula­ tion model used the approach as the model published by José A López-López (2017), to simulate reduced complication rates, lifetime costs, and quality-adjusted life-years of DOACs and warfarin [13]. One cycle in the model comprised a 3month time frame [14]. No half-cycle correction was adopted due to the short cycle-length.  

The present model focusing on the effects of three direct factor Xa inhibitors and one direct factor II inhibitor was incorporated. For every 3month cycle, a patient has a probability of suffering a clinically relevant or major (extracranial) bleed, an intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), an ischemic stroke or a myocardial infarction (MI), remaining healthy, or deceasing. Over time, it is possible for the patient to experience several different events – a recurrent event or a combination of events. Each treatment strategy has the same model structure, but different costs, benefits, and event probabilities. Patients are assumed to always switch treatment from dabigatran to warfarin if they experience an MI, due to recent findings suggesting a link between dabigatran and MI risk. Patients stop treatment with an increasing probability over time. TIA and systemic embolism were not considered, because of the transient nature. Patients can die of all conditions.  

For treatment comparison, a published network meta-analysis (NMA) [13] was used, as therapeutic alternatives were generally not studied in head-to-head trials. The meta-analysis included 23 phase II or phase III randomized controlled trials using either a superiority or noninferiority design, including 94,656 adult patients with NVAF (median age, 70). Results of four DOACs; three direct factor Xa inhibitors: apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban, and one direct factor II (thrombin) inhibitor; dabigatran were used for the costeffectiveness analysis. To determine the comparator interventions, network plots were constructed by the authors to ensure they would provide information on the relative effectiveness of the DOACs of interest.  

The following DOACs were included in the healtheconomic modeling:  

● Apixaban; 5mg twice daily   
● Edoxaban; 60mg once daily   
● Rivaroxaban; 20mg once daily   
● Dabigatran; $110\mathsf{mg}$ twice daily $(48\%)$ and 150mg twice daily $(52\%)$ , weighted according to the Austrian market share estimated based on IQVIA sales data.  

The considered DOACs were compared with the therapeutic doses of warfarin or other vitamin K antagonist with optimal INR range between 2.0 and 4.0 [13].  

Transition probabilities of incidence of major (extracranial) bleed, an intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), an ischemic stroke, or a myocardial infarction (MI) were derived from the NMA. A detailed information referring to the network and the studies considered are shown in the supplementary material (Table S1).  

The following health states were defined:  

1 Health stage considered AF Well ● 4 Health states described 4 events (ischemic strokes, heart attacks, intracranial hemorrhages, clinically relevant extracranial heavy bleeding)  

6 Health stages considered the combination of two events  

4 Health stages considered the combination of three events   
1 Health stage considered the combination of 4 events  

Death  

Health states describing the treatment algorithms consider the following treatment follow-ups to simulate the patient path­ ways. The algorithms for the untreated patient population comprise the following five arms:  

● Apixaban (5mg); after a clinically relevant bleeding $30\%$ of patients switched to warfarin   
● Dabigatran $(110/150\mathrm{mg});$ after an MI patient and $30\%$ of patients after a clinically relevant bleeding switched to warfarin   
● Edoxaban (60mg); after a clinically relevant bleeding $30\%$ of patients switched to warfarin   
● Rivaroxaban $(20{\mathsf{m}}{\mathsf{g}})$ ; after a clinically relevant bleeding $30\%$ of patients switched to warfarin   
● Warfarin (3mg)  

Costs were calculated for each of the cycles in Euros for the year 2018. Direct-costs were derived from published sources from the payer’s perspective. The model provides cumulative costs, out­ comes expressed as quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) within a lifetime horizon, as well as the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), which can be interpreted as additional cost QALY gained. QALYs, life-years (LYs), and costs were discounted $(5\%$ p.a.).  

To assess the robustness of our findings, we carried out deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses.  

The model was constructed and analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2010 (Figure 1).  

The analysis was conducted in consideration of the Modeling Good Research Practices published by the ISPOR Task Force [15] and the Austrian health-economic guide­ lines [16].

# 2.1. Simulated cohort  

The patient characteristics of the simulated hypothetical cohorts align with the patients of the included in randomized clinical trials used for the NMA by López-López (2017) [13]. Patient characteristic was summarized by the authors in appendix 3. Adults with NVAF were included eligible for oral anticoagulation. Trials in participants only eligible for parent­ eral anticoagulation were excluded. The modeled base-case population was a cohort aged 70 (ranged from 63.3 to 81.5years), which was followed to the end of life. The propor­ tions of male patients were $63.3\%$ (range from $44.9\%$ to $82.9\%_{,}$ ) with a mean body mass index (BMI) of $28.0\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ (range from of 24.4 to $30.5\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2})$ . The percentage of patients with previous stroke was $20.2\%$ (median) (range from $5.0\%$ to $63.8\%)$ ), hypertension $73.8\%$ $(38.0\%$ to $93.7\%$ ), and chronic heart failure $32\%$ $(0\%$ to $100\%$ , varied across the studies. Mean time in therapeutic range for warfarin arms amounts to $63.8\%$ (median) of the duration of treatment (ranged from $45.1\%$ to $83.0\%$ ) [13].  

# 2.2. Transition probabilities  

The present Markov model considered the course of the disease of a patient with atrial fibrillation at a median age of 70 over the remaining life. During each 3-month Markov cycle, a patient remained in the current health state ‘AF well,’ or suffers from an event (ischemic stroke, ICH, MI, clinically relevant extracranial major bleeding). Transition probabilities were derived using odds ratios (OR) for the DOACs estimated from the described published NMA. The warfarin arms were used to describe base-case risk. Consequently, the OR used derived from the NMA were presented in comparison to warfarin. Data are shown in  

Table 1. For almost all of the risks considered, DOACs show a relevant risk reduction in the NMA. Except, data exhibit that the risk of stroke with dabigatran 110mg is higher compared to warfarin; with edoxaban 60mg results are com­ parable and the risk of experience an MI is elevated with dabigatran.  

Base-case risks (ischemic stroke, ICH, MI, clinically relevant extracranial major bleeding) with warfarin were also derived from the published NMA. The highest annual risk was com­ puted for extracranial major bleeding, which exhibits an annual risk of $6.6\%$ . Hazards for the complications accounted are shown in Table 1.  

As explained the model considered recurrent events or a combination of different subsequent events. The incidences for these events could not be derived from the NMA. Here, too, we consistently comply with the approach by LópezLópez (2017) and use the analog probabilities from the litera­ ture [13]. Table 1 shows the transition probabilities for the following events.  

The model includes a discontinuation rate for anticoagu­ lants. The discontinuation rate increases over time. An adher­ ence rate of $90\%$ was used for the first year [18]. The dropout rates for the years 2 and 3 were calculated based on the evidence from the clinical studies (ARISTOTLE, RE-LY, ENGAGE AF TIMI 48, ROCKET-AF) [10,19–21]. An average drop­ out rate of $25\%$ was determined using standard time adjust­ ment. The dropout rate for year 3 of $35\%$ was also been used for the following years. The calculated dropout rates were used for all alternatives equally, because no significant differ­ ences among clinical trials were observed (see Table 1) [10,19–21].  

Mortality rates were required to estimate the number of patients who died within the time horizon. Expected mortality in patients with NVAF was significantly higher than the general population. Age- and sex-specific all-cause mortality rates for the general population were obtained from Austrian public statistics [22]. Event-related mortality rates were derived from the NMA by using the OR of death. It was expected that the risk of event-related mortality increased with the number of events (see Table 1).

# 2.3. Cost assessment  

The cost assessment was based on the assignment of costs to the health states. The costs of each health state were determined by the resource utilization associated with a health state. Resource use (e.g. the type and frequency of medical goods and services rendered to the patient) and monetary value (prices, tariffs, and/or opportunity costs) for each unit of medical goods and services were used to calculate the total direct costs in the Austrian setting. In order to estimate the costs of stroke prevention and events in Austria, only direct medical costs were included in the analysis. These comprised anticoagulation costs, event costs (ischemic stroke, ICH, MI, clinically relevant extracranial major bleeding), outpatient costs (visits to the doctor, INR tests for VKA, kidney function test, and the treatment of dyspepsia). One visit of a general practitioner (GP) or spe­ cialist was considered per month. In patients with VKA prescriptions, one INR test per month is recommended. The subgroup of patients with impaired kidney function performs a kidney function test once a year and patients with dyspepsia are treated with medication.  

Direct medical costs were collected from the payer’s per spective for the year 2018.  

Oral anticoagulant costs were evaluated on the basis of the reimbursement price and were extracted from the official Austrian classified index of goods (‘Warenverzeichnis’) of 8/2018 [23]. Dabigatran costs were weighted among strength. A weighting of $48\%$ for 110mg and $52\%$ for $150\mathrm{mg}$ was used. The estimate is based on IQVIA sales data.  

Austrian event costs (hospitalization and rehabilitation costs, outpatient costs, and medication cost) for MI and stroke were calculated based on a systematic review of the literature including resource consumption information (see figure S1, supplementary appendix). Costs were determined for the first and the consecutive years.  

The cost of major bleeding and ICH was based on the publication by Belisari et al. (2016), who documented the additional resource consumption (red blood count, partial thromboplastin time, thrombin, creatinine, specialist consulta­ tion, MRI/CT imaging diagnostics, angiography, and prolonged hospital and ICU stays) of an intervention in patients receiving oral anticoagulation [24]. The presence of idarucizumab – a humanized monoclonal antibody fragment engineered to specifically neutralize the effects of the oral direct thrombin inhibitor dabigatran – was considered for dabigatran [25]. For major bleeding and ICH, only costs in the first year $\mathbf{\chi}_{=}^{\prime}$ event costs) are taken into account.  

The annual cost of monitoring whilst on treatment was considered. Treatment-specific resource use data were obtained from relevant product information and considers the following outpatient services: (1) one visit of a GP or cardiologist per month, (2) patients who are set to VKA per­ form an INR test once a month; (3) patients with impaired kidney function perform a kidney function test once a year and (4) patients with dyspepsia are treated with medication.  

All data represented costs from 2018 and are shown in Table 2. A discount rate of $5\%$ per year was applied, due to the life-time horizon [16].  

# 2.4. Health state utilities  

Utilities are a measure of preference between health states, where preference can be equated with value or desirability. Utilities for health states included in the model were obtained from international literature, and, if necessary, re-expressed on a utility scale from 0 to 1 (where 0 represents death and 1 represents full health) by using weighting factors. The QALY (Quality-adjusted life year) concept allows combining the effects of health interventions on the quantity and quality of the remaining life-years into a single index. QALYs were calcu­ lated by multiplying the length of time spent in a certain health state by the utility score associated with it [36].  

Quality of life (QoL) of patients with NVAF was extracted from a publication determining utility among 3,045 patients with AF. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was measured with the EQ-5D questionnaire and amounts to 0.779 $(\mathsf{SD}{=}0.253$ , $\mathsf{SE}{=}0.0045^{\prime}$ ) [37].  

To calculate the QoL impact per event, the disutility per event was multiplied by the event duration. The treatmentspecific incidence of events was then used to calculate the disutility of each treatment. Data on the disutility due to events were derived from different literature sources. Our analysis was based on the assumption that these utilities can be applied to the Austrian population. All utilities are shown in Table 3.

# 2.5. Sensitivity analysis  

A deterministic one-way sensitivity analysis was done to assess how variations of individual input parameter values affect the model outputs, specifically, the resulting incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), and thus to judge the robustness of our findings. Input ranges for sensitivity analysis were obtained from $95\%$ con­ fidence intervals (CI) when available. Otherwise (e.g. for costs), input ranges were derived by adding or subtracting percentage values to or from the baseline estimates. In addition, a probabilistic sensitivity analysis was carried out. This global probabilistic sensitivity analysis allows the contribution of each parameter to model outcomes to be investigated, while also taking into account the uncertainty of other model parameters. For this purpose, we incorporated a probability distribution of the input variables by means of a second-order Monte-Carlo simulation. Each simulation was based on a different value drawn randomly from the distribution of each variable. Second-order Monte-Carlo simulations of 500 hypothetical patients were carried out based on the distributions of all input variables; gamma distribution for costs and beta dis­ tribution for probabilities and utilities.

# 3.1. Costs  

From the perspective of the Austrian health-care system, total costs of oral anticoagulation treatment with warfarin (VKAs) had the lowest discounted total cost of $12,968~\in$ (SD, $666\in$ over lifetime, followed by apixaban 5mg (15,269 €; SD, 743 €), edoxaban 60mg ( $15,534\in$ SD, $778\in)$ , dabigatran 110/150mg (15,687 €; SD, $780~\in)$ , and rivaroxaban 20mg (17,522 €; SD, $868~\in)$ .  

A closer look at the costs of the individual events shows that the greatest saving potential can be generated in major bleeding with apixaban compared to VKAs, namely $1,145\in$ $(p\mathtt{-}value{=}0,0024,$ . The reduction in ICH cases with apixaban results in cost savings of $561~\in$ compared to warfarin $(p\mathtt{-}value<0.0001)$ and expenses for outpatient services can be reduced by $298\in(p\mathrm{-}value\mathrm{=}0.0006)$ .

# 3.4. Deterministic sensitivity analysis  

A one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis was carried out to assess the impact of variations in single parameter assump­ tions on the ICER of apixaban treatment versus comparator. Results were expressed as incremental costs per QALY gained. Tornado diagrams (Figure 4) were chosen to display the results.  

As the base-case results, all input variations yielded nega­ tive ICER among DOAC comparison. One exception was the higher limit result of the variation of the discount rate in the apixaban vs. edoxaban comparison, which is still cost-effective $(11,636~\in)$ . When comparing apixaban with warfarin, one var­ iation – the OR for mortality – led to a result, which is not costeffective $\mathtt{\backslashCER{=}1}20,824\in\mathtt{\Gamma}$ .  

Results of the sensitivity analyses showed that the model was sensitive to a number of inputs, including time horizon, discount rate, OR major bleedings, and the OR mortality. For example, results were more favorable if outcomes were pro­ jected over a longer time frame rather than a shorter time period, because benefits accrue gradually over time due to events saved. Results of the deterministic sensitivity analysis demonstrated a limited impact on the results.

# 4. Discussion  

The objective of this modeling study was to analyze the costeffectiveness of apixaban compared with other DOACs and VKAs (warfarin) in patients with diagnosed NVAF, over a lifetime horizon. The results suggested that apixaban saved costs in comparison with other DOACs (apixaban vs. dabiga­ tran $[-418.09\not\in];$ apixaban vs. edoxaban $[-265.35\in];$ apixaban vs. rivaroxaban $[-2,252.47\in])$ . Compared to other ${\mathsf{DOACs}},$ apixaban was dominant, which means it is associated with lower cost and higher health benefits. Compared to warfarin, apixaban is associated with higher QALYs (5.45 vs. 5.27 QALYs) and therefore apixaban is able to increase QoL in the magni­ tude of 2.2months in perfect health and generate an ICER of $12,743\in$ which could be expressed as cost-effective.  

Publications analyzing the cost-effectiveness of DOACs among themselves are scarce. A publication from the Netherlands, with a comparable objective, to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of apixaban versus other DOACs (dabiga­ tran, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban) and VKA, for stroke pre­ vention was extracted from the literature. The authors had used clinical trial data and real-word-data (RWD). Findings from this analysis confirm the present results for Austria. Apixaban appeared to be cost-effective compared to VKA (€3,506 per QALY) and dominant over dabigatran $110\mathrm{mg},$ dabigatran 150mg, edoxaban and rivaroxaban. In the RWDbased analysis, apixaban was dominant over all other antic­ oagulants [4]. It should be noted that the proportion of the cost components to total costs is different, when comparing with Austria; the analysis from the Netherlands was con­ ducted from the societal perspective with indirect costs included. Further, the authors of the present analysis had used pharmacy purchase prices for drugs and the Dutch cost manual for assessing inpatient and outpatients cost instead of DRG lump sums and tariffs.  

An older publication conducted in the UK setting founded that apixaban was cost-effective compared to dabigatran 110mg, dabigatran 150mg, and rivaroxaban with ICERs of £4,497/QALY, £9,611/QALY and £5,305/QALY, respectively, [42]. Further results of various European cost-effectiveness analyses of apixaban in comparison with other DOACs (apix­ aban, dabigatran 110mg, dabigatran 150mg, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban) in patients diagnosed with NVAF are shown in appendix S2. The table gives an overview of total costs, total QALYs, ICER per QALY gained of the various treatment options, and the willingness to pay threshold of the different European countries. The presented data are considered from the health-care payer perspective.  

López-López’s (2017) work, from which we used the NMA, also analyzed apixaban’s cost-effectiveness for the UK. Results reviled that apixaban 5mg twice daily has the highest expected incremental net benefit (£7,533), followed by dabi­ gatran 150mg twice daily (£6,365), rivaroxaban 20mg once daily (£5,279), and edoxaban 60mg once daily (£5,212) [13]. Since we followed the modeling approach, the procedure is the most comparable with the present one.  

A systematic literature review on the cost-effectiveness analysis of apixaban for stroke prevention included 26 costeffectiveness analyses. Apixaban was compared with warfarin/ acenocoumarol in 7 of them $(27\%)$ , with warfarin/NOACs in 14 $(54\%)$ , with aspirin in 2 $(8\%)$ , and with warfarin/aspirin in 3 $(11\%)$ [12]. In models performed in Europe, ICERs of apixaban versus warfarin ranged from €5,607/QALY to €57,245/QALY, while in the USA, ICERs of apixaban versus warfarin ranged from being dominant to 93,063 USD/QALY [12].  

The key strength of the present model is that the analysis was developed based on a published NMA by López-López et al. (2017) in the absence of head-to-head RCTs [13]. Authors stated that the findings concerning the overall efficacy and safety of DOACs are consistent with previously published meta-analyses and postapproval observational studies [13].  

Resource utilization data were assessed with care – using a systematic literature search and well accept publications that meet the quality standards. All costs correspond to actual reimbursement prices. No estimates or transfers from other health systems have been made.  

Among the limitations of this analysis, one has to mention when interpreting the results, it has to be taken into account that randomized controlled trials (RCTs) recruit selected patient groups and drugs are often less restrictively applied in a real-world situation. Treatment discontinuation rates were extracted out of RCTs. In routine clinical practice, a patient is more likely to withdraw from treatment, if treat­ ment was judged to be ineffective or intolerable. Discontinuation rates may substantially influence the treat­ ment effect and costs.  

A limitation of the analysis is that this study did not con­ sider indirect costs. AF-related complications are associated with significant economic burden, not only in terms of direct costs but also indirect costs including loss of income, reduc­ tion of productivity, burden on caregivers and family mem­ bers, as well as a reduction in patient’s QoL. As indirect cost data were scarce, incorporating such costs is difficult.  

A further limitation of this study is that the therapeutic alternatives were generally not studied in head-to-head trials, despite the fact that a lot of studies and data regarding DOACs are existing. Due to that, the present model was developed based on an NMA with 23 phase II or phase III randomized controlled trials published by López-López et al. (2017).  

In patients with atrial fibrillation and an increased risk of stroke, prophylaxis with apixaban was highly cost-effective against warfarin from the perspective of the Austrian healthcare system. It should be mentioned that, comparison among ${\mathsf{DOACs}},$ only marginal differences in health effects and total costs were found.  

Future research is needed to evaluate the total economic disease burden. AF and associated embolic events are significant public health problems, which lead to high hospitalization costs, long-term maintenance costs, and indirect costs which contri­ bute to the high economic burden to health-care systems. Further research in the area of DOACs will be required to quantify the reduced economic burden, on the one hand, and the patient-related burden of illness, on the other hand.  

The following conclusions can be drawn from the present modeling study: Results highlighted the cost-effectiveness and cost-saving of apixaban compared to other DOACs from the Austrian health-care system. Apixaban dominated all other DOACs patients.  

# 5. Expert commentary  

In Austria, the list prices and the associated pricing models lead to homogenous total costs of DOACs. Therefore, it is necessary to compare the DOACs on the basis of seperate direct cost components of event costs of each DOAC. Due to the fact that each of these cost components show a different effect on the total costs and on the ICERs of each DOAC, it can be shown that apixaban is associated with the lowest total costs and apixaban dominates all other DOACs.
