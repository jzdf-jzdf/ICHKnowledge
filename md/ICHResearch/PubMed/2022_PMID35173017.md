# Mechanical Thrombectomy vs Bridging Therapy for Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusion Stroke  

Systematic Review and Meta-analysis  

Gabriela Trifan, MD, Jose Biller, MD, and Fernando D. Testai, MD, PhD  

Neurology® 2022;98:e1361-e1373. doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000200029  

Correspondence Dr. Trifan gtrifan@uic.edu  



# Abstract  



# Background and Objectives  

Current guidelines recommend the use of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) plus IV thrombolysis (aka bridging therapy [BT]) for patients with anterior circulation large vessel occlusion (LVO) stroke. However, clinical equipoise exists in relation to the use of BT vs MT alone. Our objective is to compare the efficacy and safety of BT and MT for anterior circulation LVO.  



# Methods  

A systematic search of biomedical literature databases was performed from inception to October 29, 2021, to identify prospective and retrospective studies comparing the rates for functional independence (modified Rankin Scale score 0–2) and mortality at 90 days, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), and successful recanalization rates for MT and BT. Effect size was represented by odds ratio (OR), and analysis was done with random-effects meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was assessed by $I^{2}$ and Cochrane $Q$ statistics.  



# Results  

Overall, 41 studies with 14,885 patients were included. Mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ age was $69\pm11$ years for BT and $70\pm11$ years for MT. All studies used alteplase as the thrombolytic agent. The BT group had $29\%$ higher odds for functional independence (OR 1.29, $95\%\mathrm{CI}1.16-\overset{-}{1}.44,I^{2}=42\%$ ), $25\%$ higher odds of successful reperfusion (OR 1.25, $95\%\operatorname{CI}1.08\substack{-1.44,I^{2}=42\%})$ , and $31\%$ decrease in odds for mortality (OR 0.69, $95\%$ CI 0.60–0.80, $I^{2}=47\%$ ) compared with MT. sICH prevalence was similar between groups (OR 1.10, $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.95\substack{-1.28,I^{2}=0\%}$ . Six of the studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with intention-to-treat analysis done in patients presenting directly to MT-capable centers. When analysis was restricted to these 6 RCTs ( $\mathbf{\check{n}}=$ 2,333), no differences were observed in functional independence (OR 1.08, $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.91{-}1.27_{.}$ , $I^{2}=0\%$ ), sICH (OR 1.37, $95\%$ CI 0.95–1.97, $I^{2}=\stackrel{-}{0}\%$ ), or mortality (OR 0.93, $95\%$ CI 0.74–1.16, $I^{2}=0\%$ ) between groups. However, successful reperfusion favored the BT group (OR 1.35, $95\%$ CI 1.06–1.73, $\overset{\vartriangle}{\boldsymbol{I}^{2}}=\overset{\vartriangle}{\boldsymbol{0}\%}$ ).  



# Discussion  

The odds for functional independence, successful reperfusion, and mortality for the entire dataset favored the use of BT over MT (medium heterogeneity and low quality of evidence). When analysis was restricted to RCTs, both treatments had similar functional and safety outcomes (no heterogeneity), but recanalization rates favored the BT group (no heterogeneity). Because these findings may differ in patients who present to non–MT-capable centers or with the use of other thrombolytic agents, further RCTs are needed.

Several national and international professional organizations recommend the use of IV thrombolysis (IVT) for patients with ischemic stroke presenting within 4.5 hours from symptom onset.1-3 Previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated the robust efficacy and safety of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for the treatment of patients with anterior circulation stroke due to large vessel occlusion (LVO).4,5 For patients who are eligible for both treatments, the current guidelines recommend IVT followed by MT (aka bridging therapy [BT]). IVT has a limited efficacy for achieving recanalization after LVO, and reports suggest that IVT may delay the initiation of MT, cause clot fragmentation and distal migration, and increase the risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH). Thus, several groups have investigated the efficacy and safety of BT compared to MT alone for patients with LVO. Previous metaanalyses of observational studies comparing BT to MT have suggested a benefit in favor of BT.6,7 However, the interpretation of these studies is confounded by the inclusion of posterior circulation strokes, selection bias, and variations in study design.7,8  

The results of 3 new RCTs comparing BT to MT for patients with LVO were recently published.9-11 A meta-analysis of these 3 studies showed comparable rates of functional independence and mortality for both treatments, but IVT before thrombectomy (BT) led to a higher likelihood of successful recanalization compared to receiving MT alone.12 These studies were done in Asian populations and restricted the recruitment of patients to MT-capable centers, factors that limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, several methodologic factors confound the interpretation of the results, including the failure to meet the prespecified margins of noninferiority in 1 study and the early termination of another study. The preliminary results of a fourth RCT that compared MT alone to BT were presented at the 2021 International Stroke Conference, with the final results being published recently.13 This study also failed to demonstrate noninferiority or superiority between both treatments. A meta-analysis including these 4 RCTs found that BT increases the odds of successful reperfusion. Recently, the results of 2 additional RCTs investigating BT to MT were presented at the 2021 World Stroke Congress.15,16  

Clinical equipoise persists in relation to the best treatment of patients who are eligible for both MT and IVT. Our objective is to compare the efficacy and safety of BT to MT by pooling data from all observational and randomized studies to date done in patients with anterior circulation LVO stroke.  



# Methods  



# Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents  

Our study follows the guidelines set forth by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement.17 Local Institutional Review Board approval was not required given the nature of the analysis.  



# Eligibility Criteria  

This meta-analysis included prospective and retrospective, randomized and nonrandomized studies that compared BT to MT in adult patients presenting with acute anterior circulation LVO stroke. We did not filter studies by language. Included studies must have reported the rate for at least 1 of the outcomes of interest. Studies that included anterior and posterior circulation LVO were included if the outcomes of interest for anterior circulation LVO were reported separately. Studies that reported pooled outcomes for anterior and posterior circulation LVO were excluded. For articles reporting outcomes in overlapping populations, we excluded the study with the lower level of quality.  



# Information Sources and Search Strategy  

We searched PubMed, Ovid EMBASE, Medline, Cochrane Central Registry of Controlled Trials, and ClinicalTrials.gov from database inception to October 29, 2021. The search strategy is presented in eAppendix 1 (links.lww.com/WNL/ B784). In addition, we included the results from the recently published MR CLEAN NO-IV trial13 (Intravenous Treatment followed by endovascular treatment versus direct endovascular treatment for acute ischemic stroke caused by a proximal intracranial occlusion) and those from Bridging Thrombolysis Versus Direct Mechanical Thrombectomy in Acute Ischemic Stroke (SWIFT-DIRECT) and DIRECTSAFE that were presented at the 2021 World Stroke Congress.  



# Study Selection and Data Collection Process  

The literature was independently screened by 2 authors (F.D.T. and G.T.). The full text of the eligible articles was obtained and further assessed for inclusion criteria.  

Disagreements were resolved by the third author (J.B.). eFigure 1 (links.lww.com/WNL/B784) shows the flow diagram of the included studies.  



# Data Extraction  

Information abstracted from studies included age, sex, trial design and randomization process, treatment arms (BT vs direct MT), type of analysis (intention to treat vs per protocol), stroke severity at baseline as measured by the NIH Stroke Scale, stroke etiology (cardioembolic, atherothrombotic, or other), LVO site (internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery, M1 or M2 branches, or other), efficacy times (stroke symptom onset to groin puncture, stroke symptom onset to endovascular recanalization, stroke symptom onset to IVT), and outcomes of interest: functional independence (defined as modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score 0–2) and mortality at 90 days, sICH, and successful recanalization rates for MT and BT.  



# Data Analysis  

Risk of bias for each eligible study was assessed with the Cochrane tool for assessing risk of bias in randomized trials (RoB2).18 Methodologic quality was rated with GradePro software.19 Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of the funnel plots for asymmetry for each outcome of interest and statistically with the Egger test of the intercept.20 Efficacy outcomes were the proportion of patients who achieved functional independence at 90 days (mRS score 0–2) and the frequency of successful reperfusion on postprocedural angiography (defined as Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction score ${}_{\geq2\mathrm{b}}$ or the Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction score ${\geq}2.$ ). mRS scores range from 0 (no residual symptoms) to 6 (death). Safety outcomes were based on the rate of sICH during hospitalization and 90-day mortality. Event rates, along with the total number of participants for each outcome, were obtained for the BT and MT groups. In primary analyses, we calculated the odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding $95\%$ CIs for each outcome of interest using random-effects models with the Mantel-Haenszel method. We also calculated the absolute number of events per 1,000 participants (absolute risk increase and absolute risk reduction).  

Randomized and nonrandomized trials can show a high correlation in their efficacy estimates. However, nonrandomized trials tend to show larger treatment effects.21 Therefore, we investigated efficacy and safety outcomes grouping the data by study design into (1) RCT, (2) prospective nonrandomized (PO-NR), and (3) retrospective nonrandomized (RO-NR). RCTs were further subcategorized into those that reported outcomes based on intention-to-treat analysis and those that reported outcomes based on subgroup analysis from the parental RCT. Some studies have shown that IVT may delay the initiation of MT for patients undergoing BT. Thus, in secondary analysis, we investigated time from stroke onset to endovascular recanalization between MT and BT.  

Heterogeneity was evaluated with the Cochran $I^{2}$ statistics, where $\bar{I^{2}}\leq25\%$ indicates low heterogeneity, $I^{2}=25\%$ to $75\%$ indicates medium heterogeneity, and $I^{2}>75\%$ indicates high heterogeneity.22 When necessary, means and SDs were calculated from median and interquartile ranges as previously described.23 All statistical tests were 2 sided with a significance threshold of $p\leq0.05$ . Data analyses were performed with Cochrane review Manager and MedCalc software, version 19.8 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium).

Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available by request from any qualified investigator.  



# Results  



# Study Selection and Characteristics  

A total of 41 studies with 14,885 participants were included in the analysis.e1-e41 Mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ age was similar between groups $(69\pm11\$ years for BT and $70~\pm~11$ years for MT). Men accounted for $52\%$ of the cases. Seventy-six percent of all included studies reported rates for all the outcomes of interest, and all of them used alteplase as the thrombolytic agent. Baseline characteristics of each individual study are included in Table 1 and eTable 1 (links.lww.com/WNL/B784).  



# Risk of Bias  

Risk of bias for each individual study and across all studies was overall variable (eFigure 2, A and B, links.lww.com/WNL/ B784). PO-NR and RO-NR studies had moderate to high risk for bias, mainly because of randomization process, analysis plan, and unblinded outcomes. In contrast, RCTs with intention-to-treat analysis showed low bias across all domains investigated. No publication bias was detected by visual inspection of the funnel plots or by calculating the Egger test of intercept $\binom{\cdot}{p}>0.05$ for all outcomes, eFigure 3).  



# Primary Efficacy Outcome  

Functional outcome data were available for all 41 included studies. The BT group had a $29\%$ increase in odds for 90-day functional independence (mRS score 0–2) compared with the MT group (OR 1.29, $95\%$ CI 1.16–1.44, $p<0.0001_{.}$ ), with medium heterogeneity $'I^{2}=42\%;$ ) and low quality of evidence. The anticipated absolute effects for functional independence with BT (risk difference compared with MT) was 62 more per 1,000 patients ( $95\%$ CI 36 more–90 more) (Figure 1 and Table 2). The BT group also had $25\%$ higher odds for successful reperfusion (OR 1.25, $95\%$ CI 1.08–1.44, $p=0.003,$ ), with medium heterogeneity $(I^{2}=42\%)$ ) and low quality of evidence. The anticipated absolute effects for successful reperfusion with BT (risk difference compared with MT) were 35 more per 1,000 patients ( $95\%$ CI 12 more–54 more) (Figure 2 and Table 2).

# Safety Outcomes  

Rates for sICH were available for 34 studies. The combined odds for sICH for all 34 studies were similar between groups (OR 1.10, $95\%\operatorname{CI}0.95\substack{-1.28,p=0.22},$ ) with no heterogeneity $\overset{\prime}{I}^{2}=0\%,$ ) and low quality of evidence (Figure 4). Odds for sICH remained similar between BT and MT with no heterogeneity $\left(I^{2}=0\right)$ when analyses were done by study design (Figure 4). When analysis was limited to RCTs with intention-to-treat design, no differences in safety outcomes were observed between BT and MT, with moderate quality of evidence and no heterogeneity (Figure 3).  

Mortality odds were overall $31\%$ lower in the BT group than the MT group (OR 0.69, $95\%$ CI 0.60-0.80, $p<0.001,$ ), with medium heterogeneity $\overset{\prime}{I}^{2}=47\%$ ). The anticipated absolute effects for mortality associated with BT (risk difference compared with MT) were 59 fewer events per 1,000 patients $(95\%\mathrm{CI}37{-}78$ fewer) (Figure 5 and Table 2). Mortality at 90 days was also similar between BT and MT for all RCTs, with a $32\%$ weight and an OR of 0.76 $\mathrm{'}95\%\mathrm{CI}0.57\mathrm{-}1.00,p=0.05,$ medium heterogeneity, $I^{2}=58\%$ ). Pooled data from PO-NR and RO-NR studies showed that mortality odds favored BT over MT. PO-NR studies had a $35\%$ weight, an OR of 0.69, $95\%$ CI of 0.54 to 0.89, and $p=0.004$ (medium heterogeneity, $I^{2}=58\%,$ ), while RO-NR studies had a $33\%$ weight with an OR of 0.64, $95\%$ CI of 0.50 to 0.83, and $p=0.0005$ (medium heterogeneity, $I^{2}=29\%$ ) (Figure 5).  



# Secondary Analysis  

For the secondary outcome of stroke onset–to–groin puncture time, the pooled mean difference of time was $-17.22$ minutes $95\%$ CI $-30.49$ to $-3.96\$ ) in favor of MT over BT. Different results were observed when studies were divided by design. In PO-NR studies, the mean difference was $-32.26$ minutes ( $95\%$ CI $-60.48$ to $-4.04\$ ) in favor of MT. In contrast, RCT and RO-NR studies showed no significant difference between MT and BT. However, all the comparisons had high heterogeneity (eFigure 4, links.lww.com/WNL/B784).  



# Discussion  

Our study shows that, in patients with anterior circulation LVO, BT is associated with higher odds of achieving functional independence and successful reperfusion, decreased 90-day mortality, and similar odds of experiencing sICH compared with MT alone. Our results, however, had medium heterogeneity and low to moderate quality of evidence. In comparison, when the analysis was restricted to RCTs with intention-to-treat design, no differences were observed in functional independence or safety outcomes, but successful reperfusion occurred more frequently in the BT group. The quality of evidence in this last case was moderate, and there was no heterogeneity.  

Prior RCTs have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of BT compared to IVT alone for patients with anterior circulation LVO.4,24 Similarly, a meta-analysis that pooled individual patient data from 5 RCT (Time to Treatment With Endovascular Thrombectomy and Outcomes From Ischemic Stroke: A Meta-analysis trial [HERMES]) confirmed the robust beneficial effect of MT for achieving functional independence at 90 days compared to conservative medical management.25 However, in subgroup analysis from the HERMES trial, the effect size of MT did not differ between patients who received BT and those who received MT alone $\mathrm{\Delta}p$ for interaction $=~0.43$ ). This questioned the incremental beneficial effect of IVT over MT. Multiple guidelines for the treatment of acute ischemic stroke recommend that patients who are eligible for IVT should receive IVT even if MT is being considered.1-3 IVT can lead to early recanalization, although this is influenced by clot burden and the occlusion site, with higher recanalization rates seen for distal thrombi.26 Proximal anterior circulation LVO thrombi, in comparison, are usually longer $\left(\ge8\mathrm{mm}\right)$ and have low recanalization rates with IVT alone.27 In addition, IVT is not free of complications. These include possible clot fragmentation resulting in distal migration, angioedema, and hemorrhagic transformation.

Observational studies that evaluated the efficacy and safety of BT compared to MT alone yielded mixed results. The interpretation of these studies is confounded by selection bias resulting from the treatment of sicker patients who may be ineligible for IVT with MT alone. A meta-analysis from 2019 investigated functional independence, mortality at 90 days, sICH, and successful reperfusion rates between BT and MT according to IVT eligibility. Safety and efficacy outcomes were comparable between groups except for mortality, which was observed more frequently in the MT group. In sensitivity analysis, the higher mortality in the MT group was driven by the results obtained in studies that included mixed populations of IVT-eligible and -ineligible patients.28 However, in studies that included IVT-eligible patients only, the mortality was similar in the BT and MT groups. Another meta-analysis that pooled data from nonrandomized trials and data from post hoc RCTs showed that efficacy outcomes were not different between MT alone and BT in patients eligible for IVT. However, in a pooled analysis of studies that included patients who were ineligible for IVT, BT showed better functional outcomes compared to MT alone.6 These findings may be explained, at least in part, by the characteristics of the patients who are ineligible for IVT. They are usually older, have higher rates of atrial fibrillation and anticoagulation use, or have longer times from symptom onset to hospital presentation, all factors that lead to worse 90-day functional outcome.

Efficiently in Chinese Tertiary Hospitals: A Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial (DIRECT-MT),11 SKIP,10 and $\mathrm{DEVT}^{9}$ were the only RCTs with intention-to-treat design that compared BT to MT for patients with anterior circulation LVO randomized within 4.5 hours from symptom onset. Since the publication of these studies, the results of MR  

CLEAN-NO $\mathrm{IV}^{13}$ were published, and the results of SWIFTDIRECT15 and DIRECT-SAFE16 trials were presented at the 2021 World Stroke Congress. When we restricted the analysis to these 6 RCTs, we observed that functional and safety outcomes did not differ in the BT and MT groups, but successful recanalization rates continued to favor BT. RCTs are considered the gold standard for evaluating the efficacy and safety of treatment interventions. However, several aspects confound the generalizability of the results obtained in these studies. SKIP, DIRECT-MT, and DEVT were conducted in Asian individuals. This population is known to have an elevated prevalence of large artery intracranial atherosclerotic occlusive disease,30 which is less responsive to IVT than cardioembolic stroke.31 In addition, the median door-toneedle times of 59 minutes in DIRECT-MT and 61 minutes in DEVT were significantly longer than the 40 minutes reported in the HERMES meta-analysis. Furthermore, SKIP was done in Japan, where the dose of alteplase is $0.6\mathrm{mg/kg},$ a dose that failed to demonstrate noninferiority to the standard dose of $0.9~\mathrm{mg/kg.}^{32}$ These and other factors may have underplayed the benefits of alteplase in patients receiving BT.33 It should be noted that DEVT, DIRECT-MT, and SKIP had a noninferiority design but included generous margins that exceeded the thresholds used with cardiac devices and the minimal clinically important differences recommended by stroke experts.34 Furthermore, 4 of the studies (SKIP, MR CLEAN-NO IV, SWIFT-DIRECT, and DIRECT-SAFE) did not meet the prespecified statistical thresholds for noninferiority; DEVT was terminated early due to efficacy, while DIRECT-SAFE was terminated early due to publication of the results of DIRECT-MT, DEVT, and SKIP and the low likelihood of continuing recruitment. Thus, an overestimation of the treatment effect cannot be completely ruled out.35  

Two additional factors are worth mentioning. The detractors of BT have expressed concern that IVT may delay the initiation of the thrombectomy, a factor that may adversely influence outcome. Our study is reassuring in this aspect in that it demonstrates that IVT is not associated with an adverse stroke onset–to–groin puncture time. The supporters of BT, on the other hand, have proposed that IVT before MT may facilitate successful reperfusion. To this end, the pooled results from the RCT with intention-to-treat design seem to support this hypothesis. From the service delivery standpoint, the RCTs discussed herein included only patients presenting directly to MT-capable centers. According to data obtained in Get With The Guidelines-–Stroke, ${\approx}43\%$ of patients receiving EVT arrive to the MT-capable center by transfer from another hospital.36 The delays associated with transfers, often resulting from medical decision-making and transport logistics, may allow for a full therapeutic effect of IVT. Thus, it is possible that the results obtained in the aforementioned RCTs may differ in real-world settings.  

Our meta-analysis has several limitations. First, outcomes for sICH and mortality were not available for all studies, which may introduce estimate bias. However, we observed similar rates of sICH in both treatment arms, an observation that is in agreement with previously published studies.7,8 Second, the majority of data were pooled from observational nonrandomized studies with the consequent risk of selection bias. A substantial proportion of these studies had low quality of evidence with missing information. Thus, caution should be exercised in the interpretation of results from meta-analyses that included only observational data. In comparison, our study also included data from 6 RCTs with intention-to-treat analysis comparing BT to MT alone. Third, nonrandomized trials typically include patients who may not be eligible for IVT. As previously discussed, IVT ineligibility may constitute a surrogate maker for stroke severity, presence of comorbid conditions, late presentation, and poor baseline, all factors that influence outcome. Thus, sicker patients are more likely to receive MT alone, which constitutes an important source of bias in detriment of this treatment. Fourth, our study included only patients with anterior circulation LVO. Therefore, our results cannot be extrapolated to other vascular beds. However, 2 RCTs done in patients with recent basilar artery occlusion failed to demonstrate a benefit of MT alone over best medical management, including IVT.37,38 Fifth, alteplase was the only thrombolytic agent used in the studies included in the analysis. Thus, the results may differ with tenecteplase, a thrombolytic agent with higher fibrin specificity that has been associated with improved recanalization rates and clinical outcomes compared to alteplase.39 Sixth, there were variations in the performance metrics reported by different groups. Stroke onset–to–recanalization time, in particular, was available for only a few studies. Nonetheless, the data obtained in both observational trials and RCTs confirm that the use of IVT is safe and does not delay recanalization. Seventh, we included in our study the results of DIRECT-SAFE and SWIFT-DIRECT, for which preliminary results were presented at major scientific meetings. It is possible that the final results of these studies may introduce small changes in the point estimates presented here. However, the heterogeneity was small, and our final conclusions are unlikely to change. Eighth, compared to RCTs that had no to low heterogeneity for functional and safety outcomes, the overall study had medium heterogeneity. Yet, our study is largest meta-analysis comparing BT to MT alone for anterior circulation LVO and the only study that has incorporated all the observational and RCTs available to date.  

The collective data presented here indicate that, in selected patients with anterior circulation LVO, BT may be superior to MT for achieving functional independence and successful recanalization. It is important to note that BT is safe and does not increase the risk of sICH or delay the initiation of MT. These results should be interpreted with caution because the data obtained in RCTs with intention-to-treat analysis suggest that functional and safety outcomes with MT alone may be comparable to those of BT. While the results of these studies are not strong enough to change our current practice, they constitute a foundational step in acute stroke care and support the concept of individualized treatment as opposed to the classic one-size-fits-all approach. Until more information becomes available, for patients with anterior circulation LVO who are eligible for both IVT and MT, BT remains the standard of care.
