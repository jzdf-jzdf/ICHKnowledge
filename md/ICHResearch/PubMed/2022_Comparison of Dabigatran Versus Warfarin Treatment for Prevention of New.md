# Comparison of Dabigatran Versus Warfarin Treatment for Prevention of New Cerebral Lesions in Valvular Atrial Fibrillation  

Min Soo Cho, MDa, Minsu Kim, $\mathbf{MD}^{\mathrm{b}}$ , Seung-ah Lee, $\mathbf{MD}^{\mathrm{a}}$ , Sahmin Lee, $\mathbf{MD}^{\mathrm{a}}$ , Dae-Hee Kim, $\mathbf{MD}^{\mathrm{a}}$ Jun Kim, MDa, Jong-Min Song, $\mathbf{MD}^{\mathrm{a}}$ , Gi-Byoung Nam, $\mathbf{MD}^{\mathrm{a}}$ , Sang Joon Kim, $\mathbf{MD^{\mathrm{c}}}$ , Duk-Hyun Kang, $\mathbf{MD}^{\mathrm{a,*}}$ , and Kee-Joon Choi, MDa,\*\*  

Warfarin is the standard anticoagulation therapy for valvular atrial fibrillation (AF); however, new oral anticoagulants have emerged as an alternative. We compared the efficacy and safety of dabigatran with conventional treatment in AF associated with left-sided valvular heart disease (VHD), including mitral stenosis (MS). Patients with AF and leftsided VHD were randomly assigned to receive dabigatran or conventional treatment. The primary end point was the occurrence of clinical stroke or a new brain lesion (silent brain infarct and microbleed) on 1-year follow-up brain magnetic resonance imaging. Patients in the dabigatran group were switched from warfarin $(\mathbf{n}=52)$ , antiplatelets alone $(\mathbf{n}=5)$ ), or no therapy $(\mathbf{n}=2)$ to dabigatran. In the conventional group, 53 used warfarin (including 42 MS patients), and 7 used antiplatelets. No death or clinical stroke event occurred in either group during follow-up. Silent brain infarct and microbleed occurred in 20 and 2 patients in the dabigatran group and 20 and 4 patients in the conventional treatment group. The incidence rate of the primary end point did not significantly differ between groups $(34\%$ vs $40\%$ , relative risk 0.87, $95\%$ confidence interval 0.59 to 1.29, $\mathbf{p=0.491})$ ). The primary end point rate was similar between groups in 82 patients (40 in the dabigatran group and 42 in the conventional group) with MS $32\%$ vs $34\%$ , relative risk 0.93, $95\%$ confidence interval: 0.57 to 1.50, $\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0.759})$ . In conclusion, primary end point rates after treatment with dabigatran were similar to conventional treatment in patients with significant VHD and AF. New oral anticoagulants could be a reasonable alternative to warfarin in patients with AF and VHD, which should be confirmed in future large-scale studies. $\textcircled{\odot}\mathbf{20}\mathbf{2}\mathbf{2}$ Elsevier Inc. # Introduction  

Ischemic stroke is a major complication of atrial fibrillation (AF).1 AF patients with underlying valvular heart disease (VHD) are at higher risk of ischemic stroke in population-based studies.2,3 Standard anticoagulation is, therefore, the cornerstone of managing patients with AF and VHD. The nonvitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOAC) have replaced a large portion of warfarin use given their better efficacy and safety profiles, proved in multiple randomized controlled trials.4−7 However, the role of NOACs in managing valvular AF remains undetermined, particularly in AF associated with mitral stenosis (MS).  

Dabigatran etexilate is a NOAC used to treat nonvalvular AF; evidence on the efficacy and safety of dabigatran in AF patients with native VHD is lacking. In addition, excessive risk and no benefit has been observed in patients with a mechanical prosthetic valve.9 An observational study of patients with MS and AF has suggested a positive role of NOAC in these patients; however, there were no prospective data available.10 The DECISIVE (Effectiveness of Dabigatran vs Conventional Treatment for Prevention of Silent Cerebral Infarct in Aortic and Mitral Valvular Atrial Fibrillation Patients) trial was designed to assess the therapeutic impact of dabigatran in patients with AF and leftsided VHD. We hypothesized that dabigatran would be superior to conventional treatment (warfarin or aspirin) for preventing subclinical ischemic stroke and intracranial bleeding.  

# Methods  

This study was a prospective, single-center, open-label, randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of dabigatran compared with warfarin in patients with valvular AF. Boehringer Ingelheim (Ingelheim, Germany) partially funded the study and provided the drug; however, they had no role in data collection, analysis, interpretation, or presenting the results. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of our institution (2016-0869), and written informed consent was provided by all study participants.  

Eligibility criteria included age of at least 19 years, diagnosis of AF, significant (moderate-to-severe) left-sided VHD (aortic stenosis, aortic regurgitation, MS, or mitral regurgitation) diagnosed through echocardiography and high embolic risk ( $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -VASc score ${\ge}2$ or $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -VASc score of 1 with left atrial enlargement).11  

Exclusion criteria at the screening were: previous cardiac valve replacement, stroke within 2 weeks, chronic renal failure with creatinine clearance $<30\mathrm{ml/min}$ , active hepatitis, high bleeding risk (major surgery within the past 1 month, planned surgery within 3 months, history of critical organ or gastrointestinal bleeding, uncontrolled hypertension, active malignancy), thrombocytopenia $(<80,000$ $\mathrm{cells/mm}$ ), anemia (hemoglobin level ${<}10~\mathrm{g/100~ml}$ ), contraindication to anticoagulant treatment.  

We randomly assigned patients with valvular AF at a 1:1 ratio to open-label treatment with dabigatran or conventional treatment (warfarin or aspirin) using a computerized randomization system. The randomization sequence was generated with a block size of 4 and stratified according to the presence of MS. The interactive web response system assigned a randomization number for each participant and specified a unique medication number for dispensed drugs.  

After randomization, all patients underwent baseline magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and received treatment within 24 hours of initial brain imaging. Patients in the dabigatran group were administered $150~\mathrm{mg}$ of dabigatran twice daily, which could be reduced to $110~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily in the case of creatinine clearance of 30 to $49\mathrm{ml/min}$ .12 Patients in the conventional treatment group with MS or $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -VASc score ${\ge}2$ were required to take warfarin targeting an international normalized ratio of 2 to 3, and those with $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -VASc score of 1 took warfarin or aspirin $100~\mathrm{mg}$ once daily, as appropriate. Patients in both groups were followed up every 3 months to evaluate adherence to the study medications and clinical assessment. All outcomes reported by the investigators during follow-up were assessed and categorized by the steering committee.  

Brain MRI was performed at randomization and 12 months follow-up. The MRI protocol consisted of fluidattenuated inversion recovery and gradient-recalled echo imaging to identify newly developed silent brain infarctions or microbleeds during follow-up.13 Patients who had experienced neurologic symptoms also underwent MRI at the time of clinical presentation to confirm an ischemic stroke. All patients were encouraged to complete the follow-up brain imaging regardless of compliance to the study medications and included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis. Experienced neuroradiologists masked to the patient data independently interpreted the images. The 20 random samples were then interpreted by a second radiologist with good interobserver variability (Kappa value of 0.879).  

The primary end point was the composite of clinical stroke and new cerebral MRI lesion (including new silent brain infarct or microbleed) in the follow-up brain imaging.  

Clinical stroke was diagnosed primarily based on imaging findings and clinical presentations, including neurologic deficit.14 Silent brain infarct was defined as an asymptomatic new ischemic lesion in follow-up fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging. Microbleed was defined as asymptomatic hemorrhage in follow-up gradient-recalled echo imaging. Secondary end points included each component of the primary end point, death, and major or minor bleeding according to the criteria from the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis.15  

We designed the trial to demonstrate the superiority of dabigatran over conventional treatment to prevent new ischemic stroke lesions and intracranial bleeding. The rate of silent brain infarct and microbleed rate was assumed to be $40\%$ /year in patients on conventional treatment $30\%$ year of silent infarct and $10\%$ /year of microbleed), based on previous observational studies, and we estimated that approximately $30\%$ to $45\%$ of silent infarct and $90\%$ of microbleed cases could be reduced with dabigatran treatment.16−18 Based on the assumption that the incidence of combined primary end point would be $40\%$ in the conventional treatment group and $16\%$ in the dabigatran group, we estimated that a sample size of 120 (60 patients in each group) patients would provide the trial with $80\%$ power, at a 2-sided significance level of 0.05, to detect a significant difference concerning the primary end point. We also assumed that the rate of loss to follow-up brain imaging would be $10\%$ . An independent biostatistician performed all statistical analyses.  

The primary analysis was performed on an ITT basis. A per-protocol analysis was performed in patients without protocol violation, crossover, loss to follow-up brain imaging, and ${>}80\%$ adherence to the study medication. Adverse events were assessed in all randomized patients taking at least 1 dose of the study medication. Categorical variables are presented as frequency with percentage, and continuous variables as median and interquartile range or mean and SD. Comparisons between groups were made using the chisquare test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and Student $t$ test for continuous variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 3.3.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and 2-sided $\mathsf{p}$ values $<0.05$ were considered statistically significant.  

# Results  

The number of patients who were screened, randomized, and assigned to each study group is shown in Figure 1. We enrolled 120 patients between March 2017 and October 2019 and randomly assigned them at a 1:1 ratio to the dabigatran or conventional treatment groups. One patient in the dabigatran group withdrew consent immediately after randomization, resulting in 59 patients in the dabigatran group and the baseline evaluation of 119 patients. A crossover occurred in 5 patients in the dabigatran group who switched to warfarin during follow-up, 2 of whom did not undergo follow-up MRI, and 1 patient in the conventional treatment group (Figure 1). The mean age of the study population was $61.2\pm9.0$ years, and $69\%$ were male (Table 1). The mean $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -VASc score was $2.1\pm1.2$ points, and most patients presented as persistent or permanent AF $(83\%)$ with a mean AF duration of $6.6\pm5.4$ years.  

The mean left atrial diameter was $53.9\pm7.9\mathrm{{mm}}$ in the baseline echocardiography, and moderate−severe rheumatic MS $(69\%)$ was the predominant valvular pathology. Most patients $(87\%)$ were taking warfarin before enrollment. Previous infarction and microbleeds were observed on the baseline brain MRI in $16\%$ and $16\%$ of the patients, respectively. Patients in the dabigatran group were switched from warfarin $(\mathrm{n}=52\$ ), antiplatelets alone $(\mathtt{n}=5,$ ), or no therapy $(\mathtt{n}=2$ ) to dabigatran. In the conventional treatment group, all 42 patients with MS continued anticoagulation with warfarin, and the remaining 18 patients used warfarin $(\mathtt{n}=11\$ ) or antiplatelets $(\mathtt{n}=7)$ ). The overall time in the therapeutic range of patients using warfarin was $70.4\%$ .

Clinical stroke or death did not occur in either treatment group. The primary end point (stroke or silent brain lesion) occurred in 20 patients $(34\%)$ in the dabigatran group and 24 $(40\%)$ in the conventional treatment group (relative risk [RR] 0.87, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 0.59 to 1.29, $\mathrm{~p~}=~0.491;$ (Table 2). In the modified ITT analysis that included 116 patients $(97\%)$ with follow-up brain imaging, most new brain lesions were silent brain infarct on the 1- year MRI, with no difference between the 2 groups ( $35\%$ vs $36\%$ , RR 0.99, $95\%$ CI 0.67 to 1.46, $\mathrm{p}=0.955$ ). There was also no difference in the number of new silent brain infarct lesions on MRI per patient $(1.8\pm1.1~[\mathrm{n}=20]$ vs $2.2\pm1.1$ $[\mathrm{n}=21]$ , $\mathrm{p}=0.330,$ ). There was no significant difference in microbleed on 1-year MRI $4\%$ vs $7\%$ , RR 0.66, $95\%$ CI: 0.21 to 2.10, $\mathrm{p}=0.426)$ , and the rate of any clinical bleeding event was lower in the dabigatran group $2\%$ vs $7\%$ ) but was not statistically significant. Major bleeding requiring hospitalization occurred in 1 warfarin user. The overall trend of study end points was consistent in the per-protocol analysis. In the subgroup of patients with MS, the rate of primary end points was similar between the 2 groups $32\%$ vs $34\%$ , RR 0.93, $95\%$ CI: 0.57 to 1.50, $\mathrm{~p~}=~0.759;$ (Table 3). There was also no significant difference between the dabigatran and warfarin groups after excluding patients treated with antiplatelet therapy ( $34\%$ vs $38\%$ , RR 0.92, $95\%$ CI: 0.63 to 1.34, $\mathrm{p}=0.672$ , Table 3).  

The rate of adverse events did not differ significantly between the 2 groups $17\%$ vs $8\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=\mathrm{0}.157$ , Table 4); however, the rate of gastrointestinal symptoms was significantly higher in the dabigatran group ( $15\%$ vs $0\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=\mathrm{0}.001\$ ), causing 5 patients to discontinue treatment. The rate of serious adverse events was not significantly different. Only 1 patient in the conventional group had a serious adverse event related to treatment (major gastrointestinal bleeding), with 3 serious adverse events unrelated to the anticoagulation (admission for cholecystitis, heart failure, and hyperglycemia).  

# Discussion  

In this open-label, randomized trial, we found no significant difference in the primary end points between dabigatran and conventional treatment in patients with AF and significant left-sided VHD.  

The pivotal randomized trial of 4 NOACs excluded patients with moderate or severe MS and prosthetic heart valves.4−7 In the sub-analysis of the Randomized Evaluation of Long-Term Anticoagulation Therapy (RE-LY) trial, which included patients with VHD (except moderate −severe MS or mechanical valve), therapeutic benefits of dabigatran over warfarin were not influenced by the presence of VHD.19 However, no study has prospectively evaluated the effect of dabigatran in patients with significant VHD, including significant MS. The recent study by Kim et al10 suggested a possible role of NOACs in patients with MS. By analyzing 2,230 propensity-matched patient sets, NOACs were associated with fewer thromboembolic events (hazard ratio [HR] 0.28, $95\%$ CI: 0.18 to 0.45), lower mortality (HR 0.41, $95\%$ CI: 0.30 to 0.56) and a similar rate of intracranial hemorrhage (HR 0.53, $95\%$ CI: 0.22 to 1.26). This study was limited by its retrospective, claims-based analysis and lack of data on MS severity, quality of warfarin management, and compliance with the medication. The randomized design, near-complete follow-up, and appropriate end points of our study provide more precise and unbiased data that have important implications for managing patients with VHD and AF.

In the present study, the primary outcome of subclinical ischemic lesions is a well-recognized surrogate marker for subsequent clinical stroke.20 Patients with AF were associated with a ${>}2$ -fold increase in the odds for subclinical infarct,21 primarily involving the cardioembolic mechanism.22,23 The subclinical infarct was recently focused on for its association with cognitive decline and dementia in patients with AF.24 A recent American Heart Association statement highlighted that risk stratification and preventive treatment in patients with subclinical infarct are important strategies to prevent clinical infarction or dementia.13 Cerebral microbleed, as a main component of the study end point, is an important surrogate marker for intracranial bleeding, potentially occurring through a hemorrhagic transformation of the ischemic lesion.25,2 6 We, therefore, believe that subclinical infarct and microbleed as the primary outcome of this study are appropriate surrogate markers reflecting the thromboembolic risk of AF physiology.  

Although Randomized, Phase II Study to Evaluate the Safety and Pharmacokinetics of Oral Dabigatran Etexilate in Patients after Heart Valve Replacement (RE-ALIGN) demonstrated poor outcomes with NOACs in the mechanical valve,9 we believe that their effects in native VHD and MS would differ because the exposure of metallic valve leaflets, struts, and sewing rings to the circulating blood could lead to potential activation of the extrinsic pathway and thrombus formation in patients with mechanical valves.9,27,28 S tructural changes from the pressure and volume overload led to left atrium (LA) remodeling, which resulted in AF and a higher rate of thromboembolic events in patients with VHD.29 Our study patients were comprehensively evaluated by echocardiography, and the presence of significant LA enlargement (mean LA diameter $53.9\pm$ 7.9) reflects the high-risk nature of our study population with VHD. MS is associated with the highest risk of thromboembolism because extensive LA remodeling and stasis of blood flow leads to a higher rate of thrombus formation in the LA body and the LA appendage. After 1 year of treatment, there was no difference in the incidence of the subclinical ischemic lesion and microbleed in dabigatran and conventional treatment groups. This result was consistent in the MS subgroup. Although dabigatran was associated with more gastrointestinal symptoms, most patients $(92\%)$ could complete the 1-year course of treatment. Therefore, we believe that dabigatran could be a reasonable alternative to warfarin in patients with VHD and AF.  

Our study has several limitations, including the small number of study patients and clinical events. In addition, the primary end points of our study (subclinical ischemic lesion or microbleed) were well-defined clinical surrogate markers; however, they could not be directly interpreted as a clinical event. Antiplatelet therapy alone for stroke prevention or using the term "valvular atrial fibrillation" is not recommended in the recently updated AF guidelines, which were not applicable in the current trial. Finally, the relatively short follow-up duration may not sufficiently reflect the long-term effects of anticoagulation. Thus, the interpretation and application of our results to clinical practice should be made with caution.  

In conclusion, in the patients with significant VHD and AF, the risk of clinical stroke or new cerebral lesion was similar between the dabigatran and warfarin groups. Therefore, NOACs could be a reasonable alternative to warfarin in patients with AF and VHD. However, it should be confirmed in future large-scale prospective studies.
