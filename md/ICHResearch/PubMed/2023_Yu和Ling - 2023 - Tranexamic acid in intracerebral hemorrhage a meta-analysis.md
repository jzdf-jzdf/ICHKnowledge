# ABSTRACT  

Background: Tranexamic acid(TA) is an antifibrinolytic agent, which has shown an effect on reducing blood loss in many diseases. Tranexamic acid might be beneficial for intracerebral hemorrhage(ICH). However, whether TA can treatment of intracerebral hemorrhage is still controversial.  

Objective: Evidence-based medicine was used to evaluate the efficacy and safety of tranexamic acid in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage.  

Methods: Pubmed (MEDLINE), Embase, and Cochrane Library were searched from January 2001 to October 2020 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, and retrospective case series .The Jadad scale and RevMan software version 5.3 were used for literature quality assessment and meta-analysis.  

Results: In total, 4 randomized controlled trials and 1 retrospective case series with 2808 participants were included in the meta-analysis. Compared with control intervention in intracerebral hemorrhage, tranexamic acid could significantly reduce growth of hemorrhagic mass (odds ratio (OR) $=0.81$ ; $95\%$ confidence interva $\left(\mathsf{Cl}\right){=}0.68$ to $0.99\$ ; $p{=}0.04,$ and Modified Rankin Scale score (MRS) at 90 days at 0–3 $\mathrm{OR}=1.20;$ $95\%$ ${\sfCI}=1.00$ to 1.43; $p{=}0.05^{\prime}$ , mortality by day 90 $\mathrm{\mathit{~OR}}=1.03\$ ; $95\%$ $\mathsf{Cl}=0.85{-}1.25$ ; $p{=}0.77.$ ) and major thromboembolic events $(\mathrm{OR}=1.14;\$ $95\%$ $\mathsf{Cl}=0.73\substack{-1.77}$ ; $p{=}0.58)$ .  

Conclusions: Treatment with tranexamic acid could reduce hematoma expansion in intracerebral hemorrhage, and the treatment was safe with no increase in thromboembolic complications. But showed no notable impact on good functional outcomes and mortality.

# Introduction  

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is common causes of death and disability[1, 2] .Currently, 18 million people live with the sequelae of intracerebral haemorrhage and 3 million die because of the condition each year, comprising $5\%$ of all human deaths. ICH may result in coagulopathy, the increase in hemorrhage size, disability and high mortality [1, 3–7]. This coagulopathy is mainly caused by the increase in fibrinolysis, and high levels of fibrin degradation products[8, 9]. ICH is a dynamic, complex and continuous process, there is a phenomenon of early hematoma expansion, the motive force of hematoma expansion comes from blood pressure, if blood pressure is maintained at a higher level, it will promote hematoma expansion [10, 11]. Two main non-surgical strategies have been postulated to reduce HE: ameliorating the driving force of HE and haemostatic therapy [10, 11]. Blend sign, black hole sign, and hypodensities predict hematoma expansion while black hole sign, hypodensities, and island signs predict poor functional outcome [10, 11].  

Several haemostatic agents have been researched for possible effect in limiting HE. An extensively researched haemostatic agent, recombinant activated factor VIIa, was highly effective in reducing HE. Anticoagulant reversal is the mainstay of therapy for patients with oral anticoagulant associated ICH [10, 11]. Antifibrinolytic agents such as tranexamic acid have shown some efficacy to reduce hemorrhage [8, 9]. Tranexamic acid (TA) can compete with lysine residues on the surface of fibrin, blocks the interaction of plasminogen and plasmin to inhibit the fibrinolysis and consequently stabilize the clot [12, 13].  

The main determinants of clinical outcome in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage are age, neurological deficit, and haemorrhage cause, location, and volume [12, 13]. Of these, volume is the most important factor. As with ischaemic stroke, intracerebral haemorrhage is frequently a dynamic process, with growth occurring in most patients over the first few hours from symptom onset, presenting a target window for intervention [8, 9, 12–17]. Therefore, effectively preventing hematoma expansion after hemorrhage is important for treating intracranial hemorrhage. TA has shown an effect on reducing blood loss in many diseases. In previous studies, Tranexamic acid was tested in 2325 patients with intracerebral haemorrhage within $^{8\mathsf{h}}$ of symptom onset in the TICH-2 trial, assessed the effects of tranexamic acid for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage and showed a slight but statistically significant reduction in early death but not in functional status and total mortality by day 90 [18]. Recently, the STOP-AUST-2 trial, an international, randomized placebo-controlled trial with 100 participants from three countries, does not provide evidence that tranexamic acid prevents intracerebral haemorrhage growth, although the treatment was safe with no increase in thromboembolic complications [4, 19]. However, the efficacy of tranexamic acid in reducing mortality and disability, and its safety in terms of complications during treatment of intracranial hemorrhage remains controversial. Considering these inconsistent effects, we therefore conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the influence of tranexamic acid for intracranial hemorrhage.

# Study selection and data collection  

The inclusion criteria: (a) Type of study: RCT, Cohort studies and Case-control study; (b) Language restriction: only English;(c) Participating patients: patients with ICH; (d) Intervention: tranexamic acid (TA), placebo or usual care; (e) Outcomes: hematoma expansion rate and hematoma volume change from baseline.  

Two reviewers independently evaluated study records from the reference list and electronic database based on the eligibility criteria previously mentioned. And through the discussion or the third evaluator to help resolve the differences, if the data is missing, contact the author of the literature as far as possible to obtain relevant information. After determining the studies included in the meta-analysis, two reviewers independently and in duplicate extracted information from each included trial according to our protocol. The extracted variables include Study type, sample size, age, MRS, Mortality, TA admission time, hematoma expansion rate, and hematoma volume change from baseline. # Quality evaluation of the literature  

Jadad scale was used to evaluate literature quality[24]. The quality of literature was evaluated with reference to the Jadad scale quality evaluation standard. (1) Method of generating a random grouping sequence: 2 points for a sequence generated by a computer-generated random grouping sequence or by a random number table method; 1 point for a random assignment mentioned in the trial, but not given in the paper; semi-randomized or quasi-randomized trial, which refers to the method of alternating allocation of cases, such as admission order, date of birth single and double sign, 0 point. (2) Randomization of concealment: distribution schemes controlled by a medical centre or pharmacy, use of numbered containers, present Field computer control, using sealed opaque envelopes or other methods that make it impossible for clinicians or subjects to predict the allocation of sequences, 2 points; only indicating the use of random digital tables or other random allocation schemes, 1 point; alternating allocation, series numbers, series coded envelopes, and any measures that do not prevent grouping predictability or do not use randomization hiding, 0 points. (3) Double blind method: describe the specific method of double blind implementation and are considered appropriate, e.g. a completely consistent placebo, 2 points; reference to double blind but inappropriate methods in the literature, 1 point; no reference to blind method, 0 points in the literature. (4) Exit and missing visit: on withdrawal and The number of cases lost and the reasons for withdrawal were described in detail, 1; no mention of withdrawal or missing, 0. The total score was 7, the score ${\ge}4$ was high quality literature, the score ${<}4$ was low quality literature, and the high quality clinical research included in this study was less, so it was included in the literature with Jadad scale score $^{\ge3}$ .  

# Methodology for statistical analysis  

Revman (Version 5.3) software is used for Meta analysis. The counting data with original research data are represented by Mantel-Haensze (M-H) model with ratio (OR); if there is no original research data, the OR value provided by the author of the literature is used, and if the literature only provides the correlation coefficient, It is converted to OR value by existing methods. The measurement data are expressed as mean difference (MD), interval estimation is expressed as $95\%$ CI, and the test level of effect quantity is $\mathtt{a}=0.05$ . Heterogeneity between studies was tested by $\mathsf{X}^{2}$ test and heterogeneity was quantitatively judged Using $|2$ test, when $p>0.100$ and $1^{2}{\le}50.000\%_{\cdot}$ , there is no heterogeneity, the fixed effect model is used to analyze the combined effect. When $p{\leq}0.100$ and $1^{2}{>}50.000\%,$ there is heterogeneity, the source of heterogeneity is analyzed, and the combined effect analysis is carried out by random effect model. The stability of the Meta analysis results was evaluated by sensitivity test: the fixed effect model and the random effect model were transformed $:p\leq0.100$ and $1^{2}>50.000\%$ were fixed effect model, $p{>}0.100$ and $1^{2}{\le}50.000\%$ were random effect model), and the RR value transformation ratio (OR) value is recalculated $95\%$ . The results of the above conversion show that the Meta analysis results are stable and vice versa. The publication bias was detected by Egger method, with $p>0.05$ as no publication bias [25, 26].

We identified 412 titles and abstracts through PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library (Figure 1). # Meta analysis results  

Hematoma expansion: There were 5 studies in 2808 patients, and there was no heterogeneity between the studies $\left(\mathtt{p}=0.48\right.\mathrm{.}$ , $1^{2}{=}0\%\cdot$ ), so the fixed effect model was used to analyze the combined effect. The results showed that Patients in TA group had a significantly lower rate of hematoma expansion ( $:\mathsf{OR}=0.81\$ , $95\%\mathsf{C}$ : $0.68\sim0.97;\mathsf{p}=0.02,$ Figure 2).  

Hematoma volume change: There were 4 studies in 2479 patients, and there was no heterogeneity between the studies $(\mathsf{p}=0.9,\mathsf{l}^{2}{=}0\%)$ , so the fixed effect model was used to analyze the combined effect. The results showed that TA was seemed to reduce the hematoma volume change from baseline compared with placebo $(\mathsf{MD}=-1.06,$ $95\%$ CI: $-2.02\sim0.09_{.}$ $\uprho=0.03$ Figure 3).  

Modified Rankin Scale score(MRS) at 90 days at 0–3: There were 3 studies in 2431 patients, and there was no heterogeneity between the studies $(\mathtt{p}{}=0.68\$ , $1^{2}{=}0\%$ ), so the fixed effect model was used to analyze the combined effect. The results showed that Patients in TA group was no statistically significant good outcome(MRS 0-3 )at 90 days $\mathrm{^{\prime}OR}=1.06_{i}$ $95\%\mathsf{C}$ : $0.89\sim1.26;\mathsf{p}=0.51$ , Figure 4).  

Mortality within 90 days: There were 3 studies in 2431 patients, and there was no heterogeneity between the studies $(\mathsf{p}=0.46,$ $1^{2}{=}0\%;$ , so the fixed effect model was used to analyze the combined effect. The results showed no significant difference in mortality between the two groups within 90 days( $\mathsf{OR}=1.14\$ $95\%\mathsf{C}$ : $0.73\sim1.77;\mathsf{p}=0.58,$ , Figure 5).  

Major thromboembolic events: There were 3 studies in 2431 patients, and there was no heterogeneity between the studies $(\mathsf{p}=0.80\$ $1^{2}{=}0\%;$ ).so the fixed effect model was used to analyze the combined effect. The results showed no significant difference in major thromboembolic events between the two groups within 90 days $\mathrm{OR}=1.04\$ , $95\%\mathrm{C}$ : $0.67\sim1.62;\mathsf{p}=0.87\$ , Figure 6).

# Discussion  

Tranexamic acid is an antifibrinolytic agent, which can compete with lysine residues on the surface of fibrin, blocks the interaction of plasminogen and plasmin to inhibit the fibrinolysis and consequently stabilize the clot [8, 9].Tranexamic acid is a promising haemostatic agent given its efficacy in reducing bleeding rates in surgical and trauma patients [7, 9]. And, Tranexamic acid was reported to reduce the growth of hemorrhagic lesion [7, 27]. However, there is study does not provide evidence that tranexamic acid prevents intracerebral haemorrhage growth.  

We included 4 randomized controlled trials and 1 retrospective case series conducted a meta-analysis. Arumugam et al. first randomized controlled teste tranexamic acid in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage in a small Tranexamic acid was Malaysian trial $(n=30)$ [23] and the two TICH trials [18, 22]. Australia, Finland, and Taiwan did a prospective, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, investigator-led, phase 2 trial at 13 stroke centres in 2020(STOP-AUST )[4]. The principal finding of which was that tranexamic acid did not improve good outcome(MRS 0-3) by day 90. The poor functional outcomes did not improve, but tranexamic acid did appear to decrease the incidence of hematoma expansion and change in volume from baseline. The risk of a single ischemic event, such as ischemic stroke, DVT, and PE, was similar between the groups.  

Our meta-analysis suggested that the poor functional outcomes did not improve, but tranexamic acid may still benefit those with poor prognostic factors in large haematoma .In acute trauma, tranexamic acid has a strong time to treatment interaction, with earlier treatment being better, preferably within 1 h of trauma, with treatment effect possibly lost beyond 3 h. The optimal dosing of tranexamic acid needs to be determined as well. After intravenous administration, tranexamic acid reaches peak concentration almost immediately and has an onset of action within a few minutes. It has a short plasma elimination half-life of 3 h. The tissue half-life is $17\mathsf{h}$ due to low plasma protein binding $(3\%)$ and wider distribution in the tissue compartment. Repeated dosing may result in higher concentration and accumulation in the brain. TICH, TICH-2, and STOP-AUST trials administer intravenous tranexamic acid at the same dose similar to the Arumugam trial (1 g bolus, 1 g infusion over 8 h).It is not known whether more frequent dosing will result in longer accumulation in the target organ or whether this will result in better outcome or more adverse events. At the same time, we noted that of the ongoing trials, the phase-III trial was more inclusive, include TRIAGE, TICH-NOAC, NOR-ICH and EsICH.  

Our meta-analysis suggested that tranexamic acid was associated with reduced growth of hemorrhagic mass for cerebral hemorrhage. Differences in hematoma volume changes may be influenced by CT scan time, different measurements of volume, other diagnostic criteria, and research methods. Although TA can inhibit hematoma expansion in some patients, whether it can be applied to all people still lacks sufficient evidence. Because most patients do not have hematoma enlargement, they cannot benefit from TA, but will have a higher risk of thromboembolic events. Therefore, choosing a more appropriate patient TA is more critical.  

The question remains as to why carbamate significantly reduces the incidence of brain HE without improving functional outcomes and mortality in patients. One possibility is that the role of tranexamic acid in reducing hematoma volume is insufficient to improve functional outcomes from cerebral hemorrhage in these populations. A second possibility is that the potential benefits of carbamate will be offset by an increase in combined ischemic events. Further, the total number of patients included in our meta-analysis was relatively small, large-scale randomized controlled trials are still lacking.  

This study comprehensively screened and evaluated the treatment of ICH with tranexamic acid, and carried out systematic analysis to obtain a more comprehensive understanding. However, it is still affected by many factors, such as the quality of the study, so the conclusion has some limitations. First, there may be some deviations in the results due to the large gap in the sample size of the selected study. For example, TICH-2 trial is a large population study, while several other studies have a small sample size. Second, some unpublished and missing data may lead bias to the pooled effect.  

# Conclusions  

Treatment with tranexamic acid could reduce hematoma expansion in intracerebral hemorrhage, and the treatment was safe with no increase in thromboembolic complications. But showed no notable impact on good functional outcomes and mortality.  

A larger sample size of the trials of tranexamic acid is needed in the future, along with simpler recruitment methods and earlier treatment windows.
