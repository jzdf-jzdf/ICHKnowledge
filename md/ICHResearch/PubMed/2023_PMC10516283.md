# Abstract  

# Background and Objectives  

IV thrombolysis (IVT) for suspected ischemic stroke in patients with intracranial neoplasms is off-label. However, data on risks of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) are scarce.  

# Methods  

In a multicenter registry-based analysis within the European Thrombolysis in Ischemic Stroke Patients (TRISP) collaboration, we assessed frequencies of symptomatic and fatal ICH after IVT for suspected ischemic stroke in patients with intracranial tumors by descriptive statistics and analyzed associations with clinical and imaging characteristics by binary logistic regression. Definition of symptomatic ICH was based on the clinical criteria of the European Cooperative Acute Stroke-II trial including hemorrhage at any site in cranial imaging and concurrent clinical deterioration.  

# Results  

Screening data of 21,289 patients from 14 centers, we identified 105 patients receiving IVT; among them were 29 patients $(28\%)$ with additional endovascular treatment, with suspected, that is, imaging-based, or histologically confirmed diagnosis of intracranial tumors. Among 104 patients with CT or MRI after IVT available, symptomatic and fatal ICH were observed in 9 and 4 patients ( $9\%$ and $4\%$ , respectively). Among 82 patients with suspected or confirmed meningioma, symptomatic and fatal ICH occurred in 6 and 3 patients $7\%$ and $4\%$ ), respectively. In 18 patients with intra-axial suspected or confirmed primary or secondary brain tumors, there was 1 symptomatic nonfatal ICH $(6\%)$ ). Of 4 patients with tumors of the pituitary region, 2 patients $\left(50\%\right)$ had symptomatic ICH including 1 fatal ICH $(25\%)$ . Tumor size was not associated with the occurrence of symptomatic ICH (odds ratio 2.8, $95\%$ CI 0.3–24.8, $p=0.34\$ ).  

# Discussion  

In our dataset from routine clinical care, we provide insights on the safety of IVT for suspected ischemic stroke in patients with intracranial tumors, a population that is commonly withheld

# Introduction  

IV thrombolysis (IVT) with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator for ischemic stroke is an effective treatment for patients with acute ischemic stroke improving neurologic outcome of stroke in randomized trials.1 However, for patients with intracranial neoplasms, the use of IVT for ischemic stroke is off-label.2 The guidelines of the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association report a low level of evidence for risk and benefit of IVT in patients with brain tumor mainly based on expert opinion suggesting IVT to be probably indicated in patients with intracranial extra-axial tumors such as meningiomas and advise against IVT in patients with intra-axial tumors due to potential harmful effects.3 Others consider active malignant intracranial tumors as contraindication for IVT and intracranial tumors in remission as a relative contraindication for IVT.4 However, these guidelines are mainly based on expert opinion and scarce data of case series in the literature. 5-12 Case reports and small retrospective single-center analyses may be subject to selection and publication bias with the risk of underreporting or overreporting of adverse events. Prognosis, treatment, and clinical course of intracranial tumors as classified by the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors of the $\mathrm{CNS}^{13}$ is highly heterogeneous depending on the respective entity. Therefore, we hypothesize that the safety profile of IVT including the risk of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) depends on the brain tumor characteristics. Particularly patients with brain tumors with altered blood-brain barrier integrity may be at an increased risk of ICH. Potential additional risk factors including treatment-related conditions such as prior radiotherapy of the brain could modify this risk.  

In previous studies, frequencies of symptomatic ICH were between $4\%$ and $6\%$ in patients receiving IVT for suspected ischemic stroke.14-16 Frequencies of any ICH after IVT distant from the ischemic area without ICH in the ischemic area were observed in $3\%$ of patients, with approximately half of them classified as symptomatic ICH, in a patient cohort with MRI available.17 The aim of this multicenter registry-based study was to assess the frequencies of symptomatic and fatal ICH after IVT, functional outcome after IVT, and clinical and imaging characteristics in patients with intracranial tumors.  

# Methods  

# Study Design and Study Population  

We performed a multicenter retrospective registry-based analysis within the European Thrombolysis in Ischemic Stroke Patients (TRISP) collaboration collecting data of patients admitted for suspected acute ischemic stroke.18,19 Fourteen centers participated in this study (eTable 1, links. lww.com/WNL/C990). Patients were included if they received treatment with IVT with or without additional endovascular treatment (EVT) or if they received EVT without previous IVT but with use of intraarterial thrombolysis (IAT) and if any intracranial tumor was diagnosed before stroke or during stroke including the time of follow-up. The variable of an intracranial tumor or a history of cancer was not a prespecified variable of the preexisting registry. Strategies of database screening for patient identification varied across centers and included individual match with patient records, keyword search of registry data, and/or search on available data on history of malignancy.  

# Definition of Variables  

Predefined variables were collected by the sites with a standardized form as previously described in patients admitted for suspected ischemic stroke and registered in in-hospital prospective stroke registries.20,21 Baseline characteristics included demographic data, cardiovascular risk factors, previous medication, and clinical data at admission for suspected ischemic stroke including NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) as a measure for stroke severity on a scale of 0 to a maximum of 42 points, blood pressure, international normalized ratio (INR), blood count, and functional independence before stroke as defined as a modified Rankin scale (mRS) score from 0 to 2. The time of symptom onset to IVT was defined by the time interval between the first symptom onset if witnessed or last seen normal in case of unknown onset to the application of IVT. As complications associated with EVT were defined as occurrence of any of the following events during EVT: vessel perforation, vasospasm, dissection, subarachnoid or ICH, device detachment, embolization to other or distal territory, accesssite complications, and early reocclusion. Additional predefined variables regarding patient characteristics and imaging of intracranial neoplasia were collected by the sites based on the medical records. For patients diagnosed with an intracranial tumor by imaging only, that is, without histologic confirmation, the suspected etiology was based on local radiographic reports and/or local review of imaging data and stratified by localization, extra-axial for suspected meningioma and intra-axial for primary brain tumors and secondary brain tumors (brain metastasis). Patients with leptomeningeal metastases were grouped together with those patients having brain metastasis. Histopathologic tumor diagnosis, if available, was based on local records according to the WHO classification of brain tumors valid during tumor diagnosis. Imaging data for characterization of the CNS tumor lesion were locally assessed by the centers and were preferentially based on MRI or on CT imaging if no magnetic resonance data were available. Tumor size was defined as the product of the largest bidimensional diameters of the lesion with the sum of the products of each lesion in case of multifocal disease. Given the limitations of post hoc imaging data collection with heterogeneous imaging protocols lacking dedicated postcontrast imaging in some cases in the context of acute ischemic stroke, we decided to use any largest size of the lesion including potential tumor edema using the imaging modality and sequences as available also including noncontrast CT-based measurements and T2/fluidattenuated inversion recovery sequences. For descriptive data presentation, tumor lesions were arbitrarily categorized for size as follows: ${<}1,{\geq}1$ and ${<}10$ , and $\ge10~\mathrm{cm}^{2}$ ; for statistical comparisons as binary categories ${<}1$ or $\geq1~\mathrm{cm}^{2}$ . Susceptibilityweighted imaging (SWI) or $\mathrm{T}2^{*}$ imaging sequences were used for the assessment of microbleeds.  

Occurrence of hemorrhage after IVT at any site intracranially was assessed by a retrospective review of available imaging including CT or MRI and of medical records and subclassified as either symptomatic ICH or as ICH not fulfilling the criteria for symptomatic ICH. The definition of symptomatic ICH was based on the clinical part of the European-Australasian Acute Stroke Study-II criteria,22 that is, documentation of clinical deterioration or signs of clinical worsening (e.g., drowsiness, increase of hemiparesis) or causing a decrease in the NIHSS score of 4 or more points. Among patients with symptomatic ICH, fatal ICH was recorded if a patient’s death was related to ICH. We further subclassified symptomatic ICH or ICH not fulfilling the criteria for symptomatic ICH by localization into intratumoral hemorrhage and hemorrhage in any other site of the brain. Intratumoral hemorrhage was defined as hemorrhage within the area of the tumor lesion as defined earlier. Further outcome parameters included NIHSS at 24 hours with assignment of the maximum NIHSS of 42 for deceased patients, in-hospital mortality referring to the hospitalization for acute ischemic stroke, and mRS score at 3 months defining poor functional outcome with a score from 3 to 6.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Continuous variables were described by the median and interquartile range and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons of continuous variables were performed with the Mann-Whitney $U$ test and those for categorical variables with the $\chi^{2}$ test. Binary univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association of parameters with the occurrence of symptomatic ICH. $\mathrm{~A~}p$ value of $_{<0.05}$ was considered as statistically significant. The percentages of variables with missing data were indicated. For statistical comparisons and regression analyses, patients with missing data for the respective parameters were excluded. All calculations were performed using SPSS version 26. The study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational studies.  

# Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents  

This study was conducted with consent of the institutional review board, the cantonal ethics committee of Zurich, Switzerland, available (KEK-ZH 2014-0304). Additional local ethical approvals differed across centers and were obtained if required. Patient consent was available or not needed as applicable.  

# Data Availability  

Anonymized data not published within the article will be shared on reasonable request from any qualified investigator if it is in line with the requirements of the institutional review board approval.  

# Results  

# Study Population  

The process of patient identification is summarized in the Figure. On screening data of 21,289 patients from 14 TRISP centers, we identified 106 patients with CNS tumors fulfilling the inclusion criteria, that is, admission for suspected acute ischemic stroke treated with IVT with or without EVT or treated with IAT during EVT if no prior IVT (eTable 1, links. lww.com/WNL/C990). There was only 1 patient with IAT without prior IVT. In this patient with suspected brain metastasis of lung cancer, no ICH was observed. Due to the low patient number in this subgroup, we excluded this single patient with IAT without prior IVT from further analyses.  

Baseline characteristics of the remaining 105 patients with intracranial tumors who received IVT are summarized in Table 1. There were 83 patients $(79\%)$ with suspected or confirmed meningioma, 4 patients $(4\%)$ with tumors of the pituitary region, and 18 patients $(17\%)$ with intra-axial primary or secondary brain tumors. No patient had prior anticoagulants. All but 1 patient received full-dose IVT. In this patient, IVT was prematurely stopped because of clinical improvement. In 29 patients $(28\%)$ , subsequent EVT was initiated after IVT with 9 patients ( $31\%$ of patients with EVT) experiencing EVT-associated complications (Table 1).  

# Patient Characteristics per Tumor Entity and Imaging  

We further characterized the patients stratified by suspected or confirmed tumor entity including information on tumor diagnosis including previous tumor-specific treatment and imaging features including tumor size (Tables 2–4). The presence of an intracranial tumor was known at decision for IVT in 52 patients $(63\%)$ with meningioma, 2 patients $\left(50\%\right)$ with tumors of the pituitary region, and 4 patients $(22\%)$ with intra-axial tumors. Diagnostic imaging in the context of stroke including follow-up of stroke led to a de novo tumor diagnosis in 50 patients $(60\%)$ with meningioma, all 4 patients with tumors of the pituitary region, and 4 patients $(22\%)$ with intra-axial tumors. Among the 18 patients with intra-axial tumors (Table 4), 6 patients had secondary brain tumors; among them, 5 patients with imaging-based diagnosis of multifocal brain metastasis of lung cancer and 1 patient with disseminated leptomeningeal disease of breast cancer. Among patients with suspected or confirmed primary brain tumors, 6 patients had histologically confirmed glioblastoma and 3 patients had astrocytoma WHO grades 2 and 3, and in 3 patients with imaging-based diagnosis, suspected entities included ependymoma, ganglioglioma, and lower-grade glioma. Regarding tumor size, in all groups, that is, in patients with (presumed) meningioma, with tumors of the pituitary region, and with intra-axial tumors, more than half of the patients had lesion(s) of $\geq1~\mathrm{cm}^{2}$ (Tables 2–4). We aimed to additionally characterize the frequency of microbleeds; however, the overall availability of SWI or $\mathrm{T}2^{*}$ magnetic resonance sequences was low. For those patients with adequate imaging available, in 4 of 34 patients $(12\%)$ with (presumed) meningioma, 5 or more microbleeds were observed, while for patients with tumors of the pituitary region and intra-axial tumor manifestations, no patient had 5 or more microbleeds (Tables 2–4).

# Outcome After IVT  

Next, we analyzed patient outcome after IVT including frequencies of ICH and in-hospital death of any cause and functional outcome at 3 months stratified by tumor entity (Table 5). In 104 of 105 patients, at least 1 imaging by CT or MRI for assessment of ICH was available within 36 hours after IVT. One patient with meningioma and with occlusion of the basilar artery, with an NIHSS of 39 points before IVT and EVT, and with myocardial infarction was switched to palliative care and therefore did not receive CT or MRI after IVT. Because no sufficient imaging data for assessment of ICH were available for this patient, we excluded this patient from all analyses regarding ICH.  

Any ICH after IVT was documented in 28 patients $(27\%)$ with intracranial tumors; among them were 9 and 4 patients with symptomatic and fatal ICH $9\%$ and $4\%$ ), respectively. Among 82 evaluable patients with suspected or confirmed meningioma, 6 and 3 patients $7\%$ and $4\%$ ) experienced symptomatic and fatal ICH, respectively. After exclusion of 3 patients with complete resection of meningioma before stroke and without documentation of residual tumor, the frequency of symptomatic and fatal hemorrhage was similar with 6 and 3 of 79 patients ( $8\%$ and $4\%$ ), respectively. All patients received IVT for suspected ischemic stroke; however, we identified 10 patients $(10\%)$ with final diagnosis of a stroke mimic at later workup instead of confirmation of ischemic stroke; among them were 1 patient diagnosed with migraine with aura, 1 patient with an epileptic seizure, and 8 patients with post hoc diagnosis of an intracranial tumor instead of ischemic stroke. Two patients with post hoc diagnosis of an intracranial tumor had intratumoral ICH, one of them symptomatic.  

# Characteristics of Patients With Intracranial Tumors and Symptomatic ICH After IVT  

To explore potential risk factors of symptomatic ICH among patients with intracranial brain tumors, we compared characteristics of patients with symptomatic ICH with those of patients without ICH where applicable (Table 6). Although potentially limited by sample size, we observed no differences regarding age, sex, cardiovascular risk factors, time of symptom onset to IVT, tumor size, or presence of multifocal tumor burden. Patients with IVT plus initiation of EVT were overrepresented in the group with symptomatic ICH $\left(p=0.043\right)$ . Frequencies of EVT-associated complications were not different between patients with and without symptomatic ICH after IVT. No patient with symptomatic ICH had received previous treatment with surgery, brain radiotherapy, or systemic tumor-specific treatment (Table 6). Next, we assessed associations of candidate parameters including age, sex, NIHSS before IVT, time of symptom onset to IVT, prior antiplatelets, acute treatment with IVT with or without EVT, and tumor size with occurrence of symptomatic ICH after IVT by univariable binary logistic regression analysis. None of these variables showed a significant association with occurrence of symptomatic ICH (eTable 2, links.lww.com/WNL/C991). To further test the hypothesis that tumor size is associated with occurrence of ICH, we performed multivariable binary regression analysis adjusted for age and sex as potential confounders and for acute stroke treatment in a subgroup of patients with residual tumor and information on tumor size available. Comparable with univariable analysis, tumor size was not associated with occurrence of symptomatic ICH also on adjusted analysis (eTable 2).  

# Discussion  

In this multicenter registry-based study, we collected a large cohort of 105 patients with intracranial tumors who underwent IVT for ischemic stroke, providing detailed clinical and imaging characteristics (Tables 1–4). Among 104 patients with CT or MRI after IVT available, we observed rates of symptomatic and fatal ICH after IVT of $9\%$ and $4\%$ , respectively. Stratified by tumor entity, frequencies were $7\%$ and $4\%$ in patients with suspected or confirmed meningioma, $50\%$ and $25\%$ in patients with tumors of the pituitary region, and $6\%$ and $0\%$ in patients with intra-axial tumors (Table 5). Tumor size as assessed in a subgroup of patients with residual tumor and information on tumor size available $(\mathtt{n}=94\$ ) was not associated with the risk of symptomatic ICH (eTable 2, links.lww.com/WNL/C991).

So far, there is only 1 larger study assessing the risk of ICH after IVT in patients with intracranial neoplasms, which was based on discharge data derived from the Nationwide Inpatient Sample of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project of the United States using codes of the International Classification of Disease. The authors identified 297 patients with benign brain tumors and 119 patients with malignant brain tumors not otherwise specified among 100,000 patients with ischemic stroke reporting a higher risk of ICH after IVT associated with malignant but not benign brain tumors.5 Notably, this study did not include patients with brain metastasis. Furthermore, no information on histopathologic or imaging characteristics and on clinical parameters before IVT or follow-up was provided. In this study, the overall rate of any ICH in the context of IVT was $5.7\%$ in patients with intracranial tumors; however, because there was no indication of how many of these ICH represented symptomatic or fatal ICH, interpretation is limited. Of interest, in this study, the percentages of patients with brain tumors receiving IVT increased from $0.3\%$ in 2002 to $1.4\%$ in 2011, suggesting undertreatment of patients with potential benefit of IVT in earlier years.5 Other studies reporting on the safety of IVT in patients with intracranial tumors are retrospective single-center series and case reports, mainly reporting data on meningiomas. The largest series of patients with stroke and meningiomas include 19 patients, $^{6}12$ patients,12 and 3 patients, each,7,11 r eporting an acceptable safety profile without ICH after IVT. In our study of 82 evaluable patients with meningioma, we observed symptomatic and fatal ICH in 6 and 3 patients $7\%$ and $4\%$ ), respectively (Table 5). There are only few data on IVT in patients with intra-axial primary brain tumors, especially gliomas in the literature. For patients with glioblastoma receiving IVT for suspected ischemic stroke, 1 patient experiencing symptomatic $\mathrm{{ICH}^{8}}$ was reported while in 3 patients, no ICH occurred.6,23,24 This is comparable with our observation of 1 symptomatic ICH of 6 patients $(17\%)$ with glioblastoma (Table 5). For other histologically confirmed intra-axial primary brain tumors, 1 patient with low-grade astrocytoma and 1 patient with recurrent oligodendroglioma WHO grade 3 were reported each without ICH after IVT,9,25 while in 1 case of a high-grade astrocytoma, ICH after IVT occurred.6 In our series, none of the patients with suspected or confirmed intra-axial primary brain tumors with exclusion of glioblastoma $(\mathrm{n}=6)$ ) experienced symptomatic ICH (Table 5). For brain metastasis, there is 1 patient without ICH after IVT reported in the literature, while in another case, fatal ICH occurred after partial dose of IVT.26,27 In our analysis of 6 patients with brain or leptomeningeal metastases, no symptomatic ICH occurred (Table 5). Some previous studies summarized the safety of IVT in patients with stroke mimics or in patients with off-label use of IVT; among them were single cases of brain tumors with overall favorable outcome. However, most reports lack details on tumor characteristics.11,25,28-30 A recent metaanalysis summarizing previous reports on patients receiving IVT for ischemic stroke and diagnosis of intracranial tumors indicated an overall rate of any ICH in 4 of 79 patients $(5.1\%)$ and symptomatic ICH in 3 of 79 patients $\left(3.8\%\right)$ . Notably, all 4 patients with ICH had malignant or metastatic brain tumors with limited further characterization of the specific entity. 27

However, although limited by the overall sample size, the relatively high rate of $4\%$ fatal ICH after IVT in patients with meningioma is striking. On the contrary, no fatal ICH occurred in patients with intra-axial tumors. Group comparison of patients with and without symptomatic ICH did not identify differences in demographics, distribution of risk factors, and clinical characteristics (Table 6). Patients with IVT plus initiation of EVT were overrepresented in the group with symptomatic ICH (Table 6); however, combined IVT plus EVT was not significantly associated with occurrence of symptomatic ICH (eTable 2, links.lww.com/WNL/C991), and frequencies of EVT-associated complications were not different between patients with and patients without symptomatic ICH after IVT (Table 6). Of note, we cannot exclude that combined IVT plus EVT may be still a risk factor of symptomatic ICH, also regarding the available sample size. Pretreatment characteristics in patients with diagnosis of the brain tumor before stroke were also collected, and although cerebral microbleeds as correlate for microvascular injury are common in patients receiving radiotherapy for brain tumors,32 no patient with symptomatic or fatal ICH had any pretreatment including radiotherapy (Table 6). Our hypothesis that tumor size may be a risk factor of symptomatic ICH was not confirmed (eTable 2). The type of tumor may play a more important role. Of interest, intratumoral hemorrhages were predominantly seen in intra-axial neoplasms, while the criteria for being symptomatic were only met in 1 patient with glioblastoma (Table 5). Although limited by overall patient numbers, the high rate of patients with poor outcome with an mRS score between 3 and 6 at 3 months in 3 of 4 patients $(75\%)$ with intratumoral hemorrhage indicates a potential negative impact of intratumoral hemorrhage on the clinical course of the underlying neoplastic disease.  

Differences in risk of ICH in different tumor types may depend on the level of blood-brain barrier disruption and degree of vascularization. Because in our dataset imaging modalities were not uniform and not all patients received postcontrast imaging with contrast enhancement as potential surrogate marker of a disrupted blood-brain barrier, we were not able to provide sufficient data proving this hypothesis. From the pathophysiologic point of view, ICH after IVT in patients with higher-grade gliomas may occur due to lysis of clots in leaky tumor vessels because higher-grade gliomas are known to secrete proangiogenic factors leading to dysfunctional tumor vessels with areas with vaso-occlusion and intravascular thrombosis.33 The risk of ICH in the context of therapeutic anticoagulation has been suggested to be higher in patients with gliomas compared with that in patients with brain metastasis in a meta-analysis.34  

Functional outcome of patients with tumors of the pituitary region or of patients with brain or leptomeningeal metastasis was poor. We acknowledge that functional outcome at 3 months in patients with brain or leptomeningeal metastasis could be related to the course of the malignancy itself, rather than to stroke-induced disability. For patients with meningioma or intra-axial primary brain tumors, functional outcome was comparable with that of previous analysis within the TRISP cooperation (Table 5).14-16,21  

The strengths of our study include the multicenter approach with a detailed characterization of clinical and imaging data of stroke patients with intracranial tumors after IVT. Compared with previous reports, we present the analysis of a large cohort of patients that is commonly excluded from clinical trials related to IVT and that does not routinely receive IVT in clinical practice neither. Especially in patients with intra-axial intracranial neoplasms including brain metastases, primary brain tumors, and tumors of the pituitary region, current knowledge on the safety of IVT is mainly based on case reports.  

One limitation of the study is that those patients with intracranial tumors treated with IVT may not be representative for the respective tumor entity in general because some patients may not receive IVT in case of a known intracranial neoplasm indicating potential selection bias of the treating physician. Of note, in some of patients, the intracranial neoplasm was known before IVT, which may also indicate individual risk and benefit estimations reflecting the complexity of decision-making toward IVT for ischemic stroke in patients with intracranial tumors. Further limitations include the retrospective character of the study, heterogeneity of imaging protocols and modalities, inclusion of both patients with IVT alone and combined IVT plus EVT, and limited statistical power due to the moderate sample size. In addition, there was no assessment of infarct volumes as a potential risk factor of ICH. We also did not have data on race/ ethnicity as potential risk factor of ICH because collecting of such data is commonly not foreseen in Europe. Women were overrepresented in our cohort probably because meningiomas as the most common brain tumors are more prevalent in female individuals.35 Furthermore, because the variable of an intracranial tumor was not a prespecified part of the preexisting registry and due to differences in database screening across centers, we cannot exclude that some patients with intracranial tumors were not identified within the local registries. In line with this, there was no dedicated control group without intracranial tumors; however, data from previous studies within the TRISP collaboration were available as a reference.  

We provide a dataset using data from routine clinical care assessing the frequencies of ICH after IVT for suspected ischemic stroke in patients with intracranial tumors, a patient population that is very unlikely to be subject to any future prospective clinical trial in the context of IVT. In our cohort, IVT for suspected ischemic stroke in patients with intracranial tumors including brain or leptomeningeal metastasis and nonglioblastoma primary brain tumors seemed to be relatively safe with frequencies of symptomatic ICH after IVT near the upper range of frequencies reported in previous studies of patients with ischemic stroke.14-16 Still, fatality of ICH in patients with meningioma seemed to be considerable, although generalization may be limited by the overall sample size. Furthermore, caution is warranted using IVT for acute ischemic stroke in patients with tumors of the pituitary region, given the high rates of ICH observed in our cohort. Our study may aid in risk-benefit estimations for individual treatment decisions in patients with ischemic stroke and intracranial tumors with potential benefit of IVT.
