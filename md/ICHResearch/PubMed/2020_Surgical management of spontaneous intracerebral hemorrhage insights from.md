# Surgical management of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage: insights from randomized controlled trials

# Abstract  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhages (ICH) are a major cause of neurologic morbidity and mortality. The optimal management strategy of ICH remains controversial. We examine the available randomized controlled trial (RCT) data regarding neurosurgical evacuation of ICHs. A systematic literature review on surgical evacuation of spontaneous ICHs was performed to identify pertinent RCT data published between 1980 and 2019. We identified five RCTs that assessed the clinical impact of evacuation of spontaneous ICHs. Data from two high-quality RCTs randomizing 1033 and 601 patients with spontaneous ICHs (Surgical Trial in Intracerebral Hemorrhage (STICH) I and II) (1) failed to demonstrate a significant clinical benefit of routine open surgical evacuation of spontaneous cortical ICHs and (2) reinforced the high morbidity and mortality associated with ICH. These trials were nonetheless limited by high $(>20\%)$ crossover from the medical to surgical arms. Data from three smaller RCTs on minimally invasive (stereotactic and endoscopic) surgical approaches randomizing 377, 242, and 100 patients with spontaneous ICHs suggest potential benefits relating to mortality and functional outcomes in patients with subcortical ICHs. While these RCTs do not clearly define the role of surgical resection for ICHs, they provide insights into opportunities for patient advocacy, clinical trial design, and future research studies. Ongoing studies building upon the potential for minimally invasive approaches for ICH evacuation may expand the surgical indications for ICH.

# Introduction  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhages (ICHs) account for approximately $20\%$ of all strokes and are associated with a higher risk of morbidity and mortality than other causes of stroke [1]. Available estimates suggest that nearly $60\%$ of  

ICH patients die within a year of disease onset, and $20\%$ of ICH patients will be incapacitated [2]. Prognostic factors that are associated with a more favorable clinical outcome in ICH patients include higher Glasgow coma scale (GCS) scores at presentation, younger age, supratentorial hemorrhage location, and lower ICH volume [3]. Conversely, a poor prognosis has been associated with hematoma expansion and significant peri-hematoma edema [2, 4–9].  

There has been significant interest to explore the clinical benefit of surgical intervention in ICH patients. The fundamental question in this regard involves the extent to which the irreversible neurologic injury at presentation contributes to functional outcome. The 2015 American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines reflect the current uncertainty in the field, recommending consideration for surgical evacuation in patients with cerebellar hemorrhage and neurological deterioration, brainstem compression and/or hydrocephalus from ventricular obstruction, or in select patients with life-threatening supratentorial ICHs [10].  

Here, we review the pertinent RCT data relating to the clinical impact of surgical evacuation of spontaneous ICHs.  

# Methods  

An exhaustive search of the modern English-language literature was performed using the electronic database Pubmed (MEDLINE; available at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ pubmed/) (years 1980–2019, cut off date January 1, 2019) and the search terms: intracranial/intracerebral/putaminal hemorrhage, surgery, and surgical procedures (see Supplemental Materials for full search details). Additional inclusion criteria built into this search included (1) primary investigative research as defined by a Bcomparative study^ or Brandomized controlled trial^ study design, (2) article published in the English language, and (3) involvement of human subjects. Three-hundred and eighty-nine articles were identified using this search methodology, with returned works screened by the first and senior authors. Articles that did not present high-quality RCT data (minimum sample sizes involving $\geq100$ ICH patients) with outcomes relating to the efficacy of surgical evacuation for ICH were excluded on manual review by the authors (Fig. 1). Five RCTs were identified using these criteria. A secondary interrogation of other available electronic databases (EMBASE and Cochrane) was performed and did not reveal additional eligible works.

# Results  

# Utility of stereotactic evacuation  

In 2004, Hattori et al. [11] reported a single-institutional experience (involving 5 affiliated hospitals) in which 242 spontaneous putaminal ICH patients were randomized to medical management $(n=121$ ) or stereotactic evacuation $(n=121$ ) (years 1998–2000). Inclusion criteria included (i) age $35-$ 85 years; (ii) arousability to weak or strong stimuli (criteria based on a grading scale by the Japanese Cooperative Study on Stroke Surgery (CSSG); CSSG grade II referred to patients who presented with eyes closed but opened to weak stimuli, and CSSG grade III referred to patients who presented with eyes closed but opened to strong stimuli); and (iii) interval between ictus and treatment $<24\mathrm{~h~}$ . No hematoma size cut off was specified, but the mean hematoma size of the series was approximately 40 ccs. Patients suffering from hematoma related to aneurysms, vascular malformations, neoplasms, or trauma were excluded, as were patients with bleeding disorders or those on anticoagulants. Primary endpoints were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS, with 0, 1, and 2 defined as functional independence, and 6 defined as death) at 1 year. No comments were available in the published report in terms of sample size calculation. Study description did not specify criteria for patient crossover and the data presented does not suggest that such crossover occurred. Baseline patient characteristics were comparable in the two randomized arms. At 1 year, the mortality of the surgical cohort was lower when compared to the medically managed cohort. For CSSG grade II patients, the mortality rate for the medically managed arm and surgical arm were $11.4\%$ and $4.3\%$ , respectively. The associated proportions of patients who remained functionally dependent were $42.9\%$ and $48.6\%$ . These differences did not reach statistical significance. For CSSG grade III patients, the mortality rate for the medically managed arm and surgical arm were $23.5\%$ and $11.8\%$ , respectively $(p<0.05)$ . The associated proportions of patients who remained functionally independent were $21.6\%$ and $47.1\%$ $(p<0.05)$ .

Using the Cochrane risk of bias tool [16], we assessed the quality of this data. The authors used a thorough randomization protocol, resulting in a low risk of selection bias. No significant differences were noted in baseline characteristics between groups. Patient blinding was not possible due to the invasive nature of surgical intervention; however, given the low GCS (poor neurologic status) of the study population, it is unlikely that the patient was aware of the intervention; as such, influence of outcome related to a Bplacebo effect^ was also unlikely. The risk of detection bias was low as the clinician evaluator was blinded to treatment allocation group. Finally, there is no evidence of selective reporting, with all randomized patients included in the final dataset.  

In 2009, Wang et al. [12] published a multi-institutional study that randomized 377 patients to stereotactic hematoma aspiration $(n=195)$ or medical management $(n=182$ ) (years 2003–2004). Inclusion criteria included (i) patient age 40–75, (ii) neurologic examination showing motor strength 0–3 for any limb and $\mathrm{GCS}>9$ , (iii) basal ganglia hematoma $^{25-}$ 40 ccs, and (iv) interval between ictus and presentation $<$ $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ . Patients with coagulopathies; infection; previous strokes; or underlying vascular lesions, tumors, or trauma were excluded. The primary outcome was death or dependency (defined as $\mathrm{mRS}>2$ ) 90 days after presentation. Secondary outcomes were day $14~\mathrm{mRS}$ , and day $90~\mathrm{mRS}$ and Barthel Index (BI). No comments were available in the published report in terms of sample size calculation. Study description did not specify criteria for patient crossover and the data presented does not suggest that such crossover occurred. Patient characteristics were comparable between study arms, with the exception that patients in the surgical group exhibited lowered GCS on presentation $(p=0.01)$ . The authors reported no significant difference in 90-day mortality between the surgical and the medical arms (6.7 and $8.8\%$ , respectively, $p{=}0.44,$ ). At 90 days, the proportion of dependent survivors in the surgical group was significantly lower compared to the medical group $(40.9\%$ versus $63\%$ , $p<0.01$ ). These findings were recapitulated by analysis of the secondary endpoints. The surgical group had significantly better day $14\mathrm{mRS}$ $(p=0.02)$ and day $90{\mathrm{~BI}}$ $(p<0.01)$ .  

Assessing this study with the Cochrane risk of bias tool [16], the authors used a sound randomization protocol, leaving a low risk of selection bias. The only significant difference in baseline characteristics between groups was a lower GCS in the surgical arm. Nonetheless, if this difference were to affect the data, it would likely artificially decrease any beneficial treatment effects. Patient blinding was not possible due to the invasive nature of surgical intervention. However, given the altered neurologic status of the study population, a Bplacebo effect^ unlikely contributes to the reported outcomes. There is a risk of detection bias as the clinicians evaluating patient outcomes were not blinded. They were nonetheless not the original treating physicians. Finally, patient attrition and exclusion events are adequately documented, and outcome reporting appears thorough.  

# Utility of endoscopic evacuation  

In 1989, Auer et al. [13] published a single-institutional RCT on 100 spontaneous ICH patients that were randomized to either medical management $(n=50)$ ) or neuro-endoscopic evacuation $(n=50$ ) (years 1983–1986). Patients with subcortical, putaminal, or thalamic ICHs were included. Other inclusion criteria included (i) patient age of 30–80 years, (ii) neurologic deficit or altered level of consciousness, (iii) hematoma $>10$ ccs, (iv) interval between ictus and intervention $<$ $48\mathrm{~h~}$ , and (v) patients considered fit for surgery. Patients suffering from hematomas related to aneurysms, vascular malformations, neoplasms, or trauma were excluded. Primary endpoints included functional independence (assessed by the surgeon) and mortality at 6 months. No comments were available in the published report in terms of sample size calculation. Baseline patient characteristics were comparable in the two randomized arms. Study description did not specify criteria for patient crossover and the data presented does not suggest that such crossover occurred. At 6 months, the mortality of the surgical cohort was lower compared to the medically managed cohort ( $42\%$ versus $70\%$ , $p<0.01\$ . The proportion of patients who were functionally independent was higher in the surgical arm $40\%$ versus $25\%$ , $p<0.01\$ ). Post hoc subgroup analyses suggest the clinical benefit of ICH evacuation was predominantly observed in patients $<60$ years old with hematomas $<50~\mathrm{ml}$ , and with subcortical ICHs (relative to putaminal or thalamic ICHs).  

The Cochrane risk of bias tool was used to assess the data quality from this study [16]. The risk of selection bias is low despite a randomization protocol that is based on subgroups of patient characteristics, and there were no significant differences noted in baseline characteristics between groups. Patient blinding was not possible due to the surgical intervention performed. The risk of detection bias is present, as the authors do not report that the clinician evaluator was blinded to treatment allocation group. There is no evidence of selective reporting, as all randomized patients are included in the final dataset and patient exclusion events are adequately documented.  

# Utility of surgical evacuation using any technique  

In 2005, Mendelow et al. [14] published the first of two landmark studies termed Surgical Trial in Intracerebral Hemorrhage (STICH). This international, multi-institutional study randomized 1033 patients to surgical evacuation $\left(n=\begin{array}{ll}\end{array}\right)$ 503) or medical therapy $(n=530$ ) (years 1995–2003). Inclusion criteria included (i) $\operatorname{GCS}\geq5$ , (ii) supratentorial hematoma $>2~\mathrm{cm}$ in diameter $\mathord{\left[\sim\infty\right.}4$ ccs assuming spherical volume), (iii) interval between ictus and assessment $<72\mathrm{~h~}$ and surgery performed within $^{241}$ of randomization, and (iv) the surgeon was uncertain as to the benefit of surgical intervention. No age range was mandated, but the mean age of the study population was 62 years, with an age range of 52 to 70 years. Of the patients randomized to surgery, the surgeon determined the specific operative strategy. Patients with underlying vascular lesions, tumors, trauma, or hemorrhage in the cerebellum or brainstem were excluded. The primary outcome was 6-month favorable Glasgow outcome score (GOS, favorable defined as GOS of 4–5), adjusted for the initial condition of the patient based on a score weighted by initial GCS, age, and hematoma volume [17]. Secondary outcomes included mortality and prognosis-based analyses using a dichotomized BI and mRS. The authors calculated that a sample of 1000 was sufficient to detect a $10\%$ improvement in favorable clinical outcome in the surgical arm if $40\%$ of the medically managed patients exhibited such an outcome (with an $\alpha$ of 0.05 and $\beta$ of 0.8) and crossover was $<25\%$ . There were no significant differences in baseline patient characteristics across groups. Of the patients who were randomized to medical treatment, $26\%$ underwent surgery due to neurologic deterioration. Intent-to-treat analysis was performed. The authors reported no significant difference in the primary outcome of favorable GOS at 6 months with surgery ( $26\%$ surgical versus $24\%$ medical, $p{=}0.4\$ ). Secondary outcomes were also non-significant. Of note, subgroup analysis revealed that unfavorable outcomes were less likely after surgery for patients with $\mathrm{ICHs}<1$ cm from the cortical surface ( $66\%$ versus $73\%$ , $p=0.02,$ ).  

The data quality of this study was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool [16]. The authors used an established randomization protocol, resulting in a low risk of selection bias. There were no significant differences noted in baseline characteristics between groups. Patient blinding was not possible due to the nature of the surgical intervention performed. Nonetheless, this is unlikely to have influenced outcomes given the poor neurologic status of study patients. The risk of detection bias is present, as the authors do not report that the outcome evaluator was blinded to treatment allocation group. There is, however, no evidence of selective reporting, and patient attrition and exclusion events are adequately documented.  

In 2013, Mendelow et al. [15] published a second STICH study (STICH II). STICH II was an international, multiinstitutional trial that randomized 601 patients with spontaneous ICHs to surgical evacuation $(n=307)$ ) or medical management $n=294.$ ) (years 2007–2012). Since STICH suggested a potential benefit of surgical evacuation of ICHs in patients with hematomas located $<1$ cm from the cortex, STICH II was designed to confirm this hypothesis. Inclusion criteria included (i) GCS motor score $>5$ and GCS eye score $>2$ , (ii) supratentorial hematoma located $<1$ cm from the surface and 10–100 cc in volume, (iii) interval between ictus and assessment $<48\mathrm{~h~}$ and surgery performed within $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ of randomization. No age range was mandated, but the mean age of the study population was 65 years with an age range of 55 to 74 years. Of the patients randomized to surgery, the surgeon decided on the specific operative technique. Patients with underlying vascular lesions, tumors, trauma, or hemorrhage in the cerebellum/brainstem/thalamus/basal ganglia were excluded. The primary outcome was 6-month favorable extended Glasgow outcome scale (GOSE), adjusted for the initial condition of the patient based on a score weighted by initial GCS, age, and hematoma volume [18]. Secondary outcomes included mortality, time to death, prognosis-dichotomized Rankin and GOSE, and Rankin and EuroQoL. The authors calculated that a sample size of 600 patients was sufficient to detect a $12\%$ improvement in favorable clinical outcome in the surgical arm if $37\%$ of the medically managed patients exhibited such an outcome (with an $\alpha$ of 0.05 and $\beta$ of 0.8) and crossover was $<25\%$ . There were no significant differences in baseline patient characteristics across groups. Of the patients who were randomized to medical treatment, $21\%$ underwent surgery due to neurologic deterioration. Intent-totreat analysis was performed. In terms of the primary endpoint, there were no significant differences when comparing surgical and medical arms. Fifty-nine percent of the surgical arm and $72\%$ of the medical arm had unfavorable outcomes $(p=0.367)$ . In terms of the secondary endpoints, the authors reported non-significant trends toward improved outcomes within the surgical arm: 6-month mortality ( $18\%$ versus $24\%$ , $p=0.09,$ , prognosis-based favorable GOSE ( $41\%$ versus $38\%$ , $p=0.36,$ , and prognosis-based favorable Rankin score ( $47\%$ versus $43\%$ , $p{=}0.46,$ ).  

Data quality was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool [16]. There is a low risk of selection bias from acceptable randomization, and there were no significant differences in baseline patient characteristics. Patient blinding was not possible, although this is unlikely to have influenced outcomes given the poor neurologic status of the study population. The risk of detection bias is low, as the outcome evaluator was blinded to the treatment allocation group. There is no evidence of selective reporting, and patient attrition and exclusion events are adequately documented.  

# Discussion  

The 2015 AHA guidelines for ICH evacuation are published and available [10]. The intent of this article is not to supplant these recommendations. Instead, we wish to augment the guidelines by providing the following interpretations of the RCT literature that may aid in patient advocacy and clinical management.  

Our review of the RCT literature suggests the following. First, across studies, approximately half of ICH patients incurred deficits that rendered them dependent on others for daily activities (GOSE worse than upper severe disability), irrespective of intervention. The available literature suggests that many such patients suffer severe depression [19] and regret having undergone a life-saving neurosurgical procedure [20]. In this context, the availability of advanced directives at the time of presentation would afford an opportunity to select a treatment that best conforms to the patient’s wishes. From a patient advocacy perspective, considerations should be given for advance directive discussions prior to interventions that increase risk for ICH, such as the initiation of Coumadin therapy [21]. From a preventive public health perspective, priority should be directed toward the development of anticoagulation therapies that minimize the risk of ICH given the high likelihood of devastating neurologic consequences. The observation that apixaban is associated with a lower risk of ICH relative to Coumadin is a major step in this regard [22].  

Second, a comparison of the two STICH studies highlights the potential hazards associated with post hoc analyses of RCTs. For example, while STICH I demonstrates comparable outcomes between surgical and medical arms based on primary endpoints, post hoc analysis indicated that patients with hematomas located $<1$ cm from the cortex may benefit from surgical evacuation. STICH II was designed specifically to test this hypothesis in a statistically rigorous manner, accounting for all pertinent issues including patient crossover. STICH II, however, did not yield evidence of the expected benefit. The failure of STICH II to confirm a hypothesis derived in a post hoc analysis of STICH I suggests the need for caution when evaluating hypotheses derived from a post hoc analysis.  

Third, critical evaluation of the five RCTs suggests the following considerations for future study design. (i) Outcomes of longer than 6 months were not studied except in the Hattori, et al. RCT (where functional outcome was assessed at 1 year). Given the physiology of ICH and neuroplasticity, longer periods of observation may be needed to determine the intervention’s full effect, similar to interventions for other brain injuries. (ii) While intent-to-treat remains the gold standard for RCT analysis, the $>20\%$ crossover from the medical treatment to the surgical arm in both STICH RCTs was notable. Though statistical methods were used to account for crossover, assumptions made during these calculations may be inadequate to fully account for the impact of crossover. Adaptive RCT design [23] may be helpful in this context. (iii) Functional outcome is a continuous variable that was somewhat arbitrarily dichotomized into Bfavorable^ and Bunfavorable^ by the various RCTs. While strong arguments can be made for this dichotomization [14, 15], this practice subjects studies to biases that may significantly influence study outcome [24].  

The aggregate of our RCT analysis supports the potential efficacy of minimally invasive hematoma evacuation in a subset of ICH patients. A recently completed minimally invasive surgery plus alteplase $\textsuperscript{\textregistered}$ in intracerebral hemorrhage evacuation (MISTIE III) trial provides additional insights into this matter. MISTIE III was a multicenter RCT of 499 patients that compared medical therapy $(n=249)$ ) to use of minimally invasive surgery (MIS) plus recombinant tissue plasminogen activators (rt-PA; $n=250$ ) for 3 days as treatment for spontaneous $\mathrm{ICHs}\geq30\mathrm{ml}$ [25]. Although this work did not show a difference in the primary endpoints $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}3$ at 1 year, for instance), mortality was lower in the intervention versus medical arm $9\%$ versus $15\%$ at 30 days $p{=}0.07^{}$ . The secondary analysis also showed that a clot reduction to $\leq15\mathrm{ml}$ increased the likelihood of a good functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}3$ at 1 year). These data suggest further study of this approach may be warranted.  

While the available RCTs include level 1 data regarding ICH surgical evacuation, the resulting level of evidence for specific clinical scenarios ranges from BA^ to BC^ due to the limitations noted above [10]. In terms of recommendations for ICH management, the results of the RCTs reviewed in this study support the current AHA recommendations. Specifically, the two STICH studies did not provide compelling evidence for the routine surgical evacuation of supratentorial ICHs. The three smaller RCTs nonetheless suggest that a subset of ICH patients do benefit from surgical evacuation. In the context of the available literature, considerations for surgical evacuation include patient age, functional status, medical condition, presence of an advance directive, hematoma size/location, and surgical accessibility of the ICH. Clinical decisions pertaining to surgical evacuation of ICHs should be constantly reassessed in the context of data from future RCTs.
