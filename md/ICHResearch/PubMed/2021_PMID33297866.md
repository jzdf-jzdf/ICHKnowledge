# Magnetic Resonance Imaging–Guided Thrombolysis (0.6 mg/kg) Was Beneficial for Unknown Onset Stroke Above a Certain Core Size  

THAWS RCT Substudy  

Kazunori Toyoda, MD; Manabu Inoue , MD; Sohei Yoshimura, MD; Hiroshi Yamagami, MD; Makoto Sasaki, MD; Mayumi Fukuda-Doi, MD; Kazumi Kimura, MD; Koko Asakura, PhD; Kaori Miwa, MD; Takao Kanzawa , MD; Masafumi Ihara , MD; Rei Kondo, MD; Masayuki Shiozawa, MD; Masafumi Ohtaki, MD; Kenji Kamiyama, MD; Ryo Itabashi , MD; Toru Iwama, MD; Junya Aoki , MD; Kazuo Minematsu, MD; Haruko Yamamoto, MD; Masatoshi Koga, MD; for the THAWS trial investigators\*  

BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: We determined to identify patients with unknown onset stroke who could have favorable 90-day outcomes after low-dose thrombolysis from the THAWS (Thrombolysis for Acute Wake-Up and Unclear-Onset Strokes With Alteplase at 0.6 mg/kg) database.  

METHODS: This was a subanalysis of an investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, open-label, blinded–end point trial. Patients with stroke with a time last-known-well ${>}4.5$ hours who showed a mismatch between diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive alteplase at $0.6\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg}$ intravenously or standard medical treatment. The patients were dichotomized by ischemic core size or National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, and the effects of assigned treatments were compared in each group. The efficacy outcome was favorable outcome at 90 days, defined as a modified Rankin Scale score of 0 to 1.  

RESULTS: The median DWI-Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) was 9, and the median ischemic core volume was $2.5~\mathsf{mL}$ . Both favorable outcome $(47.1\%$ versus $48.3\%$ ) and any intracranial hemorrhage $26\%$ versus $14\%$ ) at 22 to 36 hours were comparable between the 68 thrombolyzed patients and the 58 control patients. There was a significant treatment-by-cohort interaction for favorable outcome between dichotomized patients by ASPECTS on DWI $(P=0.026)$ ) and core volume $\mathrm{\'P{=}0.035\mathrm{\'}}$ ). Favorable outcome was more common in the alteplase group than in the control group in patients with DWI-ASPECTS 5 to 8 (RR, 4.75 $[95\%$ CI, 1.33–30.2]), although not in patients with DWI-ASPECTS 9 to 10. Favorable outcome tended to be more common in the alteplase group than in the control group in patients with core volume ${>}6.4~\mathrm{mL}$ (RR, 6.15 $[95\%$ CI, 0.87–43.64]), although not in patients with volume ${\le}6.4~\mathrm{mL}$ . The frequency of any intracranial hemorrhage did not differ significantly between the 2 treatment groups in any dichotomized patients.  

CONCLUSIONS: Patients developing unknown onset stroke with DWI-ASPECTS 5 to 8 showed favorable outcomes more commonly after low-dose thrombolysis than after standard treatment.

atients with acute ischemic stroke with unknown time of onset, including those with symptoms on awakening, are promising targets for intravenous thrombolysis based on advanced imaging selection criteria. The WAKE-UP trial (Efficacy and Safety of Magnetic Resonance Imaging [MRI]-Based Thrombolysis in Wake-Up Stroke) showed the efficacy of alteplase (a recombinant plasminogen activator) at 0.9 mg/kg guided by the presence of a mismatch between diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and fluidattenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) in patients with unknown onset stroke,1 and the EXTEND trial (Extending the Time for Thrombolysis in Emergency Neurological Deficits) showed similar efficacy guided by perfusion imaging on MRI or computed tomography.2,3 Current guidelines and consensus statements recommend the MRI-based thrombolysis chosen in the WAKE-UP trial for patients with unknown onset stroke.4–6  

The official dosage of alteplase for stroke thrombolysis is $0.6\mathrm{~mg}/\mathrm{kg}$ in Japan6 based on the results of a dose comparison trial using duteplase7 and the multicenter single-dose Japan Alteplase Clinical Trial.8 Concern for a risk of thrombolysis-associated intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) in Asians was strong motivation for the trials of low-dose thrombolysis.9 Several Asian physicians also used alteplase at $0.6~\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg}$ from the perspective of ethnical similarity to Japanese, safety and economy.10,11 In ENCHANTED (Enhanced Control of Hypertension and Thrombolysis Stroke Study), low-dose alteplase was not shown to be noninferior to the standard dose of $0.9\mathrm{{mg}/kg}$ with respect to death and disability and decreased symptomatic ICH for mainly Asian cohorts.12 We performed the THAWS trial (Thrombolysis for Acute Wake-Up and UnclearOnset Strokes With Alteplase at $0.6\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{kg},$ ) with an almost identical design to that of the WAKE-UP trial, except for the use of alteplase at $0.6~\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg}$ in the alteplase group and standard medical treatment instead of placebo use in the control group.13 However, no difference in favorable outcome at 90 days was seen between the 2 treatment groups. The higher proportion of favorable outcome in the control group $(48.3\%)$ ) compared with the other trials might be the main cause of the neutral results.  

Possible reasons for control patients having too good outcomes included a strong tendency for exclusion of patients with emergent large vessel occlusion from randomization according to the strong accumulation of scientific evidence for mechanical thrombectomy during the first half of the enrollment period14 and a strong tendency for prompt initiation of intensive antithrombotic medication to control patients because of the open treatment design.15 It is important to determine if this low-dose thrombolysis benefited specific patient groups or if it showed neutral effects for any type of patients.  

The aim of this subanalysis of THAWS was to identify patients with unknown onset stroke who could have favorable outcomes after low-dose thrombolysis.  



# METHODS  

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.  

THAWS was an investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, open-label, blinded-end point evaluation, controlled trial (UMIN clinical trial ID: UMIN000011630). Full details of the trial design have been reported previously.16 Briefly, patients with an unknown onset stroke who met the clinical criteria for intravenous thrombolysis in Japan at that time,17 other than a time last-known-well ${>}4.5$ hours, underwent randomization if MRI showed DWI-FLAIR mismatch (a negative FLAIR pattern).18,19 The trial was approved by the local ethics committee or institutional review board at each participating center. Patients or their relatives provided written, informed consent according to ethical regulations.  



# Patients  

Patients with stroke symptoms on awakening or with unknown time of onset were eligible if they presented $>4.5$ hours since last-known-well and within 4.5 hours after symptom recognition, were 20 years or older, and had a premorbid modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score available. Patients were excluded if they had a mild stroke with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score ${<}5$ (revised to ${<}2$ in August 2015 to match this eligibility criterion with that of the WAKE-UP trial)  

or severe stroke with an NIHSS score ${>}25$ . Otherwise, patients fulfilled the standard eligibility criteria for the use of alteplase.17  

Patients underwent MRI examination with DWI, FLAIR, T2\*, and time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography of the circle of Willis. The details of DWI and FLAIR parameters were matched to those used in the WAKE-UP trial. Patients underwent randomization if they showed mismatch between the presence of an abnormal signal on DWI (or absence of abnormal signals on DWI in patients with clinically diagnosed acute ischemic stroke) and no marked signal change on FLAIR in the corresponding region of the acute stroke. As MRI criteria, patients with ICH or large infarct with Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) on DWI ${\le}4$ in the territory of the middle cerebral artery, with visual lesion volume ${>}50\%$ of the anterior cerebral artery or posterior cerebral artery, or with more than half of the brain stem or more than half of the unilateral cerebellar hemisphere involved were excluded.  



# Randomization and Treatment  

Eligible patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either intravenous alteplase (alteplase group) or standard treatment (control group), based on a minimization scheme with stratification by severity of symptoms as assessed using the NIHSS score $(\leq11$ or ${>}11$ ). Treatment was allocated by an in-house, validated, interactive email-based system, immediately after a patient was enrolled by an investigator from the study site. Both patients and investigators were aware of treatment allocation. Just after randomization, patients randomized to the alteplase group were treated with intravenous alteplase at $0.6~\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg}$ (with $10\%$ bolus administration and $90\%$ by 60-minute infusion) within 4.5 hours after symptom recognition, and those randomized to the control group were treated with the standard treatment using 1 to 3 antithrombotic drugs, including oral aspirin, oral clopidogrel, intravenous argatroban, or intravenous unfractionated heparin but excluding the combination of argatroban and heparin. All antithrombotics were basically prohibited for use in the alteplase group within the initial 25 hours. Treatment was initiated as soon as possible within 60 minutes after MRI examination.  



# Clinical and Imaging Assessments  

Certified neurologists or neurosurgeons performed clinical assessments including the NIHSS at baseline. Outcomes at 90 days were blindly assessed without information about treatment allocation by independent and certified examiners. Brain MRI was performed at baseline, at 22 to 36 hours to identify ICH, and at 7 to 14 days to delineate final infarct volume. Ischemic core size was assessed semiquantitatively using DWIASPECTS at each site20 and later assessed semiautomatically in the central office by 2 expert stroke neurologists (M. Inoue, K. Miwa) using NIH-approved software (MIPAV, Center for Information Technology, NIH, version 8.0.2) as described elsewhere.13 Initial lesion masks were created by manual outlining to all DWI-positive regions with adequate windowing and converting into short masks for volumetric quantification. Ischemic core size was also centrally measured using an automated image postprocessing system (RAPID, iSchemaView, Menlo Park, CA) by apparent diffusion coefficient $<620\times10^{-6}\mathrm{mm^{2}/}$ second on b0/b1000 image on MRI.  



# Outcome Measures  

The primary efficacy outcome was favorable outcome, defined as an mRS score 0 to 1 at 90 days after stroke onset. Secondary efficacy outcomes were category shift in mRS score at 90 days and change in NIHSS score at 7 days. Safety outcomes were any ICH, parenchymal hematoma type II ICH, and symptomatic ICH at 22 to 36 hours and major extracranial bleeding.21 The definition of symptomatic ICH was an increase in the NIHSS score by ${\ge}4$ from baseline and on MRI at 22 to 36 hours after initiation of treatment.  



# Statistical Analysis  

Data are presented as medians (interquartile range [IQR]) or numbers $(\%)$ . Efficacy and safety analyses were performed on the intention-to-treat population. The treatment groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the $\chi^{2}$ test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate, for categorical variables. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cutoff levels of DWI-ASPECTS score and ischemic core volume at baseline that dichotomized patients into the group where patients with favorable outcomes were common and the group where patients with favorable outcomes were uncommon. In each dichotomized group, crude analysis was performed to determine the difference in frequencies of favorable outcomes. A value of $R{\cdot}0.05$ based on a 2-sided test was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed using JMP version 14 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and STATA (version16, StataCorp LP, College Station, TX).  



# RESULTS  

Of a total of 131 patients undergoing randomization in THAWS from May 2014 through July 2018, 126 patients (median age, 77 years, 53 women) with 90-day follow-up assessment within the allowed schedule were studied, and they were identical with the cohort for the primary outcome analysis in the main article. Both favorable outcome (32/68, $47.1\%$ versus 28/58, $48.3\%,$ $\scriptstyle{\mathsf{P=0.892}},$ ) and mortality (2/68, $2.9\%$ versus 2/58, $3.4\%$ $P{=}0.871$ ) at 90 days were comparable between the alteplase group and the control group. The NIHSS score changed from 7 (IQR, 4.25–13) at baseline to 3 (0.25–7) at 7 days in the alteplase group (difference: −4 [−6 to −1]) and from 7 (4–12.25) to 1.5 (0–7) in the control group (difference: $-4\left[-6\right]$ to $-2]$ ; $_{P=0.432}$ ). Overall ICH was present in 26 patients $(21\%)$ at 22 to 36 hours and classified into hemorrhage infarction type 1 in 10, HI2 in 11, PH1 in 1, and PH2 in the remaining 4; of these only one who showed PH2 was symptomatic. Any ICH (18/68, $26\%$ versus 8/58, $14\%,$ $\mathsf{P{=}0.080},$ , PH2-type ICH (2/68, $3\%$ versus 2/58, $3\%$ , $P{=}0.871$ ), and sICH (1/68, $1\%$ versus 0/58, $P_{=}0.354_{.}$ ) were comparable between the alteplase group and the control group. Overall, no patients developed major extracranial bleeding.  



# Dichotomization Based on DWI-ASPECTS  

The baseline DWI-ASPECTS was 10 in 38 patients, 9 in 41, 8 in 18, 7 in 11, 6 in 11, and 5 in 7, with a median of 9 (IQR 8–10). The cutoff level of DWI-ASPECTS based on the ROC curve analysis was 8 with sensitivity of 0.90, specificity of 0.35, and area under the curve of 0.65. Favorable outcome was seen in $56\%$ (44/79) of patients with DWI-ASPECTS 9 to 10 and $34\%$ (16/47) of patients with DWI-ASPECTS 5 to 8 $\mathrm{\Delta}P=0.027.$ ). The baseline clinical characteristics of the patients are shown in Table  1. Patients with DWI-ASPECTS 5 to 8 were older $\mathrm{\Delta}P=0.009,$ ), more commonly had atrial fibrillation $\mathrm{\:\ensuremath{\langleP=0.005}\ensuremath{}}$ ), less commonly recognized symptoms at waking up $(P<0.001).$ ), had higher NIHSS score $(P{<}0.001$ ), and had larger core infarct volume $\mathrm{\Delta}P<0.001$ ) than those with DWI-ASPECTS 9 to 10.  

There were significant treatment-by-cohort interactions for all the primary and secondary efficacy outcomes between patients with different DWI-ASPECTS ranges (Table 2). In patients with DWI-ASPECTS 9 to 10, the percentage of favorable outcome was not different between the alteplase and control groups $_{(P=0.071}$ ). In patients with DWI-ASPECTS 5 to 8, favorable outcome was more common in the alteplase group than the control group (RR, 4.75 $[95\%$ CI, 1.22–18.5]; $_{P=0.026,}$ ). Both category shift in the mRS score at 90 days and change in the NIHSS score at 7 days were larger in the control group for patients with DWI-ASPECTS 9 to 10 and larger in the alteplase group for those with the a DWI-ASPECTS 5 to 8.  

Each of any ICH, PH2-type ICH, and sICH was comparable between the alteplase group and the control group (Table 3).  



# Dichotomization Based on Ischemic Core Volume  

The median baseline ischemic core volume was $2.5\mathrm{mL}$ (IQR, $0.5\mathrm{-}11.5\mathrm{mL}$ ) by semiautomatic measurement. The cutoff level of volume based on the ROC curve analysis was $6.4~\mathrm{mL}_{\cdot}$ , with sensitivity of 0.82, specificity of 0.47, and area under the curve of 0.67. Favorable outcome was seen in $58\%$ (49/84) of patients with volume ${\le}6.4\mathrm{mL}$ and $26\%$ (11/42) of patients with volume ${>}6.4{\mathrm{~mL}}$ $(P<0.001$ ). Patients with volume ${>}6.4$ mL more commonly had atrial fibrillation $\left(P{=}0.014\right.$ ), less commonly recognized symptoms at waking up $\mathrm{\Delta}P{=}0.007$ ), had higher NIHSS score $^{'}P{<}0.001$ ), and had lower DWI-ASPECTS $:P{<}0.001.$ ) than those with volume ${\le}6.4\mathrm{mL}$ (Table 1).  

There were significant treatment-by-cohort interactions for all the primary and secondary efficacy outcomes between patients with different volumes (Table  2). In patients with volume ${\leq}6.4~\mathrm{mL}$ , the percentage of favorable outcome was not different between the alteplase and control groups $\mathrm{\}P{=}0.376\mathrm{\rangle}$ . In patients with volume ${>}6.4~\mathrm{mL}$ , although not statistically significant, favorable outcome was more common in the alteplase group than in the control group (RR, 6.15 $[95\%$ CI, 0.87–43.64]; ${\ttP=}0.069\$ ). Change in the NIHSS score was larger in the control group for patients with volume ${\le}6.4\mathrm{mL}$ and category shift in the mRS score was larger in the alteplase group for those with volume ${>}6.4~\mathrm{mL}$ .

# DISCUSSION  

The major new finding of the present subanalysis of THAWS was that patients with unknown onset stroke who showed DWI-FLAIR mismatch with baseline DWIASPECTS 5 to 8 more commonly had favorable outcome, corresponding to mRS score 0 to 1, at 90 days after intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase at $0.6\mathrm{mg}/$ $\mathsf{kg}$ than standard medical treatment without thrombolysis.  

The median ischemic core volume in the present patients ${\mathrm{2.5~mL}}$ by semiautomatic measurement) was similar to that of the WAKE-UP participants $(2.0-2.5\mathrm{mL}$ semiautomatically) and relatively small compared with that of the EXTEND participants $(2.4-4.6~\mathsf{mL}$ on DWI or perfusion computed tomography).1,2 More than threefifths of patients in smaller volume groups $\mathrm{\acute{\varepsilon}{\le}}6.4\mathrm{mL}$ semiautomatically and $\leq10\mathsf{mL}$ automatically) showed favorable outcome after standard medical treatment. It seemed to be difficult to prove the additional benefit of thrombolysis in such patient groups, which was the motivation for the present subanalysis. After excluding toogood patients using the cutoff core volume derived from the ROC curve analysis, low-dose thrombolysis was marginally beneficial for patients with volume ${>}6.4{\mathrm{~mL}}$ , equal to the volume of a sphere with diameter of $23\mathrm{mm}$ . Automatically measured volume was similarly useful. Thrombolysis also showed better outcomes based on category shift in the mRS score and change in the NIHSS score for patients with larger ischemic core volume. Statistical significance of the benefit of thrombolysis might be shown if the number of patients was larger.

Median baseline core volumes on DWI in known clinical studies on stroke thrombolysis were $18mL$ in EPITHET (Echoplanar Imaging Thrombolytic Evaluation Trial)22 and $10mL$ in diffusion and perfusion imaging evaluation for understanding stroke evolution.23 A pooled analysis of the 2 study indicated that patients with mismatch imaging between DWI and perfusion imaging, whose median core volume was 17.9 mL (IQR, 10.8–38.5 mL), had lower risk of infarct growth and higher reperfusion rate after alteplase than after placebo.24 Thus, the present cutoff value of core volume $(6.4\mathrmmL)$ ) was smaller than that for most mismatched patients in the 2 studies. However, it should be noted that the present cutoff value was calculated based on a small number of patients and was not validated by other cohorts. We do not intend to emphasize the value itself as a criterion of patients with unknown onset stroke eligible for thrombolysis.  

DWI-ASPECTS 5 to 8 was another optimal cutoff to exclude too-good patients and show the benefit of thrombolysis for the others. This eyeball-judged score is available in the emergent clinical setting. Based on the present results on the primary and secondary efficacy outcomes, low-dose thrombolysis seemed to be beneficial for patients with unknown onset stroke with an ischemic core expanding to multiple regions of interest on ASPECTS because of a large diameter or because of multiple lesions, roughly corresponding to a nonsingle perforator arterial territory stroke or multiple embolic stroke. A subgroup analysis on patients with DWI-ASPECTS 5 to 9 was performed in the main article and did not show the advantage of the alteplase group.7 Thus, a single small ischemic core in patients with unknown onset stroke, corresponding to DWI-ASPECTS of 9, did not seem to be a good target for low-dose thrombolysis. Although any ICH was insignificantly more common after thrombolysis in the larger core groups judged by absolute volume and by DWI-ASPECTS, their ICHs were mostly HI-type and asymptomatic. Thus, low-dose thrombolysis was safe. Although DWI-ASPECTS is semiquantitative, it does not evenly divide the territory of the middle cerebral artery into 10 regions of interest but partly according to functional significance of each region. It might be a reason why the score was a better indicator than quantitative core volume about the assessment of the primary outcome. Otherwise, the discrepancy might be simply because of statistical limitations caused by the small patient number.  

In contrast, favorable outcome was around $10\%$ to $20\%$ less common, although not significant, and the secondary efficacy outcomes showed worse findings in thrombolyzed patients than in control patients in the smaller core groups judged by absolute volume and by DWI-ASPECTS. The effect of excess ICH by thrombolysis on this paradoxical result seemed trivial. Antithrombotic therapy was initiated within the initial 24 hours in $13\%$ of thrombolyzed patients and $85\%$ of control patients, with dual antiplatelet therapy in $3\%$ and $23\%$ , respectively, as stated elsewhere.13 In known randomized trials, the impact of dual antiplatelet therapy on prevention of recurrent stroke compared with single antiplatelet therapy was especially evident in the initial few days.15,25 Thus, a possible beneficial effect of low-dose thrombolysis for small infarction like lacunes might be offset by intensive antithrombotic therapy.26 It remained unclear if alteplase at $0.9\mathrm{{mg}/kg}$ changed this paradoxical result for patients Japanese unknown onset stroke.  

The percentage of occlusion of any major arteries was significantly different between dichotomized patient groups by DWI-ASPECTS and by core volume. Thus, artery occlusion might be another good indicator of patients eligible for thrombolysis. However, it was difficult to assess the significance of artery occlusion in THAWS, since patients in whom thrombectomy was planned were excluded. As a result, favorable outcome was comparable between the alteplase group and the control group with artery occlusion (RR, 1.56 $[95\%$ CI, 0.66–3.64], not shown in Results).  

A critical limitation of this subanalysis was that this is an unpredefined subgroup analysis from the trial with neutral results. Second, the small number of patients in each dichotomized group might weaken statistical power and especially make multivariable adjustment difficult. To compensate for this limitation, we are recruiting thrombolyzed patients with unknown onset stroke according to the recent domestic guidelines6 to perform a multicenter registration survey that includes patients with additional mechanical thrombectomy (the THAWS-2; UMIN clinical trial: UMIN000040408). Third, generalization of the present findings to non-Asian populations would be difficult with ethnicity limited to Japanese. The THAWS had other limitations as described elsewhere, including the difference in the officially approved dose of alteplase between Japan and the other countries.13 The essential problem about the dose of alteplase would be resolved if tenecteplase will replace alteplase as an agent for stroke thrombolysis with the same dose between Japan and the other countries.  

Stroke thrombolysis using alteplase at $0.6\mathrm{{mg}/kg}$ is unique in Japan, and some Asian countries use it. Based on the results of the present subanalysis, low-dose thrombolysis seems to also be effective for patients with unknown onset stroke if those with small-size infarcts are appropriately excluded. Since DWI-FLAIR mismatch demonstrates the absence of an ischemic lesion age of longer than 4.5 hours with severe and irreversible tissue damage in the area where DWI shows abnormality,19 such salvageable area seems to be small if the core size on DWI is small. By excluding patients with a small core, we reproduced positive results of WAKE-UP for patients with unknown onset stroke with DWI-FLAIR mismatch.1 However, the results of the present subanalysis are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in future studies.
