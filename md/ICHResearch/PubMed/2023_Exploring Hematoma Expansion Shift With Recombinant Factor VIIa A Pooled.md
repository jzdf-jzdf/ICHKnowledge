# Exploring Hematoma Expansion Shift With Recombinant Factor VIIa: A Pooled Analysis of 4 Randomized Controlled Trials  

Vignan Yogendrakumar, MD, MSc; Stephan A. Mayer, MD; Thorsten Steiner, MD, PhD; Joseph P. Broderick, MD; Dar Dowlatshahi, MD, PhD  

BACKGROUND: Hematoma expansion shift (HES) analysis can be used to assess the biological effect of a hemostatic therapy for intracerebral hemorrhage. In this study, we applied HES analysis to individual patient data from 4 randomized controlled trials evaluating rFVIIa (recombinant factor VIIa) $80\upmu{}\mathrm{g}/\upk{}9$ to placebo.  

METHODS: We generated polychotomous strata of HES using absolute growth thresholds $(\le0/<6/\ge6\mathrm{mL})$ ) and quintiles of percent volume change. The relationship between treatment and HES was assessed using proportional odds models. Differences in subgroups based on baseline volume $\mathrm{\(\geq}$ or ${<}20\mathsf{mL}$ ), and time from symptom onset to treatment $\leq$ or ${>}2$ hours) were explored with testing for interactions.  

RESULTS: The primary analysis included 721 patients. At 24 hours, $36\%$ (134/369) of rFVIIa-treated patients exhibited no hematoma expansion as compared with $25\%$ of placebo (88/352)-treated patients. Significant expansion $\left(\ge6mL\right)$ was reduced by $10\%$ in those treated with rFVIIa-(adjusted common odds ratio [acOR], 0.57 $[95\%$ CI, 0.43–0.75]). An examination of percent change similarly showed a shift across the spectrum of expansion (acOR, 0.61 $[95\%$ CI, 0.47–0.80]). In both groups, mild-to-moderate expansion was observed in $38\%$ to $47\%$ of patients, depending on the threshold used. Differences in absolute HES between the rFVIIa and placebo groups were more pronounced in patients with baseline hemorrhage volumes ${\ge}20~\mathrm{mL}$ (acOR, 0.48 $[95\%$ CI, 0.30–0.76] versus ${<}20$ mL: acOR, 0.67 $[95\%$ CI, 0.47–0.95]; $P_{\mathrm{{interaction}}}=0.02;$ . No treatment interaction in patients treated within 2 or after 2 hours from onset was observed (acOR, 0.42 $[95\%$ CI, 0.19–0.91 versus ${>}2$ hours: acOR, 0.59 $[95\%$ CI, 0.44–0.79]; $P_{\mathrm{{interaction}}}=0.30\$ ).  

CONCLUSIONS: The association between rFVIIa and hematoma growth arrest is most pronounced in patients with larger baseline volumes but is evident across the full spectrum of treated patients.

Hematoma expansion shift (HES) analysis is an approach that can assess the biological effect of acute ICH therapies targeting hematoma expansion.4 When applied to the ATACH-2 (Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage-II) acute blood pressure lowering trial, HES analysis revealed that intensive blood pressure reduction preferentially benefitted patients with major hematoma expansion, with little effect on those having minor or moderate hematoma expansion. This approach can be similarly applied to rFVIIa hemostatic trials. In the rFVIIa trials, the drug modestly reduced median hematoma expansion by $15\%$ .1,2 HES analysis can help determine if rFVIIa modestly mitigates expansion across all patients equally or whether the treatment effect is more robust within specific patient subgroups.  

Our objective was to gain additional insight into the biological effect of rFVIIa therapy across a range of patients with hematoma expansion. We applied HES analysis to pooled individual patient data from 4 randomized controlled trials evaluating rFVIIa as a treatment for acute ICH. This knowledge will inform ICH trial design and improve patient selection in future trials.  

# METHODS

# Subjects  

We pooled individual patient data from 4 randomized controlled trials. Subjects were participants enrolled in Safety and Feasibility of Recombinant Factor VIIa for Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage (the Europe/Australasia NovoSeven ICH Trial5), Recombinant Activated Factor VII for Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage: US Phase IIa Trial (the US NovoSeven ICH Trial6), Recombinant Activated Factor VII for Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage (the Recombinant Activated Factor VII ICH Trial1) and Efficacy and Safety of Recombinant Activated Factor VII for Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage (the FAST Trial2).  

The Europe/Australasia and US NovoSeven ICH trials were dose-escalation phase IIa assessments of rFVIIa (6 sequential dose tiers, 8 patients per tier in the Europe/Australasia study;  

4 sequential dose tiers; 8 patients per tier in the US study). The Recombinant Activated Factor VII ICH Trial was an evaluation of rFVIIa at doses: 40, 80, and $160~{\upmu\mathrm{g}}/{\upk\mathrm{g}}$ . Lastly, the FAST trial was an assessment of rFVIIa at 20 and $80~{\upmu\mathrm{g}}/{\upk\mathrm{g}}$ dosing. All 4 trials were double-blinded comparisons to placebo, with patients randomized within 3 hours of symptom onset, and treatment administered within 1 hour of baseline imaging. Exclusion criteria were identical between the 4 trials and included low Glasgow Coma Scale score (3–5) on initial presentation, performed or planned surgical hematoma evacuation, suspected secondary cause (aneurysm, trauma, tumor, etc), and known anticoagulant use or coagulopathy. In this analysis, we chose to evaluate and compare patients who received rFVIIa $80~{\upmu\mathrm{g}}/{\upk\mathrm{g}}$ and placebo, as this is the major comparison being undertaken in ongoing clinical evaluations of rFVIIa.7 Patients treated with alternate doses of rFVIIa (eg, 10, 20, 40, 120, and $160~\upmu{\mathrm{g}}/$ $\mathsf{kg})$ were excluded. Patients with missing baseline or follow-up imaging were also excluded.  

# Imaging Analysis and Outcomes  

All patients received baseline and follow-up computed tomography scans at 24 hours. In each trial, intraparenchymal and intraventricular hemorrhage volumes were measured centrally in a blinded fashion with semiautomated planimetric analysis and manual correction (Analyze Software, Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN). Similar to the methodology described previously,4 we assessed HES with the following approaches:  

1.	Trichotomous analysis using conventional definitions of hematoma expansion: We converted conventional dichotomous definitions of intraparenchymal hematoma expansion8 into 3 bins: significant expansion (eg, ${\ge}6$ mL), minimal expansion (eg, between 0 and $6mL$ ), and nonexpansion or hematoma retraction (eg, $\leq0mL,$ ). The definitions utilized in this analysis include: $6mL$ (absolute intraparenchymal expansion), $33\%$ (relative intraparenchymal expansion), and $6\mathsf{mL}$ or $33\%$ (combined intraparenchymal expansion).   
2.	Percent expansion: We generated quintiles of percent intraparenchymal volume change: nonexpansion or hematoma retraction, ${<}25\%$ expansion, $25\%$ to $49.9\%$ expansion, $50\%$ to $75.9\%$ expansion, and ${>}75\%$ expansion.   
3.	Twenty-four-hour follow-up volumes: We generated quartiles of 24-hour follow-up intraparenchymal volumes. The bounds of each quartile were determined by measuring and utilizing the median and 25th/75th percentiles of baseline hematoma volumes.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Individual patient data were pooled across all 4 data sources. Fishers exact test, ANOVA, and Mann-Whitney $U$ test were used as appropriate when evaluating baseline characteristics. We used mixed effects proportional odds models to investigate the association of rFVIIa on HES with a priori adjustments for baseline intraparenchymal volume, time from symptom onset to treatment start, and study as a random effect. Proportional odds assumptions were assessed using the Brant test. Exploratory analysis with the aforementioned methodology was performed in several patient subgroups: patients with baseline intraparenchymal hemorrhage volumes ${\ge}20$ and ${<}20~\mathrm{mL}$ and patients with onset to treatment times ${\le}2$ and ${>}2$ hours.9,10  

Differences between subgroups were assessed via interaction testing. Finally, in a sensitivity analysis, we assessed HES utilizing total hematoma volume (sum of intraparenchymal and intraventricular hemorrhage volumes). Patients with missing data about the variables and outcomes of interest were excluded from our analysis. All reported $P$ values are 2-sided with $R{<}0.05$ regarded as significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and STATA v17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Local research ethics board approval was obtained at all participating sites with written informed consent obtained from the participant or a legal representative before enrollment. This study complies with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. # RESULTS  

Of the 1328 patients enrolled among the 4 randomized trials, we utilized 721 in our primary analysis (Figure 1; Table S1). Five hundred forty-one patients were treated with alternate doses of rFVIIa and therefore not selected for this analysis. We further excluded 66 patients due to missing 24-hour follow-up scans or baseline volumes that were not reported. Of the 34 patients with missing follow-up scans, the mean baseline hemorrhage volume was $54~\mathsf{mL}$ and 21 of 34 patients died within 15 days of randomization. Patients excluded from analysis due to missing imaging were more likely to be male, were more clinically severe at presentation, and had higher baseline hemorrhage volumes (Table S2). Three hundred sixty-nine patients were treated with $80~\upmu{}\mathrm{g}/\upk{}\mathrm{g}$ rFVIIa compared with 352 patients treated with placebo. Baseline patient characteristics are outlined in the Table. No major differences between the 2 treatment cohorts were observed.

# Subgroup Analysis: Baseline Hemorrhage Volume and Time to Treatment  

Two hundred fifty patients presented with baseline intraparenchymal volumes ${\ge}20$ mL and 471 patients presented with baseline volumes ${<}20~\mathrm{mL}$ . Trichotomous (using $6mL$ ) and percent hematoma volume change horizontal bar graphs are presented in Figure 4. Among patients with baseline hemorrhage volumes ${\ge}20$ mL, those patients-treated rFVIIa exhibited a higher degree of nonexpansion (Figure 4A; $19\%$ [25/126] placebo versus $40\%$ [50/124] rFVIIa, $21\%$ difference) and a lower degree of significant intraparenchymal expansion (Figure  4A; $49\%$ [62/126] placebo versus $37\%$ [46/124] rFVIIa, $12\%$ difference; acOR, 0.48 $[95\%$ CI, 0.30–0.76]). A similar pattern was observed when using percent change. In patients with baseline hemorrhage volumes ${\ge}20\mathsf{mL},$ the most pronounced effect was observed between patients who exhibited no growth, favoring rFVIIa (Figure  4B; $21\%$ difference), with between-group differences in the remaining bins ranging from $2\%$ to $8\%$ (acOR, 0.45 $[95\%$ CI, $0.28\substack{-0.72}].$ ). # Sensitivity Analysis: Total Hematoma Volumes  

A sensitivity analysis of total hematoma volume (sum of intraparenchymal and intraventricular hemorrhage volumes), included 506 patients $(70\%)$ : 215 patients from the original sample were excluded due to lack of intraventricular hemorrhage measurements. Of the 506 patients, 240 were treated with placebo and 266 were treated with rFVIIa $80\upmu{}\mathfrak{g}/\upk{}9$ . Trichotomous (using 6 mL) and percent hematoma volume change horizontal bar graphs are displayed in Figures S2 and S3. The results of the sensitivity analysis were consistent with the overall results. In the trichotomous model, as in the primary analysis population, a quarter of patients treated with placebo exhibited no growth or hematoma retraction at 24 hours versus $37\%$ of patients treated with rFVIIa $(+12\%$ difference). Significant intraparenchymal expansion was reduced by $8\%$ in those treated with rFVIIa (Figure S2); ordinal analysis showed a significant between-group difference favoring treatment with rFVIIa (acOR, 0.59 $[95\%$ CI, 0.42–0.82]).  

In an assessment of percent hematoma volume strata, shift across the spectrum of hematoma expansion was again observed (Figure S3). Between-group differences in the remaining strata ranged from $4\%$ to $12\%$ and an ordinal analysis of percent hematoma volume change showed a significant between-group difference favoring rFVIIa (acOR, 0.56 $[95\%$ CI, 0.40−0.76]).  

# DISCUSSION  

Using individual patient data pooled from 4 randomized controlled trials, we applied a HES analysis model to better understand the biological effects of rFVIIa $80~{\upmu\mathrm{g}}/$ kg on hematoma expansion in patients presenting with acute ICH. One of several notable findings was the association of rFVIIa with hematoma stabilization (ie, either no hematoma expansion or hematoma retraction). In the main analysis of 721 patients (Figures 1 and 2), $>11\%$ of patients in the rFVIIa group exhibited hematoma stabilization when compared with placebo. Significant hematoma expansion $\left(\ge6\mathrm{mL}\right.$ , $33\%$ or $6mL$ or $33\%$ ) was also reduced in the rFVIIa group by $9\%$ to $11\%$ . In both treatment groups, we observed a similar proportion of patients who exhibited some degree of hematoma growth. Based on these observations, we propose that rFVIIa treatment

This is in contrast to the HES analysis previously reported with intensive blood pressure reduction, where hematoma stabilization rates were similar between control and intervention arms.4 In that analysis, intensive blood pressure reduction appeared to preferentially mitigate high-volume hematoma expansion. The 4 trials used in this analysis were performed before the use of blood pressure reduction in routine practice. We, therefore, hypothesize that these 2 therapeutic approaches may be complementary and synergistic. It is also theoretically possible that an increase in thrombotic complications may occur with the combined treatments. The ongoing FASTEST trial (Factor VIIa for Intracerebral Hemorrhage Treatment at Earliest Possible Time Point) may shed further light on these concepts.  

In this analysis, a significant proportion of patients had neither hematoma stabilization nor met the classical thresholds for significant expansion. The largest strata, ranging between $38\%$ and $41\%$ in either treatment arm, exhibited mild to moderate expansion. The presence of this large proportion of patients with some degree of hematoma expansion may in part explain the neutral clinical effect reported in randomized controlled trials, despite the overall shift in expansion.  

Hematoma shift appeared more pronounced in patients with hematoma volumes ${\ge}20\mathsf{mL}$ and those patients presenting under 2 hours from ictal onset. This is likely related to the higher risk of expansion in patients presenting with increased baseline ICH volumes and shorter times from onset.11,12 For our subgroup analysis, we stratified baseline volume at $20\mathsf{mL}$ based on our prior studies,9,13 whereas the 2-hour time threshold reflects ongoing rFVIIa (NCT03496883) and tranexamic acid (NCT03385928) clinical trials.7,14  

In patients with baseline ICH volumes ${\ge}20$ mL, hematoma stabilization occurred in a significant proportion of those treated with rFVIIa $40\%$ [50/124] versus $19\%$ [25/126]; $21\%$ difference; Figure 4). However, a substantial proportion of patients in both groups also had significant hematoma expansion, despite a $12\%$ reduction in the rFVIIa arm. In contrast, patients with baseline volumes ${<}20~\mathsf{mL}$ had similar stabilization rates in both arms and, moreover, there was a relatively small number of patients in this group with significant expansion. This is in keeping with prior studies suggesting small hemorrhages are less likely to expand.9 These findings suggest rFVIIa can help to stabilize larger hematomas, but concurrent therapies, such as blood pressure reduction may be required to further limit significant expansion. The selection of $20~\mathsf{mL}$ volume was based on the volume thresholds from our prior studies exploring the relationship between baseline volume and hematoma expansion.9,13 Analysis of additional baseline volume thresholds ( $\mathrm{30}\mathrm{mL}$ , $40~\mathsf{mL}$ , etc) is warranted in future studies.  

In patients treated ${\le}2$ hours from onset, we observed a greater reduction of absolute significant expansion $(\ge6$ mL) in the rFVIIa arm (a $25\%$ absolute difference if onset ${\le}2$ hours versus an $8\%$ difference if ${>}2$ hours; Figure 5). A similar trend was observed when looking at percent expansion: in patients treated ${\le}2$ hours from onset, extreme expansion $(>75\%)$ ) was reduced by $12\%$ in the rFVIIa arm versus a $4\%$ reduction in patients ${>}2$ hours from onset. However, when assessing the full spectrum of hematoma growth, no significant treatment interaction was observed. This may be a reflection of a reduced sample size, but further investigation is required.  

Our study used a novel HES approach and is strengthened by the use of pooled individual patient data. However, there are important limitations. First, we did not have segmented parenchymal versus ventricular hemorrhage volumes in all patients. In those patients with intraventricular hemorrhage volume measurements, segmentation between ventricular and parenchymal compartments can be unreliable.15 To mitigate this limitation, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the total hematoma volume approach (ventricular combined with parenchymal) in a subset of patients, where volumes were available and demonstrated that the findings remained consistent. Second, we were not able to adjust for location, as detailed information on this was not available in the pooled dataset. Third, the results of our subgroup analysis should be interpreted with caution: whereas the thresholds used in this analysis were selected a priori, the source clinical trial data had specific time and volume inclusion criteria that may result in selection bias. In addition, we excluded patients who did not receive follow-up imaging. The baseline volumes of this excluded group were large and the majority passed away within the first month after randomization, thereby representing a more severe subset of patients. Their exclusion may increase the risk of selection bias and reduce generalizability. These results are therefore speculative pending the outcome of ongoing trials using these specific thresholds. Moreover, the HES is not meant as a novel surrogate outcome or an attempt to find a post hoc positive signal in neutral trials. Finally, we noted an interaction between the 2 subgroups stratified by baseline volume when using an absolute expansion approach, but not when using a percent expansion approach. This difference in interaction assessments is potentially due to a tendency of relative thresholds to underestimate treatment effects in large-volume hemorrhages and highlights one of the limits of using percentile quintiles to assess hematoma shift.16 This drawback will need to be balanced with the benefits, including, but not limited to, the increased discriminative ability, and data-driven nature of this approach in future studies.  

In summary, the HES analysis shows that rFVIIa is associated with increased hematoma stabilization and a shift across the full arge volume spectrum of hematoma expansion. However, small to moderate hematoma expansion still occurs following rFVIIa therapy, suggesting the possible need for concurrent complementary therapies and bundled care hemostatic trial designs.
