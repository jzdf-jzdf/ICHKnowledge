Abstract: Introduction: Hyponatremia is the most frequently occurring electrolyte disorder in neurocritical care and traumatic brain injury, aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), neurosurgery, and ischemic stroke are the clinical conditions more often associated with this condition. SIADH and CSWS are the main causes of hyponatremia in neurologically ill patients. Since hyponatremia is a negative prognostic factor for neurocritical patients, early diagnosis and consequent targeted therapy are of fundamental importance. The present review was carried out to provide a brief recap on the main causes and management of hyponatremia in the neurocritical patient.  

Methods: A methodical search of the medical literature using the online database MEDLINE was carried out and studies comprising case reports, prospective and retrospective observational studies, or randomized controlled clinical trials in which there is a diagnosis of hyponatremia in neurocritical patients were included.  

Results: 18 articles were analyzed, consisting of 8 case reports, 4 case series, 3 prospective trials, 1 retrospective study, and 1 multicenter trial. A total of 1371 patients from 18 studies were included. Patients’ average age was $29.28\pm20.9$ , respectively. TBI was the main cause of hyponatremia in the literature reviewed; 12 studies were about the relationship between TBI and hyponatremia, 2 studies about stroke, 2 studies about SAH and 1 about hyponatremia postneurosurgical procedure.  

Discussion: Hyponatremia is the most common electrolyte disorder in hospitalized patients and the main scenarios of hyponatremic neurocritical patients are subarachnoid hemorrhage, ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury and iatrogenic hyponatremia due to neurosurgical cases.  

Conclusion: Hyponatremia is a frequent finding in neurocritical care and is also a recognized negative prognostic factor leading to increased mortality and ICU length hospitalization. Its diagnosis and therapy are essential for correct neurocritical management. The most common cause of serum sodium abnormality is SIADH, and an early diagnosis for target treatment is paramount to prevent delayed symptoms and complications.  

Keywords: Hyponatremia, neurocritical-care, traumatic brain injury, head trauma, stroke, SAH, neurosurgery.  

# 1. INTRODUCTION  

In neurocritical care, fluid balance alteration occurs often as it is diagnosed with serum sodium disorder that has a sodium concentration ${<}135\mathrm{mEq/L}$ , this is called defined hyponatremia, frequently found in up to $50\%$ of all cases of neurological injury [1, 2].  

Traumatic brain injury (TBI), aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), neurosurgery and ischemic stroke are the clinical conditions with the highest incidence of delayed hyponatremia in neurocritical care [3].  

After neurological events, the main differential diagnoses of low serum sodium are: fluid volume depletion, iatrogenic alterations, syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH), and cerebral salt wasting syndromes (CSWS) [4].  

CSWS and SIADH begin with severe hyponatremia and natriuresis (urine sodium level $>40\mathrm{mEq/L}$ ) and the differential diagnosis is not so easy and could represent a critical point to find the best strategy of therapy. In essence, acute therapy for both conditions is fluid replacement [4-6].  

Sub-acute therapy includes corticosteroids and antagonists of vasopressin receptors, furthermore, hypo volemia related to CSWS needs the replacement of sodium and water, whereas the combination of hyponatremia and excess fluid in SIADH is classically treated via water restriction [7-9].  

Hyponatremia is recognized as a predictor of poor neurologic outcomes in patients with TBI: Stelfox et al. showed that hyponatremia acquired in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) increases hospital mortality significantly [10].  

Hyponatremia can develop into an isotonic, hypertonic, or hypotonic volemic state. It is, therefore essential when the diagnosis of hyponatremia is made to calculate the plasma tonicity (osmolality) to have an estimate of the intracellular volume. Based on the detected plasma tonicity, the therapy will be adapted accordingly, allowing the use of hypo, iso or hypertonic infusions [11].  

In a neurocritical assessment, it is even fundamental to evaluate urine osmolality. In normovolemia and normonatremia conditions, the value of urine osmolality is like the serum osmolality, which is around 275-290 mosmol/Kg. In the hypovolemic state, the value of urine osmolality is superior to $450\mathrm{mosmol/Kg}$ [12].  

Although hypotonic hyponatremia involves the presence of an excess of extracellular water over extracellular sodium, in cases of iso or hypertonic hyponatremia, the total body sodium content varies depending on the underlying condition [13]. Despite the pathophysiology of hyponatremia, the most common and feared complication of hyponatremia is cerebral edema and incorrect or inappropriate treatment leads to increased morbidity or mortality [14].  

In neurocritical practice, it is a challenging differential diagnosis between CSWS and SIADH, considering that both clinical conditions have a comparable biochemical testing profile presenting hyponatremia with normal renal, adrenal and thyroid function, hypouricemia, natriuresis with high urine sodium levels, elevated urine osmolality and a high fractional excretion of uric acid [15].  

The differential diagnosis should be focused on the assessment of extracellular volume (founded normo-volemia in cases of SIADH against hypovolemia in patients with CSWS), which is often difficult in clinical practice [16]. For example, in a non-edematous neurologically ill patient with diagnosticated hyponatremia, the founding of evidence of volume depletion (for example, hypotension and increased hematocrit) suggests CSWS rather than SIADH as the primary cause of hyponatremia [17].  

If untreated, severe hyponatremia can lead to worsening cerebral edema and seizures. If sub-optimally treated, there is an increased risk of delayed ischemic deficits and demyelination syndrome [18, 19].  

Considering how frequent it is to find an alteration of serum sodium in neurocritical care and the amount of literature about it, the present review was carried out to provide a brief recap on the main causes and management of hyponatremia in the neurocritical patient.  

# 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS  

Our workgroup performed a methodical search of the medical literature using the online database MEDLINE.  

Research included case reports, prospective and retrospective observational studies, or randomized controlled clinical trials in which there is a diagnosis of hyponatremia in neurocritical patients. Searching criteria included the following terms: “hyponatremia”, “neurocritical care”, “traumatic brain injury”, “head trauma”, “stroke”, “SAH”, “neurosurgery”.  

Clinical trials, reviews, prospective and retrospective studies, case reports and case series published in the last 20 years, and in all languages, were selected. All patients’ ages were included (Fig. 1) [20].  

# 3. RESULTS  

The literature search initially resumed 37 articles. After the removal of duplicates, 25 articles were assessed for eligibility. Of the records screened, 7 were excluded from our study because the data found were not enough or not related to neurocritical care management.  

Finally, 18 articles were eligible, consisting of 8 case reports, 4 case series, 3 prospective studies, 1 retrospective study, and 1 multicenter trial (Table 1).  

A total of 1371 patients from 18 studies were included. Patients’ average age was $29.28\pm20.9$ , respectively. TBI was the main cause of hyponatremia in the literature reviewed, 12 studies were about the relationship between TBI and hyponatremia, 2 studies about stroke, 2 study about SAH and 1 about hyponatremia post-neurosurgical procedure. The timing of hyponatremia development was 7.5 $\pm8.4$ . Intravenous therapy was the main therapy, and the patients had a restored $\mathrm{Na+}$ serum concentration after therapy. Literature reported 1 case of death [6].  

# 4. DISCUSSION  

The paper aims to create a brief overview of the main causes, pathogenesis, and current therapy of hyponatremia in neurocritical care settings.  

In neurocritical patients, plasma’s electrolyte alterations trigger a protective mechanism by neuroglia.  

These protective mechanisms lead to an increase of antidiuretic hormone (ADH)-serum levels that act on vasopressin receptors leading to the development of various syndromes of serum sodium disbalance, like SIADH and CSWS. The stimulation of these receptors produces endothelium vasoconstriction, the vasoconstrictor effect reduces the cerebral blood flow and consequently the oxygen supply to the astroglia with the establishment of a vicious circle that leads to a dysregulation of electrolyte homeostasis [21].

Cerebral salt wasting syndrome and SIADH have been ruled out as two typical syndromes related to hyponatremia in the neurocritical patient, but despite the amount of literature about the etiology and differential diagnosis of these two syndromes, the differentiation between CSWS and SIADH is often difficult, due to the clinical manifestations that are not quite differentiated. Therefore, the current tendency is to identify both under one or the same clinical disorder. Typically, hypovolemia has been considered the typical element of CSWS, even though it is frequently difficult to identify it in neurocritical patients [22].  

As mentioned above, hyponatremia is the most common electrolyte disorder in hospitalized patients, and the main scenarios of hyponatremic development in neurocritical patients are subarachnoid hemorrhage, ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury and iatrogenic hyponatremia due to neurosurgical cases.  

# 5. SUBARACHNOID HEMORRHAGE  

Hyponatremia is the most common electrolyte disbalance that occurs in patients after subarachnoid hemorrhage and is described in $10{-}50\%$ of the cases [7].  

After subarachnoid hemorrhage, the outflow of blood into the space between the pia and arachnoid matter leads to brain cell alteration and triggers protective mechanisms that lead to a disbalance in cellular electrolyte metabolism [23].  

The most common cause of SAH in neurocritical patients is after a traumatic brain injury; nontraumatic and spontaneous diagnosis of SAH is mainly associated with a ruptured cerebral aneurysm [24].  

Hyponatremia following SAH is mostly associated with SIADH. In 2005, Kao et al.[25] found $22.9\%$ of the case of hyponatremia to be secondary to CSWS, while $35.4\%$ corresponded to SIADH.  

In CSWS, there is a normal secretion of antidiuretic hormone but an increase of urinary sodium excretion and a reduction of extracellular fluid and blood volume with, consequently, serum hyponatremia. CSWS is reported as the cause of hyponatremia in only a subgroup of cases [26].  

SIADH is correlated with a disproportionate secretion of antidiuretic hormones, which causes alterations in urinary dilution and retention of water. In patients with CSWS, the alterations started by SAH lead to alterations and dysregulation of natriuretic hormone release and, consequently, excessive natriuresis.  

In SAH-linked SIADH, the nociceptive input of the hypothalamus leads to the altered secretion of the antidiuretic hormone, with consequently increased reabsorption of free water in the distal convoluted tubule of the kidney leading to water retention and diluting hyponatremia [27].  

Other associated causes include acute cortisol insufficiency, excessive fluid therapy, and/or inappropriate diuretic therapy [28]. Notwithstanding the principal etiology, hyponatremia in these patients is related to increased hospitalization, heightened morbidity and probably leads to an elevated risk of vasospasm [29]. Hyponatremia post-SAH usually develops in a few days, typically in the first 48 hours. Sodium serum disorders are linked to a poor prognosis in patients with SAH, so its paramount anticipation, sensible recognition, and proper treatment to improve clinical patient outcomes [30].  

The correct etiology of hyponatremia must be identified early for setting up an appropriate therapy to help reduce the morbidity or mortality associated with hyponatremia in patients with SAH.  

Clinically founding a clear differentiation between SIADH and CSWSS may not be easy in SAH. Multiple factors can occur in various combinations, as well as an inhibition of the renin-aldosterone axis, an increase in the secretion of natriuretic peptide, and unnecessary adrenergic activity, causing, at the end, hyponatremia [23].  

Early administration of fludrocortisone acetate can reduce the risk of hyponatremia in SAH [8]. Triple H therapy (hypertension, hypervolemia, and hemodilution), the standard therapy for SAH, is great as an anti-vasospasm strategy but leads to an increased risk of natriuresis and consequent hyponatremia [6, 31].  

# 6. ISCHEMIC STROKE  

Hyponatremia is the most founded electrolyte disorder in neurological intensive care units in patients with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke.  

It can present with signs and symptoms simulating a neurological disorder and can aggravate pre-existing neurological deficits [32].  

When present, hyponatremia is one of the causes of lasting altered sensorium in stroke patients. It can also lead to numerous other neurological signs and symptoms like seizures deteriorating the consciousness status. There are many factors which can precipitate hyponatremia in this patient like sodium restriction for pressure control, diuretics, and lung infections. Finding an abnormal serum sodium level should lead to specific investigations into the cause of disbalance to promptly find appropriate therapies and obtain the best outcome [33].  

In patients with stroke, conditions which are related to hyponatremia are secondary adrenal insufficiency due to pituitary ischemia or hemorrhage, SIADH and CSWS [34].  

In a recent study of 100 stroke patients with acute stroke, CSWS was the principal cause of hyponatremia and only a minority of the cases were related to SIADH [35].  

Numerous observational studies have evaluated the effects of hyponatremia in patients with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke outcomes, finding an increased risk of short-term and long-term mortality [36].  

Hyponatremia related to ischemic stroke is a predictor of poor outcomes, although the underlying etiopathogenesis is not fully understood [32]. Huang et al. [37], in a large cohort of patients with an ischemic stroke, recorded an incidence of hyponatremia of $11.6\%$ , related to a significant increase in mortality after three years. Likewise, Rodrigues et al. [38] recently identified hyponatremia as the most frequent electrolyte disturbance in stroke patients with a significant increase in mortality at three months and one year later.  

# 7. TRAUMATIC BRAIN INJURY  

In traumatic brain injury, hyponatremia is the most common electrolyte disorder; approximately $20\%$ of patients with TBI showed low serum sodium concentrations [39].  

The etiology of hyponatremia is probably related to alterations of the hypothalamic-hypophyseal axis and most studies define that the most common cause is SIADH. Mostly, SIADH is often diagnosed in the first $^{48\mathrm{~h~}}$ following TBI, during neurocritical care assessment. The major differential diagnosis of SIAD is acute glucocorticoid deficiency and CSWS [40].  

In younger patients with TBI, electrolyte disorders are often associated with panhypopituitarism [41, 42].  

It is strongly recommended the systematic assessment of hypophyseal function in patients with skull base fractures and diffuse axonal damage consequent to traumatic brain injury [39]. SIADH is related to one-third of the cases, in particular to subdural hematoma and focal contusions [43]. Often, iatrogenic hyponatremia may result from overdosing on drugs for intracranial pressure or central diabetes insipidus (mannitol - desmopressin) [44].  

# 8. NEUROSURGERY  

Trans-sphenoidal surgery, working in the hypophyseal areas, is one of the surgical procedures most associated with electrolyte imbalance. In particular, hyponatremia, followed by hypernatremia, is the most frequently found and has a peak incidence on day 7 after surgery [45-47]. Hussain et al. [48] recorded a $15\%$ incidence of postoperative hypo natremia in the first 30 days after surgery with half of the cases registered as mild hyponatremia. Hyponatremia is anyway frequently diagnosed after other intracranial procedures. The acute onset of a serum sodium disorder creates an osmotic gradient between brain cells and plasma that promotes water entry into brain cells, causing brain edema resulting in neurological decay and, potentially, brain herniation death. In most cases, it is linked to SIADH, but a good part is linked to acute glucocorticoid insufficiency [49].  

In cases established after an operation in the pituitary region, the presence of preoperative hypopituitarism makes the development of postoperative hyponatremia more likely. An alteration of the homeostasis of the neurohypophysis and of the pituitary stalk is believed to be the pathophysiological cause responsible for the impaired secretion of vasopressin with consequent water retention [50].  

Several studies indicate that preventive treatment with peri-operative hydrocortisone can reduce the incidence of acute glucocorticoid insufficiency [51]. Transient hypo natremia following pituitary surgery may be part of the socalled triple stage response. This is characterized by diabetes insipidus followed by hyponatremia from the release of preformed vasopressin from damaged neurons. Diabetes insipidus usually occurs in the first 48 hours after surgery and lasts between 24 and 48 hours. Finally, gliosis occurs with persistent diabetes insipidus [46].  

Electrolyte monitoring in neurosurgical patients is of paramount importance to identify, treat, and prevent neurological complications.  

# 9. TREATMENT OF HYPONATREMIA  

In neurocritical care, the treatment strategy of hyponatremia requires particular attention to identify the severity, duration, and volemia status of the patients to obtain tailored therapy [2].  

Regardless of the underlying etiology and that the nociceptive cause is not always clear, it justifies that the presence of hyponatremia in neurocritical patients may group the two main causes under a single therapeutic strategy [44].  

For this reason, in neurocritical care, the treatment of hyponatremia requires early identification of its severity, duration (distinguishing acute and chronic forms), and the patient's blood volume.  

In these patients, hyponatremia must be considered as a neurocritical emergency that requires the immediate setting of a corrective strategy [10].  

Pontine myelinolysis, related to rapid overcorrection, is the worst and dreaded complication, conversely, insufficient sodium replacement may lead to permanent brain damage [52].  

Recently, the literature suggested that in patients with a brain injury and symptomatic for hyponatremia (e.g., development of hyponatremic encephalopathy), therapy with intravenous injection of $150~\mathrm{ml}$ of $3\%$ hypertonic solution in 20 minutes must be adopted immediately, regardless of the underlying cause of the sodium serum disorder, pushing the differential diagnosis between SIADH and CSWS to the background. This infusion can be repeated over the next 20 minutes until the symptoms persist or if the serum sodium concentration does not increase significantly. The infusion of $3\%$ HSS allows to increase the natremia of $1{-}2\mathrm{mmol/h}$ [53, 54].  

The main therapies available for sodium correction are the hypertonic saline solution, fludrocortisone acetate, and vasopressin antagonist.  

# 9.1. Hypertonic Saline Solution  

Three percent HSS, via intravenous infusion, can safely and rapidly increase serum sodium concentration in a safe way, without causing neurological, cardiac or renal alterations [55, 56].  

$3\%$ HSS contains the minimum concentration of sodium able to exceed the maximum osmolar concentration of urine. Consequently, natremia will always improve. Current clinical evidence indicates that the continuous infusion of $3\%$ HSS is a safe therapy if there is strict and continuous monitoring of patient natremia respecting the correction range. This therapy can cause collateral effects such as rebound hypernatremia, hyperchloremia, metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, and acute renal failure.  

Conversely, aggressive initial therapy can cause transient hypotension, hypervolemia, hearth failure, and hemodynamic pulmonary edema [57].  

# 9.2. Fludrocortisone Acetate  

Fludrocortisone acetate is a mineralocorticoid-like drug that induces the renal tubular reabsorption of sodium. It can decrease the natriuretic response consequently avoiding the appearance of hyponatremia [58].  

Current guidelines suggest the administration of HSS and fludrocortisone as adequate neurocritical management for hyponatremia, leading to a reduction of effective circulating volume in SAH aneurysmal related [59].  

Nakagawa et al. [60], in a case series, also found a protective role of fludrocortisone in symptomatic vasospasm in patients with SAH.  

In neurocritical care, guidelines for patients with osmotic diuresis and natriuresis, the recommended dose of fludro cortisone is $0.1/0.2\mathrm{mg}$ , oral, 2-3 times a day. This treatment must be stated until the stabilization of natremia and volemia [61].  

# 9.3. Vasopressin Antagonists  

Vaptans are a class of drugs that work by antagonizing V1a and V2 vasopressin receptors, thus inhibiting the activation of aquaporin-2, inducing aquaresis. They are used in the treatment of mild or moderate forms of hyponatremia secondary to SIADH [62, 63]. This class of drugs is related to an increased risk of rapid correction of serum sodium levels with an increased risk of pontine myelinolysis [64].  

Conivaptan, a member of the vaptan class of drugs, is $\mathrm{V}1\mathrm{a}/\mathrm{V}2$ vasopressin receptor antagonist and is the first accepted for the treatment of euvolemic and hypervolemic hyponatremia, such as with SIADH. Antagonizing V2 receptors can produce aquaresis, excreting free water without losing electrolytes. Aquaresis is the opposite of natriuresis, typically caused by diuretics, which cause the excretion of water and electrolytes with urine with tonicity similar to blood tonicity. The most common adverse reactions include hypotension, headache, potassium disorders, vein inflammation, and atrial fibrillation. In patients with hypovolemic hyponatremia, like in CSWS, conivaptan is not indicated as therapy. Conivaptan offers a more manageable pharmacologic effect over hypertonic saline and fluid restriction, typically used in hyponatremia treatment. Potential adverse reaction includes the risk of volume depletion, and consequently, low cerebral perfusion pressure and the feared rapid overcorrection of sodium. The threshold for rapidity of sodium replacement with vaptan-like drugs is patient dependent, and, in general, is recommended not exceeding $10\mathrm{mEq/l}$ in 24 hours [65-67].  

# CONCLUSION  

Hyponatremia is a frequent finding in neurocritical care and is also a recognized negative prognostic factor leading to increased mortality and ICU length hospitalization if adequate and rapid treatment strategies are not provided.  

There is a strong association between hyponatremia and neurologically ill patients, making its diagnosis and consequent therapy essential for a neurosurgeon. The most common causes in neurocritical care of serum sodium abnormalities are SIADH and CSWS, with a prevalence of SIADH in patients undergoing neurological surgery.  

Syndromes of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion and cerebral salt wasting syndrome have been recognized as the two main causes underlying hyponatremia linked to increased natriuresis in neurocritical patients. The evaluation of a patient’s volemia sometimes allows to establish a differential diagnosis between these two conditions, but more often, differentiation between these clinical conditions represents a clinical challenge. In a neurocritical set-up nowadays, a differential diagnosis is pushed backward, and it is of paramount importance an early correction of serum sodium disorders to avoid brain irreversible damage.  

Although mild hyponatremia is often self-limiting, severe forms need an early diagnosis for emergent treatment due to the propensity to cause severe symptoms and complications.
