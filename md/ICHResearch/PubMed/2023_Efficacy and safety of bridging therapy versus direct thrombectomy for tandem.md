# Efficacy and safety of bridging therapy versus direct thrombectomy for tandem lesions in acute stroke: A systematic review and meta-analysis  

Chenxi Liu a,1, Mingsu Liu b, Xun Yang c,1, Jinping Wang d, Guangqin Li a, a Department of Neurology, The Frist Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, Chongqing 400016, China b Department of Neurology, Zhongshan Hospital Affiliated to Fudan University, Shanghai 200032, China Department of Neurology, Hechuan District People’s Hospital, Chongqing 401500, China d Department of Neurology, Chongqing University Central Hospital, Chongqing 400050, China  

# ARTICLE  INFO  

# ABSTRACT  

Keywords: Tandem lesions Thrombectomy Bridging therapy  

Background: Current studies have concluded that MT (Mechanical Thrombectomy) is safe and effective for tandem lesions (TL). However, The benefit of bridging therapy for TL is controversial.  

Objective: To compare efficacy and safety between bridging therapy and direct thrombectomy of tandem lesions. Method: We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies comparing bridging therapy versus direct thrombectomy among TL patients with regards to symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage(sICH), Parenchymal hemorrhage (PH), 3-month mortality, modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score within 3 months, successful reperfu­ sion, and excellent reperfusion. The meta-analysis of proportions was conducted with a common effects model. Result: Five studies $({\ttn}=1198\$ patients) were identified for the systematic review. For safety outcomes, the bridging group had no significant difference in the rate of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage $(\mathrm{OR}=0.78\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}=0.49\ –1.25$ , $\mathrm{P}=0.31$ ) and the rate of PH $\mathrm{\zetaOR}=0.67$ , $95\%$ $5\%~{\mathrm{CI}}=0.39–1.1{\mathrm{:}}$ 3, $\mathbf{P}=0.13)$ but significantly lower rate of 3-month mortality $(\mathrm{OR}=0.53\$ , $95\%~\mathrm{CI}=0.37–0.75$ , $\mathbf{P}=0.0004.$ ) compared to the direct throm­ bectomy group. In terms of efficacy outcomes, the bridging therapy group had a significantly higher rate of 3- month good functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2)$ $\mathrm{\zetaOR}=1.76$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}=1.38–2.24$ , $\mathrm{~P~}<0.00001_{.}$ ) and successful recanalization $(0\mathrm{R}=1.69\$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{\Delta_{0}CI}=1.27{-}2.25$ , $\mathbf{P}=0.0003$ ) but no significant difference in the rate of excellent recanalization $\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{OR}=1.21\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}=0.91–1.59$ , $\mathrm{P}=0.19_{\circ}$ ) in patients with TL compared to direct thrombectomy group.  

Conclusion: Bridging therapy is effective in improving the 3-month functional prognosis and increasing the rate of arterial recanalization without increasing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage in patients with TL compared to direct thrombectomy. A large multicentre clinical RCT is expected, as are advanced intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy techniques.  

# 1. Introduction  

Acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) tandem lesions (TL) are defined as high stenosis or occlusion of the extracranial segment of the internal carotid artery combined with occlusion of intracranial vessels. They account for $10{-}20\%$ of large vessel occlusions (LVO) [1,2]. Endovascular throm­ bectomy (EVT) for LVO in AIS is safe and effective, and current studies suggest that EVT may provide a higher rate of recanalization and functional independence for tandem lesions [3,4]. However, several recent high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have reported conflicting results on the efficacy of bridging therapy compared to direct thrombectomy [5–10]. There are more clinical and technical challenges with tandem lesions than with single lesion bridging. First, in patients with tandem lesions with mechanical thrombectomy (MT) combined with internal carotid artery stenting (ICA) placement, the risk of re-embolization and bleeding is increased because of the large thrombus load and inconsistent perioperative antithrombotic regimens [11,12]. Second, the impact of different lesion sites or pathology types, such as the tandem internal carotid and middle cerebral artery (TIM) occlusion have high mortality and poor prognosis [13], or the risk of hemorrhage associated with internal carotid artery dissection [14,15], a set of effects that cannot be ignored. For these reasons, bridging therapy for tandem lesions is likely to be more controversial.  

As the current guidelines are unclear and there are no previous systematic reviews, we conducted a systematic review and metaanalysis of studies related to bridging therapy and direct thrombec­ tomy of tandem lesions to investigate their effectiveness and safety. It is hoped that this will provide a reference for the clinical management of tandem lesions.  

# 2. Method  

This systematic review is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta--Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [16].  

# 2.1. Search strategy  

We systematically searched Pubmed, Embase, Cochrane library, and Web of science for randomized controlled trials and cohort studies (published before 25 March 2023). Two investigators independently conducted a literature search and we used a combination of relevant terms: tandem lesions (Mesh), tandem occlusion, thrombectomy (Mesh), mechanical thrombectomy, endovascular thrombectomy, endovascular treatment, thrombolysis (Mesh), alteplase, intravenous thrombolysis, and bridging therapy. All of the above search terms were retrieved from the above databases. The citations generated from these searches were imported into the Zotero software and duplicate citations were removed. Subsequently, a systematic screening was carried out against the titles and keywords of the journals according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria developed below.  

# 2.2. Selection criteria  

Inclusion criteria: (1) Tandem lesion was defined as a proximal intracranial occlusion (intracranial occlusion of the internal carotid ar­ tery (ICA) or occlusion of M1--M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery (MCA)) combined with a cervical ICA steno--occlusion (complete oc­ clusion or ${>}0.90\%$ stenosis). (2) imaging (CTA or DSA) suggesting the presence of occlusion of a major artery. (3) timely mechanical throm­ bectomy. (4) Cervical ICA occlusion treatment options included carotid artery stenting with and without angioplasty, angioplasty alone, or no intervention. (5) Eligible patients received intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) according to the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines. (6) clinical randomized controlled trials or cohort studies. (7) clear safety and efficacy outcome measures.  

Exclusion criteria: (1) unfinished research projects; (2) letters, re­ views, newsletters, conference abstracts, descriptive studies, animal studies, case reports; (3) studies with unextractable data, duplicate data, unclear descriptions; (4) studies with less than 15 total cases.  

# 2.3. Data extraction and quality assessment  

Data were extracted independently by two recorders from studies that met the criteria and relevant disagreements were resolved by dis­ cussion. All data were extracted from article texts, tables, and figures. The data extracted included: basic information about the study, baseline characteristics, the device used, site of intracranial occlusion, effec­ tiveness outcomes, and safety outcomes. The corresponding authors were contacted by email to request further information when necessary. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to assess cohort studies, including three components of study population selection, compara­ bility between groups, and outcome measures with eight items. A NOS score of $\geq7$ is a high-quality study, 4–6 is a moderate-quality study and 0–3 is a low-quality study. Overall, all cohort studies were of high quality and achieved a high NOS score.  

# 2.4. Safety and efficacy outcomes  

Safety outcomes for the study: symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), parenchymal hemorrhage (PH), and mortality. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage and parenchymal hemorrhage are defined by the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study III. Efficacy outcomes for the study: good functional outcome, excellent function outcome, suc­ cessful reperfusion, excellent reperfusion. Good functional outcome was defined as an mRS(modified Rankin scale) score of 0–2 within 3 months, excellent functional outcome was defined as an mRS score of 0–1 within 3 months, successful reperfusion was defined as a TICI (thrombolysis in cerebral infarction) score $\geq2\mathrm{b}$ , and excellent reperfusion was defined as a TICI score of 3.  

# 2.5. Statistical analysis  

Data were imported and analyzed by Review Manager (5.3) and the R programming language (4.4.2). The statistical significance was defined as $\mathrm{~P~}50\%$ or $\ensuremath{\mathrm{P}}\le0.10$ and the fixed effects model with $12<50\%$ and $\mathrm{P}>0.10\%$ and $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially removing studies one by one to assess the impact of each study on the overall results. Publication bias was detected by funnel plots, which were assessed for symmetry by the Begg and Egger tests, and significant publication bias was defined as $\mathbf{p}<0.1$ . Forest plots and funnel plots were produced by Review Manager (5.3), and sensitivity analyses and publication bias were assessed by the R programming language (4.4.2).  

# 3. Result  

# 3.1. Search Results and Study Characteristics  

The initial literature search yielded a total of 390 studies. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria described above, 5 studies Anadani et al. [17], Anadani et al. [18], Marnat et al. [19], Pikija et al. [20], Sanak et al. [21] were carefully evaluated and screened for suitability for this meta-analysis. The specific literature selection process is illus­ trated in Fig. 1. All of the studies were observational cohort studies. The NOS score was 9 points for 4 studies and 8 points for 1 study. All seven studies were multicentre observational cohort trials with patients from Europe or the USA. All with tandem lesions of the anterior circulation (ICA, MCA). The underlying information and quality assessment results of the studies are shown in Table 1.  

# 4. Safety outcome  

# 4.1. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH)  

Pooled analysis of the four studies showed no significant difference in sICH rates between the bridging therapy and direct thrombectomy groups in patients with tandem lesions $(0\mathrm{R}=0.78\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}=0.49–1.25$ $\mathrm{P}=0.31$ ; Fig. 2A). No significant heterogeneity was found here either $(\mathbf{P}=0.14\$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=46\%;$ ; Fig. 2A). The results of the sensitivity analysis suggested that Marnet’s study [19] was the main source of heterogeneity and that the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ value decreased to $15\%$ after the removal of this study. The reason for this is that Marnat et al. [19] studied patients with tan­ dem lesions associated with carotid artery dissection, and the risk of perioperative bleeding and dissection cannot be ignored.  

# 4.2. parenchymal hemorrhage (PH)  

A pooled analysis of three studies showed that in patients with tan­ dem lesions, the bridging therapy group had no significantly lower rate of PH compared with the direct thrombectomy group $\mathrm{{(OR=0.67}}$ , $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}=0.39\$ –1.13, $\mathrm{P}=0.13$ ; Fig. 2B). There was no significant heteroge­ neity between the two groups $(\mathbf{P}=0.69$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ; Fig. 2B).

# 4.3. Mortality  

A pooled analysis of five studies showed that in patients with tandem lesions, the bridging therapy group had significantly lower mortality compared with the direct thrombectomy group $\mathrm{\nabla}{\mathrm{OR}}=0.53$ , $95\%{\mathrm{~CI~}}=$ 0.37–0.75, $\mathbf{P}=0.0004$ ; Fig. 2C). There was no significant heterogeneity between the two groups $(\mathrm{P}=0.54\$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ; Fig. 2C).  

# 5. Efficacy outcomes  

# 5.1. Good functional outcome (mRS 0–2)  

A total of five studies were included in the pooled analysis and the results showed that there was a significant difference between the bridging therapy group and the direct thrombectomy group in terms of the mRS score 0–2 within 3 months $\mathrm{\zetaOR}=1.76$ , $95\%~\mathrm{CI}=1.38–2.24,$ $\mathbf{P}<0.00001$ ; Fig. 3). The bridging group had a better clinical prognosis than the direct thrombectomy group. In terms of heterogeneity, no significant heterogeneity was found between the two groups $(\mathbf{P}=0.34\$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=11\%$ ; Fig. 3).  

# 5.2. Successful reperfusion  

A pooled analysis of the four studies showed that the bridging therapy group had a significantly higher rate of successful reperfusion than the direct thrombectomy group $(0\mathrm{R}=1.69\$ , $95\%~\mathrm{CI}=1.27–2.25,$ $\mathbf{P}=0.0003$ ; Fig. 4A). The bridging group had a higher rate of successful arterial recanalization than the direct thrombolysis group. No significant heterogeneity was seen between the two groups $(\mathbf{P}=0.39\$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ; Fig. 4A).  

# 5.3. Excellent reperfusion  

A total of three studies were included in the pooled analysis, and the results showed no statistically significant difference in excellent reper­ fusion between the bridging therapy group and direct thrombectomy group $(\mathrm{OR}=1.21\$ , $95\%\mathrm{CI}=0.91–1.59_{\mathrm{:}}$ , $\mathrm{P}=0.19$ ; Fig. 4B). There was no significant heterogeneity between the two groups $(\mathbf{P}=0.41\$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ; Fig. 4B).  

# 6. Sensitivity analysis and publication bias  

We apply sensitivity analysis in two ways: (1) changing the analyt­ ical model to a random effects model, and the results were consistent with the preliminary analysis (Fig. S1). (2) Individual studies were removed sequentially using the R programming language (4.4.2). Sensitivity analysis for sICH showed that Marnat et al. [19] was the main source of heterogeneity, and the removal of this study significantly reduced the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ value to $15\%$ $\mathbf{\tilde{P}}=0.83$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=15\%\$ ) (Fig. 5). The reason for this is that Marnat et al. [19] studied patients with tandem lesions associated with carotid artery dissection, and for this particular sub­ group of patients, there were factors such as bleeding from dissection, recurrent embolism, and cervical loops. The results of the other sensi­ tivity analyses were consistent with the results of the preliminary analyses.

# 7. Discussion  

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the safety and efficacy of the treatment of patients with AIS due to tandem lesions by bridging therapy or direct thrombectomy. The current evaluation showed that bridging therapy was associated with good functional outcomes, successful reperfusion, and lower mortality, without signifi­ cant differences in the rates of sICH or excellent reperfusion compared with direct thrombectomy.  

Although alteplase may cause blood-brain barrier disruption and coagulation impairment in bridging therapy compared with direct thrombectomy, there is increasing evidence that bridging therapy does not significantly increase the rate of intracranial hemorrhage after EVT [22,23]. Studies of hemorrhagic transformation in tandem lesions sug­ gest that IVT is not an independent predictor of hemorrhagic trans­ formation in patients with tandem lesions in AIS [24]. Our study found no statistical difference in the incidence of sICH and PH between bridging therapy and direct thrombectomy. Another important consideration in bridging therapy is the relative operational complexity of tandem lesions compared with simple lesions and the difficulty of antiplatelet therapy after stenting. This is because the more complicated operation may damage the intima and cause bleeding, and immediate post-stenting antiplatelet therapy may also increase the risk of intra­ cranial hemorrhage. Fortunately, previous clinical studies have dis­ proved the hypothesis that mechanical thrombectomy combined with stenting of the internal carotid artery increase the probability of symp­ tomatic intracranial hemorrhage in patients with tandem lesions [25, 26]. One explanation is that although antiplatelet therapy is adminis­ tered immediately, the dose and duration of antiplatelet agents are usually strictly controlled by the interventionalist. A second explanation is that patients with acute LVO already have high perfusion after EVT, and previous studies have shown that complete recanalization after embolization is a protective factor against intracranial hemorrhage [27]. The mechanism underlying this effect may be that timely revasculari­ zation slows the expansion of the intracranial infarct focus and reduces the incidence of hemorrhagic transformation [28]. We believe that a limitation of the present study is that there is no consensus on anti­ platelet regimens for emergency internal carotid artery stenting, and there was considerable variation in the use of antiplatelet agents in the included studies. The sensitivity analysis for sICH in this meta-analysis found the greatest heterogeneity in both studies by Anadani et al. [17, 18]. We speculate an important reason for this is the extensive use of intravenous GPIIb/IIIa receptor antagonists in the trials, whereas Mar­ nat et al. [19] and Sanak et al. [21] used only intravenous pumped aspirin and oral antiplatelet agents, and the former has a higher risk of hemorrhagic transformation. However, we performed a.

More standardized and uniform antiplatelet regimens are needed to further balance the risk of bleeding and re-embolism with antiplatelet therapy for tandem lesions.  

The several available RCTs on the efficacy of direct thrombectomy and bridging therapy have provided insufficient evidence of efficacy because of overly broad cut-off values for non-inferiority, different time intervals between onset, and different etiological compositions. Although prior IVT may increase the risk of bleeding, it has a definite advantage in softening emboli and recanalizing distal arteries [29]. Our study shows that bridging therapy is associated with better functional outcomes, higher reperfusion success, and lower mortality in patients with anterior circulation tandem embolism compared with non-tandem embolism. Mechanistically, we believe that a higher recanalization success rate leads to less infarct size expansion and, thus, to an improved functional prognosis. Also, the study by Lin et al. [30] confirmed that in acute stroke patients with fast-growing infarct cores within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ , prior IVT made embolization easier, effectively reducing the number of em­ bolizations and achieving earlier revascularization and reperfusion, thus effectively improving prognosis. In contrast, in the study of Sanak’s study [21], ischemic heart disease and atrial fibrillation were more predominant in the direct thrombectomy group compared with the bridging treatment group, which, to some extent, increased post­ operative mortality and worsened the clinical prognosis.  

Second, collateral circulation is also a factor that should not be ignored in studies. It has been found that good collateral circulation not only leads to higher reperfusion rates and better clinical prognosis in patients with EVT [31–33] but is also an independent predictor of better IVT prognosis [34,35]. However, there is insufficient evidence to prove that IVT prior to EVT is effective in promoting the generation of collateral circulation. In terms of tandem embolism itself, carotid stenting and adjunctive antiplatelet therapy in patients with tandem lesions may greatly facilitate the formation of collateral circulation. Therefore, further studies are needed to determine whether patients with tandem lesions have better collateral circulation than those with single lesions and whether bridging therapy effectively improves the blood supply to the damaged cerebral tissues through collateral circu­ lation, thus improving the clinical prognosis. In clinical practice, a high proportion of patients with direct thrombectomy have a M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery, which makes thrombectomy more difficult [36]. Pikija et al. [20] and Gory et al. [37] analyzed the factors influ­ encing intravenous thrombolysis before tandem lesion thrombectomy and concluded that intravenous thrombolysis was an independent pre­ dictor of a good prognosis and venous recanalization, similar to our findings.  

Notably, bridging therapy significantly increased the rate of good functional outcomes; however, there was no significant difference in the rate of excellent recanalization $\mathtt{(mTICI}=3\$ between the two groups (OR $=1.21$ , $\mathrm{P}=0.19.$ ). There is debate as to whether patients with EVT who achieve excellent recanalization $\mathrm{(mTICI}=3\mathrm{.}$ ) have a better functional prognosis compared with those who achieve successful recanalization $\mathrm{(mTICI\geq2b)}$ . Zhang et al. [38] suggest that the differences in NIHSS scores and good functional outcomes at discharge between the suc­ cessful and excellent recanalization groups are not significant. In contrast, LeCouffe et al. [39] used an extended TICI scoring scale (eTICI) with the additional category, TICI 2C, which was defined as almost complete perfusion except for a few distal cortical vessels with slow blood flow or the presence of small distal cortical emboli. The authors found that patients with TICI 3 or TICI 2C were more likely to have a good functional outcome compared with patients with TICI 2B. How­ ever, in this study, eTICI grade 3 patients had a shorter operative time and fewer thrombectomy removals this time, i.e. such patients are more likely to be operated on ideally. This may make it difficult to achieve an eTICI grade 3 reperfusion level if the patient is more difficult to operate due to differences in the anatomy and thrombotic characteristics of the affected vessels. It is more complex to retrieve emboli with tandem embolization compared with single lesions, and the duration of the operation is often prolonged to achieve excellent recanalization. All of the studies we included indicated no significant difference in the time from onset to recanalization between the bridging treatment and direct thrombectomy groups. Both the SKIP study [40] and the DEVT study [41] suggested no statistical difference in DTP (time from admission to puncture) between the two groups. On the other hand, most patients were found to have an onset to recanalization time at the 6-hour level. This suggests that we do not need to consider the prognostic impact of EVT beyond the time window on patients. We propose the inference that because it has not been proven that mTICI 3 has a better clinical prog­ nosis than mTICI 2b within the specified time frame, in order to keep the time window for recanalization within $^{6\textrm{h}}$ , interventionalists prefer to reperfuse the grade $\mathrm{mTICI}\geq2\mathrm{b}$ , while IVT before EVT softens the thrombus, making the process of thrombectomy easier and therefore significantly increasing the arterial recanalization rate.

Tandem lesions complicated by carotid artery dissection are a pathological subgroup with a low incidence; however, the impact of luminal stenosis and bleeding risk cannot be ignored. Previous studies have confirmed the safety of IVT for carotid artery stenting for strokes associated with carotid artery dissection and tandem lesions [42–44]. However, studies have also noted that cervical loops, challenging catheterization of the true lumen, and the perioperative risk of an intracranial embolus increase the technical difficulty and may limit the use of IVT to intracranial hemorrhage [44]. Marnat et al. [19] specif­ ically evaluated the efficacy of bridging therapy in patients with tandem embolization with carotid artery dissection and, interestingly, found a low incidence of any form of intracranial hemorrhage and sICH in the bridging therapy group. The reasons for this are that compared with the bridging therapy group, the direct thrombectomy group in the study comprised more patients with an ultra-thrombolytic time window and a larger initial infarct core. For the bridging therapy group, the extra­ cranial carotid artery wall has an outer elastic layer that is relatively less prone to rupture and bleeding compared with intracranial arterial dissection. In addition, softening the embolus by IVT reduces the diffi­ culty of retrieval, making thrombolysis retrieval faster compared with direct retrieval, which is more likely to damage the vessel wall of the dissected artery. Regarding good outcomes and prognosis, Marnat et al. [19] did not conclude that prior IVT was effective in improving func­ tional prognosis $(0\mathrm{R}=1.69\$ ; $\mathrm{P}=0.16^{\circ}$ . Pathologically, we believe that intramural hematomas resulting from endothelial tears or rupture of trophoblastic vessels in the vessel wall are more likely to lead to flow restriction and occlusion compared with hematomas resulting from other causes [45]. Conventional intravenous thrombolysis with alte­ plase dissolves thrombi composed of fibrin and does not eliminate he­ matomas within the vessel wall. Therefore, this treatment is ineffective in improving perfusion and clinical prognosis.  

# 8. Limitations and perspectives  

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, the included studies are observational studies with a certain risk of bias, and more high evidence-based RCTs need to be conducted in the future. Further­ more, some important information, such as baseline ASPECT, change in NIHSS score values, doses of intravenous thrombolytic drugs, and spe­ cific antiplatelet regimens, were not systematically described in the study. Thirdly, the specific thrombectomy method was not specifically stated in the study and it remains to be confirmed whether the successful recanalization and re-embolization rates of aspiration are significantly different from those of stent.  

Of course, with the rapid development of endovascular thrombec­ tomy techniques and the continuous breakthroughs in the field of reperfusion therapy in recent years, we can make an outlook. First, as a genetically modified derivative of rt-PA, tenecteplase (TNK) has higher fibrin specificity, longer half-life, and stronger fibrinogen activator in­ hibitor properties, and several RCTs have confirmed the better safety and convenience of tenecteplase compared with alteplase [46–49]. If TNK is used for tandem lesions before EVT, is it likely to reduce hospital-to-puncture time and is it better for tandem lesions? There are no clinical studies on whether hospital-to-puncture time may be reduced and whether there is better efficacy for tandem lesions if TNK is used for pre-EVT thrombolysis. The results of the ongoing BRIDGE-TNK study (NCT04733742) to assess whether tenecteplase bridging combined with EVT is superior to direct EVT in stroke patients with large vessel oc­ clusion within $4.5\mathrm{h}$ of onset are promising. Furthermore, in the last year, the publication of two high-quality clinical studies marked a breakthrough in basilar artery thrombectomy, confirming the feasibility of EVT in patients with acute basilar artery occlusion [50,51]. However, the ratio of intravenous thrombolysis in the studies was low. A series of controlled clinical studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated that bridging therapy in patients with basilar artery occlusion significantly reduces mortality compared with direct thrombolysis and improves clinical prognosis in patients with large atherosclerotic arteries [52–55]. Further, we can think that in patients with basilar artery tandem lesions (defined as basilar artery occlusion combined with proximal extracra­ nial segmental vertebral artery occlusion or ${>}70\%$ stenosis), it is still a topic of debate whether bridging therapy can provide better outcomes.  

# 9. Conclusions  

Our study shows that in patients with tandem lesions in AIS, bridging therapy is effective in improving the 3-month functional prognosis and increasing the rate of arterial recanalization without increasing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage compared to direct thrombectomy. IVT can be an independent predictor of good prognosis in patients with tandem lesions treated with EVT. A large multicentre clinical RCT is expected, as are advanced IVT and EVT techniques.
