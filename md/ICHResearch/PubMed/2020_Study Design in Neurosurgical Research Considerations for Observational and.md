There is inadequate neurosurgical literature discussing appropriate clinical study design. Here, we explore considerations for 2 fundamental study designs of epidemiology: experimental and observational cohort studies, through examples of theoretical yet realistic neurosurgical research questions. By examining 2 common neurosurgical procedures— namely, subdural drains for evacuation of chronic subdural hematoma, and the utility of navigation for placing external ventricular drains—we characterize the framework of cohort study models for clinical research applications.

Often a neurosurgical question derives from a significant clinical problem that merits an improved treatment paradigm, clinical guideline, and/or multidisciplinary regimen in the perpetually evolving “standard of care” best practice. Appropriate study design differs based on the type of research question. Considerations such as patient population, exposure(s), outcome(s), prevalence, incidence, confounders, and timeline are important for results of practical utility.1 Cohort studies are a fundamental study design of epidemiology that considers distinct populations with shared characteristics (cohorts), variable clinical exposures, and patient outcomes. The various methods used in cohort studies are multifactorial, including observational or experimental techniques and prospective or retrospective temporal frameworks. In this article, we discuss 2 commonly utilized cohort design models—randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational cohort studies—coupled with examples of theoretical research studies on chronic subdural hematoma (SDH) evacuation and extraventricular drain (EVD) placement. # RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIALS  

Re-accumulation of SDH remains a common, but unresolved, problem in the neurosurgical field, with incidence of chronic SDH estimated at 60 000 per year by 2030,2 and estimates of recurrence ranging from $5\%$ to $30\%$ .3 There is no established ideal method to prevent reaccumulation, with apparent equipoise between methods of SDH management, including bedside twist-drill craniostomy with subdural drain placement, subdural evacuating port system, burr hole with or without irrigation, and craniotomy. Subdural drains have been studied in small trials as a method to prevent re-accumulation, but no study has conclusively demonstrated whether this method is beneficial.  

A practical question arises: “does the addition of a subdural drain after evacuation of SDH lead to reduced postoperative re-accumulation”? This focused question frames a logical paradigm that informs future interventions and patient care. To explore this question, a retrospective analysis of routinely collected clinical data could be informative, or perhaps monitoring patients prospectively, comparing those with or without a drain following SDH, and observing outcomes. Patients could be randomized to receive a drain or not, depending on feasibility and ethical approval. Furthermore, determining a valid outcome measure (such as reaccumulation) is also critical, along with an appropriate follow-up period.  

RCTs are the scientific gold standard for characterizing causal relationships: evaluating the effectiveness of novel interventions relative to the standard of care. By design, patients are randomly allocated to an experimental group or a control group.4 The power of RCTs is largely due to the unique element of “randomization”, a technique that ideally allows 2 or more interventions to be compared based on outcomes, free from selection bias (even when influenced by unknown factors, as these are randomly distributed).5 For example, if a surgeon had an inclination that certain patients would benefit from an intervention more so than others, this knowledge could influence treatment groups and skew the outcomes data analysis. The randomization process of included subjects is critical, as it ensures no discernable pattern and independent assortment between individuals, whereby assignment of one participant does not influence the assignment of another; this distinguishes true vs pseudo-randomization, in which allocation is via a deterministic model or algorithm that produces statistical randomness but is not truly random (such as allocation by birth date or medical record number).6  

RCTs also utilize allocation concealment (researchers do not have foreknowledge of upcoming patient-group assignments during randomization), reducing information bias as well as selection bias.5 Studies can furthermore be single or double“blinded”, the former indicating that subjects or outcomeassessors do not know the assignment status during the study, and the latter indicating that neither subjects nor researchers know assignment status. Although double-blinding may not always be achievable (ie, if a procedure is performed), allocation concealment is always possible during the randomization process. In effect, allocation concealment and blinding mitigate bias both from the subject (which may otherwise impact adherence and/or attrition) and researcher (ie, observer-expectancy effect and confirmation bias).4  

Additionally, RCTs aim for reproducibility as study variables, including stringently predefined patient inclusion and exclusion criteria, intervention(s), outcome(s) of interest, and time to follow-up are mandated (note these characteristics are also critical for observational cohort studies, discussed below). Reproducibility also requires accurate and transparent reporting of the RCT design, as is explained in the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials statement, an evidence-based summary of recommendations for reporting RCTs.  

Analysis based on patient distribution to treatment groups is also critical, as it helps verify that randomization has allocated reasonable similarity based on possible exposure. For example, this would be problematic if it was observed that gender, age, and/or comorbidities were significantly skewed after randomization; notably, there may be a few maldistributions by chance;  

however, if there are many, it may indicate a systematic issue. It is also theoretically possible that distribution of baseline prognostic factors is unequal because of chance alone (keeping in mind that randomization is designed to balance unknown confounders). One strategy to mitigate this issue is by stratifying groups based on known characteristics considered to have important prognostic value. Ongoing monitoring of the randomization system to correct for important imbalances before recruitment closes can identify these potential issues. Alternatively, adjusted analysis can often account for baseline differences that are not clinically important but are statistically significant.6 The ability to balance confounders ultimately requires that all potential variables associated with the intervention and outcome of interest are measured and known, which is often not possible.  

One topic for consideration is the notion of “crossover”. Planned crossovers are typically reserved for observing longitudinal effects of a treatment, whereby the experimental treatment is ceased and the patient is transferred to the control group; this is a separate study design beyond the scope of this discussion. Unplanned crossovers, in contrast, sometimes occur if a patient in the experimental treatment group, for whatever reason, unexpectedly could not participate in the treatment protocol yet could become part of the control group. Unplanned crossovers represent a challenge in data analysis as well and introduce another possibility for bias, as it may undermine randomization.8 Analyzing outcomes according to a patient’s original assignment, termed intention to treat (ITT) analysis, ultimately overlooks the intervention the patient actually received, theoretically mitigating the effects of unplanned crossover, dropout, etc.9 This technique addresses potential effects of bias that underlie nonrandom crossovers (ie, undermining the initial randomization if there is an unidentified confounder causing nonrandom dropout), and therefore preserves the initial randomization and allocation distribution. It is common practice to conduct a primary analysis by ITT methodology. Good RCT practice minimizes or completely eliminates unplanned crossovers and dropout; however, this is clearly not always feasible.  

A notable example of ITT analysis conducted in the context of a randomized neurosurgical research trial is the Spine Patient Outcomes Research Trial (SPORT) for which our institution was a participant. This tri-phasic study compared surgical vs nonsurgical interventions for the 3 of the most common spinal conditions (intervertebral disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and degenerative spondylolisthesis), followed by cost-benefit studies.10 Because patients in multiple randomized study groups “crossed over” from the nonsurgical group to the surgical group and vice versa, careful consideration for confounding was required during data analysis, which were initially analyzed via ITT.  

A proposed RCT design evaluating the efficacy of adding a subdural drain after evacuating SDH begins with identifying a clear population of interest with distinct inclusion and exclusion criteria. The demographics should be clear enough such that there is no subjective decision-making when deciding which patients are invited to take part in the study and randomized, if consented. Namely, these would be patients with documented chronic SDH as determined by computed tomography requiring surgical evacuation secondary to symptomatic clinical presentation. The comparison would be randomized controls, receiving standard of care (ie, no drain placement) to the experimental treatment (ie, drain placement). The primary outcome measure of interest is postoperative re-accumulation within 3 mo across the study groups, as observed by standard monthly followup. Outcomes would further be calculated via ITT analysis to mitigate potential unknown confounding factors, ie, unidentified factors that may predispose an outcome among certain patients.  

# OBSERVATIONAL COHORT STUDIES  

Another example of a common and unresolved issue in the neurosurgical field is EVD placement techniques. Approximately 24 000 EVDs are placed annually in the United States with at least 1 catheter misplacement per year, reported by $80\%$ of surgeons in 1 survey.11 Technology such as image-guided navigation provides a potential avenue for improving accuracy of drain placements; however, indications for navigation are not clear. Navigation is ostensibly useful when patients demonstrate slit ventricles or other anatomical challenges, but navigation also requires the investment of additional time for setup, which is not always available in an urgent context.  

The question follows, “does image-guided navigation improve accuracy of EVD placement”? Unlike in the above subdural drain example, in which years of debate have focused the question to a dichotomy of whether or not a drain improves outcomes, the question regarding EVDs has many different parameters and is therefore less well defined.  

There are several considerations designing a study to explore this paradigm, such as the number of procedures done per year, availability of historical data, time needed to conduct the study, projected budget, and number of participants needed to provide enough power to detect statistically significant differences (that are also clinically relevant). As in the above example, a retrospective review of routinely collected clinical data might prove informative, or patients with a drain placed either with or without navigation could be prospectively evaluated for placement accuracy. Patient cohorts could be randomized to receive a drain either with or without navigation, depending on feasibility and ethical appropriateness. A valid method for identifying placement accuracy, and determining length of follow-up is also needed.  

The high frequency of EVD placement makes both RCT and observational cohort study designs potentially feasible.12 An observational cohort study is a nonexperimental, longitudinal study in which one or more groups of people with shared characteristics (cohorts) who differ with respect to exposures are followed to determine if these exposures are associated with the rates of certain outcomes.13 The lack of clear guidelines for image-guided EVD placement suggests high variation based on the patient, physician, and institutional preferences. An observational cohort study could not only characterize the accuracy and complication rates of image-guided vs nonimage-guided placement, but also the rates of each placement technique relative to other factors (ie, underlying diagnosis and imaging characteristics).  

In this context, an observational cohort study has several advantages over the RCT design. An RCT is not able to determine frequency of each procedure as the trial protocol mandates intervention distribution. RCTs also require stricter inclusion and exclusion criteria and can become ethically problematic as the randomization process severely limits physician autonomy. A patient with slit ventricles is more likely to benefit from navigation, or a patient needing a rapid intervention may not have time for navigation setup; these considerations prove ethically and clinical problematic for randomization. An RCT for EVD placement with or without image guidance would likely necessitate excluding urgent procedures, or account for possibly significant unplanned crossover of subjects designated for EVD with guidance transferring to the EVD without guidance group; in this context, characteristics associated with the need for an urgent EVD placement represent a potential source for bias. These issues deem randomization a suboptimal modality for comparing EVD placement with vs without image guidance, whereas an observational cohort design offers a real-world analysis of clinical practice and clinical outcomes.  

When the outcome or exposure (ie, treatment) is rare, both RCTs and prospective observational cohort studies are less feasible because of the time required to collect a large sample size, and the amount of controls that would be accumulated for one case would be too high. For instance, placing EVDs in pediatric patients is a less-common procedure; a prospective observational cohort study evaluating EVD placements would have to enroll large numbers of participants for a small number of pediatric EVD cases, which is labor intensive, inefficient, and costly.  

However, observational cohort studies can be prospective or retrospective. The proposed EVD study, like most cohort studies, is discussed as a prospective cohort design. In some cases, a retrospective cohort study is performed, in which investigators use available data to find exposures in question, then evaluate the downstream effects and outcomes. This is particularly useful for rare exposures, as it would be hard to gather large sample sizes of participants exposed in a timely manner. For example, to identify accuracy rate of EVD with vs without guidance in the pediatric population, a retrospective observational cohort study would be ideal, pending a large enough dataset. Retrospective observational cohort studies are therefore optimal for rare exposures, and/or outcomes with long latency from exposure. They require less time and funding to conduct compared to prospective studies. The main disadvantage of retrospective observational cohort studies is the dependence on available data, which may be influenced by sources of bias that are difficult to identify. These studies are therefore more susceptible to selection and information bias than RCTs.13

Strengths and weaknesses of RCTs and observational cohort studies are summarized in the Table. It is generally agreed that RCT is the gold standard for evaluating efficacy of a novel therapy. RCT with allocation concealment (to avoid selection bias prior to randomization) and blinding (to avoid information bias with knowledge of treatment group) further minimizes confounding and bias (theoretically maximizing generalizability) through randomization.15 In theory, randomization also equally distributes unidentified participant characteristics (ie, potential confounders) among study groups that may inadvertently influence the study outcome; this is unique to the RCT model. However, an RCT is not always a feasible study design. A large population is required to achieve results that have external validity (the extent to which the results of the study can be generalized to individuals and circumstances beyond those in the study). Clear inclusion and exclusion criteria must be established to assure homogeneity of groups (although this is also true of observational studies), and the stringency of this aspect can prove an insurmountable obstacle if the incidence or prevalence of the clinical issue is not high enough. RCTs are not immune to bias, as nonparticipation, nonresponse, and dropout can introduce significant bias if there are systematic and/or nonrandom underlying reasons, as discussed above. Likewise, it has been demonstrated that patients selected for participation in RCTs (ie, meeting both the inclusion and exclusion criteria) are generally healthier, younger, and better educated than the average patient requiring treatment, demonstrating that generalizability even from RCTs may be limited.17 RCTs also typically require significant investment of time and funding.  

If an RCT is not possible, feasible, or ethical, an observational cohort study may prove the next best option. In the example of EVD placement, randomization would potentially be biased and unsafe. Therefore, an observational cohort study (ie, prospective and/or retrospective) could be suitable. As in an RCT design, multiple outcomes can be examined for a given exposure (ie, length of hospital stay, in addition to catheter placement accuracy), and multiple exposures can be examined for a given outcome (ie, reasons for requiring an EVD, in addition to possible use of navigation) in observational cohort studies.18 Additionally, observational study designs are more practical for “rarer exposures”, as discussed above. Incidence and prevalence can also be determined from observational cohort studies, but not RCTs, as these are controlled.  

The primary weakness of observational cohort studies stems from the lack of randomization, where the risk of confounding limits generalizability.19 Groups undergoing EVD placement with navigation would not necessarily fit the same demographic characteristics as groups undergoing EVD placement without navigation. There are several strategies attempting to mitigate this potential limitation. With risk-adjustment and stratification of participants, more subgroups with different exposures can be correlated retrospectively to eliminate confounding, such as by severity of presentation, intervention, and outcome, but conclusions drawn from a study like this would be accordingly limited in their external validity. Conclusions from observational cohort studies can be limited by unknown confounders that were not collected or adjusted for in the statistical analysis, such as nonrandom loss to follow-up.  

# CONCLUSION  

It is crucial to carefully consider study design prior to initiating a research study. Considerations such as patient population, exposures, confounding factors, time to follow-up, and available resources are important to determine answers of practical utility. The RCT, a theoretical gold standard in research because of its ability to demonstrate causal inferences, is not always feasible and can limit the autonomy of a clinician in medical decisionmaking due to stringency of design considerations. Observational studies can be used when RCT is impractical or unethical. Observational cohort studies also offer some advantages, such as the ability to evaluate rare exposures and outcomes (ie, retrospectively) and avoid potential ethical challenges, with the possible limitation of confounding that can limit generalizability.
