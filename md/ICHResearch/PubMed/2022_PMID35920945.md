# Cervical spinal cord stimulation for prevention and treatment of cerebral vasospasm after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage: clinical and radiographic outcomes of a prospective single‑center clinical pilot study  

Konstantin V. Slavin $^{1,2}\textcircled{\textcircled{1}}$ · Prasad Vannemreddy1  

Received: 21 February 2022 / Accepted: 21 July 2022 / Published online: 3 August 2022   
This is a U.S. Government work and not under copyright protection in the US; foreign copyright protection may apply 2022  



# Abstract  

Background  Cerebral vasospasm induced by aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a major cause of high morbidity and mortality, for which there is no consistently effective treatment. Cervical spinal cord stimulation (cSCS) has been shown to induce vasodilatation and improve peripheral and cerebral blood flow in both animal and human studies. This pilot study was performed to assess the clinical effect and long-term results of cSCS treatment in aSAH patients.  

Methods  This was the first IRB- and US FDA-approved prospective non-randomized non-controlled study comprising of 12 aSAH patients (8 women, 4 men, age range 34–62 years) treated between May and November 2008. All patients underwent up to 2 weeks of cSCS with a single percutaneously implanted 8-contact electrode. Neurological outcomes at discharge and follow-up of up to 13 years and mortality/complications rates were analyzed.  

Results  All 12 aSAH patients underwent cSCS electrode implantation immediately after securing the aneurysm. Patients were stimulated for 10–14 consecutive days starting within 3 days of aneurysm rupture. Angiographic vasospasm occurred in six patients; two patients developed new vasospasm-related neurological symptoms; both recovered completely by discharge time. One patient died from unrelated multi-system failure; the rest were followed up clinically (average, 7.5 years; range, 12–151 months) and angiographically (average, 6.5 years; range, 36–125 months). No delayed ischemic neurological deficits/strokes and no cSCS-related adverse effects were observed.  

Conclusions  Our short- and long-term data suggest that cSCS is feasible and safe for patients in the acute aSAH settings. Small size of the patient cohort and lack of control do not allow us to conclude whether cSCS is able to prevent cerebral vasospasm, decrease its severity, and improve clinical outcomes in aSAH patients. However, our findings support further clinical trials and development of cSCS as a new concept to prevent and treat cerebral vasospasm. Trial registration.

# Introduction  

Symptomatic cerebral vasospasm that commonly occurs between 3 and 12 days after aneurysmal SAH (aSAH) [4, 26] is still a leading risk factor for poor outcome in such patients. Angiographic vasospasm that develops in $50{-}70\%$ of patients with aSAH [19] remains the most common cause of delayed ischemic neurological deficit (DIND) after aSAH. With $20-30\%$ of patients with cerebral vasospasm experiencing DIND, large percentage of the patients either have severe permanent neurological dysfunction if they survive aSAH or die [3, 16, 18, 26, 31]. The existing treatment approaches [7], including prophylactic administration of oral calcium channel blockers, hemodynamic augmentation, intra-arterial administration of vasodilatory agents, and endovascular angioplasty, are not completely effective in patients with vasospasm caused by SAH. In such patients, new treatment options are needed to prevent and/or treat cerebral vasospasm.  

Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) has been accepted as a treatment of choice for chronic refractory pain due to central and peripheral problems [6, 34]. It is known that SCS can induce vasodilatation and improve peripheral and cerebral blood flow [9, 13, 15, 17, 20, 28, 44]. This point of view suggests that cervical SCS (cSCS) may be an alternative treatment option to prevent cerebral vasospasm in patients with aSAH. It has been reported cSCS can be used to prevent and/or treat arterial vasospasm in the acute setting of aSAH [32, 38]. Importantly, implantation of cSCS system has been proven to be safe and feasible in such patients [32, 35, 38]. The purpose of our pilot study was to create a foundation for evaluation of the clinical efficacy and determine long-term results of cSCS in patients with aSAH. We verified that cSCS is a feasible surgical intervention in acute period following aSAH but the study fell short of suggesting possible effectiveness of cSCS in preventing cerebral vasospasm, decreasing its severity, and improving clinical outcomes in aSAH patients.  



# Patients and methods  

The University Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the protocol of this single-center non-randomized noncontrolled study (protocol # 2007–0899) and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) assigned it to a category of Investigational Device Exemption (G060177); the study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT00766844). Of 75 patients with intracranial aneurysms treated at University of Illinois at Chicago between May and November 2008, 12 patients qualified for this study based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria (see below; Tables 1 and 2; Fig. 1). The limitation in the number of patients was dictated by pilot nature of the project and inherent budgetary limitations of the investigator-initiated and investigator-sponsored study.

Eleven patients had postoperative follow-up, ranging from a minimum of 12 months (as per FDA requirement) to 13 years (range, 12–151 months; mean, 90 months, median, 118 months). # Inclusion and exclusion criteria  

The study inclusion criteria are summarized in Table 1. Patient, or the family if the patient for medical reasons was unable to, had to agree to participate in research by signing an IRB-approved informed consent. It was also required that the ruptured aneurysm is secured with surgical or endovascular procedure before implantation of electrode and that the SCS electrode is inserted within $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ after aSAH. Not satisfying even a single inclusion criterion was considered grounds for exclusion. The flowchart of patients’ qualification is listed in Fig. 1. There was no data collection on patients who were not enrolled in the research, so the nonincluded patients could not be used as a control arm of the study.  



# Implantation of cervical SCS electrode  

The electrode insertion was performed immediately upon completion of endovascular or surgical procedure to secure the ruptured aneurysm, while the patient remained under general anesthesia. For those undergoing endovascular treatments (coiling), the patients were turned into lateral position once the compression of the catheter insertion site was completed and the anticoagulation (if any) was reversed based on the normalization of activated clotting time. Angiographic equipment was used for intraoperative fluoroscopy needed for SCS electrode insertion and its position confirmation. The patients undergoing surgical aneurysm treatment were turned into a lateral position after the cranial incision was closed and dressed and the head-holder was released. The lumbar drain (if used) was removed prior to starting the SCS insertion procedure. The C-arm fluoroscopy device was brought in for radiographic guidance during electrode insertion and positioning.  

In all cases, a single 8-contact electrode lead (Octrode, St. Jude Medical, Plano, TX) was inserted using a percutaneous approach. For this, an entry point into the epidural space was chosen at T1-2 or T2-3 level (Fig. 2). The needle was inserted through a standard paramedian approach in all cases with epidural entry confirmation using a standard loss-of-resistance technique before the electrode lead was inserted and advanced in the posterior epidural space over the radiographic midline in cephalad direction until resistance was encountered. The live fluoroscopy was utilized during the electrode lead advancement, and the level of the electrode contacts was documented. Once the position of electrode lead was confirmed, the lead tail was tunneled $7{-}8\mathrm{cm}$ away from midline using the “needle over the stylet” technique and anchored in place using a 3–0 nylon suture, with application of sterile dressings over the needle entry point and the electrode exit site. The electrode lead was connected to the screening cable and taped to the skin in order to minimize pull-out tension and reduce chance of electrode migration. The screening cable was connected to MTS external stimulator-programmer (St. Jude Medical), and the batteries in this external device were changed every 2 days for the entire study duration to assure uninterrupted stimulation delivery.

# Results  

Eight patients were female and four were male. Patient age ranged between 34 and 62 years, with a mean age of 50.1 years. Most patients were in their fifth and sixth decades of life $(66.7\%)$ . The median value for both H&H and Fisher grades was 3, both ranging from 2 to 4. The ruptured aneurysms were located at the anterior communicating artery in 5 patients, posterior communicating artery in 3, middle cerebral artery in 2, internal carotid artery in 1, and pericallosal artery in 1. Nine patients had their aneurysms clipped and three coiled (Table 2).  

All patients were implanted with a single percutaneous 8-contact electrode (St. Jude Medical) immediately after the procedure to secure the ruptured aneurysm was over and before awakening from general anesthesia (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Patients were stimulated for 10–14 consecutive days or until discharge through MTS external stimulatorprogrammer (St. Jude Medical), starting within 3 day of the aneurysm rupture, at day 0 in 3 patients, day 1 in 8 patients, and day 3 in 1 patient. The frequency of $60\mathrm{Hz}$ and the pulse width of 200 microseconds of stimulation were kept uniform across the entire SCS treatment period. The range of stimulation amplitude was $1.3{-}3.0\mathrm{mA}$ , and the polarity of electrode contacts varied from patient to patient. The paresthesia pattern was steered to elicit bilateral tingling sensation in the upper neck and occiput, but in many patients, paresthesias were also felt in the arms/ the entire body. We kept stimulation amplitude at the level where these paresthesias were clearly noticeable by the patients but did not produce any discomfort. Actual duration of stimulation is listed in Table 3. Altogether, the stimulation was delivered $\mathrm{on}>90\%$ of study days, taking into consideration shorter than expected stimulation duration in 5 patients (1 patient died, two were discharged home before full 14-day period after their treatment, and two had electrodes unintentionally pulled out before study completion). The pullout incidents that occurred in 2 out of 12 patients took place on day 6 in patient $\#6$ and day 13 in patient #8; this was not unexpected based on the wellknown high incidence of electrode pull-outs during SCS trials in conventional pain practice that do not use internal anchoring and tunneled extensions [33].

All patients received oral nimodipine at a dose of $60~\mathrm{mg}$ every $^{4\textrm{h}}$ as per standard of care. Angiographic vasospasm was detected in 6 patients ( $50\%$ ; mild-to-moderate in 4, severe in 2); with combination of cSCS and standard-ofcare interventions that were routinely used at the time of our study (intra-arterial verapamil, transluminal balloon angioplasty or hemodynamic augmentation), their vasospasm uniformly resolved during hospitalization. It must be noted that at the time of the study performance in 2007 and 2008, the intra-arterial administration of verapamil and angioplasty was routinely performed for even slightest suggestion of vasospasm; this practice was subsequently abandoned. Two patients ( $16.7\%$ , both with severe angiographic vasospasm) were found to have transient neurological symptoms related to cerebral vasospasm. These two patients with symptomatic vasospasm recovered from DIND completely by the time of hospital discharge. One patient died from multi-system failure 11 days after aSAH that was deemed unrelated to cSCS. No cerebral vasospasm, DIND and stroke, and no adverse effects from cSCS placement were found during the entire follow-up period.  

In terms of long-term follow-up, all 11 surviving patients were seen in neurosurgery outpatient clinic for at least 12 months (as per FDA requirements). At the time of mean follow-up of 7.5  years (range, 12–151  months; median, 118 months), none of the patients developed any neurological deficit related to their aSAH; there were no long-term side effects of vasospasm in those who had it during the study, and each patient maintained independent level of functioning.  

Similarly, long-term angiographic follow-up was obtained in 9 of 11 surviving patients as two patients, both from the neighboring states, refused to return for an angiogram despite repeated invitations. Conventional cerebral angiograms were completed at a mean follow-up time of 6.5 years (range, 36–125 months; median, 89 months); none showed any development, persistence, or recurrence of cerebral vasospasm. The angiographic follow-up was done as a part of routine postoperative care for patients with ruptured aneurysm and not as a part of our research study, but we used this opportunity to check the patients’ clinical and radiographic condition in order to avoid burdening the patients with additional visits or tests.

# Discussion  

The lack of reliable treatment of vasospasm induced by aSAH with its associated neurological deficits and high levels of morbidity and mortality [3, 22] prompted multiple investigators to conduct clinical studies of additional therapeutic options in such patients. Over the last 30 years, multiple neuromodulation approaches have been proposed to either prevent or treat cerebral ischemia in many clinical situations, including aSAH-induced cerebral vasospasm. These included cSCS [9, 17, 20, 40, 41], electrical stimulation of trigeminal nerve [1, 23], Gasserian ganglion [10, 29], sphenopalatine ganglion [2, 37, 45], facial nerve [30], transcranial direct current stimulation [24], transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation [39], and electroacupuncture [21, 36].  

Based on first clinical experience of Japanese colleagues [32, 38], the use of cSCS appeared to be the most promising intervention, but the small number of patients, inconsistence in the reported data, and lack of crucial details in the publications raised questions about feasibility of such approach in the intensive care unit setting where aSAH patients receive their treatment for the entire duration of the vasospasm or the vasospasm risk period. Therefore, we decided to conduct a single-center prospective study [35], the goal of which was to evaluate clinical safety and efficacy of cSCS in a small group of selected aSAH patients in a North American medical center. This study was undertaken in preparation for a possible larger scale prospective randomized trial of cSCS for prevention and/or treatment of cerebral vasospasm following aSAH.

In addition to assessment of the effect of cSCS on prevention and/or treatment of cerebral vasospasm, the present study evaluated very-long-term clinical outcomes (up to 13 years) in patients who underwent cSCS shortly after clipping or coiling of their ruptured cerebral aneurysm. Our results highlight that treatment of vasospasm with cSCS carries an excellent safety profile. There were no complications and no deaths related to the electrode insertion or to stimulation during the study. We also observed potentially promising results related to possible efficacy of cSCS in patients with aSAH. In this study, $50\%$ of patients developed angiographic cerebral vasospasm during the initial 2 weeks, which is at the lower end of the overall incidence $(50-70\%)$ of cerebral vasospasm in patients with aSAH. This suggests that cSCS may reduce the incidence of cerebral vasospasms in patients with ruptured aneurysms. Moreover, the overall incidence of DIND in patients with aSAH is $20-30\%$ [3, 16, 18, 26, 31]. In our study, there were fewer patients $16.7\%$ , 2 out of 12 patients) who developed DIND, but the number was too low to determine statistical significance. It is important, however, that these two patients recovered from DIND completely by the time of their hospital discharge. Our findings are consistent with the results reported by a Japanese group in 2000 and 2001 [32, 38]. In their first publication, 10 patients received cSCS placement after aneurysmal clipping; angiographic vasospasm was observed in 4 patients and symptomatic vasospasm in 3, with one patient expiring on post-bleeding day 9; and two of 9 surviving patients remaining severely disabled due to a surgical complication in one patient and vasospasm plus re-bleeding in another [38]. Subsequent publication from the same group described the use of cSCS in 12 patients with no mortality and severe disability in two patients [32].

An important finding of our study is that cSCS did not interfere with other therapeutic modalities of vasospasm management. Those 6 patients in our group who developed cerebral vasospasm while undergoing cSCS were treated with intra-arterial verapamil infusion, angioplasty, or hemodynamic augmentation, and in each case, the vasospasm resolved completely during the hospitalization, with ultimate functional independence in all 11 surviving patients, suggesting that addition of cSCS may make other modalities more effective in treating vasospasm after aSAH. Overall, cSCS appears to be safe and minimally invasive and may potentially contribute to reduction of cerebral vasospasm incidence and decrease its severity and improvement in clinical outcomes in aSAH patients. Together with previous Japanese experience, our findings support maintaining interest in further evaluation of cSCS potential in cerebral vasospasm prevention and treatment with future larger-scale studies.  

Animal experiments [9, 13, 15, 17, 20, 28, 44] and human studies [5, 14, 27, 42] have convincingly showed that SCS can induce vasodilatation and increase CBF. However, the mechanisms by which cSCS prevents and treats delayed cerebral vasospasm remain unclear. Previously, we presented several possible explanations for cSCS effect. We hypothesized that cSCS at lower cervical spinal level may modulate sympathetic activity of the superior cervical ganglion to restore sympathovagal balance, thereby reducing arterial resistance and stiffness [11]. We and others before us also suggested that a different mechanism is involved with CBF increase from stimulation of the upper cervical spinal levels, perhaps through central pathways and brainstem connections [11, 15, 25]. We further suggested a possibility of cSCS effect on vasodilatation is related to phosphodiesterases and nitric oxide (NO) synthase activity modulation, resulting in enhancement of NO-cyclic guanosine monophosphate pathway, which may help prevent and/or treat vasospasm after aSAH [46]. Recent experimental studies of SCS support presence of antidromic activation of sensory fibers to release calcitonin gene-related peptide [8, 43] and NO from small sensory neurons expressing the transient receptor potential vanilloid-1 receptor [12] possibly contributing to cerebral vasospasm prevention.  

There are certain limitations to this study. First, it included only a small sample size; with such small cohort and with manifestations being so variable, the results of our study did not show any proof for or against efficacy. A larger number of patients is needed to build the evidence of cSCS effect on cerebral vasospasm and clinical outcomes of aSAH patients. Second, there was no control group in the study; we could not compare patients treated with and without cSCS. Third, the patients in this study were not randomized, and similar procedure was done in all candidates who satisfied specific inclusion and exclusion criteria.  

In addition to all this, we used the best cSCS technology that was available in 2007. Since then, SCS devices and paradigms have significantly evolved [34], and the subsequent introduction of miniaturized wireless technologies, paresthesia-free programming (with high frequency $(10\mathrm{kHz})$ , burst, and DTM approaches), MRI-compatible equipment, and closed-loop principles made many more options available for testing and investigation. With increasing versatility of devices, it is also conceivable that future “cSCS for vasospasm” studies will employ comparative analysis of different options that are delivered by the same generator but with random assignment of stimulation settings.  

Our original plan was to pursue a large-scale randomized study soon after completion of the current pilot project based on overall optimistic results, but the change in clinical circumstances prompted placing this research on hold. It appears that multiple innovations in neuromodulation technology and the paucity of published data on this particular approach resulted in recent surge in interest to this concept. Having noticed enthusiasm of our colleagues from several continents, we feel that addition of our experience to the existent body of information will support a foundation for future studies in this direction.

# Conclusions  

Our results suggest that cSCS for aSAH-induced cerebral vasospasm is feasible and has excellent safety profile. Small size of the patient cohort and lack of control do not allow us to conclude whether cSCS is able to prevent cerebral vasospasm, decrease its severity, and improve clinical outcomes in aSAH patients or to suggest cSCS as an alternative or adjunctive treatment option for cerebral vasospasm in such patients. However, in light of our overall potentially promising results, we feel that a randomized controlled trial is warranted to address the limitations of this study and further define cSCS benefits in a larger patient population.
