The patient discontinued tazemetostat and started to receive lymphoma therapy as described in the Cancer and Leukemia Group B (CALGB) 10403 trial. The patient had an excellent response and no recurrence of SCCOHT 9 months later.  

This remarkable response to an otherwise fatal disease underscores the potential of targeted epigenetic therapies. However, further investigation is needed to understand the doubleedged nature of such treatments. The association of this novel drug with secondary cancer presents not only a risk to mitigate but also a new avenue to explore oncogenesis.

# Andexanet for Factor Xa Inhibitor–Associated Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage  

To the Editor: In the ANNEXA-I trial (Trial of Andexanet Alfa in Intracranial Hemorrhage Patients Receiving an Oral FXa Inhibitor) conducted by Connolly et al. (May 16/23 issue),1 investigators evaluated the use of andexanet alfa in cerebral hemorrhage associated with factor Xa use, which is currently the subject of debate, given that guidelines are contradictory.2,3 We are concerned about the potentially unbalanced data presentation.  

In contrast to the andexanet group, in which $98.2\%$ of the patients received the trial drug, only $85.5\%$ of the patients in the usual-care group received prothrombin complex concentrate. This situation disadvantages the control group. If $98.2\%$ of the patients in the usual-care group had received prothrombin complex concentrate, 139 patients (instead of 121) could potentially have met the criteria for hemostatic efficacy (the primary end point), which would have resulted in similar outcomes in the two groups. A perprotocol analysis that includes the dose and composition of prothrombin complex concentrate would be valuable.  

Moreover, the number of patients with a modified Rankin scale score of 0 to 3 (range, 0 [no deficit] to 6 [death]) at 30 days was lower in the andexanet group than in the usual-care group, and the number of patients with ischemic stroke was substantially higher in the andexanet group. These results are reminiscent of those in the Factor Seven for Acute Hemorrhagic Stroke (FAST) trial, which showed no clinical benefit despite the reduction in hematoma expansion with recombinant activated factor VIIa.4

To the Editor: Intracerebral hemorrhage complicated by factor Xa anticoagulation is a difficult clinical problem. The field needs more data, but the report of the ANNEXA-I trial is disappointing in two ways. First, the primary end point of hemostatic efficacy was a short-term composite of both imaging and clinical measures, but the positive result was driven primarily by improvements as assessed on the basis of imaging. Most literature in stroke uses a longer-term clinical outcome, such as the 90-day modified Rankin scale score to assess disability. Conditions in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage improve slowly, and a longer-term clinical outcome could better show the balance of benefits and harms. Furthermore, andexanet use was associated with ischemic stroke in $6.5\%$ of the patients, as compared with $1.5\%$ of those in the usual-care group. The authors mention that andexanet therapy did not lead to lower modified Rankin scale scores at 30 days than usual care, which may be justification for another trial.  

Second, too few participants in the usual-care group may have received prothrombin complex concentrate. Major organizations favor the use of prothrombin complex concentrate over nothing.1-3 In this trial, the apparent advantage of andexanet therapy may have arisen from hindering the control group.

Although the trial results are compelling, several caveats should be considered. First, $85.5\%$ of the patients in the usualcare group received prothrombin complex concentrate, at a median dose of 3000 IU (interquartile range, 2000 to 3500). Studies in animals and studies involving humans have suggested a dose of $50~\mathrm{IU}$ per kilogram of body weight.1 Second, the treatment strategy that was used in the remaining $14.5\%$ of the patients in the usual-care group is unclear (possible receipt of a hemostatic agent or plasma, which are ineffective treatments), and ideally, these participants should be excluded in the comparison with patients who received andexanet.
