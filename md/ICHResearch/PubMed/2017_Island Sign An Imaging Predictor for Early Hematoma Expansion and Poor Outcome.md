# Island Sign An Imaging Predictor for Early Hematoma Expansion and Poor Outcome in Patients With Intracerebral Hemorrhage  

Qi Li, MD, $\mathrm{PhD^{*}}$ ; Qing-Jun Liu, MD\*; Wen-Song Yang, MD; Xing-Chen Wang, MD; Li-Bo Zhao, MD; Xin Xiong, MD; Rui Li, MD; Du Cao, MD; Dan Zhu, MD; Xiao Wei, MA; Peng Xie, MD  

Background and Purpose—The aim of the study was to investigate the usefulness of the computed tomography (CT) island sign for predicting early hematoma growth and poor functional outcome.  

Methods—We included patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) who had undergone baseline CT within 6 hours after ICH symptom onset in our hospital between July 2011 and September 2016. Two readers independently assessed the presence of the island sign on the admission noncontrast CT scan. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the association between the presence of the island sign on noncontrast admission CT and early hematoma growth and functional outcome.  

Results—A total of 252 patients who met the inclusion criteria were analyzed. Among them, 41 $(16.3\%)$ patients had the island sign on baseline noncontrast CT scans. In addition, the island sign was observed in 38 of 85 patients $(44.7\%)$ with hematoma growth. Multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that the time to baseline CT scan, initial hematoma volume, and the presence of the island sign on baseline CT scan independently predicted early hematoma growth. The sensitivity of the island sign for predicting hematoma expansion was $44.7\%$ , specificity $98.2\%$ , positive predictive value $92.7\%$ , and negative predictive value $77.7\%$ . After adjusting for the patients’ age, baseline Glasgow Coma Scale score, presence of intraventricular hemorrhage, presence of subarachnoid hemorrhage, admission systolic blood pressure, baseline ICH volume, and infratentorial location, the presence of the island sign (odds ratio, 3.51; $95\%$ confidence interval, 1.26–9.81; $P{=}0.017$ ) remained an independent predictor of poor outcome in patients with ICH.  

Conclusions—The island sign is a reliable CT imaging marker that independently predicts hematoma expansion and poor outcome in patients with ICH. The noncontrast CT island sign may serve as a potential marker for therapeutic intervention. (Stroke. 2017;48:00-00. DOI: 10.1161/STROKEAHA.117.017985.)  

Key Words: computed tomography ◼ hematoma growth ◼ intracerebral hemorrhage ◼ predictors ◼ stroke ntracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is the least treatable form of stroke that accounts for ${\approx}10\%$ to $30\%$ of all strokes worldwide.1 The 1-month mortality rate ranges from $30\%$ to $50\%$ , and half of the deaths occur within 48 hours after the onset of symptoms.2–5 Early hematoma growth occurs in approximately one third of ICH patients who present within a few hours and is an independent predictor of a poor functional outcome and increased mortality in patients with ICH.6–8  

Recent studies suggest that ICH is a dynamic event, and early identification of patients at risk for further hematoma expansion is important in terms of therapeutic intervention.9,10  

The computed tomography (CT) angiography (CTA) spot sign is a reliable predictor of hematoma expansion.11–16 However, it requires early CTA examination, which is not readily available in many institutions.16 Therefore, identification of novel imaging markers based on noncontrast CT (NCCT) is crucial for triaging patients for future therapeutic interventions. In a recent review, Boulouis et al17 proposed that NCCT biomarkers may have the potential to become an easy-to-use and readily available tool to stratify the risk of hematoma growth in patients with ICH. Several NCCT imaging predictors, including the blend sign, CT hypodensities, the black hole sign, swirl sign, and the irregularity of hematoma have been associated with early hematoma expansion in patients with ICH.18–20 However, most imaging markers focus on heterogeneity of hematoma. In previous reports, several authors categorized the shape of hematoma into round and irregular. However, the classification is subjective, and we found that only the most irregularly shaped hematoma accurately predicts hematoma expansion.  

Here, we proposed a CT-based imaging predictor of hematoma growth called the island sign to describe extreme margin irregularities. The aim of our study was to investigate the value of the island sign for predicting early hematoma growth and functional outcome in patients with ICH.  

# Methods  

# Patients  

The Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University approved this prospective study. Consecutive adult patients $(>18$ years) with spontaneous ICH who had undergone baseline CT within 6 hours after ICH symptom onset in our hospital between July 2011 and September 2016 were screened for inclusion into this study. A follow-up CT scan was performed within 30 hours after the initial CT scan. Patients who were operated on before the follow-up CT scan were excluded from the study. Also excluded were patients whose ICH was secondary to arteriovenous malformation, head trauma, cerebral aneurysm, brain tumor, and hemorrhagic transformation of a brain infarction. Anticoagulant-associated ICH patients were also excluded. The time to baseline and follow-up CT scans and baseline clinical variables were recorded for each participant. The functional outcome was assessed using the modified Rankin Scale at 90 days. Patients with a modified Rankin Scale score ${\ge}3$ were considered to have a poor outcome according to previous definitions.8,9  

# Imaging Analysis  

The admission and follow-up CT scans were performed using standard clinical protocols. The images were acquired through the picture archiving and communication system and saved as DICOM format (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) for further review. For this study, we defined hematoma growth as a $33\%$ increase in hematoma volume or ${>}6~\mathrm{mL}$ at the time of the follow-up CT scan according to previous definitions.14,21

The island sign was defined as (1) ${\ge}3$ scattered small hematomas all separate from the main hematoma (Figure  1A through 1C) or $(2)\geq4$ small hematomas some or all of which may connect with the main hematoma (Figure 1D). The scattered small hematomas (separate islands) could be round or oval and are separate from the main hematoma. The small hematomas that connect with the main hematoma (connected islands) should be bubble-like or sprout-like but not lobulated. The detailed methodology for identifying the island sign and its mimics is shown in Figures 2 and 3. Two experienced readers who were blinded to the clinical profiles of the patients independently reviewed all images to assess the presence or absence of the CT island sign. Discrepancies regarding the presence of the CT island sign were settled by consensus.

Continuous data are expressed either as medians and interquartile ranges or mean±standard deviation (SD). The demographic, clinical, and radiological characteristics were compared between patients with the island sign and those without it using Fisher exact test, student’s t test, or the Mann–Whitney

# Results  

# Baseline Characteristics  

A total of 252 patients were included in the study. The study population included 167 $(66.3\%)$ men and 85 $(33.7\%)$ women, with a mean cohort age of 60.0 years. The baseline demographic and clinical cohort characteristics are described in Table I in the online-only Data Supplement. Among the 252 patients, the baseline hematoma was located in the basal ganglia in 154 $(61.1\%)$ , thalamus in 51 $(20.2\%)$ , cerebral lobes in 29 $(11.5\%)$ , brain stem in 8 $(3.2\%)$ , and cerebellum in 10 $(4.0\%)$ . The median time from symptom onset to the baseline CT scan was 2 (interquartile range, 1–4) hours. The median baseline hematoma volume was $13.0\mathrm{mL}$ (interquartile range, $7.3{-}22.4\mathrm{mL}$ ). Intraventricular hemorrhage was observed in 80 of 252 $(31.7\%)$ patients on the baseline CT scan. Significant hematoma growth was present in 85 of 252 $(33.7\%)$ patients with ICH.  

# Island Sign Prevalence and Characteristics  

A total of 41 $(16.3\%)$ patients had island on the baseline NCCT scan. The island sign was observed in 38 of 85 $(44.7\%)$ patients with hematoma growth, compared with 3 out of 167 $(1.8\%)$ without hematoma growth on admission CT. The baseline clinical and radiological characteristics of patients with and without CT island sign are shown in Table 1.  

The presence of the island sign was associated with lower Glasgow Coma Scale scores (10 versus 14; $P{<}0.001$ ) and a larger baseline hematoma volume $29.9~\mathrm{mL}$ versus $11.8~\mathrm{mL}$ ; $P{<}0.001$ ). The time interval from symptoms onset to the admission CT scan, the presence of intraventricular hemorrhage, and subarachnoid extension of hemorrhage on the baseline CT scan were not statistically different in patients with or without the island sign $(P{>}0.05)$ ).  

Interrater agreement was excellent between the 2 readers for identifying island sign $\left(\mathrm{{\bfK}}{=}0.907\right.^{}$ ). The sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values of island sign for predicting hematoma growth were $44.7\%$ , $98.2\%$ , $92.7\%$ , and $77.7\%$ , respectively.  

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were performed to assess the association between various clinical and radiological parameters and early hematoma growth (Table 2). In the univariate analysis, the time to baseline CT scan, the initial hematoma volume, the admission Glasgow Coma Scale, and the presence of the island sign on the admission CT scan were associated with early hematoma growth. The multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that the time to the baseline CT scan (odds ratio, 0.64; $95\%$ confidence interval, 0.51–0.81; $P{<}0.001$ ), initial hematoma volume (odds ratio, 1.04; $95\%$ confidence interval, 1.01–1.07; $P{=}0.005$ ), and presence of the island sign on the baseline CT scan (odds ratio, 31.89; $95\%$ confidence interval, 8.67–117.29; $P{<}0.001$ ) independently predicted early hematoma growth. The results of logistic regression analysis for poor outcome are listed in Table 3. The baseline hematoma volume, age, intraventricular hemorrhage, admission Glasgow Coma Scale, and the presence of island sign were predictors for poor outcome in the univariate logistic analysis. After adjusting for age, baseline Glasgow Coma Scale score, presence of intraventricular hemorrhage, presence of subarachnoid hemorrhage, admission systolic blood pressure, baseline ICH volume, and infratentorial location, the presence of the island sign (odds ratio, 3.51; $95\%$ confidence interval, 1.26–9.81; $P{=}0.017$ ) remained independent predictor of poor outcome in patients with ICH.

# Discussion  

Early hematoma growth is a dynamic process that usually occurs within 24 hours after the onset of ICH symptoms. In patients presenting within 3 hours after the ictus, absolute hematoma expansion has been observed in $\leq73\%$ of ICH patients.7,22 Identifying imaging predictors for hematoma growth and poor outcome is important for appropriate selection of patients for aggressive treatment in these patients.  

Previous reports proposed several imaging predictors for identifying the hematomas that will expand.11–15 The CTA spot sign has been well established as an imaging predictor of hematoma growth in patients with ICH.11,12,16 More recently, several CTA-based imaging markers, such as the spot-and-tail sign and the leakage sign, have been shown to be independent predictors of hematoma expansion.22,23 All the above-mentioned imaging predictors, however, have the disadvantage of requiring emergency CTA with contrast administration, which is not readily available in many institutions.  

Hence, there is growing need for NCCT imaging predictors of hematoma growth that are readily available and easy to use in clinical practice. Recently, several NCCT imaging markers, including the black hole sign, the blend sign, and CT hypodensities, have been proposed for predicting hematoma expansion.18–20 In a study of 182 patients with ICH, Sporns et $\mathrm{al}^{24}$ demonstrated that the blend sign is a reliable predictor of early neurological deterioration. The predictive value of the blend sign and black hole sign for hematoma expansion has been recently validated, and they correlated well with the CTA spot sign in patients with spontaneous ICH.25,26 In a study of 1029 patients with ICH, Boulouis et $\mathrm{al}^{20}$ defined 4 types of CT hypodensities and demonstrated that they were independent predictors of hematoma expansion. The blend sign, black hole sign, and CT hypodensity are imaging markers that reflect the heterogeneity of hematoma.

In our study, the island sign was observed in 38 of 85 $(44.7\%)$ patients with hematoma expansion. It is, thus, a promising imaging marker with high interobserver agreement $\scriptstyle(\kappa=0.907$ ). Our study also demonstrated that the island sign is a highly specific predictor of hematoma expansion. We found that patients with the island sign are at higher risk of hematoma growth than those without the sign. The island sign may be described as a special type of extremely irregularly shaped hematoma. Irregularly shaped hematomas have been associated with hematoma expansion in several studies.27–29  

Previous reports have offered various definitions of irregularly shaped hematomas. Fujii et $\mathrm{al^{6}}$ first categorized them as round, irregular, and separated. In a study of $627\mathrm{ICH}$ patients admitted within 24 hours of the onset, Fujii et $\mathrm{al}^{27}$ then dichotomized the shape into round and irregular. They found that an irregularly shaped hematoma foreshadows its expansion in patients with ICH. In another study of 83 patients, Huttner et $\mathrm{al}^{28}$ classified warfarin-associated ICH as having hematomas that are round-to-ellipsoid, irregular with frayed margins, and multinodular to separated. They found that ${>}50\%$ of oral anticoagulant-related ICH hematomas are irregularly shaped. This association, however, could not be confirmed in another study of 50 patients with warfarin-related ICH.29 In most previous studies, the shape of the hematoma was categorized as round or irregular. In fact, hematoma shape is extremely variable, and the definition of irregularly shaped hematoma is subjective and lacks stringent morphological criteria. In other words, the term irregularly shaped hematoma is inaccurate and may encompass a broad spectrum of hematomas. Recently, Barras et al30 found that hematomas with increasing shape irregularity may increase the chance of hematoma expansion. The authors have classified the shape of the hematoma into 5 categories. The island sign is a refinement of the shape irregularity scale and represents extreme margin irregularities. In our study, we did not define island sign based on the 5-point hematoma shape scale proposed by Barras et al because we found that some hematoma irregularities do not fit into any category in the Barras scale (Figure  3C). In addition, the methodology for judgment of hematoma border irregularity was different between Barras scale and island sign (Figure  3). The spikeHeartStroke like hematomas with a broad base were considered daughter hematomas on Barras rating scale (Figure  3D). However, this type of hematoma was considered lobulated hematomas rather than islands. Only those bubble-like or sprout-like small hematomas that connected with main hematoma were considered islands.

We attempted to develop a CT imaging criterion that could depict a multifocal bleeding hematoma. This island sign that we proposed is an NCCT imaging marker that reflects a special type of irregularly shaped hematoma characterized by multifocal small bleeding around the main hematoma. The exact underlying cause of island sign remains unclear. We propose that the main hematoma represents bleeding from a ruptured blood vessel. The small islands may be caused by active bleeding from adjacent arterioles when the hematoma enlarges. Another explanation for the island sign is that it may represent multifocal active bleeding of several ruptured arterioles, an observation that is consistent with Fisher’s avalanche model of hematoma enlargement.31 More importantly, we demonstrated that the presence of the island sign is associated with early hematoma expansion and poor outcomes of ICH patients. It, therefore, might be an indicator that early antiexpansion treatment is needed in these patients.  

The strengths of our study are its relatively large sample size and the simplicity of identifying the island sign in all clinical settings. There are also several limitations in our analysis. First, our study was a single-institution research that requires validation in other centers. Second, correlation of island sign with the CTA spot sign was not analyzed. Future studies are needed to clarify the association between the CTA spot sign and the island sign in patients with ICH.
