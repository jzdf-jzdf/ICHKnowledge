# Abstract  

Background: Decompressive craniectomy is performed to relieve intracranial pressure as an emergency procedure. There is no large study to systematically evaluate the benefit of decompressive craniectomy versus best medical therapy. This study evaluates the survival and long-term functional outcomes of decompressive craniectomy for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage.  

Methods: A total of 54 eligible patients with spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage (median age 55, IQR 47-64) who underwent decompressive craniectomy were retrospectively matched to 72 patients managed with best medical treatment (median age 58, IQR 32-74). Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) scores were dichotomized into favorable and unfavorable outcomes. Survival and functional outcomes were analyzed at discharge, 3, 6 and 12 months. Results: Survival in the craniectomy group was significantly higher compared to the medical treatment group at 30 days, 6 and 12 months $76\%$ , $70\%$ , $70\%$ versus $60\%$ , $57\%$ , $52\%$ respectively, all $\mathrm{p}\le0.05,$ ). There was no difference in functional outcomes at discharge, 3, 6 or 12 months post-hemorrhage (all p > 0.05). Decompressive craniectomy was associated with longer hospital stay (median of 30 days versus 7 days in the control group, $\mathrm{p}<0.001\$ ). Hospital adverse events were more frequent in the craniectomy group than the control group ( $76\%$ versus $33\%$ , $\mathbf{p}<0.001,$ , the commonest adverse events being pneumonia and urinary tract infections. Conclusions: We showed that decompressive craniectomy significantly improved survival compared to medical treatment with lasting benefits. This came at a cost of increased length of hospital stay and related adverse events. There was no improvement in functional outcome.  

# Introduction  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage is a common type of stroke with a reported prevalence of $3.65\%$ and incidence of 1.8 per 1000 population. Hemorrhagic stroke accounts for approximately a quarter of all strokes in our population. Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) can generally be managed medically but in a subgroup of patients with large clot sizes or increased cerebral edema causing acute neurological deterioration, decompressive craniectomy may be advocated as an emergency procedure to relieve the raised intracranial pressure. The role of decompressive craniectomy in the management of malignant cerebral infarction and in traumatic brain injury has previously been investigated. However, the optimal surgical procedure for acute deterioration in ICH is unknown. This study aims to evaluate the survival and functional outcome of patients undergoing decompressive craniectomy for ICH.  

# Aims  

To investigate whether decompressive craniectomy increases survival and improves functional outcomes, assessed at discharge, 6 and 12 months, as compared to conservative medical treatment.  

# Methods  

A total of 64 patients with radiologically confirmed intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) based on either computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and underwent decompressive craniectomy were identified from an existing prospectively collected database of 1,356 consecutive patients who were admitted to neurointensive care unit for the management of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) at the National Neuroscience Institute (NNI), Singapore between  

2009 and 2013. Seven patients who were lost to follow-up within 30 days and three patients who had secondary ICH (due to Moyamoya disease, vascular malformation and acute leukemia) were further excluded (Figure 1). Ethics approval was obtained from the Centralized Institutional Review Board.  

# Treatment guidelines  

Our routine practice would be to perform surgery in patients with acute neurological deterioration and to medically managed those patients who did not fulfill the criteria for surgery. In line with AHA/ASA guidelines for the management of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, decompressive craniectomy with hematoma evacuation were performed on patients with lobar clots which are more than 30 cc and extending to within 1 cm of the brain surface. Medical treatment included blood pressure control with intravenous anti-hypertensives aiming for systolic blood pressure of at least less than 160 - 180 mmHg, sedation and ventilation if necessary, treatment of cerebral edema, hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, optimization of fluid balance and electrolyte imbalances, and reversal of anticoagulation.  

# Screening for predictors of 30-day mortality  

Potential predictors of 30-day mortality in the overall population were screened using univariate analyses. The chi-square test, Student's t-test, and the Mann–Whitney U test were performed for nominal, normally and non-normally distributed variables respectively. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for normality. Variables which fulfilled the cut-off of $\mathrm{p}\leq0.2$ in the univariate analyses were entered into a multivariate logistic regression with backward stepwise elimination.5,6 In the final model, ten predictors for 30-day mortality in the overall population were identified based on $\mathrm{p}<0.05$ : age, use of warfarin, use of anti-platelets, admission GCS, admission systolic blood pressure, white cell count, hemoglobin level, location of bleed, maximal volume of clot, and presence of intraventricular hemorrhage. Volumetric analyses was performed to derive the size of hematoma before and after clot evacuation.  

# Case matching  

Matched case-controls were identified using the genetic matching R package GenMatch7 based on predictors identified in the multivariate regression. The algorithm uses a genetic search algorithm to optimize the balance of the different covariates that may influence 30-day mortality. This allowed the independent effect of craniectomy to be ascertained by comparing the two matched groups, after having controlled for the effects of the other covariates. The R package MatchIt was used in R to implement the GenMatch algorithm and generate the final matched datasets. The final study sample consisted of 54 patients in the decompressive craniectomy group and 72 in the best medical treatment control group. The results of the univariate and multivariate regression are shown in the Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.  

# Survival and functional outcomes  

Mortality status and dates of death were verified with the Singapore National Registry of Diseases Office. Mortalities at 30 days, 3, 6 and 12 months in the decompressive craniectomy and best medical treatment groups were compared using the log-rank test and Cox regression. In the surgical group, the mortality at 3-year follow up was $41\%$ (20 out of 49 patients) for those who underwent decompressive craniectomy and hematoma evacuation. The mortality at 3 years for those who underwent decompressive craniectomy alone was $40\%$ (2 out of 5 patients).  

Mortality between the two groups was not statistically significant $(\mathtt{p}=0.97$ , chi-square test. In view of the lack of statistical difference between these two groups, the data were combined and analyzed together.  

The five-point Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) scores were determined retrospectively from electronic medical records. Both conventional and sliding dichotomy approaches were used to dichotomize the GOS at discharge, 6 and 12 months post-ictus into a binary measure (favorable versus unfavorable outcomes). In the conventional fixed dichotomy approach, a favorable outcome was defined as moderate to mild or no disabilities $({\mathrm{GOS}}\geq4)$ ). In the sliding dichotomy approach, the point of dichotomy was individualized based on the prognostic risk as predicted by the initial ICH score. ICH score for each patient was calculated using the method by Hemphill et al.12 For patients in the worst prognostic category with an ICH score $\geq3$ , the split between unfavorable and favorable outcomes was made between death or vegetative $({\mathrm{GOS}}\leq2)$ ) and severe disabilities $(\mathrm{\bfGOS}\ge3\$ ). For patients with intermediate prognosis (ICH score of 2), the split was made between severe $\mathrm{\textbf{GOS}}\le3)$ and moderate disabilities $(\mathrm{GOS}\ge4\$ ). For patients with the best prognosis (ICH score $\leq1$ ), outcomes worse than mild disabilities (GOS of 5) were considered unfavorable (GOS ≤ 4).  

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 3.3.0 and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.  

Overall, there were 11 patients $(1\%)$ with no available CT reports, 12 patients $(1\%)$ with incomplete blood results, 62 $(6\%)$ with no admission PTT and/or INR, 9 $(1\%)$ with no admission blood pressures, and 115 $(11\%)$ with no admission capillary glucose. For univariate analyses, pairwise deletions were used to remove missing data. For multivariate analysis, list-wise deletion was used to remove missing data. During matching, missing values were imputed with sample means to allow for balancing of the covariates. These imputed values were not used in the final analysis.  

# Results  

In our cohort of 1018 patients, 54 patients (41 men) with a median age of 55 (IQR 47 – 64) years underwent decompressive craniectomy for the management of spontaneous ICH. The demographic, clinical and radiological variables are summarized in Table 1. Forty-nine $(4.8\%)$ underwent decompressive craniectomy with hematoma evacuation compared to 5 $(0.5\%)$ who had decompressive craniectomy only. The median size of hematoma on the first CT scan was 51ml (IQR 34 – 37), and in some patients, expanded to reach a maximum volume of $62\mathrm{ml}$ (IQR 36 – 39) before surgery. The median volume of clot evacuated was $49\mathrm{ml}$ (IQR 25 – 71) and $78\%$ (IQR 57 – 92) of clot was evacuated. The median time from ictus to admission was 5 hours (IQR 3 – 12), and ictus to decompressive craniectomy was 8 hours (IQR 4 – 26). In 17 patients $(31\%)$ , an external ventricular drain was also used. Intracranial pressure monitor was inserted in 5 patients $(9\%)$ following decompressive craniectomy.  

Mortality  

Survival was significantly higher in patients in the decompressive craniectomy group compared to the medical treatment group at 30 days ( $76\%$ versus $60\%$ , $\mathrm{p}<0.05,$ ), 3 months ( $74\%$ versus $57\%$ , $\mathrm{p}<0.02,$ ), 6 months ( $70\%$ versus $57\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=0.05,$ ) and 12 months $70\%$ versus $52\%$ , p < 0.05; Figure 2). The hazard of dying for patients with medical treatment was 2.0 times $95\%$ CI: 1.1 – 3.9, $\mathbf{p}=0.033$ ) higher than those who underwent decompressive craniectomy at 30 days, 2.0 times ( $95\%$ CI: 1.0 – 3.8, $\mathrm{p}<0.05)$ at 3 months, 1.8 times $95\%$ CI: $1.0-3.3,\mathrm{p}=0.06)$ at 6 months, and 2.0 times $95\%$ CI: 1.1 – 3.6, $\mathrm{p}<0.05\$ ) at 12 months. When compared to the predicted mortality at 30 days using the ICH score, overall survival at 30 days in the decompressive craniectomy group was also higher than the predicted mortality $76\%$ observed versus $58\%$ predicted, $\mathrm{p}<0.05\$ ), while $60\%$ survival was observed in the medical treatment group compared to $51\%$ predicted $(\mathtt{p}=0.31\$ ). Chi-squared test did not show any survival differences between those who underwent the decompressive craniectomy only $40\%$ died) versus those who had decompressive craniectomy with hematoma evacuation ( $31\%$ died, $\mathrm{p}=$ 0.97).  

# Functional Outcomes  

The decompressive craniectomy group did not have more patients with favorable outcomes on the GOS compared to the medical treatment group at discharge ( $4\%$ versus $7\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=0.43,$ ), 6 months ( $11\%$ versus $5\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=0.27,$ and 12 months $13\%$ versus $11\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=0.69,$ using the conventional dichotomy approach. Similarly, there was no significant difference in functional outcomes between the two groups using the sliding dichotomy approach (Table 2 and Figure 3).  

Adverse events were recorded for the entire duration of hospital stay. In our cohort, this resulted in a significantly longer median length of stay in the hospital in the decompressive craniectomy group than the medical treatment group, 30 (IQR 18 – 65) days versus 7 (IQR 2 – 19) days, p < 0.001) (Figure 4). One patient data in the decompressive craniectomy group who stayed in hospital for 294 days was excluded as the hospital stay was extended due to social reasons. One or more adverse events following decompressive craniectomy were reported in 41 $(76\%)$ patients, as compared to 24 $(33\%)$ patients in the matched non-surgical group $(\mathtt{p}<0.001)$ . The most common adverse events were pneumonia, in 24 $(44\%)$ of surgical patients, followed by urinary tract infections in 15 $(28\%)$ patients. In contrast, there were $5(7\%)$ cases of pneumonia $(\mathtt{p}<0.001)$ and 11 $(15\%)$ cases of urinary tract infections $(\mathtt{p}=0.09)$ in the matched controls. Details of the adverse events are described in Supplementary Table 3.  

# Discussion  

The main finding of our study was that decompressive craniectomy after spontaneous ICH improved survival at least up to 12 months compared to a matched group of patients receiving medical treatment alone. The survival benefit observed with decompressive craniectomy is also evident in other indications.13 The patients in our medical treatment group were actively managed in our intensive care unit and showed similar survival as that predicted by the ICH score.12 However, despite the improved survival in the decompressive craniectomy group, we did not find any improvement in functional outcome at 12 months, as measured using the GOS. During their inpatient hospital stay, there was also an increase in the reported number of medical adverse events (mainly pneumonia and urinary tract infection) in the decompressive craniectomy group.  This contributed to the increase in the length of hospital stay in the decompressive craniectomy group.  

Previous case-control studies or randomized controlled trials largely compared decompressive craniectomy versus craniotomy with hematoma evacuation.14-21 The only other study to our knowledge that had compared decompressive craniectomy alone with best medical therapy was that of Fung et al.22 In their cohort of 12 patients with ICH of mixed etiology, mortality at six months was lower in the craniectomy group $(25\%)$ as compared to medical treatment group $(53\%)$ but that did not reach statistical significance $(\mathtt{p}=0.24)$ . In our study using a larger cohort of patients with spontaneous ICH only, we demonstrated a significant survival advantage in the decompressive craniectomy group and additionally showed that the effect was sustained up to at least 12 months.  

Fung et al. used a cut-off of modified Rankin scale $(\mathrm{mRS})\leq4$ (no symptoms to severe disabilities) to define a favorable outcome, and found that $75\%$ in the decompressive craniectomy group had favorable outcomes at 6 months compared to $47\%$ in medical treatment group $(\mathtt{p}=0.24)$ . As most trials for ICH or acute stroke used a cut-off of $\mathrm{mRS}\leq1$ or 2 (normal to slight disabilities) as criteria for favorable outcome, we calculated Fung et al.'s outcome data using a conventional dichotomy approach with a cut-off of $\mathrm{mRS}\leq2$ (roughly equivalent to GOS $\geq4$ cut-off used in our study). The difference in favorable outcomes between decompressive craniectomy and medical treatment group became even smaller $33\%$ versus $27\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=1.000,$ ). In our study, we found no significant functional difference at 6 months in the outcomes of decompressive craniectomy group ( $12\%$ versus $7\%$ , $\mathrm{p}=0.37)$ .  

A limitation of our study was its retrospective nature. We have controlled for the effects of other possible confounders by matching to controls using a genetic matching algorithm. Heterogeneity was minimized by selecting only patients with spontaneous ICH. It was difficult to control for the neurosurgical decision to perform surgery, as well as individual surgeon variations and surgical techniques. We acknowledge that individual neurosurgeons practice differ based on circumstances and vary among countries.  

Although previous studies have shown that a combination of decompressive craniectomy and hematoma evacuation is associated with improved survival and functional outcomes, 24-26 the number of patients who underwent decompressive craniectomy only without hematoma evacuation $(n=5)$ ) was too small in our cohort and underpowered to show any difference in survival with the group who underwent decompressive craniectomy and hematoma evacuation (n $=49\AA.$ ). We acknowledge that there may be an undetected difference between these two techniques as a result of the small sample size of the decompressive craniectomy alone group.  

In our study, we have compared the clinical outcomes between those who underwent decompressive surgery with medical management. We were unable to calculate the exact cost associated with the two different management as this data is not available to us. It is also likely that the cost would vary among different countries with different health care economics. We believe that our results would help clinicians and by extension, patient’s families in their decision-making process. The value of long-term survival and cost to society would also have to be considered.  

# Conclusions  

Decompressive craniectomy improved survival in patients compared to medical management alone. However, this appeared to be at a greater burden of increased adverse events and increased length of stay. In our group of patients, the value of long-term survival over the importance of the inherent cost to society had to be weighed.
