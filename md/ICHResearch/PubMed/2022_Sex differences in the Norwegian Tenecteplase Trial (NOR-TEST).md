# Abstract  

Background and purpose: Sex differences in acute ischemic stroke is of increasing interest in the era of precision medicine. We aimed to explore sex disparities in baseline characteristics, management and outcomes in patients treated with intravenous thrombolysis included in the Norwegian Tenecteplase trial (NOR-TEST)  

Methods: NOR-TEST was an open-label, randomized, blinded endpoint trial, performed from 2012 to 2016, comparing treatment with tenecteplase to treatment with alteplase within $4.5~\mathrm{h}$ after acute ischemic stroke symptom onset. Sex differences at baseline, treatment and outcomes were compared using multivariable logistic regression models. Heterogeneity in treatment was evaluated by including an interaction term in the model. Results: Of 1100 patients enrolled, $40\%$ were women, and in patients aged ${>}80$ years, the proportion of women was greater than men $19\%$ vs. $14\%$ ; $p=0.02$ ). Women had a lower burden of cardiovascular risk factors, such as diabetes mellitus ( $11\%$ vs. $15\%$ ; $p=0.05;$ ) and a higher mean high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level $(1.7\pm0.6\mathrm{{mmol/L}}$ vs. $1.3\pm0.4\mathrm{{mmol/L}}$ ; $p<0.001_{\cdot}$ ), and a higher proportion of women had never smoked $45\%$ vs. $33\%$ ; $p<0.001$ ) compared with men. While there was no sex difference in time from onset of symptoms to admission, door to needle time or in-hospital workup, women were admitted with more severe stroke (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score $6.2\pm5.6$ vs. $5.3\pm5.1$ ; $p=0.01$ ). Stroke mimic diagnosis was more common in women ( $21\%$ vs. $15\%$ ; $p=0.01$ ). There were no significant sex differences in clinical outcome, measured by the NIHSS, the modified Rankin Scale, intracranial hemorrhage and mortality.  

Conclusion: Women were underrepresented in number in NOR-TEST. The included women had a lower cardiovascular risk factor burden and more severe strokes.  

KEYWORDS intravenous thrombolysis, sex differences, stroke, tenecteplase, women's health  

# INTRODUCTION  

Sex differences in stroke risk factors, treatment and outcome have been described in different populations, and are influenced by age and comorbidities [1-3]. In most populations, men experience stroke and other cardiovascular events at an earlier age than women, however, women have worse outcomes after stroke, also when adjusting for age [4]. Differences in in-hospital diagnostic work-up and treatment have been reported, are likely to be influenced by societal factors and may contribute to the worse outcomes in women [5].  

Overall, women are underrepresented in clinical stroke trials, even when adjusting for participation to prevalence ratios. In addition, there is an underreporting of sex disaggregated subgroups in trial results [1]. Accordingly, we explored sex disparities in baseline characteristics, management and outcome in intravenous thrombolysistreated patients included in the Norwegian Tenecteplase Stroke Trial (NOR-TEST).  

# METHODS  

NOR-TEST is an open-label, randomized, blinded endpoint trial, comparing treatment with tenecteplase versus alteplase in patients with acute ischemic stroke eligible for treatment with intravenous thrombolysis within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ of symptom onset. In short, from 2012 to 2016, all actual patients aged 18 or above admitted to one of the 13 participating centers across Norway were invited to participate, and allocated to treatment with either tenecteplase $0.4~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ or alteplase $0.9~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ . The primary endpoint was excellent functional outcome according to the modified Rankin Scale (mRS; scores 0-1) at 3 months [6].Secondary outcomes were major neurological improvement at $^{24\textrm{h}}$ , defined as either National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 0 or a reduction of at least 4 points at $^{24\textrm{h}}$ compared with the baseline score, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) occurring within $48~\mathrm{h}$ , any ICH within $24-48~\mathfrak{h}$ and death within 3 months [6].  

# Statistics  

The intention-to-treat NOR-TEST population (alteplase and tenecteplase group combined) was used for the analyses. The lastobservation-carried-forward method was used to handle missing data. Descriptive clinical measures are given as mean $\pm$ standard deviation, median and interquartile range, or as numbers and percentages, as appropriate. Traditional parametric and non-parametric tests were used to compare differences in baseline characteristics and treatment between women and men. Multiple and logistic regression was performed to adjust for age. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess sex difference in outcome and treatment. Heterogeneity in treatment was evaluated by including an interaction term. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs). The significance level was set at $p<0.05$ in the multivariate analysis. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software.  

# Ethics  

The study was approved by the Regional Committee for Ethics in Medical Research (2011/2435) and by the Norwegian Medicines Agency. All the participants signed written consent before entering the study.  

# RESULTS  

Out of 1100 included study participants, $40\%$ (440) were women, with a mean age of $67.9\pm17.2$ years. There was no difference in the mean age between sexes $(p=0.95^{\circ})$ , however, there was a higher proportion of women in the age group ${>}80$ years compared with men $19\%$ vs. $14\%$ ; $p=0.01$ ).  

Women had a lower burden of cardiovascular risk factors due to a lower prevalence of diabetes mellitus $11\%$ vs. $15\%$ ; $p=0.05$ ), had a higher mean high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level $(1.7\pm0.6\$ vs. $1.3\pm0.4\mathrm{mmol/L}$ ; $p<0.001$ ), and comprised a higher proportion that had never smoked ( $45\%$ vs. 33; $p<0.001$ ). Further, fewer women had a history of ischemic heart disease ( $7\%$ vs. $17\%$ ; $p<0.001$ [Table 1]). The results did not change after adjusting for age.  

There were no differences in time from onset of symptoms to admission to hospital $91.6\pm55.1$ vs. $95.3\pm51.5$ min; $p=0.29.$ ), or door to needle time $38.6\pm24.5$ vs. $37.0\pm21.6\min$ ; $p=0.31$ ). Women had significantly higher NIHSS score on admission compared with men ${\cdot}6.2\pm5.6$ vs. $5.3\pm5.1$ ; $p=0.01$ ) and more frequent motor limb function symptoms ( $65.2\%$ vs. $54.0\%$ ; $p{<}0.001$ ), and the proportion with moderate or severe stroke (NIHSS score ${>}5$ ) was higher among women compared to men $40.7\%$ vs. 32.4; $p=0.006\$ ). There were no sex differences in atypical stroke symptoms, such as level of consciousness $5.9\%$ vs. $5.5\%$ ; $p=0.49$ ) or disorientation $(23.6\%$ vs. $19.4\%$ ; $p=0.18^{\prime}$ ). There were significantly more stroke mimics $(21.1\%$ vs. $14.7\%$ ; $p=0.01$ ) in women (defined as patients with a different diagnosis at discharge than ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack).  

There were no sex differences in clinical care, including the proportion receiving additional endovascular treatment in case of large vessel occlusion (Tables S1-S2). Women were as frequently as men directly discharged to their home ( $65\%$ vs. $66\%$ ; $p=0.89$ ), regardless of $\mathsf{mRS}$ score.  

There were no sex differences in excellent functional outcome (mRS scores 0-1) at 3 months (OR [women vs. men] 0.76, $95\%$ CI $0.60\substack{-0.98}$ ; $p=0.68_{.}$ ), and there was no heterogeneity in treatment effects (alteplase vs. tenecteplase) between sexes. There were no differences between men and women in the secondary outcomes (Table 2 and Table S3).

# DISCUSSION  

Only $40\%$ of participants in NOR-TEST were women, which is in line with the Alteplase versus Tenecteplase for Thrombolysis after Ischemic Stroke Study (ATTEST) [7], but slightly lower than the number of women in the Norwegian National Stroke Registry $(45\%)$ [8], and lower than in the International Stroke Trial-3 (IST-3), which included $52\%$ women [9]. Several factors can explain these differences. First, over half of the patients included in the IST-3 trial were older than 80 years. Randomizing women in acute settings may be more challenging as they tend to be older at stroke onset, more often live alone and are more likely to have impaired premorbid function [1] In NOR-TEST, frail patients with premorbid impaired function (mRS score ${\ge}3$ ) were excluded. Hence, we cannot rule out that a higher proportion of women did not meet the inclusion criteria. Another potential cause of a lower proportion of women in the study may be the delayed recognition of acute stroke symptoms in women [10,11], as arrival at the hospital later than $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ from the onset of symptoms excluded patients from participating in our study. Awareness of symptoms, marital status with a “surviving spouses” effect and social factors are contributing factors affecting time from symptom onset to admission [10], and could explain some of the observed differences. However, among the recruited patients in NOR-TEST, we did not observe any sex differences in time from onset of symptoms to hospital admission. A higher NIHSS score at admission among women supports the notion/possibility that women may neglect and underreport acute stroke symptoms.  

The observed differences in cardiovascular risk burden are in line with other studies [12,13]. Historically, men have had higher tobacco consumption than women [14] and a lower prevalence of diabetes [15]. Further, women in the fertile age range have a lower risk of developing atherosclerosis and coronary heart disease compared to men at the same age. This may be attributable to differences in vascular biology, including endothelial function [16], as well as the protective effect of oestrogen [17]. Contrary to what is usually described, we did not see any sex differences in the prevalence of atrial fibrillation (AF). While women tend to have a lower incidence of AF, the prevalence of AF in older age is greater in women due to their longevity, which results in a fairly even distribution between the sexes overall [18]. The relatively low mean age of the study population, combined with a higher proportion of women aged above 80 years, may explain the equalized prevalence of AF in the study population.  

Thrombolysis, irrespective of agent (tenecteplase/alteplase), was equally effective and safe in both women and men in our population. Women also received the same diagnostic workup and treatment as men.  

There are several study limitations. First, the study was not designed to explore sex differences, and not powered to answer whether tenecteplase is equal to alteplase in subgroups of patients. Secondly, due to a lack of screening logs, we cannot identify the exact reasons for the lower number of women. Third, the homogeneity of the study population selection makes our findings less generalizable, but still relevant. However, the major strength of the study is that we present data on sex differences in a welldescribed population recruited in an acute setting. Our results confirm that there are sex differences in acute stroke medicine studies.  

In conclusion, there were fewer women available for thrombolysis in the NOR-TEST study population, and women differed from men in risk profile, stroke symptoms and stroke mimic occurrence. There were no sex differences in in-hospital work-up or effect and safety of tenecteplase.
