Meta-analysis Background. – Minimally invasive puncture and conventional craniotomy are  both utilized  in  the  treatment of spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage. The  purpose of  this  study is  to  review evidence  that compares the safety and  effectiveness of  these  two  techniques.   
Methods. –  We searched EMBASE, Cochrane Library,  Web of  Science, and  PubMed  for  studies published between 2000  and  2019  that  compared the minimally invasive puncture procedure with  the conventional craniotomy for the  treatment of  spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage.   
Results. – Seven  trials  (2  randomized control trials  and  5  observational studies) with a total of  970 patients were  included. The  odds  ratio  indicated a statistically significant difference between the  minimally  invasive puncture and  conventional craniotomy in  terms  of  good functional outcome (OR  2.36, $90\%$ CI 1.24–4.49). The  minimally invasive puncture procedure was  associated with lower  mortality rates (OR 0.61, $90\%$ CI 0.44–0.85) and rebleeding rates  (OR  0.48, $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.24–0.99$ ; $P=0.003$ ).   
Conclusions. – The  use  of  the  minimally invasive puncture for  the  management of spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage was  associated with  better  functional outcome results, a  lower  mortality rate, and decreased rebleeding rates.  However, because insufficient data  has  been  published thus far, we  need more robust evidence to  provide a better  guide  for  future  management.

# 1. Introduction  

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a stroke subtype with a 30- day mortality rate of $35{-}52\%$ , and there are about two million cases worldwide per year [1–3]. Patients that survive an ICH often have severe neurological impairments. The mass of the hematoma can increase intracranial pressure and lead to herniation. Some studies found that the cerebral flow and metabolic rate for oxygen were dramatically decreased around the perihematomal zone, which could further increase brain damage and tissue swelling [4]. In addition, the release of clot components can cause an inflammatory reaction, brain edema, and excitotoxicity [1]. These reactions will deteriorate the neurological status of patients with ICH. Therefore, clot reduction or removal plays an important role in alleviating brain edema and neurological injury, which can improve the functional outcome of patients following an ICH.  

Minimally invasive puncture (MIP) and conventional craniotomy (CC) are both commonly utilized surgical techniques for spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage. MIP is an innovative technique. It is less invasive, and there is little damage to brain tissue. However, hemostasis cannot be achieved under direct vision during the procedure, and this may increase the risk of rebleeding or hematoma enlargement after an operation. Moreover, drainage tube placement may also increase the risk of infection [5,6]. Conventional craniotomy is a standard technique for treating ICH long term. The hematoma can be removed quickly, and the high intracranial pressure can be relieved promptly. However, the procedure itself can also damage brain tissue [7]. Thus, we analyzed metadata from systematically identified studies to determine if there was an advantage in functional outcomes and mortality using MIP versus CC in the treatment of spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage.  



# 2. Methods  



# 2.1. Search strategy  

The EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and PubMed databases were searched between January 2000 to December 2019. The search terms used were: (puncture or craniopuncture or surgical evacuation or aspiration or minimally invasive or catheter drainage or craniotomy) AND (intracerebral or intracranial or intra

parenchymal or basal ganglia or spontaneous supratentorial) AND (hemorrhage or hematoma). Reference lists of all selected reports were also reviewed to identify additional eligible manuscripts.  



# 2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria  

Two investigators (W.L.D and J.C.L) independently identified relevant studies using the above search strategy, and they reviewed all titles and abstracts. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion. The inclusion criteria used to select from the pool of studies were:  

• feature spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage confirmed by CT scan, and (2) studies comparing MIP versus CC in patients for spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage. The exclusion criteria were: (1) hemorrhage caused by trauma, aneurysms, arteriovenous malformation, or secondary to coagulopathy or ischemic infarction, (2) infratentorial intracerebral hemorrhage, and (3) case reports, editorials, review articles, letters to the editor, and animal experimental studies were excluded.  



# 2.3. Data abstraction and synthesis  

The following information for the selected studies were extracted: first author, publication year, study design, study population characteristics (age, volume of hematoma, number of patient and gender), glasgow coma scale (GCS) on admission, follow-up, and outcome measures.  

The primary outcome chosen was mortality rates at the end of the follow-up. Secondary outcomes were outcome and perioperative complication: (1) good functional outcome, defined by certain activities of daily living scales. Barthel index $>60$ , glasgow outcome scale $({\bfGOS})\le3$ and modified rankin score $(\mathrm{mRS})\geq2$ were the cutoff points of dependence in certain activities of daily living; (2) rebleeding rates were assessed after the operation; (3) occurrence of digestive tract hemorrhage; and (4) occurrence of pulmonary infection.  



# 2.4. Quality assessment and statistical analysis  

The methodological quality of observational studies was assessed by the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which consists of three components: patient selection, comparability of the study groups, and number of outcomes reported [8]. A score of 0–9 was allocated to each study, and each score is represented by one star. Studies achieving 6 or more stars were considered high quality studies. The quality of the randomized control trials (RCTs) was assessed by the Jadad Scores [9], and scores equal to or less than 2 points were defined as low quality (Table 1).  

The meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.0 software (The Cochrane Collaboration). The odds ratio (OR) was used to for the statistical analysis of dichotomous variables, which represented the odds of an event occurring in the MIP group compared to the CC group and approximates the relative risk. Pooled OR were reported with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined as a $P$ value less than 0.05.  

Heterogeneity between studies was evaluated by the $I^{2}$ statistic. If an $I^{2}$ statistic greater than $50\%$ presented a high heterogeneity between studies, the random-effects model was used to quantify the heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses were performed for high quality studies. Otherwise, the fixed-effects model was applied for this meta-analysis. A Funnel plot was used to assess the presence of publication bias [10].  



# 3. Results  

Seven studies [5,6,11–15], which include 970 cases (509 cases for MIP and 461 cases for CC), fulfilled the predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1). The study characteristics and patients’ demographic details are shown in Table 2. Among the included studies, there are 2 RCTs [6,13], 3 retrospective studies [5,12,14], and 2 prospective studies [11,15]. Results of meta-analysis comparison of MIP and CC were shown in Table 3.  

Pooling the data of six studies [5,6,11,13–15], we  assessed the mortality rate. There was a significant difference favoring the MIP group (OR 0.61, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.44–0.85; $P=0.003$ ). The heterogeneity test showed slight significant differences in each study $(I^{2}=26)$ . Thus, the fixed-effects model was applied, as shown in Fig. 2.  

All of the seven studies [5,6,11–15] assessed the outcomes at the end of the follow-up. However, two studies [5,6] did not use binary outcomes to compare the outcome between the MIP group and the CC group. Thus, we cannot extract data for analysis. The other five studies assessed the outcomes of 762 patients. There was a significant difference in good functional outcomes, favoring the MIP group (OR 2.36, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 1.24–4.49; $P=0.0009$ ). There was high heterogeneity between studies $(I^{2}=69\%)$ , as shown in Fig. 3.  

All seven of the included studies [5,6,11–15] investigated rebleeding following operation in 970 cases. The results showed that the MIP group demonstrated a significant reduction in the OR of rebleeding compared the CC group (OR 0.48, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.24–0.99; $P=0.003$ ; Fig. 4), and the heterogeneity was moderate $(I^{2}=50\%)$ .  

The perioperative digestive tract hemorrhage data were available for 728 patients across four studies [6,13–15]. The digestive tract hemorrhage rate was higher in the CC group than in the MIP group, but the difference was not statistically significant (OR 0.67, $95\%\operatorname{CI}0.31-1.47$ ; $P{=}0.32$ ; Fig. 5). The between-study heterogeneity was high $(I^{2}=68\%)$ ).  

Perioperative pulmonary infection was reported in five studies [6,12–15] that investigated 703 patients (Fig. 6). MIP was associated with a lower overall postoperative pulmonary infection rate compared to the CC group, but this difference was not statistically significant (OR 0.63, $95\%$ CI 0.36–1.11; $P{=}0.11$ ). There was high heterogeneity $(I^{2}=64\%)$ .  

All studies included in the meta-analysis were subjected to sensitivity analysis by deleting each individual study to evaluate the influence of the study on the outcomes (Table 4). There was no change in the significance of any of the outcomes. The degree of between-study heterogeneity decreased dramatically for rebleeding rates and pulmonary infection rates. However, the between-study heterogeneity remained moderate for perioperative perioperative digestive tract hemorrhage rates and good functional outcome rates. The results indicated that no individual study significantly affected the pooled ORs.  

A funnel plot of the studies included in this meta-analysis that reported mortality rate are shown in Fig. 7. All studies were within the $95\%$ CIs, and they demonstrated a normal distribution, which suggests no obvious publication bias.  



# 4. Discussion  

This meta-analysis of 2 RCTs [6,13] and 5 non-RCTs [5,11,12,14,15] that included 970 patients compared the efficacy and safety of the minimally invasive puncture and conventional craniotomy techniques. The results indicated that MIP was safe because it had significantly lower mortality rates, better functional outcomes, and reduced rebleeding rates. We  found no significant difference in perioperative digestive tract hemorrhage and pulmonary infection rates.

The complications caused by intracerebral hemorrhage are among the major predictors of early mortality and poor outcome. These complications include hematoma expansion, intraventricular extension of hemorrhage with hydrocephalus, brain edema, seizures, and increased intracranial pressure, which have been shown to be associated with early neurological deterioration and poor outcome [16,17]. Because of the physical and toxic effects of the hematoma, several clinical trials have been performed to evaluate the effects of clot removal. STICH II was a randomized trial that did not find significant evidence to support the hypothesis that early surgery could improve the outcome in conscious patients with superficial intracerebral hematoma compared to a conservative treatment. However, the study did confirm that early surgery to move the clot might have a small, but clinically relevant, survival advantage, and it did not increase the rate of disability or death after 6 months [18]. The present study indicates that the minimally invasive puncture procedure has a low mortality rate and could improve the functional outcome of patients with intracerebral hematoma. This finding is consistent with the MISTIE study, which is a randomized, controlled, open-label, phase 2 trial. This study demonstrated that minimally invasive surgery is safe in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage, and it lead to the addition of surgical management as a therapeutic strategy for intracerebral hemorrhage [19].  

For the application of any surgical procedure, the rebleeding rate following operation is always of paramount importance. Rebleeding after surgery can be life-threatening, especially for intracerebral hemorrhage [20,21]. Theoretically, the advantage of the conventional craniotomy is that the clot is removed under direct vision and bleeding can be stopped effectively. Conversely, the minimally invasive puncture procedure cannot achieve hemostasis under direct vision. However, in our study, the pooled analysis showed a lower postoperative bleeding rate than the conventional craniotomy. Shi et al. suggests this was due to a rapid decrease in pressure in the hematoma cavity caused by the removal of the hematoma and bone flap during the craniotomy procedure. The sharp fluctuation in intracranial pressure could potentially cause rebleeding of the ruptured vessels. But, after stereotactic catheter drainage for the hematoma, the increased intracranial pressure gradually decreased. In turn, a specific pressure was maintained in the ruptured vessels. The mechanism of the slow reduction in pressure was attributed to the prevention of rebleeding events [12].  

One of the severe complications following acute cerebral hemorrhage is gastrointestinal bleeding. The mortality rate of patients with intracerebral hemorrhage complicated with gastrointestinal bleeding is as high as $87.9\%$ [22]. Nosocomial pneumonia is another frequent complication among spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage patients, and it is associated with a three-fold increase in mortality [23]. Therefore, early identification and prevention of gastrointestinal hemorrhage and pneumonia is crucial for patients with intracerebral hemorrhage, and it may effectively reduce the mortality rate. However, the pooled analysis of the postoperative gastrointestinal hemorrhage rate and pulmonary infection rate did not indicate a statistically significant difference between MIP and CC.  

The present meta-analysis study has certain limitations. First, all of the studies included were observational, except for two RCTs of low quality. These RCT studies did not provide adequate random sequence generation and blinding, which can increase the risk of bias. Second, these studies were carried out in medical centers that probably had different levels of surgical expertise and varying protocols. According to a phase III clinical trial, the incidences of poor catheter placement that require replacement trended inversely with the experience of the surgeon. Fully qualified neurosurgeons were found to have lower rates of poor catheter placement compared to prequalified neurosurgeons, although it did not reach statistical significance [24]. Third, there was significant heterogeneity for the good functional outcomes, digestive tract hemorrhage rates, and pulmonary infection rates. The differences in patient age, sample size, volume of hematoma, location of hematoma, status of patients on admission, and other factors might be responsible for the high heterogeneity. When we  did the sensitivity analysis to eliminate the heterogeneity, we found that the study carried out by Shi et al. had a significant impact on heterogeneity. The patients included in their study had a Glasgow Coma Scale score $\leq8$ and hematoma volume $\geq30~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ , and the severity of these patients was worse than other studies. Finally, all studies included in this meta-analysis were reported in Asia. Six studies [5,6,12–15] were reported by Chinese clinical centers, and one [11] was reported by a South Korean clinical center.  

Given the limitations of the present meta-analysis, we cannot reach the conclusion that MIP is superior to CC as a treatment for spontaneous supratentorial hemorrhage. In addition to China, some studies in western countries have also demonstrated the safety and effectiveness of MIP [19,24,25]. However, the effectiveness of CC as a classic treatment for intracerebral hemorrhage was also beyond doubt, especially for patients that require an emergency decompressive craniectomy [7]. While we did not provide a guide, we have drawn together the available evidence and attempted to highlight key factors that neurosurgeons should consider when they choose between these treatment options for an appropriate treatment strategy.  



# 5. Conclusion  

This meta-analysis showed that the minimally invasive puncture procedure may be associated with lower mortality rates, decreased rebleeding rates, and a better functional outcome. The two surgical techniques appeared to be equivalent in terms of the perioperative digestive tract hemorrhage rates and pneumonia rates. However, given the inherent limitations of the included studies and despite our rigorous methodology, we  still cannot reach definitive conclusions. In the future, additional well-designed and large-volume RCTs are required to update and confirm the findings of the present analysis.
