# White Matter Hyperintensities and Blood Pressure Lowering in Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage: A Secondary Analysis of the ATACH‑2 Trial

# Abstract  

Background:  It is not clear whether subsets of patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) benefit from inten‑ sive blood pressure (BP) lowering. We evaluated whether white matter hyperintensities (WMH) burden influences response to this therapy.  

Methods:  Retrospective secondary analysis of the Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage 2 trial. Patients were randomized to intensive (systolic BP target: $110{-}139\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg)}$ versus standard (systolic BP target: $140{-}179\mathrm{mmHg};$ ) BP treatment with intravenous nicardipine within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ from onset between May 2011 and Sep‑ tember 2015. WMH were rated on magnetic resonance images (fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences), defining moderate–severe WMH as total Fazekas scale score $\geq3$ (range 0–6). The main outcome was death or major disability at 90 days (modified Rankin scale ≥ 3). The secondary outcome was ICH expansion, defined as hematoma growth $>33\%$ from baseline to follow-up CT scan. Predictors of the outcomes of interest were explored with multivari‑ able logistic regression.  

Results:  A total of 195/1000 patients had MRI images available for analysis, of whom 161 $(82.6\%)$ had moderate– severe WMH. When compared to patients with none–mild WMH, those with moderate–severe WMH did not have an increased risk of death or major disability (adjusted relative risk: 1.83, $95\%$ CI 0.71–4.69) or ICH expansion (adjusted relative risk: 1.14, $95\%$ CI 0.38–3.37). WMH burden did not modify the effect of intensive BP treatment on outcome (all $p$ for interaction $\ge0.2$ ).  

Conclusion:  The majority of acute ICH patients have moderate–severe WMH, but advanced small vessel disease burden marked by WMH does not influence ICH-related outcomes or response to intensive BP reduction.  

Keywords:  Stroke, Intracerebral hemorrhage, Small vessel disease, White matter hyperintensities, Leukoaraiosis, Blood pressure  

# Introduction  

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) on magnetic resonance imagining (MRI) are believed to mark underlying cerebral small vessel disease [1]. Patients with advanced WMH may have adverse outcomes from intensive blood pressure (BP) lowering, due to impaired cerebral autoregulation from long-standing hypertension [2–4]. The presence of advanced white matter lesions rated on noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT) has been shown to predict poor prognosis in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) but did not modify the effect of BP lowering on outcome [5]. Furthermore, the biological mechanisms underlying the association between white matter lesions and unfavorable prognosis remain unclear [5]. In the Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage 2 (ATACH-2) trial, intensive BP treatment (systolic $\mathrm{BP}<140\mathrm{mmHg},$ compared to standard BP treatment did not reduce the primary outcome of death or major disability in acute ICH patients. It is possible that cerebral small vessel disease marked by advanced neuroimaging markers, such as WMH, may identify subgroups that respond differently to intensive BP reduction. In this secondary analysis of the ATACH-2 trial, we sought to: (1) characterize the prevalence, associated factors and prognostic significance of WMH in acute ICH patients and (2) assess whether any effect of BP reduction on outcome is modified by the patient’s small vessel disease burden marked by moderate–severe WMH evaluated on magnetic resonance images.  

# Methods  

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the dataset from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to the corresponding author.  

# Patients Selection  

We performed a secondary analysis of the ATACH-2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01176565) [6]. Participating sites received approval from local institutional review board (IRB) for all aspects of the study, and all patients or their legally authorized representative provided written informed consent [7]. Subjects with ICH were randomized to receive intensive (systolic BP target: $110{-}139\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg})$ versus standard (systolic BP target: $140{-}179\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg})$ BP lowering with intravenous nicardipine within $4.5\mathrm{~h~}$ from symptom onset. ATACH-2 participants were eligible for the current analysis if they had an interpretable fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence available for central review as part of their clinical brain MRI obtained during their initial hospitalization.  

# Image Acquisition and Analysis Computed Tomography  

Baseline and 24-h follow-up ICH volumes were calculated with semiautomated volumetric analysis (Analyze Direct 11.0 Software) on axial NCCT images obtained with local acquisition protocols. ICH expansion was defined as hematoma growth $>33\%$ from baseline to follow-up scan [7]. NCCT images were also reviewed for determination of ICH location (deep or lobar) and presence of intraventricular bleeding (IVH).  

# Magnetic Resonance Imaging  

WMH were rated as per the Fazekas scale on FLAIR images [8]. Periventricular WMH and deep WMH were individually graded as 0–1 (none–mild) and 2–3 (moderate–severe) on the opposite side of the bleeding. Total Fazekas scale was calculated as the sum of periventricular and deep WMH and coded as 0–2 (none–mild WMH) and 3–6 (moderate–severe WMH). Multiple raters analyzed all the MRI images (AS primary rater, AM, JOF, FS secondary raters). Other MRI markers of interest included diffusion-weighted image hyperintense lesions, and cerebral microbleeds evaluated according to previously proposed diagnostic criteria [9–12]. The primary rater (AS) has previously demonstrated good inter-rater reliability ( $90\%$ agreement, Cohen $\kappa0.73$ ) for WMH using the Fazekas scale in a separate cohort [13]. All the raters analyzing NCCT and MRI images were blinded to treatment randomization, clinical variables and outcomes of interest. Discrepancies in imaging ratings were discussed with joint reanalysis of controversial images and adjudicated by consensus.  

# Outcomes of Interest  

The primary outcome was the presence of death or major disability (modified Rankin scale ${>}3$ ) at 3  months from the index event. ICH expansion was the secondary outcome of interest of our study.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) based on their distribution and compared using $t$ test or Kruskal–Wallis test as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as count (percentage) and compared using Chisquare or Fisher exact test. The association between WMH, hematoma expansion and functional outcome was explored with generalized linear models with log-link function with Poisson distribution (rather than binomial distribution, because of convergence issues) with adjustment for the effects of known predictors of outcome included in the ICH score (age, baseline Glasgow Coma Scale score, the presence or absence of IVH at baseline, ICH volume) [14] and other covariates identified with the results of univariable analysis. Interaction between WMH and BP treatment on death/major disability was assessed. Analyses followed the intent-to-treat paradigm. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at the 0.05 level. Analyses were performed with statistical software (SAS, version 9.4; SAS Institute).  

# Results  

A total of 195/1000 $(19.5\%)$ patients enrolled in ATACH-2 between May 2011 and September 2015 had images available to assess for WMH and were included in this analysis. Of those excluded, the majority did not undergo MRI, and 18 participants were enrolled at centers where the IRB did not approve central review of MRIs. Subjects included in this analysis had lower National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores, lower frequency of IVH and deep hemorrhages and had a more favorable functional outcome at 3 months compared with the excluded patients, as summarized in Table  1 in the supplementary material.  

Table  1 shows the clinical and laboratory characteristics of the study population. Overall, 161 $(82.6\%)$ had moderate–severe WMH. Compared with patients with none–mild WMH, subjects with moderate–severe WMH were significantly older (mean age 62.6 vs. 57.1  years, $p{=}0.02,$ ). Table 2 summarizes the neuroimaging features of the study population stratified by WMH burden. Subjects with moderate–severe WMH had higher cerebral microbleeds burden (median count 2 vs. 1, $p{=}0.006)$ , whereas the remaining NCCT and MRI findings were similar between the two groups.  

In univariable analysis, patients with moderate–severe WMH had a twofold increase in the relative risk (RR) of death or major disability compared to subjects with none–mild WHM ( $15.5\%$ vs. $31.3\%$ , unadjusted RR 2.01). However, this association was not statistically significant after adjustment for potential confounders in multivariable analysis (adjusted RR 1.83, $95\%$ CI 0.71, 4.69), as shown in Table 3. Multivariable analysis showed also that the risk of ICH expansion was not significantly increased in patients with moderate–severe WMH (adjusted RR 1.14, $95\%$ CI 0.38, 3.37). All these results were confirmed in secondary analysis with WMH stratified by location (deep vs. periventricular). Figure 1 illustrates the effect of intensive BP lowering on the risk of death or major disability at 90  days, stratified by WMH severity and location. We observed no evidence of a significant interaction between WMH status and the effect of intensive BP reduction on the primary outcome of death or major disability (all $p$ for interaction $\ge0.2$ ).  

# Discussion  

In this secondary analysis of a well-characterized clinical trial population, ICH patients with moderate–severe WMH did not have an increased risk of death or major disability at 90 days from the index event. We also found no evidence of an interaction between the extent and topography of WMH and the effects of intensive BP reduction on the outcome of death or major disability.  

The lack of association between WMH and outcome in our study may simply reflect the absence of adequate statistical power because of the limited sample size or lack of variability in moderate–severe WMH, which was present in $83\%$ of participants. Another possibility is that our results were influenced by selection bias in who received MRI [15]. Less than one in five patients enrolled in ATACH-2 underwent MRI and compared to those who did not these subjects were less severely affected as highlighted by lower NIHSS scores and frequency of intraventricular bleeding and were more likely to have favorable functional outcome at 3  months. A secondary analysis of a different large clinical trial cohort (INTERACT-2, $n=2069.$ ) showed that leukoaraiosis assessed on NCCT with the van Swieten scale was an independent predictor of poor prognosis but did not modify the effect of BP lowering on functional outcome [5]. Besides statistical power, the different sensitivity of NCCT and MRI for detecting white matter lesions between ATACH-2 and INTERACT-2 may also explain this discrepancy [16, 17]. Our results are consistent with the lack of association reported with cerebral microbleeds, and clinical outcomes in ATACH-2 [12]. We speculate that ICH subjects with white matter disease visible on NCCT may have a more advanced cerebral small vessel disease burden that is not captured simply by indirect comparisons of visualized rating scales between MRI and NCCT, which rely on topographic distributions of white matter disease rather than the degree of hyperintensity or hypodensity seen within a given region/voxel. Moreover, both studies were limited by the lack of volumetric WMH measurements.  

The observation that patients with advanced WMH do not have an increased risk of ICH expansion is consistent with previously published studies. In a large retrospective study, WMH volume quantified with volumetric analysis on MRI was not associated with ICH expansion in deep or lobar hemorrhages (OR 1.00, $p{=}0.77$ and OR 1.01, $p{=}0.47$ , respectively) [18]. Similar findings were reported in a NCCT-based study where leukoaraiosis graded with van Swieten scale did not correlate with the odds of ICH growth [19]. Taken together, all these findings support the hypothesis that ICH expansion is not the biological mechanism underlying the previously described negative prognostic impact of white matter disease in ICH patients and that surrounding brain tissue gliosis/rarefaction does not facilitate hematoma growth.

To our knowledge, this secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial is the first study exploring a possible interaction between location and severity of WMH evaluated on MRI and response to systolic BP treatment in the acute phase of ICH. In line with a secondary analysis of the INTERACT-2 trial, we also observed that intensive BP lowering is safe in patients with advanced white matter disease [5]. This may have important implications for clinical practice and future clinical trials, eliminating the concern that patients with advanced WMH may be more prone to develop adverse effects from intensive BP reduction because of impaired cerebral autoregulation.

Another interesting observation of our study is the inverse relationship between WMH burden and platelet count. Higher mean platelet volume may explain this finding as it is inversely correlated with platelet count and previous studies suggested an association with leukoaraiosis in healthy elderly subjects [20]. However, the unadjusted association between platelet count and leukoaraiosis does not necessarily represent causality.  

Platelet count decrease is a known age-related change, and therefore, a lower platelet count in patients with moderate–severe WMH may be an epiphenomenon of higher age [21].  

Some limitations should be considered in the interpretation of our findings. The main limitation of this study is the presence of selection bias, reducing the generalizability of our observations, as subjects not receiving an MRI were more severely affected. Furthermore, our study cohort may not be representative of the ICH population seen in everyday clinical practice also because the ATACH-2 trial excluded patients with very large hemorrhages and severe reduction in admission Glasgow Coma Scale [7]. The relatively small sample size of our cohort is another important limitation, and confirmation of our findings in a larger population with adequate statistical power is needed. Third, volumetric analysis of WMH might be a more reliable and accurate method to quantify the extent of white matter disease, compared to Fazekas scale [22]. Finally, the small vessel disease underlying the bleeding (hypertensive arteriopathy versus cerebral amyloid angiopathy) [23] may have influenced our results, but we were underpowered to explore this possibility because only a minority of patients enrolled in ATACH-2 with MRI images available suffered a lobar hemorrhage.

# Conclusion  

In this secondary analysis of the ATACH-2 trial, we did not observe an association between WMH and the risk of unfavorable functional outcome. Subjects with advanced WMH did not have higher odds of ICH expansion. The effect of intensive BP reduction (SBP target $<140\mathrm{mmHg})$ in the acute phase of ICH did not differ between individuals with none–mild WHM and those with moderate– severe WMH.
