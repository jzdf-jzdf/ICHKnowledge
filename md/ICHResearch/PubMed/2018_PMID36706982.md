# Outcomes of Combined Endoscopic Surgery and Fibrinolytic Treatment Protocol for Intraventricular Hemorrhage: A Randomized Controlled Trial  

Raywat Noiphithak, Warot Ratanavinitkul, Vich Yindeedej, Pree Nimmannitya, Pornchai Yodwisithsak - BACKGROUND: Intraventricular fibrinolysis (IVF) and endoscopic surgery (ES) are the new promising treatment strategies to enhance the rate of hematoma clearance, which might improve functional outcome. This study investigated and compared the outcomes among these interventions.  

- METHODS: A randomized (1:1) double-blinded trial was carried out between August 2018 and December 2021. The intervention and control groups comprised patients receiving IVF and/or ES and external ventricular drainage (EVD), respectively. All participants had experienced primary or secondary intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) from spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage with obstructive hydrocephalus complications. The primary outcome was modified Rankin Scale score 180 days post treatment. Interim assessments were planned for every 50 participants enrolled to ensure safety and efficacy.  

- RESULTS: After enrollment of 110 participants (55 participants in each group), there was a difference in 30-day mortality (2 $[3.6\%]$ vs. 13 $[32.7\%]$ in the EVD group, $P=$ 0.002), reaching the predetermined boundaries for termination of the trial. We demonstrated a better favorable outcome (modified Rankin Scale score 0e3) at 180 days in the intervention group, compared with the control group (35 $[63.6\%]$ vs. 24 [43.6%], $P=0.04)$ . Participants in the intervention group experienced a higher IVH removal rate $(91\%$ [9.0] vs. $69.5\%$ [38.0], $P<0.01$ ) and had lower shunt conversion (1 $[1.8\%]$ vs. 16 $[29.3\%]$ , $P<0.01\$ ). Treatment complications were comparable between the two groups.  

- CONCLUSIONS: This study demonstrated that combined ES and IVF is safe and effective for the treatment of IVH. In addition, it concluded that aggressive but safe procedures used to remove IVH could improve clinical outcome in patients with IVH.

Several studies have reported a strong relationship between the degree of IVH removal and treatment outcomes.9 Accordingly, favorable outcomes have been reported after intraventricular clot removal through intraventricular fibrinolysis (IVF) and endoscopic surgery (ES).10-19 A previous large-scale trial showed that IVF with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator could reduce the 6-month mortality rate; however, there was no difference in functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score).20 Besides, ES has been found to increase the rate of hematoma clearance and improve outcomes.11,21-26 Moreover, ES has been shown to decrease the mortality rate and shunt conversion, as well as improve functional outcomes.27-29 Compared with IVF and EVD placement, ES allows a significantly higher clearance rate of hematoma.30-32 However, there is no current standard protocol for the use of these interventions.  

We proposed a combination of ES and IVF protocol for a large degree of IVH removal. This study aimed to conduct a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to evaluate the efficacy of a combination of ES and IVF for IVH compared with EVD placement alone as a standard treatment.  



# METHODS  

This RCT was approved by the local institutional review board following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was registered with Thai Clinical Trials Registry, TCTR20191122002. We enrolled participants between August 2018 and December 2021. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age $\scriptstyle\mathbf{I}8-80$ years; time from symptom onset to surgery ${<}24$ hours; newly diagnosed primary or secondary IVH from spontaneous ICH with ICH volume $<20~\mathrm{mL}$ ; obstructive hydrocephalus caused by a blood clot in the third or fourth ventricle; baseline systolic blood pressure (SBP) or mean arterial pressure $<200\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ or $<\mathbf{I}30\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg}$ , respectively; and mRS score ${\bfO-I}$ before ICH or IVH onset. The diagnosis of ICH was initially evaluated with computed tomography (CT) and was further assessed with magnetic resonance imaging and/or digital subtraction cerebral angiography, if indicated.  

The exclusion criteria were as follows: secondary IVH due to infratentorial hemorrhage, vascular anomalies, trauma, or bleeding tumors; Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score ${<}4$ or bilateral fixed dilated pupils; having undergone brain surgery within 3 months; uncorrectable coagulopathy; severe unstable medical conditions; and pregnancy.  



# Randomization  

After informed consent was obtained, participants were randomly assigned to undergo EVD placement (control group) or a combination of ES and IVF for clot removal (intervention group). A statistician created a randomization sequence using Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA) with 1:1 allocation through random block sizes of 2 and $4$ . Outcome measurements were collected by assessors blinded to the treatment allocation.  



# Procedure  

All participants were managed based on the American Heart Association recommendations for treating spontaneous ICH to stabilize the preoperative medical condition.33 Patients in the control group underwent EVD placement using a single or bilateral 3.1- mm silicone catheter inserted through the frontal burr hole at the side of the lateral ventricle with IVH. Participants in the intervention group underwent IVF and/or ES following our protocol (Figure 1). Specifically, they underwent IVF using recombinant tissue plasminogen activator at a dose of 1 mg at 8 hour intervals until the ventricular obstruction was opened or until 12 doses were administered. Additionally, participants with IVH volumes ${>}20$ mL underwent ES for clot removal. During the surgery, the surgical trajectory was planned with an image guidance system. Ventricular access was obtained using a single or bilateral frontal burr hole. After the dura was opened, a transparent sheath with a $4^{-\mathrm{mm}}$ endoscopic lens (Karl Storz Endoscopy, Tuttlingen, Germany) was inserted until it reached the ventricle. Subsequently, the IVH was removed from the lateral ventricles, foramen of Monro, and third ventricle, in that order. Bleeding was controlled using a monopolar suction coagulator. Attempts were made to open the obstructed ventricle and extensively remove the IVH. After hemostasis was achieved, warm saline irrigation was used to further remove the residual clot and explore the ventricle (Figure 2). Finally, EVD was placed into the ventricle and closure was performed in a routine manner.  



# Postoperative Management  

Participants were postoperatively managed in the neurosurgical intensive care unit for $\geq24$ hours and subsequently transferred to the general ward. EVD weaning was initiated on postoperative day 5 through daily water elevation by $5~\mathrm{cm}$ until it reached 20 cm. Next, the drain was clamped for 8 hours. In case of weaning failure, which was characterized by clinical deterioration and the presence of hydrocephalus, a permanent ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) was indicated. After discharge to home or referral, participants were scheduled for follow-up at 30, 90, and 180 days. During the follow-up visits, blinded assessors collected variables including the GCS score, mRS score, and other neurological functions.  



# Radiographic Analysis  

Postoperative brain CT was performed daily to evaluate ventricular obstruction, catheter position, and residual hematoma. Both IVH and ICH volumes were calculated as the sum of the surface areas of each slice to measure and compare the preoperative and postoperative clot volumes using OsiriX software (version 4.1, Pixmeo; Geneva, Switzerland).  



# Outcomes  

The primary outcome was the mRS score at 180 days, which was dichotomized into $_{0^{-}3}$ and $4^{-6}$ , with an mRS score ${\le}3$ indicating a favorable outcome. Additionally, mRS scores on days 30 and 90 were measured. The secondary outcomes included changes in IVH volume, VPS rate, complications, mortality, and duration of hospital stay.

# Statistical Analysis  

On the basis of the previous studies,20 we assumed that our combination protocol and EVD would yield favorable outcomes at a rate of $65\%$ and $45\%$ , respectively. A sample size of 210 patients (105 patients per group) would detect an $80\%$ power at a type I error rate of 0.05. Data were monitored by a blinded statistician to confirm efficacy and safety in the interim analyses after including 50, 100, and 150 participants. The analyses included between-group differences in favorable outcomes (mRS $_{0^{-3)}}$ at 6 months, meningitis, rebleeding, and mortality. The guidelines for trial discontinuation were based on the O’BrienFleming method to determine the critical boundaries for each interim test.34 The alpha level for each interim test was 0.00005, 0.0039, $\phantom{-}0.0\ I84$ , and $0.0412$ .

Continuous variables were presented as a median (interquartile range [IQR]) and were analyzed using independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, while categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and were analyzed using chisquare and Fisher exact tests. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to evaluate predictors of favorable outcomes. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (Version 14.1, StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Statistical significance was set at $\mathrm{~P~}<0.05$ for the 2-tailed test.  



# RESULTS  

We randomly allocated 110 participants to the intervention ${\mathfrak{n}}=$ 55) and control $({\mathfrak{n}}=55)$ groups. Figure 3 presents the flowchart for study enrollment. There was a significant difference in the 30-day mortality rate between the intervention and control groups (2 $\left[3.6\%\right]$ vs. 13 $[32.5\%]$ , relative risk [RR] 0.2, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] $0.1^{-0.7}$ , $\mathrm{P}=0.002$ ), with the mortality rate in the control group reaching the boundaries for trial termination. In intervention group, 2 patients died from neurologic death, whereas 13 patients in control group died: 6 from neurologic death, $4$ from severe infection, 2 from pulmonary embolism, and 1 from myocardial infarction.  

There was no significant between-group difference in the demographic characteristics (Table 1). Median ages in the intervention and control groups were $56.0$ (13) years and $56.0$ (18) years, respectively. Median GCS scores in the intervention and control groups were 8 (2) and $9\left(7\right)$ , respectively. There was no significant between-group difference in the preoperative IVH volume (16.4 [11.0] mL vs. 16.4 [17.1] mL, $\mathrm{P}=0.82$ ) and Graeb scores (6 [3] vs. 6 [3], $\mathrm{~P~}=\mathrm{~0.75}$ ). Median ICH volumes in the intervention and control groups were 9.6 (8.0) mL and 9.0 (10.8) mL, respectively $\textstyle(\mathrm{P}=0.52,$ ). In both groups, the most common ICH site was the thalamus (39 $\left[70.9\%\right]$ vs. $38[69.\mathrm{{I}}^{0}/\mathrm{{{0}}]}$ , $\mathrm{P}=0.58$ ).

# DISCUSSION  

This study was prematurely terminated given the significantly higher 30-day mortality rate in the control group $3.6\%$ vs. $32.5\%$ , $\mathrm{P}<0.0\mathrm{I}\$ , which reached the boundaries for trial termination. In addition, the mortality rate in the intervention group was significantly lower than the control group at all follow-up time points. The mortality rate of IVH in our study was consistent with previous reports.35 With respect to IVH treatment, the mortality rate after EVD is $24\%-5\Sigma\%,^{36,37}$ while that after ES and/or IVF is $5\%{-}\mathbf{I}4\%$ .20,27,38 The most common cause of death in our study was the neurological deterioration as a sequelae of severe IVH that was related to IVH volume. Currently, it remains unclear whether additional treatment, either with IVF or ES, improves outcomes in patients with IVH. Regarding the comparison between IVF and EVD, RCTs, including CLEAR III, have shown that IVF improves survival.20,38,39 However, there were inconsistent reports regarding the effects on functional outcomes. This could be attributed to the efficacy of clot reduction in IVF, which is dependent on a fibrinolytic agent regimen and ventricular catheter placement. CLEAR III found that a low percentage $\left(2\mathtt{I}^{0}/0\right)$ of participants achieved $80\%$ IVH removal based on their protocol, with the functional benefit being positively correlated with the reduction of IVH volume. Li et $a\vert^{27}$ reported that ES and IVF had evacuation rates of $88.9\%$ and $29{\cdot}4\%$ , respectively. Several studies have suggested that compared with IVF or EVD, ES might have better outcomes with respect to survival and prognosis, as well as fewer complications. $^{27,35,40,41}$ However, these results were inconclusive due to insufficient data. Therefore we established a new protocol that combined IVF regimen from CLEAR III with ES to improve IVH clearance. Compared with EVD alone, our combination protocol showed better clot reduction, resulting in improved survival and prognosis.  

The prognosis of IVH is strongly correlated with hematoma volume.42,43 Brain damage in IVH occurs as a result of acute obstructive hydrocephalus and elevated intracranial or periventricular inflammation causing neurologic deterioration in a delayed period.44 Thus, aggressive clearance of IVH is reasonable in order to improve outcome. For mRS outcome, this study showed that additional treatment for IVH removal improved functional outcome over time. There was significant difference in mRS outcome between the 2 groups at 180-day follow-up. The mRS distribution in Table 3 and Figure 4 showed that participants who received combined IVF and ES increasingly recovered during the entire follow-up, compared with those treated with EVD alone. However, the rate of severe disability was comparable between the 2 groups and remained constant during the follow-up period. All of these patients had severe clinical scores at the onset that might be irreversible neurological deficits.  

Thus, this reflected that early and larger clot reduction potentially ameliorated the sequelae of IVH and contributed to the better functional outcome.  

For complications, postoperative rebleeding occurred in $3.6\%$ in the intervention group, compared with none in the control group. However, all of these patients had minor bleeding and did not require further treatment. In contrast, there were $3\left(5{\cdot}5\%\right)$ patients in the control group who had EVD obstruction and required revision of the catheter, whereas the obstruction did not occur in the intervention group. In addition, the intervention group showed less shunt dependency than the control group $\mathrm{(r.8\%)}$ vs. $29.1\%$ , $\begin{array}{r}{\mathrm{P}<0.0\mathrm{I},}\end{array}$ ). The combination of IVF with ES has been shown to reduce the VPS rate. $^{26,27,35,45}$ In fact, the early EVD weaning in our study might have resulted in an increased shunt conversion rate. The VPS rate in our study $\left(\mathtt{I}5.5^{\mathrm{{0}}}/\mathrm{{0}}\right)$ was lower than that in previous reports $\left(\mathrm{\mathrm{I}}8\%{-}6\mathrm{\time4}^{0}\%\right)$ .20,45-47 Although high protein and red blood cells in CSF during the acute period of IVH may cause shunt obstruction, prolonged EVD or delayed shunt conversion can increase risk of catheter-related infection.48,49 Conversely, several studies have shown that blood products in CSF did not adversely affect shunt function in terms of obstruction and infection rates.50,51 Thus, early conversion of EVD to VPS can be safely performed even high proteinaceous and hemorrhagic CSF.52,53 Regarding this, we did not find that shunt malfunctions, including obstruction or infection, occurred with the early shunt conversion protocol used in this study. Moreover, delayed hydrocephalus required VPS and occurred less frequently in the intervention group compared with the control group ( $\mathrm{~I~}[\mathrm{{I}}.8\%]$ vs. 7 $\left[\tau2.7\%\right])$ . Again, this implied that effective IVH clearance could benefit ventricular obstruction in both short- and long-term settings.  

Multivariate analysis in this study revealed that the combined treatment, lower body mass index, lower SBP, and higher GCS were predictors of favorable outcomes, which was consistent with previous reports that treatment outcomes of IVH were associated with age, GCS, ICH and IVH volumes, and IVH extension.4,20,31 Although preoperative IVH volume or severity, measured by the IVH or Graeb scores, might affect treatment outcomes, these factors were not significant in our study. This could be attributed to our protocol’s considerable efficacy for IVH clearance resulting in higher clot reduction, compared with EVD alone.  

This study has several limitations. First, this was a small-scale single-center trial involving nonblinded surgeons, which could have led to selection bias. Second, the premature termination of the trial could have affected the results, which may differ from the results of the target sample size. Finally, the IVH clearance protocol was individualized. Currently, there remains no clear consensus regarding the use of ES and/or IVF for IVH treatment. Therefore, adopting our treatment protocol in daily practice should be considered.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

We proposed a treatment protocol combining ES with IVF for patients with IVH. Our protocol was safe and effective in reducing IVH volume, which allowed better survival and prognosis than EVD alone. However, this treatment strategy is not the standard care for IVH. Therefore large-scale multicenter RCTs are warranted to validate our findings. In addition, it is important to investigate other treatment strategies for improving IVH clearance and treatment outcomes in patients with IVH.
