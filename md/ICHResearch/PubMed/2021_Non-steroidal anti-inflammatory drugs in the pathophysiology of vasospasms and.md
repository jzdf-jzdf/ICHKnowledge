# Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in the pathophysiology of vasospasms and delayed cerebral ischemia following subarachnoid hemorrhage: a critical review  

Peter Solar $^{1,2,3}\textcircled{\textcircled{1}}$ & Zdenek Mackerle1,2 & Marek Joukal3 & Radim Jancalek1,2  

Received: 28 November 2019 / Revised: 3 February 2020 / Accepted: 19 February 2020 / Published online: 2 March 2020   
$\copyright$ Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2020  

# Abstract  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a life-threatening condition associated with the development of early brain injury (EBI) and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI). Pharmacological treatment of vasospasm following aSAH currently mainly comprises nimodipine administration. In the past few years, many drugs that can potentially benefit cases of subarachnoid hemorrhage have become available. The objective of this review is to critically assess the effects of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) following aSAH. A systematic literature review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. The search was aimed at studies addressing aSAH and NSAIDs during the 2010 to 2019 period, and it yielded 13 articles. Following the application of search criteria, they were divided into two groups, one containing 6 clinical articles and the other containing 7 experimental articles on animal models of aSAH. Inflammatory cerebral changes after aneurysm rupture contribute to the development of EBI, DCI and cerebral vasospasm. It appears that NSAIDs (especially coxibs) are even more effective in reducing vasospasm than nimodipine. Other beneficial effects of NSAIDs include reduction in mortality, improved functional outcome and increased hypoaggregability. However, despite these positive effects, there is only one randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial showing a tendency towards a better outcome with lower incidence of vasospasm or mortality in patients following aSAH.

# Introduction  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a severe type of stroke with significant morbidity and mortality, affecting between 0.5 and 28 people per 100,000 population worldwide, although this can vary regionally [7]. Although the pathophysiology of aSAH is not fully understood, an important initial event is early brain injury due to increased intracranial pressure. It results in global cerebral ischemia and a cascade of pathological changes including inflammation, lipid peroxidation, cell death, and disruption of the blood brain barrier [14, 37]. Patients who survive the initial bleeding can face secondary vasospasms and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) followed by focused neurological signs that contribute to the high morbidity and mortality [35]. However, the pathogenesis of DCI is not fully understood. Pharmacological treatment of aSAH is currently focused mainly on preventing DCI, and comprises nimodipine administrated intravenously or orally. Although nimodipine is a calcium antagonistic vasodilator, there is no convincing evidence that it can reduce either the incidence or the degree of cerebral vasospasm and thus reduce DCI [6]. The positive impact of nimodipine on clinical outcomes has been described in several studies indicating a cerebroprotective effect [28, 38, 39]. However, nimodipine can induce dose-related arterial hypotension and increase the risk of secondary ischemia [42, 51]. There is a growing body of evidence indicating that neuroinflammation plays a crucial role both in early and delayed pathophysiology of DCI [34]. Therefore, various anti-inflammatory drugs have been studied as supplementary treatment for the consequences of aSAH. This has meant an increased clinical interest in nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) that have been studied for their anti-inflammatory and vasodilatory effects and thus can be useful for the prevention and treatment of aSAH [2, 25, 29].  

It is known that all NSAIDs are essentially cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitors with different degrees of cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) inhibition that is considered a “side-effect.” Rofecoxib, etoricoxib, and lumiracoxib are NSAIDs with highly selective COX-2 inhibition, while nimesulide, meloxicam, diclofenac, and celecoxib are drugs with intermediate COX-2 selectivity. Ibuprofen and ketoprofen are drugs that inhibit COX-1 and COX-2 to similar extents, and are known as nonselective NSAIDs [3]. Experimental studies described NSAIDs effects mainly on cerebrovascular inflammation and vasospasm, while the clinical articles focused predominantly on the patient outcomes after aSAH. The primary objective of this review was to critically assess the effects of NSAIDs on the development of early brain injury and ischemic complications following aSAH.  

# Material and methods  

The PubMed and Web of Science databases were searched using the search expression “subarachnoid hemorrhage” AND “nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.” We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (see Supplementary Material for the PRISMA-P 2015 checklist) [30], and the search was focused on experimental or clinical articles in English over the period from 2010 to 2019. Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts to assess the relevance and full texts for adherence to eligibility criteria. A bibliography search of these articles led us to include other eligible experimental and clinical studies. Review articles, duplicates, case reports, non-English articles, and editorials were excluded. After a full-text review of all 106 articles, we chose thirteen studies that satisfied the following criteria: clinical studies describing the effects of NSAIDs in patients following aSAH or experimental studies using animal models to describe the effects of NSAIDs in the pathophysiology of aSAH (Fig. 1).  

# Results  

A systematic search of the PubMed and Web of Science databases identified 13 relevant original articles focused on the  

administration of NSAIDs following aSAH. The papers were divided into two subgroups: experimental studies on animal models (Table 1) and clinical studies (Table 2).  

# Animal studies describing NSAID effects following experimentally induced aSAH  

Experimental studies dealing with NSAIDs following aSAH focused mainly on cerebrovascular inflammation and vasospasm rather than acute or delayed brain reactions. The most attractive field of interest seems to be the effect of NSAIDs on the vascular wall following aSAH (Fig. 2).  

Celik et al. [4] put forward a theory that the inflammatory response induced by aSAH contributes to the development of vasospasms. They tested the effect of a COX-2 inhibitor on cerebral vessel diameter in the rat aSAH model and found that intramuscular administration of parecoxib significantly increased the mean cross-sectional area of the basilar artery compared to that in animals undergoing experimental aSAH without parecoxib $(p<0.05)$ or vehicle controls treated with intramuscular saline $(p<0.01)$ . Moreover, the thickness of the basilar artery wall in the parecoxib group was significantly lower than in the untreated SAH group $(p<0.01\$ ) or the vehicle control group $(p<0.05)$ suffering vasoconstriction.  

The effect of COX-2 inhibitors on the vascular wall was also studied by Mani et al. [26] who found that NSAIDs (especially the COX-2 inhibitor, celecoxib) can counteract the effect of vasoconstrictors (serotonin, endothelin, and vasopressin) on basilar artery myocytes by opening vascular voltage-gated potassium ion channel 7 (Kv7).  

Using the aSAH animal model, Mani et al. showed that suppression of Kv7 flux was a common effect of basilar artery spasmogens and opening the Kv7 potassium channel by celecoxib attenuated the aSAH-induced basilar artery spasm. In this study, celecoxib was shown to change basilar artery outer diameter which was significantly larger in the treated group compared to vehicle controls $(p<0.001)$ . However, this effect of celecoxib on basilar artery spasm was not accompanied by any significant change in mean systemic arterial pressure [27].  

Another pathway that could be involved in the development of vasospasms following aSAH is nicotine-induced arterial contraction. This phenomenon is mediated by metabolites of arachidonic acid induced by temporary overexpression of COX-2 in the arterial endothelium [18]. Overexpression of COX-2 has been observed up to 1 week following aSAH in a canine basilar artery model, and it leads to nicotine-induced contraction of the arterial wall induced by thromboxane A2 (TXA2). Thus, COX-2 inhibitors like nimesulide and celecoxib attenuated nicotine-induced contraction of the basilar artery wall in a concentration-dependent manner [19].  

The finding that COX-2 plays an important role in the pathogenesis of cerebral vasospasms following experimental aSAH is also supported by Munakata et al. [31] who showed significant narrowing of the basilar artery and enhanced COX2 expression in endothelial cells following aSAH compared to the control group. This effect was accompanied by downregulation of endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) and upregulation of endothelin-1 (ET-1) and endothelin A (ETA) receptor. After treatment with celecoxib the influence of aSAH on eNOS, ET-1, and ETA receptor was significantly reversed, pointing to their participation in aSAH pathogenesis. Thus, the authors concluded that selective COX-2 inhibitors such as celecoxib may potentially serve as agents for the prevention of cerebral vasospasms following aSAH.

Recent study (Silav et al. [49]) suggests that not only celecoxib but also dipyrone (metamizole), one of the most commonly used NSAID, has a vasodilatory effect following aSAH. The authors used digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to monitor basilar artery diameter in rabbits following aSAH and treated with metamizole. This experimental study proved that intramuscular administration of dipyrone repeated at 8-h intervals significantly attenuated basilar artery contraction 1 and $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ after the first dose. TEM imaging also showed attenuation of endothelial cell edema in the basilar artery.  

Nevertheless, there is increasing evidence that pathophysiology of aSAH is not related only to, or even mainly with vasospasm but other factors such as microcirculatory disturbance, early brain injury, cortical spreading depression, and venous drainage disturbance are also implicated [22].  

The complex proinflammatory reaction associated with brain tissue COX-2 was described by Chang et al. [5]. COX-2 up-regulation in injured neurons has a widespread effect on the brain via prostanoids that modulate glutamate release, cerebral vasoconstriction, release of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and alterations in neuroendocrine function and could thus contribute to neuronal death following aSAH. This study showed that parecoxib is able to reduce early COX-2 expression in brain via dose-dependent inhibition of C-Jun Nterminal kinase (cJNK) p55 expression. This then leads to the reduction of proinflammatory cytokines $(\mathrm{IL}{-}1\beta$ , IL-6 and MCP-1, IL-8) in the cerebrospinal fluid, as well as to the cleavage of caspase-3 and caspase-9a in brain tissue following aSAH. Moreover, administration of parecoxib diminished aSAH-induced cerebral edema and subsequent glutamaterelated excitotoxicity by N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor-1 (NMDAR-1) and N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor-2a (NMDAR-2a) in a rodent model. A further cerebroprotective effect of parecoxib following aSAH was related to enhanced cytoplasmic B cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) protein with antiapoptotic features. This study suggests that parecoxib, an early COX-2 inhibitor, could prove clinically useful in treating aSAH-induced early brain injury.

# Clinical studies describing the effect of NSAIDs on aSAH  

The promising results from experimental animal models showing positive effects of NSAID on aSAH-related vasospasms and neuroinflammation formed the basis for several clinical studies over the past decade.  

It was found that COX-1 is critical to normal platelet function, whereas the COX-2 enzyme mediates the inflammatory response [23]. Since patients with aSAH are vulnerable to rebleeding as well as DCI, alteration of platelet function may influence their outcomes in a positive or negative direction. Parkhutik et al. [36] conducted a pioneering study focused on platelet function in patients taking NSAIDs following aSAH. This study monitored platelet function in patients following aSAH who had received the intravenous analgesics dexketoprofen $(n=10)$ ) and dipyrone $(n=17)$ ), i.e., NSAIDs inhibiting both COX-1 and COX-2 pathways, and compared them to patients with no analgesia. The authors confirmed the initial hypoaggregability following aSAH (arachidonic acid– induced aggregation in the no-analgesia group was $72.4\%$ ), which was significantly lowered by administration of COXinhibiting dexketoprofen and dipyrone ( $17.2\%$ and $5.7\%$ , respectively; $p=0.001$ ). Moreover, the platelet function analyzer-100 closure time for untreated patients was within the normal range (138.1 s), while the closure time for both treatment groups was significantly prolonged (237.2 s in dexketoprofen group, $198.4\mathrm{~s~}$ in dipyrone one; $p=0.02,$ ). Even if affected thrombocyte aggregation is generally associated with a tendency for surgical, posttraumatic, and spontaneous bleeding, no increased rebleeding rate was seen in the NSAID group. Albeit the sample size of the study cohort was small, the results are in line with recent literature that show no increase in the risk of rebleeding or any other negative effect of anti-platelet drugs on aSAH patient outcome.  

Along with the platelet-inhibitory action of NSAIDs, they are well-known as anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antipyretic agents. They showed an inverse correlation between the NSAIDs dose applied and systemic IL-6 and CRP levels in patients with aSAH. Further, a higher dose of NSAIDs reduced the odds of an unfavorable outcome. They used not only “true” NSAIDs like dipyrone, diclofenac, and ibuprofen but also acetaminophen that is generally not considered a NSAID because of its weak anti-inflammatory properties. However, acetaminophen (paracetamol) is a selective COX-2 inhibitor with some effect on the inflammatory response including suppression of prostaglandin synthesis in the central nervous system [11]. Muroi et al. found only weak correlation of the NSAIDs effect on IL-6 and CRP levels when acetaminophen was excluded from the analysis.  

In Toronto, Canada Nassiri et al. [33] performed an exploratory analysis using propensity score matching of subjects enrolled in the CONSCIOUS-1 (Clazosentan to Overcome Neurological iSChemia and Infarction OccUring after Subarachnoid hemorrhage) study to assess whether NSAIDs use is associated with improved outcomes following aSAH. They concluded that administration of NSAIDs (salicylates, propionic acid derivatives, acetic acid derivatives, enolic acid derivatives, and selective COX-2 inhibitors), after aSAH is associated with significantly reduced mortality $(p=0.035)$ and improved functional outcomes (modified Rankin score $<2;p=0.083,$ . These effects were independent of the development of delayed ischemic neurological deficit, DCI, or vasospasm. Furthermore, patients treated with NSAIDs had reduced lengths of intensive care unit and hospital stay.  

Although there are positive signs of NSAIDs treatment in patients with aSAH, we cannot ignore their possible adverse effects. A study [46] analyzing prospectively collected data of 29 consecutive aSAH patients with brain multimodal neuromonitoring showed that parenteral diclofenac effectively reduces body temperature but may lead to decreased mean arterial pressure as well as cerebral perfusion pressure, resulting in brain-tissue hypoxia, which are associated with a poor outcome following aSAH. The authors recommend multimodal monitoring of hemodynamic side effects when using parenteral diclofenac in patients with poor-grade aSAH because braintissue oxygen tension $\mathrm{(PbtO}_{2}<30\mathrm{mmHg)}$ before parenteral diclofenac was highly predictive of brain-tissue hypoxia $\mathrm{(PbtO}_{2}<20\mathrm{mmHg)}$ after intervention.  

A recent clinical study [47] by the same groups, however, justifies parenteral diclofenac use for the attenuation of brain extracellular proinflammatory response characterized by IL-6 up-regulation in poor-grade aSAH patients.  

Although there is increasing evidence that inflammation may play a crucial role in poor outcomes following aSAH, and that NSAIDs may be a useful therapeutic option, the only randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical study was conducted by Ghodsi et al. [10]. Patients diagnosed with aSAH documented by computed tomography $(n=81$ ) used  

$7.5~\mathrm{mg}$ meloxicam oral tablets $(n=40)$ ) or placebo $(n=41$ ) twice daily for 7 days. The authors concluded that the meloxicam group showed lower incidence of cerebral vasospasm, lower mortality, shorter hospital stay, improvement in Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) at discharge, and thus a better overall outcome compared to the placebo group. However, they did not find any statistically significant difference among the groups in these parameters. The fact that meloxicam administration was not associated with any adverse events, along with the positive outcome trends point to a promising potential for NSAIDs with COX-2 inhibitory activity in patients with aSAH and argue for larger clinical trials in future.  

# Discussion  

It has been shown that inflammation plays an important role in the development, growth, and rupture of an intracranial aneurysm leading to aSAH [8]. Moreover, recent studies also consider inflammation to be a key factor in the cerebrovascular reaction to aSAH, including development of early brain injury (EBI) and DCI [9]. Glucocorticoids are among the most widely used group of anti-inflammatory drugs, but their positive effect in patients with aSAH remains controversial [28]. There are a growing number of studies dealing with the effect of NSAIDs on the pathophysiology of aSAH. Apart from common NSAIDs like diclofenac or ibuprofen, the most promising and newly studied group of NSAIDs in aSAH pathophysiology are competitive COX inhibitors, mainly the COX-2 isoenzyme group (i.e., celecoxib, parecoxib, meloxicam). The overexpression of COX-2 enzymatic activity in reaction to aSAH leads to the conversion of arachidonic acid to proinflammatory prostaglandins and TXA2, thereby enhancing neuroinflammation and vasoconstriction [13, 48, 53].  

The initial event of aSAH after aneurysm rupture is blood spillage under arterial pressure into the subarachnoid space. The acute increase in intracranial pressure (ICP) and reduction in cerebral blood flow causes a diffuse brain reaction including global brain ischemia and a neuroinflammatory response [48]. The pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 is a significant marker of the severity of EBI, DCI, and axonal injury, but parenteral diclofenac can attenuate its elevated levels in brain extracellular space following aSAH [47]. Moreover, acute cerebral hypoperfusion following aSAH is also associated with glutamate-induced hyperstimulation of NMDA receptors that can also be attenuated by NSAIDs [45]. The anti-apoptotic effect of the COX-2 inhibitor parecoxib mediated by decreased NMDAR-1 and NMDAR-2a as well as reduced caspase-3 and caspase-9a and upregulation of the antiapoptotic Bcl-2 protein has been shown in an animal model of aSAH resulting in diminished cerebral edema and reduced glutamate-related excitotoxicity [5].  

The attenuation of cerebrovascular inflammation by COX-2 inhibition observed in experimental animal models of aSAH has been also observed in clinical trials. Recent clinical studies suggest that NSAIDs may attenuate the brain proinflammatory response, similar to their effect on the systemic inflammatory response mediated by the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6 [15, 32]. Apart from studies dealing with neuroinflammation, there are also clinical studies confirming the positive effect of NSAIDs on clinical outcomes in aSAH patients [10, 33].  

It is believed that consequences of aSAH such as intracranial vasoconstriction and microvascular dysregulation contribute to DCI and affect the clinical outcome. Proinflammatory cytokines like IL- $\cdot1\upbeta$ and MCP-1 can potentiate aseptic inflammation and are overexpressed following aSAH. On the other hand, neutralization of IL- $1\upbeta$ by $\mathrm{IL-l}\upbeta$ antibodies and decreased levels of MCP-1 reduce cerebral vasospasm after SAH [17, 20]. Based on the findings that parecoxib is able to decrease the level of IL- $\cdot1\upbeta$ and MCP-1 in the CSF, we can expect a positive effect of NSAIDs on cerebral vasospasms following aSAH. The perivascular concentration of blood degradation products including oxyhemoglobin, serotonin (5-HT), and bilirubin oxidation products are elevated as a consequence of bleeding into the subarachnoid space [26, 41]. These substances lead to membrane depolarization by activating voltage-gated calcium channels and inhibition of Kv7 channels, resulting in the constriction of vascular smooth muscle cells [21]. In this context, the COX-2 inhibitor celecoxib can counteract membrane depolarization by activating Kv7 channels and inhibiting voltage-gated calcium channels. The vasodilatory effect of celecoxib was verified in an animal model of aSAH and was even stronger than the effect of the routinely used cerebroprotective drug nimodipine [26].  

Coxibs are not the only NSAIDs in which vasodilatory effects after aSAH have been described. Dipyrone (metamizole) is a highly popular analgesic [16]. Apart from the analgesic and antipyretic effects of dipyrone, an effect on the vascular wall following aSAH has also been documented [49]. A possible explanation for the spasmolytic effect of dipyrone may be mediated by central inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2, and the formation of dipyrone metabolites like arachidonoyl-4-methylaminoantipyrin and arachidonoyl-4-aminoantipyrin. These metabolites are capable of stimulating cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1) and cannabinoid receptor type 2 (CB2) [44]. CB1 and CB2 receptors are widely distributed in vascular smooth muscle and their activation leads to changes in vascular resistance [50].  

Under physiological conditions, the COX-2 cascade is responsible for the regulated production of vasoconstrictors like TXA2 and vasodilators like prostacyclin (PGI2). Vascular pathology such as aSAH potentiates the conversion of arachidonic acid to TXA2 and results in a vasoconstrictive response [1]. Expression of TXA2 is stimulated by nicotine through the activation of central nervous system type of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors [24]. This pathophysiological cascade may be responsible for the increased incidence of vasospasm in smokers following aSAH. There is an indication that nicotine contributes to increasing the incidence of symptomatic vasospasms [56].  

Thus, attenuation of COX-2 activity by COX-2 inhibitors results in depletion of TXA2 and may be an important mechanism by which NSAIDs contribute to reduced vasospasm following aSAH.  

The strong vasoconstrictor ET-1 may also play a role in the development of vasospasm and DCI following aSAH [52]. Although the exact mechanism by which the COX-2 inhibitor celecoxib decreases the level of ET-1 miRNA is not known, it may follow from its inhibition of COX-2 enzymatic activity [31]. The innate neurovascular protection against aSAHinduced vasospasm is probably mediated by eNOS [40, 54]. As celecoxib significantly increases the level of eNOS in intracranial endothelial cells following aSAH, it may thus aid neuroprotection [31].  

There are three different experimental models of aSAH— the perforation model, the prechiasmatic model and the cisterna magna model. In all the animal studies mentioned above, the authors use only the cisterna magna model to describe the effect of NSAIDs following aSAH. Despite the wide prevalence of this model, it has several disadvantages like variations in the amount of blood retained in the intracranial subarachnoid space, blood clots predominantly located in the posterior fossa, and quick restoration of cerebral blood flow. Based on these findings, it can be assumed that the effects of NSAIDs in the acute phase following aSAH described in animal models by injecting blood into the cisterna magna are questionable. In this context, the prechiasmatic SAH model seems to be more suitable for studies of acute or late pathophysiological cascades and their modulation by NSAIDs [43].  

Although experimental studies provide proof for a positive effect of NSAIDs on cerebral vasospasms, corresponding clinical trials have largely not been carried out. This may be due to the unclear nature of evidence claiming that cerebral vasospasms have a direct impact on clinical outcomes [12, 55]. Only a few clinical studies have shown reduced mortality and improvement of functional outcomes after NSAIDs administration in patients following aSAH [10, 32, 33]. However, this may a result of other mechanisms than vasoprotection like NSAID-induced reduction of cerebrovascular inflammation and their general neuroprotective effects.  

Despite the positive effects of NSAIDs, we cannot ignore their possible side effects. The study investigating brain-tissue oxygen tension and cerebral metabolism in patients following aSAH concluded that parenteral diclofenac may lead to decreased cerebral perfusion pressure as well as brain-tissue hypoxia, which can together contribute to a poor clinical outcome following aSAH [46]. Nevertheless, fears that NSAIDs can increase the risk of rebleeding in patients with aSAH have not been confirmed [34].  

We can conclude that neuroinflammation and cerebrovascular dysregulation are important steps in the pathophysiology of aSAH and their extent could be a factor leading to DCI and unfavorable outcome. Experimental studies using the cisterna magna model have shown that NSAIDs may have a positive effect following aSAH, but whether this model is relevant remains questionable. Current guidelines for pharmacotherapy in patients with aSAH are restricted to administration of the calcium channel blocker nimodipine. It appears that NSAIDs administered following aSAH especially coxibs are more effective in affecting vasospasm than nimodipine, and parallel administration of NSAIDs may even have a complementary effect. However, there is only one randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial showing a tendency towards a better clinical outcome, lower incidence of cerebral vasospasm, and mortality in patients after aSAH and NSAIDs administration. These promising results justify randomized trials that could support clinical use of NSAIDs in patients diagnosed with aSAH.
