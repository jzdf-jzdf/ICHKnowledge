# ABSTRACT  

Previous studies indicate that the effect of prism adaptation training (PAT) on unilateral neglect may depend on clinical characteristics. In this explorative work, we re-analyzed data from a previously conducted randomized controlled trial $(N=23)$ to investigate whether age, etiology, severity of motor impairments, and visual field deficits affect the efficacy of PAT. Additionally, we reviewed PAT studies that reported lesion maps and distinguished responders from non-responders. We transferred these maps into a common standard brain and added data from 12 patients from our study. We found patients suffering from subarachnoid bleeding appeared to show stronger functional recovery than those with intracranial hemorrhage or cortical infarction. Furthermore, patients with visual field deficits and those with more severe contralateral motor impairments had larger after-effect sizes but did not differ in treatment effects. In addition, patients with parietal lesions showed reduced recovery, whereas patients with lesions in the basal ganglia recovered better. We conclude that PAT (in its current form) is effective when fronto-subcortical areas are involved but it may not be the best choice when parietal regions are affected. Overall, the present work adds to the understanding on the effects of clinical characteristics on PAT.

# Introduction  

Visuospatial neglect is a common and disabling neuropsychological syndrome following right-hemispheric damage (Priftis et al., 2013), e.g., due to cortical infarctions (CI), intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), or subarachnoid bleeding (SAB). One of the interventions used for treating neglect is prism adaptation training (PAT; Rossetti et al., 1998). Patients undergoing PAT repeatedly make pointing movements to visual targets, while wearing prism goggles that induce a visual field deviation of about $10^{\circ}$ to the right. Pointing movements are biased at first to the right (direct effect; Prablanc et al., 2020). However, after a few trials, patients learn to correct for this displacement (GutierrezHerrera et al., 2020). After the training, patients show the “after-effect”: their movements are now biased to the left. The size of the after-effect is considered to be an important indication of the efficacy of PAT (Farne et al.， 2002; Gutierrez-Herrera et al., 2020; Sarri et al., 2008; Striemer et al., 2016), although cases have been described showing an after-effect but no neglect improvements or vice versa (Frassinetti et al., 2002; Pisella et al., 2002).  

PAT has repeatedly been shown to reduce neglect symptoms (e.g., Champod et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2021). However, results from randomized controlled trials (RCT) in stroke patients show rather mixed results (e.g., Scheffels et al., 2021; Ten Brink et al., 2017; Vilimovsky et al., 2021). Due to the many differences between these studies, one might argue that these contradictory results concerning the effectiveness of PAT may depend on a variety of factors, which can be divided into (1) methodological factors belonging to the PAT protocol itself, and (2) clinical characteristics.  

Methodological factors may include the number and duration of PAT sessions, the number of pointing movements to be executed, the extent of visual shift induced by the prism goggles, and the degree of visual feedback availability during training. Furthermore, there is preliminary evidence that also the frequency of donning and doffing of the prism goggles may affect the outcome (Scheffels et al., 2021). Clinical characteristics that may influence the outcome after PAT may be age, lesion parameters (site, volume, etiology), contralateral motor impairments and hemianesthesia, and visual field deficits. We will discuss these factors in more detail.  

For stroke in general, it is known that younger patients tend to show a better outcome following rehabilitation than older patients (Kammersgaard et al., 2004; Kelly-Hayes et al., 2003; Paolucci et al., 2003), although inpatient rehabilitation shows substantial efficacy also for older patients (compare Langhorne et al., 2011; Lui & Nguyen,  

2018; Paolucci et al., 2003; Young & Forster, 2007). The complexity of the medical condition and high comorbidity after stroke in older patients may play a role here (Sozzi et al., 2012). However, Sozzi et al. (2012) have shown that specifically for neglect patients, a cognitive (top-down) treatment was effective for older patients and another study by Kintzel et al. (2015) found a positive effect of a combined treatment using PAT and alerting tones only for older patients, while they found negative effects for younger adults.  

Prior studies have examined the relevance of lesion etiology on functional outcome after neurorehabilitation (e.g., measured by the Functional Independence Measure; FIM) for CI, ICH, and SAB (Katrak et al., 2009; Kelly et al., 2003; Stabel et al., 2017). Compared with CI and ICH, patients with SAB showed a lower functional status at admission but showed significantly larger gains after rehabilitation (Katrak et al., 2009; Kelly et al., 2003). However, to the best of our knowledge, the meaning of etiology for recovery from neglect and, more specifically after treatment with PAT, has not been analyzed hitherto.  

Reduced improvement after PAT may be associated with parietal damage, but also with lesions in the right inferior and middle temporal gyri, thalamus, angular and supramarginal gyri, postcentral gyrus, fusiform gyrus, and hippocampus (Gutierrez-Herrera et al., 2020). In contrast, neglect caused by subcortical frontal lesions seems to respond positively to PAT (Gossmann et al., 2013). Therefore, lesion site may be an important predictor for the responsiveness to this neglect treatment.  

Etiology and lesion site also may play a role in the clinical manifestations. The presence of contralesional hemiparesis or hemiplegia depends on lesions to different branches of the middle cerebral artery (MCA; Nieuwenhuys et al., 2007). CIs, affecting the parietal, temporal, and/or frontal cortex, are due to strokes of the M2-segment of the MCA and will likely lead to a hemiparesis (at least if they are not very large), whereas hemiplegia usually results from a complete interruption of the corticospinal tract and thus by occlusion of the M1-segment of the MCA (Nieuwenhuys et al., 2007). Furthermore, particularly lesions in arteries of the basal ganglia tend to cause hemiplegia (Nieuwenhuys et al., 2007). Unfortunately, studies on the effect of associated clinical manifestations such as contralesional motor impairment on PAT are still lacking. To the best of our knowledge, there is only a single study investigating the effect of PAT on neglect hemianesthesia, finding a positive effect on this symptom (Facchin et al., 2019).  

Furthermore, it remains unclear whether the presence and extent of contralesional field deficits (e.g., hemianopia) aggravate neglect symptoms independent of the nature of the intervention (Behrmann et al., 1997; Cassidy et al., 1999; Gainotti et al., 2009; Halligan et al., 1990), and whether these reduce the responsiveness to PAT (Angeli et al., 2004; Fortis et al., 2010; Rousseaux et al., 2006; Sarri et al., 2008; Serino et al., 2006, 2007; Vangkilde & Habekost, 2010). For instance, Serino et al. (2007) and Angeli et al. (2004) have found that the proportion of those with left hemianopia was higher in patients showing poor adaptation after PAT. In contrast, Vangkilde and Habekost (2010) and Fortis et al. (2010) found no evidence that patients with hemianopia responded differently to PAT.  

Information on clinical characteristics influencing the efficiency of PAT is of great practical relevance since it can help the clinician to decide whether a neglect patient should be treated with PAT. Therefore, in the present exploratory study, we re-analyzed data from an RCT study (Scheffels et al., 2021) to investigate whether specific clinical characteristics have an influence on the neglect outcome and the size of the after-effect following PAT in early rehabilitation patients. Furthermore, in a second step, we reviewed the existing literature on the relevance of lesion sites for responders versus non-responders to PAT.  

For this purpose, we (1) analyzed clinical characteristics concerning their influence on neglect recovery under PAT, (2) investigated whether they also have an impact on the after-effect size, and 3) following a short literature review, we integrated our lesion data and those from relevant studies to look for brain regions distinguishing responders from non-responders to PAT.  

# Materials and methods  

# Participants, design and interventions  

Detailed information on the study protocol can be found in Scheffels et al. (2021). All patients had been diagnosed with visuospatial neglect after right-hemispheric lesions (defined as having more than two omissions more on the left side of the page than on the right side in the Apples Cancelation Test (Bickerton et al., 2011)). Furthermore, patients had to be able to sit in a wheelchair for at least $45\mathrm{min}$ and to participate in at least 10 of 15 planned therapy sessions. Patients were excluded from participation when they were diagnosed with secondary normal pressure hydrocephalus (indicated by medical report and computer tomography scans) or premorbid dementia (verified by medical report, computer tomography/MRI scans, and reports of relatives). Two series of patients have been recruited. They were randomly allocated to either a continuous (PAT-c) or intermittent PAT (PAT-i) protocol. In PAT-c, wedged-shaped prism goggles were worn continuously during the entire session. In PAT-i, goggles were put on two (first recruitment series) or three times (second series). Pointing movements and time with goggles on were kept constant across the two protocols. Goggles always shifted the visual field $10^{\circ}$ to the right. A cloth attached laterally to the glasses was used to prevent seeing the right arm before movement execution (terminal exposure).  

Therapy sessions lasting 30–40 minutes were offered for 15 days, with one session per day and with the weekends off. Patients were evaluated twice before the start of the treatment (T1 and T2, three days in-between) and one day after the last session of the first intervention (T3). Thus, only data $N=23$ ; PAT-i: $n=10$ , PAT-c: $n=13$ ) from the first phase of the cross-over design from the previous study were used. We excluded one patient with traumatic brain injury from the analysis. Scores from T2 were used as the pre(PRE) and from T3 for the post-treatment measurement (POST).  

A detailed description of the tasks used for PAT can be found in Scheffels et al. (2021). A set of individually adjustable tasks was offered over the whole intervention period. Thus, PAT was combined with a visual search task (e.g., searching for a distinct picture, number, word) and tasks were accomplished when wearing the goggles and without them. The number of pointing movements was kept equal for the two protocols and a minimum of at least 50 movements over the tasks was required (Barrett et al., 2012).  

All patients provided written consent. The study was approved by the Bremen Medical Board’s Ethics Committee and registered at the German Clinical Trials Register (IDs: DRKS00018813, DRKS00021539).  

# Outcome measures  

For evaluating the effectiveness of PAT, a series of neglectspecific tests and functional assessments were conducted. Primary outcome measures were the Apples Cancelation Test (Bickerton et al., 2011), the German version of the Early Rehabilitation Barthel Index (ERBI; Schonle, 1995), and the Functional Independence Measure (FIM; Keith et al., 1987). Secondary outcome measures included text reading performance (Wilson et al., 1991), a line bisection task (Schenkenberg et al., 1980), Clock Drawing (Wilson et al., 1987), spontaneous and cued body orientation (Karnath & Dieterich, 2006), as well as the size of the aftereffect. From the medical reports, we collected information on age, lesion etiology (CI, ICH, SAB), contralateral motor impairment, and visual field deficits. If the information on the latter was not given, a trained neuropsychologist obtained it through confrontation visual field testing.  

# Statistical analysis  

All statistical analyses were performed with JASP, version 0.16 (JASP Team, 2021). Means and standard deviations were calculated. For intra-group comparisons of test scores, parametric dependent t-tests and non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were conducted, depending on whether or not data were normally distributed. To investigate the effect of clinical characteristics (etiology, contralateral motor impairment, visual field deficits, age) on the improvements in test scores, we first categorized them into variables with two categories (Etiology: ICH/CI vs. SAB; Motor Impairment: hemiparesis vs. hemiplegia; Visual Field Deficit: yes vs. no; Age: 60 years of age and lower vs. above 60 years). Age was binary coded based on the median value in the sample. Subsequently, we conducted parametric independent t-tests and non-parametric Mann-Whitney $U$ tests with the Clinical Characteristics as grouping variables and After-Effect Size as well as the Difference Scores of the Apples Cancelation Test, FIM and Spontaneous Body Orientation as the dependent variables. In order to check the association of test score improvements and the aftereffect size, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients.  

# Lesion mapping and analysis  

Searching with PubMed, studies on PAT and neglect were selected from the literature that provided information on lesion sites (using MRIcron overlap-images; Rorden et al., 2007) for responders and non-responders. The following query was used: [(“prism adaptation” OR “prismatic adaptation” AND neglect AND (improvement OR recovery OR responder OR non-responder)]. We only included studies using the same brain atlas (as in MRIcron) and those who recruited at least four patients per group. The brain images from these studies were used and combined with the results of this study (see details below) to look for a lesion area that might be associated with the success of treatment common to all studies.  

In the studies selected from the literature, lesions had been displayed on axial slices. To identify the z-score (the dorsal to ventral position) of the axial slices, we used either the information in the images or the values of the $\mathbf{Z}\cdot$ -scores mentioned in the text. For the studies not giving information about the precise dorsal to ventral positions, we compared the slices displayed in the article with slices obtained when scrolling through MRIcron for identifying the axial positions of the slices used in these studies.  

The combination of the lesion maps was done by one of the authors (HH) who has extensive experience with MRIcron lesion mappings, documented by several published articles in this field. To integrate the findings from the various studies, we classified the z-values into categories ranging from 40 to 50, 50 to 60, etc. We choose a slice position for each range that minimized the distance to $\mathbf{Z}$ -scores of the studies. For example, when a study used a $\mathbf{Z}$ -score of, for instance, 63, 62, 60, or 59, we transferred the lesion manually to the slice with a z-score of 61. For that purpose, we used (1) the overlap of at least two patients and (2) the overlap that was visible in all but one patient. We decided to use the overlap shared by at least two patients (criterion 1) to avoid outliers with a large lesion. Using the overlap shared by all but one patient (criterion 2) avoided the following problem: previous studies used and combined MRI/ CT scans of patients with different slice distances and some studies included only a few patients, resulting in only tiny overlapping brain areas, with a high risk of artifacts or false negatives. From a methodological viewpoint, the usage of overlaps from at least two patients for the analysis resulted in a liberally defined common brain region, whereas the usage of overlaps shared by all but one patient resulted in a conservatively defined common brain region. We will use the terms “liberally defined” versus “strictly defined” in the text below.  

Additionally, twelve CT scans were available from the patients of the current study. To identify typical brain regions for responders versus non-responders to PAT, a trained researcher (SK) first delineated the lesion borders on the brain scans (T1-weighted) on a $6\mathrm{mm}$ slice-by-slice basis using the MRIcron software (Rorden et al., 2007). We then used the slices mentioned in the paragraph above to merge the data from this study with those from the previous studies.

# Clinical characteristics and neglect recovery after PAT  

In order to reduce the number of comparisons, we only performed analyses for the following three outcome parameters: Apples Cancelation Test (primary outcome parameter), FIM (primary outcome parameter), and spontaneous body orientation (previous positive findings in our past RCT).  

Concerning etiology, we found significant group differences between SAB $(n=6)$ and CI/ICH $(n=17),$ ) on the FIM $[t(21)~=~2.61$ , $\textit{p}=.016$ , $d=1.24]$ , with SAB patients improving more than those having suffered from CI or ICH. However, CI/ICH patients did not differ significantly from SAB patients with regard to PRE $[t(21)=-1.84$ , $p=.081]$ and POST $[t(21)=1.14$ , $p=.267]$ FIM scores, or the difference scores of the Apples Cancelation Test $\left[t(21)=-.38\right]$ , $p=.708]$ and spontaneous body orientation $[t(20)~=~.81$ , $p=.428]$ . Furthermore, patients with SAB were not found to be significantly older than patients with CI/ICH $\left[t(21)=\right.\qquad$ $-1.77$ , $p=.092]$ .  

With respect to the presence of contralesional motor impairments (hemiparesis: $n=8$ , hemiplegia: $n=15$ ), visual field deficits (yes: $n=12$ , no: $n=11$ ) and age $\leq60$ years of age: $n=11$ ; $>60$ years, $n=12$ ), we did not find any significant group difference in improvement.

# Post-hoc analyses of the two PAT protocols  

As a post-hoc analysis, we checked whether there were significant group differences between the PAT protocol (PAT-i and PAT-c) for those clinical characteristics with sample sizes of at least 10 patients per group (a minimum of 5 patients in each PAT protocol for the clinical characteristic: patients without visual field deficits, with hemiparesis, CI/ ICB, and $>60$ years of age). We did not find significant differences between PAT-i and PAT-c in either of the analyses for the test scores or the after-effect size.  

# Lesion topology for responders versus non-responders to PA  

Searching the literature we found 10 relevant studies, with 5 of them fulfilling our criteria (Gossmann et al., 2013; Gutierrez-Herrera et al., 2020; Lunven et al., 2019; Sarri et al., 2008; Tissieres et al., 2017). Responses to PAT were dichotomized into responders vs. non-responders. The classification depended either on significant individual improvements from pre- to post-treatment on a cancelation task (Sarri et al., 2008), the normalization of performance on dichotic listening (Tissieres et al., 2017), the mean percentage of performance change from pre- to post-treatment (as measured by a neglect severity score; Lunven et al., 2019), or median splits calculated for improvements on a landmark and cancelation task (Gutierrez-Herrera et al., 2020), cued body orientation (Gossmann et al., 2013) or the Apples Cancelation Task (present study). Since we did not find significant differences between the two types of protocols (PA-i and PA-c) except for spontaneous body orientation in our previous work (Scheffels et al., 2021), we merged the data from these when separating responders from non-responders.

# Discussion  

The present study is a post-hoc exploration using data from of a previous RCT to investigate whether clinical characteristics have an influence on the neglect outcome after PAT and  

the after-effect, and whether there are certain lesion sites distinguishing responders from non-responders to PAT.  

# Clinical characteristics and functional recovery  

Concerning functional recovery following PAT, the change in FIM appeared to be influenced by etiology: in comparison to patients with CI and ICH, patients with SAB tended to start the intervention with lower FIM scores (albeit not significantly), they recovered better, and they did not differ in functional status from the combined CI/ICH group after PAT. Overall, this better recovery rate in SAB patients is in line with the study of Stabel et al. (2017) who compared two

Prior research has found that SAB patients tend to be younger than those suffering from brain lesions due to other causes, indicating that age per se could explain stronger functional improvements after rehabilitation (Kammersgaard et al., 2004; Kelly-Hayes et al., 2003; Paolucci et al., 2003). However, in the present work, we could not find such an effect of age. Thus, in our view, recovery from more global effects (e.g., SAB involves the ventricle system around the brain and leads to hematomas that compress brain tissue, whereas CIs often more selectively concern certain brain areas; see Adams & Victor, 1993) than age per se seem to be likely to play a more important role for functional improvements after cerebrovascular events.  

Furthermore, we did not find a negative effect of visual field deficits on recovery following PAT. Prior studies have, however, found mixed results in this regard (Fortis et al., 2010; Sarri et al., 2011; Serino et al., 2007; Vangkilde & Habekost, 2010). An interpretation could be that the effect of visual field deficits on the after-effect vanishes with the number of treatment sessions. Three studies with a limited number of PAT sessions (10 or less) have found that visual field deficits may interfere with the neglect outcome: Dijkerman et al. (2003) treated 3 patients with neglect (2 with left hemianopia) and showed no beneficial effect of PAT, whereas Sarri et al. (2011) and Serino et al. (2007) found that the proportion of patients with left hemianopia was higher in non-responders than in responders to PAT. However, two further studies (Fortis et al., 2010; Vangkilde & Habekost, 2010) offering more sessions (15–20) could not find a negative effect of hemianopia on the outcome. Since the question of whether or not visual impairments aggravate neglect symptoms seems to be still open, future studies should record their presence and extent more precisely, and also examine their influence on recovery over time.  

As mentioned before, we did not find significant differences in test results between younger (60 years of age and below) and older (above 60 years) patients. Although younger patients with strokes tend to have better functional outcomes following neurorehabilitation (Kammersgaard et al., 2004; Kelly-Hayes et al., 2003; Paolucci et al., 2003), it is indisputable that inpatient rehabilitation is substantially effective also for older patients (Paolucci et al., 2003). In particular for neglect, studies have shown that older age can even be a positive predictor for treatment outcomes after a top-down (Sozzi et al., 2012) or bottom-up (Kintzel et al., 2015) treatment.  

# Clinical characteristics and the after-effect  

Although the after-effect size was not found to be associated with improvements in test scores in our study, some other studies have assumed that it is an important indication of the efficacy of PAT because it may predict neglect amelioration (Farne et al., 2002; Gutierrez-Herrera et al., 2020; Sarri et al., 2008; Striemer et al., 2016). Therefore, it is important to examine the effects of clinical characteristics on this measure. In the present study, we found that patients with a hemiplegia showed larger after-effects than those with residual left-sided motor functions. This finding seems to contradict the notion that hemiplegia would be the more severe syndrome and therefore is associated with a reduced expectation for recovery. However, as mentioned in the introduction, hemiplegia as a clinical symptom is often associated with rather small subcortical infarctions (e.g., common after M1-segment occlusions of the MCA), or large cortical infarctions (e.g., M2-segment of MCA; Nieuwenhuys et al., 2007). Therefore, clinical reasoning suggests that the reason why patients with hemiplegia show a strong aftereffect may be due to the primary subcortical localization of their strokes. Systematic studies on the effect of hemiparesis and hemianesthesia on PAT are yet to come.  

Concerning visual field deficits, we found that patients with hemianopia had larger after-effects than those with intact visual functions. To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies on the association between after-effect size and presence of visual field deficits available hitherto. A possible explanation for a larger after-effect is that patients with hemianopia are required to move their head in order to visually detect the entire target stimulus for the pointing movement. Subsequently, the head-eye reference frames need to be adjusted accordingly. With these additional adjustments, patients with hemianopia may adapt more extensively, possibly leading to larger after-effects. Further studies are required to test this hypothesis.  

# Lesion site in responders and non-responders  

Lastly, using data reported in the literature and adding brain imaging data from 12 patients from the current study, we found distinct brain lesion patterns that distinguish responders to PAT from non-responders. In most articles, it is argued that patients with parietal lesions do not show a strong response to PAT, in contrast to patients with frontal lesions (e.g., Goedert et al., 2020; Gossmann et al., 2013; Gutierrez-Herrera et al., 2020; Saj et al., 2019). An inspection of the lesion overlap images of the corresponding studies showed that responders have rather diffuse lesions in the frontal cortex, the radiate corona, and the basal ganglia. In line with this observation, our integration of the overlapping lesion images from these studies revealed that the affected brain areas involved the basal ganglia, the frontal white matter anterior to the basal ganglia (especially the putamen), the insular cortex, and the frontal triangulum. Our integration of the overlapping figures also supports the notion of parietal lesions interfering with the recovery of neglect after PAT. However, the question of whether frontal lesions after strokes of the M2-branches increase the prospects of PAT in neglect patients seems to be still open, because the integration of the overlapping figures did not point to a focus in areas of these M2-branches, but to a subcortical focus. To answer the question of frontal involvement, one would have to compare only patients with M2-strokes of the frontal versus parietal branches of the MCA. In this context, it is of interest that the only monkey study on the topic of PAT and neglect we are aware of indicates that frontal cortex integrity is important for the efficacy this method (Bossom, 1965).  

It should be mentioned that poor recovery of patients with parietal lesions may be due to the application of PAT, but it also may be the result of other factors related to neglect. Instead of the specific mechanisms at work during this rehabilitation method, such factors could rather include brain pathological differences due to lesion etiology (see discussion above on improved functional recovery following SAB compared to CI/ICH) or lesion site. Concerning the latter, prior studies using the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (Phan et al., 2013; Rangaraju et al., 2015; Seyedsaadat et al., 2020), as well as common clinical observations, confirm that the involvement of parietal lesions highly predicts a poor outcome in acute stroke patients. Thus, the different lesion localization for responders and non-responders may not be related to the kind of PAT but simply due to a difference between cortical and subcortical lesions and therefore between different branches of the MCA.  

# Limitations of the present study  

Our study faces some limitations that need to be mentioned. The first limitation is the nature of the post-hoc design that leads to significant results that were not predicted and formulated before conducting the actual RCT. Nevertheless, we think that our analysis of the data on potential factors influencing neglect recovery under PAT provides important but preliminary information. This information can be used in future studies on PAT, especially since it is easy to collect and usually available very early in the rehabilitation process.  

Second, the sample size in our study was rather small. Consequently, the analyses of subgroups (e.g., hemiparesis vs. hemiplegia, SAB vs. CI/ICH) included only a few patients, impairing explanatory power. However, since we found some significant results even in our small groups (e.g., the influence of motor deficits on the after-effect size), we believe that reconsidering clinical characteristics is highly valuable in future studies on PAT.  

Third, due to the exploratory nature of our study, we conducted a short literature review instead of a systematic meta-analysis. This may have biased the selection of relevant studies, at least to some extent. For instance, because we only searched in PubMed for relevant articles, some existing studies listed only in others databases could have been missed. However, since we integrated data of 75 patients from 6 studies, the corresponding exploratory analyses seem to be fair and relevant.  

Fourth, the studies used to obtain information on typical brain lesions for responders versus non-responders differed concerning their applied PAT procedure. Although all studies used prism goggles inducing a $10^{\circ}$ shift to the right, treatment sessions offered (one to fifteen), the number of pointings to be executed (up to 100), and the treatment session duration (from 10 to 30 minutes) varied across the studies. As outlined in the introduction, such methodological variations will probably also influence the efficiency of PAT. Nevertheless, the studies giving information on lesion-dependent responsiveness to PAT seem to reveal quite similar results (see above). Our analysis showed a rather consistent profile of brain lesions distinguishing responders from non-responders. However, in line with McIntosh et al. (2019), we recommend establishing a PAT protocol that fulfills at least some minimum requirements in terms of prism power, number of treatment sessions, session duration, and number of pointings. This will be especially important when comparing the effects of clinical characteristics across future studies.  

Fifth, the approach to divide patients into responders versus non-responders differed between the studies and the construction of lesion overlays did not follow particular conventions (e.g., use of similar axial z-positions or number of slices). This might bring along some issues: for instance, the used test scores (cancelation and bisection tasks, body position in the wheelchair, dichotic listening task, composite score) are measuring different neglect symptoms. It is known that not all symptoms are affected by PAT (Striemer & Danckert, 2010) and that neglect domains do not all follow the same recovery rate (Moore et al., 2021). To prevent or minimize an influence of the exact approach it is desirable to standardize the classification procedure for responders and non-responders in the long run. This will enable better comparability across studies, especially when specific conventions for lesion overlay construction are followed.  

# Conclusion  

To decide who should be offered PAT, the clinician needs to know which clinical characteristics are important to consider and which may enhance the chance that the patient will profit from this rehabilitation method. Based on the findings of the present exploratory work, we suggest that the implementation of PAT when primarily parietal regions are involved would not be effective and would therefore be clinically suboptimal. In contrast, PAT appears to be effective when patients have lesions to more frontal areas (including the basal ganglia, areas anterior to the basal ganglia, insular cortex, and the frontal triangulum). Since information on the exact lesion site is not always available (at least in Germany because MRI scans are rarely conducted in neurorehabilitation centers), we propose the following rule of thumb: instead of being a contraindication for the efficacy of PAT, hemiplegia as well as the combination of hemiplegia and an intact touch sensation of the left hand may be an indication to opt for a treatment with PAT. For contralesional visual field deficits, our study did not find a significant effect on the functional outcome. However, its influence on the after-effect size and results of previous studies indicate that patients with hemianopia may need more PAT sessions to show (improved) recovery and that they may possibly undergo more extensive adaptation of head-eye reference frames. Nevertheless, this clinical characteristic, together with the age of the patient and lesion etiology (CI, SAB, ICH) may only play a secondary role in the clinician’s decision.  

Overall, the present work provides important and easyto-apply information on the effects of clinical characteristics on PAT. However, it should be noted again that our conclusions are based on a post-hoc evaluation of a single clinical trial and an integration of brain imaging data from studies found by a literature review. Hence, as with other neglect rehabilitation methods, more research is still necessary to enable the clinician to make more profound decisions on whether or not a patient should be offered PAT.
