# Abstract

# Introduction  

In 2018, andexanet-alpha was approved by the FDA for the reversal of the direct factor Xa inhibitors apixaban and rivaroxaban. Andexanet-alpha is a biologic factor Xa decoy that binds to and disables direct and indirect factor Xa inhibitors. Recently, the ANNEXA-4 study [1] found excellent/ good hemostasis in $82\%$ of patients on a dFXaI with acute major bleeding who received andexanet along with significant reductions in anti-Xa activity levels.  

However, the cost of andexanet $(\$24,750–\$49,500$ per dose) is nearly six times the cost of the most commonly used alternative pro-thrombotic agent (4-factor prothrombin complex concentrates) for dFXaI-associated hemorrhage (Kcentra?, 4-factor PCC; $\$4050–$ perdose).Recentprospective, retrospectiveandpooledanalyseshavesuggestedthattheoff-labeluseof4F-PCCmayhaveratesofgood/excellenthemostasis $(65{-}84\%)$ and30-daythromboticeventrates $(4-8\%)$ thatcomparefavorablywithandexanet, atafractionofthecost [2–6].Inthisstudy, ourprimaryaimwastoprojectpotentialutilizationratesandcostofandexanetforreversalofdFXaIrelatedmajorhemorrhagecomparedto4F-PCC, whichiscurrentlyinuse.Secondaryaimsincludedacomparisonofratesofgood/excellenthemostasisand30-daythromboticeventsamongpatientswhoreceived4F-PCCinourcohortversuspublisheddataofpatientswhoreceivedandexanet.

# OutcomemeasuresTheprimaryoutcomeofthisstudywastheprojectedcostofandexanetcomparedtotheactualcostof4F-PCCforthereversaloforaldFXaI-relatedmajorbleeding.Thecostofeachdrugaswellasthepercentageoftotalhospitalreimbursementattributedtoeachdrug, andtheamountbywhichthecostofeachdrugexceededtotalhospitalreimbursementsperpatientwerecompared.Theactualcostof4F-PCCwasobtainedforeachpatientfromhospitalfinancialdatabasesandwasbasedonthedoseutilized.Theprojectedcostofandexanetwasbasedonthemanufacturer’ssuggestedretailprice (MSRP) perdose $(\mathbb{5}24,750–\mathbb{5}49,500)$ , adjustingforanyeligibleMedicareadd-onpayments.TheCentersforMedicareandMedicaidServices (CMS) grantedaMedicareNewTechnologyAdd-OnPayment (NTAP) forandexaneteffectiveOctober1, 2018 [12].ForeligibleMedicarepatients, theNTAPprovidesadditionalreimbursementequaltothelesserofoneofthefollowing: $50\%$ ofthecostofandexanet; or $50\%$ oftheamountbywhichthecostsofandexanetexceedtheentireMS-DRGpaymentwithapaymentcapof $\$14,062.50$ for fiscal year 2019 [13]. The adjusted projected andexanet cost was calculated as the MSRP minus the NTAP add-on amount for eligible patients. The NTAP add-on payment was applied if Medicare was the primary insurer (even if there was an additional secondary insurer). Patients with the following insurances are not eligible for the NTAP add-on payment: Medicare HMOs, Medicare Part B only, Medicare as a secondary insurer, Medicaid, or commercial insurance.  

The total payment per hospitalization was based on the MS-DRG (Medicare Severity Diagnosis Related Group) assigned to each patient [14]. For generalizability, a nationwide average hospital reimbursement per MS-DRG was utilized. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) provides two types of DRG payment data: (1) Average Medicare Payments, which reflects Medicare reimbursement for the MS-DRG amount, teaching, disproportionate share, capital and outlier payments; and (2) the Average Total Payments, which account for both Medicare reimbursements for the MS-DRG (including adjustments for teaching, disproportionate share, capital and outlier payments), as well as reimbursements from other beneficiaries or third party co-payments or deductibles for which the patient is responsible. Patients with Medicare only were coded as receiving the Average Medicare Payment. Patients with Medicare and another insurance (whether primary or secondary) were coded as receiving the Average Total Payment. When patients had only commercial insurance, the Average Total Payment amount was applied. No patient in this cohort who met inclusion criteria had Medicaid or managed Medicaid.  

Secondary outcomes included hemostatic efficacy and 30-day thrombotic events in patients who received 4F-PCC for dFXaI-related major bleeding compared to published data of andexanet for dFXaI-related major bleeding [15]. Hemostatic efficacy was assessed according to Sarode criteria [16], (Supplementary Table 1). For intracranial hemorrhage patients, head computed tomography (head CT) was assessed at baseline (prior to 4F-PCC administration), 6 and $^{24\textrm{h}}$ post 4F-PCC administration. All head CTs were manually reviewed by one author (R.L.) and hemorrhage volumes were measured using the ABC/2 method [17] for intraparenchymal hemorrhages, the Gebel method [18] for subdural hematomas and the modified Fisher Score [19] for subarachnoid hemorrhage. For patients who underwent a surgical intervention or procedure within $^{24\textrm{h}}$ of 4F-PCC administration, any active, spontaneous bleeding documented prior to or during the intervention was adjudicated as “poor” hemostasis. If a new intracranial hemorrhage occurred in a different location than the index hemorrhage following 4F-PCC administration, hemostasis was coded as “poor”. Hemostasis was not rated among patients who received multiple simultaneous reversal agents.  

Thromboembolic events that occurred within 30 days after receiving 4F-PCC included deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolus (PE), cerebral infarct, myocardial infarct or peripheral arterial occlusion. If no records were available after the index hospitalization, the 30-day thromboembolic events were considered “unknown”. Only 2 patients $(4\%)$ were missing 30-day thrombotic event data.  

Discharge disposition and length of stay were assessed as tertiary outcomes. Discharge disposition was coded as good if a patient was discharged home, home with health services or to an acute rehabilitation facility. Disposition was coded as poor for patients who expired or were discharged to a long-term acute care hospital, subacute rehabilitation center, skilled nursing facility, psychiatric center, hospice or home hospice.  



# Statistical analysis  

Demographics, 24-h hemostatic efficacy and 30-day thromboembolic event rates were qualitatively compared between our cohort and published data from ANNEXA-4 [1]. Differences between included patients and excluded patients who received 4F-PCC to reverse a dFXaI were evaluated using Chi squared tests for categorical values and Mann–Whitney U non-parametric tests for continuous, non-normally distributed values. The primary outcome of percent of hospital reimbursement attributed to andexanet was calculated as the NTAP-adjusted projected cost of andexanet divided by the total payment per hospitalization (depending on insurance status) $\times100$ . The total payment per hospitalization was calculated using a national average reimbursement per MS-DRG. Similarly, the percent of hospital reimbursement attributed to 4F-PCC was based on the dose-based cost of the 4F-PCC divided by the total payment per hospitalization based on the national average MS-DRG reimbursement. The actual cost of 4F-PCC and projected cost of andexanet, as well as the percent of hospital reimbursement attributable to each drug, and the amount (in US\$) bywhichthecostofthedrugexceededtotalhospitalreimbursementswerecomparedacrosstheentirecohortandamonghemorrhagetypesusingMann–WhitneyUnon-parametrictests.AllanalyseswereconductedusingIBMSPSSStatisticsforWindowsversion25 (IBMCorp., Armonk, NY).

Overall, hemostasis rates were excellent or good in $72\%$ of the cohort that met inclusion criteria, compared to $82\%$ of the ANNEXA-4 cohort (Table 3). Among those with intracranial hemorrhage, excellent/good hemostasis occurred in $69\%$ , compared to $80\%$ of patients with intracranial hemorrhage in ANNEXA-4. Thrombotic events at 30-days were less frequent in our cohort $(4\%)$ compared to $10\%$ in ANNEXA-4 and consisted of one patient with acute ischemic stroke and one with deep vein thrombosis. The rates of excellent/good hemostasis were not significantly different between included and excluded patients ( $72\%$ among included patients vs. $79\%$ among excluded patients, $\mathrm{P}{=}0.369,$ . Fifty percent of patients had a good discharge disposition and the overall median length of stay was 5 days (Supplementary Table 2). These data support the concept that patients with life-threatening hemorrhage can do well with coagulopathy reversal using 4F-PCC along with aggressive medical and surgical management.

# Discussion  

Andexanet-alpha is the only FDA-approved specific antidote and reversal agent for the direct oral Factor Xa inhibitors apixaban and rivaroxaban. Several international societies have recommended first line utilization of specific antidotes, when available, to reverse direct oral anticoagulants in the context of acute major life-threatening bleeding [20–24]. However, given limited availability and high cost, adoption of andexanet alfa as the preferred reversal agent for dFXaI has been slow in many health-systems.  

In this study, we found that the projected cost of andexanet would be four times greater than that of 4F-PCC, even after accounting for Medicare discounts (NTAP add-on payments). Indeed, the NTAP-adjusted cost of andexanet would exceed the entire hospital reimbursement in nearly $75\%$ of patients by over $\$7500$ .Atanaveragesize, urbanmedicalcenter, wefoundthatapproximately10patientsperyearwouldbeeligibleforandexanetbasedonourrestrictedinclusion/exclusioncriteria.Thisamountedto $\$221,200$ per year in pharmacy costs, $25\%$ of which would not be covered at all by MS-DRG reimbursements. While we applied strict criteria for the utilization of andexanet, it is possible that some institutions may be more liberal. If the 80 patients we excluded (who received 4F-PCC to reverse a dFXaI) received andexanet, this would result in a total of 126 patients (28 patients per year) eligible for andexanet, at a cost of $\$565,768$ peryear.Ofthis, $\$188,832$ per year would not be covered at by MS-DRG hospital reimbursements. A strength of our study was that we used a nationwide average hospital reimbursement data based on MS-DRG, and the manufacturer’s suggested retail price for Andexanet and 4F-PCC, so that our results are generalizable across states and hospital systems. The rates of thrombotic events for 4F-PCC reversal of dFXaI-related major hemorrhage were lower in our cohort compared to published rates for andexanet, but rates of good/excellent hemostasis were also lower. Because thrombotic events within 30 days of discharge may lead to readmissions, there is an incalculable additional cost of andexanet compared to 4F-PCC. However, in contradistinction, poor hemostasis following a major bleeding event is also associated with incalculable costs, which may favor targeted andexanet use over 4F-PCC use.  

The exceptional cost of andexanet (\$24,750–\$49,500 per dose) has led to heightened scrutiny of the trial that led to its approval [1]. Criticisms of the ANNEXA-4 trial include that it was an open-label study without a comparator group, and the rate of 30-day thrombotic events was high $(10\%)$ , compared to other reversal agents $5\%$ for idarucizumab [25] at 30-days, $7.8\%$ for Kcentra at 45-days [16], and $4\%$ for 4F-PCC used in dFXaI-related hemorrhage [26]). While many of these thrombotic events occurred prior to re-initiation of anticoagulation in a population already at risk for thrombotic events, the thrombotic risk profile of patients in ANNEXA-4 [ $80\%$ with a history of non-valvular atrial fibrillation and $17\%$ with prior venous thromboembolism (VTE)] did not vary substantially from that of RE-VERSE AD study of idarucizumab $95\%$ atrial fibrillation, $2\%$ VTE) [25]. Patients in the Sarode Kcentra study [16] arguably may have had a higher thrombotic risk profile $57\%$ with a history of arrhythmia, $18\%$ with a history of thromboembolism, and $13\%$ with an artificial heart valve/joint). Thus, patient demographics may not entirely explain the excess thrombotic events documented in ANNEXA-4. In our cohort, where $94\%$ of patients had NVAF, the 30-day thrombotic event rate was less than half that in published studies of andexanet ( $4\%$ vs. $10\%$ ) [1].  

Another concern identified in the ANNEXA-4 trial was the rebound in anti-factor Xa activity at the end of the 2-hour andexanet infusion [1]. Though in the entire ANNEXA-4 cohort there was no statistical correlation between anti-Xa activity and hemostasis, there was a significant correlation among patients with intracranial hemorrhage (perhaps due to more quantitative measures of hemostasis in this subgroup). This early rebound in anti-Xa activity leads to concerns over whether off-label redosing of andexanet may incur additional expense. Given these factors, the cost-efficacy of andexanet compared to 4F-PCC remains unclear.  

Prior to the FDA approval of andexanet, 4F-PCC and activated PCC (aPCC; FEIBA?) were commonly used to promote hemostasis in the context of dFXaI-related hemorrhage [9, 10]. However, neither 4F-PCC nor aPCC reverses the anticoagulant effect of these drugs, but rather, promotes a prothrombotic state while the anticoagulant is naturally metabolized. Furthermore, the clinical efficacy and safety of off-label use of 4F-PCCs for dFXaI-related hemorrhage has been reported only in studies of healthy volunteers and single-arm case series, none of which had comparator groups [2–6]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that on the basis of very low-quality evidence with very serious risk of confounding and selection bias, it is impossible to determine whether 4F-PCC is more effective than cessation of drug plus supportive care alone for managing dFXaI major bleeding [26].  

We found that rates of good or excellent hemostasis using 4F-PCC in dFXaI-related major hemorrhage were $10\%$ lower than published data for andexanet, suggesting that andexanet may be a superior agent for promoting the primary outcome of interest: hemostasis. However, due to a lack of head-to-head studies comparing andexanet versus 4F-PCC, it is unclear if this difference is clinically significant or has an impact on long term functional outcomes. Since the FDA has mandated that the manufacturers of andexanet conduct a randomized trial of andexanet versus the current routinely utilized reversal agents (e.g. PCCs) for dFXaI related intracranial hemorrhage [27], it would be useful to examine not just hemostasis, and thrombotic complications, but longterm functional outcomes. Overall, our discharge disposition data suggests that over half of intracranial hemorrhage patients had favorable outcomes, suggesting that aggressive coagulopathy reversal is warranted.  

Several limitations to this study bear mention. First, this retrospective study does not represent a direct comparison of 4F-PCC to andexanet and relative rates of hemostasis and thrombosis should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, though we had follow-up data on thrombotic events at 30-days in all but two patients, adjudication of safety events in clinical trials is more rigorous. Despite these limitations, patients in our cohort were demographically similar to those in the ANNEXA-4 trial and we utilized similar inclusion/ exclusion criteria as ANNEXA-4. We did not, however, restrict projected andexanet use based on intracranial hemorrhage size or admission Glasgow Coma Score (GCS), because the location of the hemorrhage and the possibility of surgical intervention may allow for a favorable outcome despite hematoma volume. Similarly, admission GCS scores may reflect sedation, seizure, metabolic disarray or other factors not directly related to the hemorrhage itself. For patients who have herniated or sustained devastating intracranial hemorrhage, the exclusion criteria of life expectancy $<1$ month were applied. Despite more inclusivity of intracranial hemorrhage patients, all intraparenchymal hemorrhage patients had a hematoma volume $\leq66~\mathrm{ml}$ , which approximates the $60~\mathrm{ml}$ exclusion cutoff in ANNEXA-4.  

Second, we assumed that commercial payers would reimburse for hospitalizations at rates similar to the CMS published Average Total Payment schedule, though individual hospitals may contract for different reimbursement rates. We used a national average of Medicare payments per MS-DRG to allow for generalizability of the data, however, individual hospital and regional reimbursements will vary. We did not have any uninsured or Medicaid patients in our payer mix of patients who met inclusion criteria.
