# ABSTRACT  

Keywords:

Fluoxetine   
Clinical trial Objectives: Acute intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a very common cause of disability. Previous evidence suggests that fluoxetine and other selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors improve, the recovery of motor function in patients with cerebral infarct. The purpose of this study was to investigate whether fluoxetine also improves motor recovery in patients with ICH.   
Patients and Methods: This is a double blind, placebo controlled, multicenter randomized trial, patients recruited from three centers were assigned to receive $20\mathrm{mg/day}$ of fluoxetine or matching placebo for three months from within ten days after onset of symptoms. Primary outcome was change in Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale from baseline to day 90.   
Results: Thirty patients $(50\%$ women) were recruited to the fluoxetine $(\mathbf{n}=14)$ or placebo $(\mathbf{n}=16)$ groups. Median age was 55 years, the cause of the ICH was hypertension in $93.3~\%$ , median volume of the hematomas was $22\mathrm{mm}^{3}$ . Basal ganglia hematoma was present in $67\%$ and, lobar location in $20\%$ of the patients. Improvement in FMMS at day 90 was significatively higher in the treatment group (median score 23) than in the placebo group, (median score 48), $\mathbf{p}=0.001$ . No serious adverse events occurred.   
Conclusion: In addition to standard treatment, early prescription of fluoxetine was safe and helped to increase motor recovery 90 days after ICH. This finding adds to the evidence regarding its beneficial effect upon stroke related disability.  

Clinical Trial Registration-URL: http://www.clinicaltrials.gov. Unique identifier: NCT01737541.  



# 1. Introduction  

Contrary to what happens in high-income countries (HIC), in low and middle-income countries (LMIC) the global burden of disease caused by stroke is increasing. [1] This is especially true for intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), which contributes to most of the stroke burden [2]. Also, the overall number of ischemic stroke (IS) survivors is also increasing [3] with many of them requiring anticoagulation, a known risk factor for ICH.  

Still, treatment for ICH has received comparatively little attention when compared to IS and, apart from physical rehabilitation; its treatment has undergone no major improvement for years. [4]  

Pharmacological interventions utilizing selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) to enhance recovery from IS have been previously studied with promising results, but have been only scarcely studied in patients with ICH. [5] There are several mechanisms by which SSRIs might enhance functional and structural recovery in the brain, including: the stimulation of neurogenesis, anti‐inflammatory neuroprotection, improved regulation of cerebral blood flow, and modulation of the adrenergic neurohormonal system [6]. Since these mechanisms are thought to apply regardless of the mechanism of brain injury, the purpose of this study was to investigate whether fluoxetine improves motor recovery in patients with ICH.  



# 2. Material and methods  

The methods of the FMRICH Trial have already been described in detail elsewhere. [7] Briefly, this was a double-blind, placebo controlled, multicenter randomized trial. Patients recruited from three centers received either Fluoxetine $20\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{OD}$ or matching placebo for 90 days. The primary outcome was the change in the Fugl-Meyer Motor Scale (FMMS) score between inclusion (day 0) and day $90~\pm~7$ days. The secondary outcomes were the change in the National Institutes of Health stroke scale (NIHSS), the Barthel Index (BI) and the Modified Rankin scale (mRs). FMSS and NIHSS were measured at inclusion, at day $42\pm7$ days and at day 90. BI and mRs were registered at day $90~\pm~7$ days.  

Due to slow recruitment and lack of funding this trial had to be suspended after enrolling 32 patients on august 2014. Psicofarma SA de CV, a Mexican pharmaceutical was responsible for the manufacture, packaging and randomization of the study medication kits. A web-based randomization service was used to generate the randomization sequence and then the medication kits were blindly labeled and sent to recruiting centers. None of the people responsible for randomization was at any point in contact with any of the investigators.  



# 3. Ethical issues  

This study has been approved by the institutional review boards (IRBs) at the recruiting centers, and by the Bioethics Committee (reference: CB/304/11) and the Scientific Committee (reference: DIC/ 547/11) of the Instituto Nacional de Neurología y Neurocirugía in Mexico City. Written informed consent was obtained from each individual prior to enrollment. This trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT01737541.  



# 4. Results  

32 patients were enrolled and received the study medication, however two patients (one in each group) did not take any medication returning the unopened bottles at visit 1 and had to be excluded from analysis. Thus, this report includes data from 30 patients (14 patients in the fluoxetine group and 16 in the placebo group), half of them were women, with median age 55 years. Hypertension was the cause of the ICH in $93.3~\%$ . Median volume of the hematoma was $22\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ . There were two cases of drug related ICH, one in the fluoxetine group associated with anticoagulants and one in the placebo group due to sympathomimetics. The most common location of the hematomas were the basal ganglia $(67\%)$ and, lobar location $(20\%)$ . As shown in Table 1, regarding age, sex and recorded characteristics of the ICH, there were no statistically significant differences between treatment groups.  

Primary outcome: FMMS total scores (TS) did not differ at baseline: [median (IQR)] 23 (14) vs 25.5 (8), $\mathbf{p}=0.179$ nor at day 45: 42 (22) vs 54 (26), $\mathbf{p}=0.193$ , in the placebo and fluoxetine group respectively (Fig. 1). This was also the case for the upper (ULS) and lower limbs (LLS) separate scores. Nevertheless, at day 90 the three scores were statistically different between groups: 48.5 (35) vs 75.5 (20), $\mathbf{p}=<0.001$ , 29 (31) vs 46.5 (22), $\mathbf{p}=0.003$ and, 17.5 (10) vs 31.5 (5), $\mathbf{p}=0.001$ for the TS, ULS and LLS placebo vs fluoxetine group, correspondingly. Table 2 depicts the median changes in FMMS and, NIHSS from inclusion to outcome. The median change for the TS was 38.5 (35) the major contributor to the increase in the FMMS was the ULS, 26.5 (23.8).  

At day 90, NIHSS scores were reduced a median of 3 points in the treatment group vs 0 in the placebo group, nevertheless this difference did not reach statistical significance $(\mathtt{p}=0.131\$ ).  

Table 3 shows the results for the secondary outcomes, the proportion of patients within categories of the $\mathrm{mR}s\le2$ as well as the medians for Barthel Index scores at day 90 were statistically different between groups. There were no serious adverse events during follow-up, all adverse events recorded were mild and no patient discontinued the study medication. The most common reported side effect was drowsiness (Table 4).

# 5. Discussion  

This is the first trial designed to test a pharmacological intervention to aid motor recovery in patients with ICH. Its results suggest a positive effect of fluoxetine on the reduction of stroke-related disability analogous to that showed in IS patients, [8] unfortunately the impossibility to recruit the calculated sample constitutes a major caveat that hinders a solid conclusion.  

Since in comparison with IS, ICH produces a higher degree of initial impairment and, a worse functional recovery [9] we consider the results of this trial significant, especially for the countries where the proportion of cases of ICH are higher from that reported in the developed countries [10].

The present results indicate that in the population studied, not only motor recovery is better in patients that received fluoxetine but also, that such improvement can be translated into less severity as evidenced by a significative reduction in their NIHSS scores and better performance in activities of daily living as measured by the BI, without the presence of any major adverse events, especially ICH recurrence.  

Our results do differ from those found in recent trials on the use of SSRI´s in stroke patients. The TALOS trial [11] that found only a borderline statistically significant improvement in functional outcomes in IS patients treated with citalopram and the FOCUS trial [12] in which fluoxetine did not improve functional outcomes 6 months after acute stroke.  

It is worth noting that while the TALOS trial did not include patients with ICH, the FOCUS trial did include data from 309 patients with ICH. Nonetheless, some key differences are worth considering. First, both the FOCUS and TALOS trial are based on data from HIC, whereas our results derive from a LMIC. There are clear differences in the epidemiology of stroke between LMICs and HICs, not only regarding the proportion of patients with ICH, but also in its severity and mortality. [13,14]  

And second, patients from LMICs also have limited access to proper and frequent physical therapy [14] thus explaining that Age-standardized Years Lived with Disability have not changed or have increased since 1990 [15].  

If we look at data from studies performed in LMICs the results tend to be more positive. For instance, a trial from Iran showed a $50~\%$ reduction in the 3-month NIHSS compared with the baseline scores in 79 $\%$ of the stroke patients treated with citalopram vs $54\%$ in the placebo group, $\textbf{p}<.001$ [16]. And a Chinese trial showed that treatment with fluoxetine was an independent factor affecting the NIHSS and BI scores on day 180 after stroke [17]. Nevertheless, neither of these trials included patients with ICH. Another trial from China that included 4 patients with ICH reported improvement of swallowing function in the group treated with fluoxetine [18].  

We acknowledge that the main weakness of this study is the failure to recruit the calculated sample which impedes any claim of efficacy. That said, a post hoc analysis based on the difference in FMMS at day 90 showed a power $>80\%$ for the results of this trial. Also, a recent metaanalysis concluded that the administration of SSRI´s may improve gross motor function, reduce disability and enhance quality of life for patients recovering from stroke. Once again, it is to be noted that none of the 44 RCTs included in this meta-analysis enrolled exclusively patients with ICH as we did in this trial. [19]  

Based on our results and those from the aforementioned trials, we think that there might still be a potential role for SSRI´s as an aid to motor recovery in ICH patients. Consequently, we advocate for subsequent confirmatory trials.
