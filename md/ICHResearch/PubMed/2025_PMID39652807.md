# Abstract  



# Background and Objectives  

Randomized trials have proven the benefit of endovascular therapy (EVT) for acute large ischemic stroke. This study was to characterize the effect of time to treatment on benefit of EVT vs medical management (MM) among patients with large ischemic stroke.

# Methods  

This was a post hoc analysis of the Endovascular Therapy in Acute Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusive Patients with a Large Infarct Core randomized trial. Patients who had an Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score of 3–5 or an ischemic core volume of $70{-}100~\mathrm{mL}$ within 24 hours of symptom onset were treated with EVT plus MM or MM. Onset-to-expected arterial puncture time (OPT) was analyzed as a categorical variable $(<6,6\mathrm{-}<12,$ and 12–24 hours) using binary logistic regression and as a continuous variable using a multivariable fractional polynomial interaction. The primary efficacy outcome was favorable outcomes (modified Rankin Scale scores 0–3) at 90 days. Safety outcomes included any intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), symptomatic ICH, and mortality.  



# Results  

Among 451 patients (median age 68 years; $61.4\%$ were men; median OPT 487 minutes [interquartile range 328–802]), 226 patients received EVT and 225 patients received MM. The EVT group showed higher rates of favorable outcomes than the MM group within OPT of 6 hours ( $44.4\%$ vs $29.9\%_{;}$ , adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.78, $95\%$ CI 1.22–6.32) and 6–12 hours ( $45.7\%$ vs $29.6\%$ , aOR 2.39 $95\%$ CI 1.21–4.71]), but not in OPT beyond 12 hours ( $51.6\%$ vs $41.4\%$ , aOR 2.05 $95\%$ CI 0.88–4.77]). The benefit became nonsignificant after OPT of 13 hours and 22 minutes. In 3 OPT intervals, the rates of symptomatic ICH and mortality were similar between EVT and MM although the rate of any ICH increased. However, no interaction effect was found (all $p$ interactions ${>}0.10$ ).  



# Discussion  

These findings strengthen the benefit of EVT initiated within 13 hours and 22 minutes after symptom onset compared with MM alone in patients with large ischemic stroke, but EVT should not be withheld for patients presenting beyond 13 hours and 22 minutes. Pooled analysis of larger sample sizes is needed.

# Introduction  

Endovascular therapy (EVT) is the standard first-line treatment of acute ischemic stroke caused by intracranial large vessel occlusion in patients with small-to-moderate ischemic cores.1-3 Recent multicenter, randomized trials (the Endovascular Therapy in Acute Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusive Patients with a Large Infarct Core [ANGEL-ASPECT] trial,4 the Recovery by Endovascular Salvage for Cerebral Ultra-Acute Embolism-Japan Large Ischemic Core Trial [RESCUE-Japan LIMIT],5 the Randomized Controlled Trial to Optimize Patient’s Selection for Endovascular Treatment in Acute Ischemic Stroke [SELECT2],6 the Efficacy and Safety of Thrombectomy in Stroke With Extended Lesion and Extended Time Window [TENSION],7 and the Large Stroke Therapy Evaluation [LASTE] trial8) confirmed that EVT combined with medical management (MM) was also efficacious in improving functional disability compared with MM alone in patients with large ischemic stroke.  

Time to treatment is an important determinant of clinical outcomes of reperfusion therapies and can guide treatment decision in clinical practice. The HERMES meta-analysis of 5 trials demonstrated that EVT significantly reduced disability compared with MM alone within, but not beyond, the first 7.3 hours after symptom onset among patients with anterior circulation large vessel occlusions, predominantly small-tomoderate ischemic cores.11 In the later 6-hour to 24-hour time window, EVT has also been shown beneficial among patients with small-to-moderate ischemic cores and in imaging selection for favorable penumbral profiles.12-14 These early and late window studies excluded patients with large ischemic core because of concerns for reduced potential benefit and increased hemorrhagic transformation, especially in the late time window.15 Previous randomized clinical trials in large ischemic stroke had formulated different inclusion criteria on time windows, but most of the participants were randomized within 12 hours of symptom onset.4-8 A thorough evaluation of the effect of time to treatment on EVT benefit is helpful to guide implementation and decision making in large ischemic stroke.  

Consequently, this study was undertaken with 2 aims: (1) evaluating within the ANGEL-ASPECT trial data set whether time to treatment modified the efficacy and safety of EVT in acute large ischemic stroke and (2) characterizing how the EVT effects varied with target treatment time metrics useful for quality improvement programs. The primary research question was whether EVT could be effective and safe for patients with large ischemic stroke presented within different time windows.  



# Methods  



# Study Design and Patient Selection  

This study was a post hoc analysis of the ANGEL-ASPECT trial, which was a multicenter, randomized, open-label trial at 46 comprehensive stroke centers in China from August 2020 to October 2022.4,16 Details of the trial protocol and patient selection criteria have been reported.16 The key inclusion criteria included age of 18–80 years, baseline NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores of 6–30, randomization finished within 24 hours of last known well time, acute occlusion of the internal carotid artery terminus or M1 segment of middle cerebral artery, and large ischemic core. The imaging evidence of large infarct was a baseline Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score [ASPECTS] of 3–5 based on noncontrast CT, or ASPECTS 0–2 or ASPECTS ${>}5$ combined with ischemic core (defined as a relative cerebral blood flow of $<30\%$ on CT perfusion or an apparent diffusion coefficient $<620\times10^{-6}\mathrm{~\bar{m}m}^{2}/s$ on MRI) of $70{-}100~\mathrm{mL}$ . The neuroimaging exclusion criteria included presence of midline shift, herniation, mass effect with effacement of the ventricles, any intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) on CT, and multiple intracranial vessel occlusions. Eligible patients were randomly assigned to the EVT or MM groups at a 1:1 ratio using a central, web-based randomization system based on the simple procedure without stratification.  



# Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents  

ANGEL-ASPECT was registered at clinicaltrials.gov/, under number NCT04551664. The trial protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at Beijing Tiantan Hospital (number: KY2020-072-02) and all participating centers, and the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. Each patient or their legally authorized representative signed written informed consent before enrollment.  



# Procedures and Data Collection  

Patients in the MM group received MM in accordance with the Chinese Stroke Association guidelines.17 This includes IV thrombolysis for patients meeting the guideline, but most patients enrolled in the ANGEL-ASPECT trial did not receive IV thrombolysis because of the wide time window and large ischemic core. Patients who had completed IV thrombolysis before randomization were also eligible. Patients without IV thrombolysis will be treated with aspirin, unless an indication for early anticoagulation is present. Patients in the EVT group received MM plus EVT. Stent retriever and/or contact aspiration were recommended as the first-line approach. If successful reperfusion is not achieved, other techniques including replacement of the device, intra-arterial thrombolysis, balloon angioplasty, stent implantation, or combinations were left to the discretion of interventionists.  

Demographic data, vascular risk factors, severity of stroke (assessed by NIHSS), ischemic injury, and time metrics were collected. Hypoperfused tissue was estimated with time-tomaximum (Tmax) volumes ${>}6$ seconds, and mismatch volume was defined as Tmax volume ${>}6$ seconds minus ischemic core volume. For comparisons of treatment groups, we used the time intervals from symptom onset to expected arterial puncture time (OPT) because it was a practical and widely adopted clinical marker in the delivery of EVT in national guidelines for treatment recommendations. The symptom onset time was defined as the time of last known to be well if the patient had a wake-up or unwitnessed onset of stroke. Given the EVT was not administrated in the MM group, we evaluated the OPT by adding the mean time from randomization to arterial puncture of the ANGEL-ASPECT trial (derived from patients allocated to the EVT group) to the symptom onset-to-randomization time (ORT) in each patient in the MM group according to previous research.11 Based on the time windows in previous randomized trials in large ischemic stroke,4-8 OPT was categorized into 3 subgroups: ${<}6.$ , $_{6-<12}$ , and 12–24 hours. Other workflow time intervals of interest were onset to successful reperfusion time (defined as modified treatment in cerebral infarction [mTICI] 2b–3 first achieved), emergency department (ED) arrival to randomization time, ED arrival to arterial puncture time, and ED arrival to reperfusion time.  



# Outcomes  

The functional outcome was assessed according to the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) through face-to-face visits or structured telephone interviews by trained third-party investigators who were unaware of the trial-group assignments. The primary efficacy outcome was favorable outcomes defined as a mRS score of 0–3 at 90 days. In this study, the mRS score of 3 was also considered as favorable outcome, given the prognosis is generally poor because of a large amount of brain tissue deactivated in acute large ischemic stroke.15 The secondary efficacy outcomes were level of disability (shift in distribution of all $7\mathrm{mRS}$ levels) and functional independence (mRS scores 0–2) at 90 days. The safety outcomes included any ICH, symptomatic ICH (sICH) within 48 hours, and mortality within 90 days. The sICH was defined as ICH according to the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification accompanied with following situations: an increase in a subcategory of the NIHSS score of ${\ge}2$ points with any parenchymal intracerebral hemorrhage or an increase of $\geq4$ points in the NIHSS score from baseline, absence of alternative explanation for deterioration, and presence of parenchymal hematoma type 2.18 All imaging data were collected and centrally evaluated by an imaging core laboratory (Tiantan Neuroimaging Center of Excellence) in a blind manner.  



# Statistical Analysis  

The comparisons of baseline variables were analyzed using the one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the Fisher exact test or $\chi^{2}$ test for categorical variables. No imputation was performed because there were no missing data of 90-day mRS scores and baseline values of the main characteristics.  

The associations between treatment modality and binary outcomes were analyzed in trichotomized time subgroups using binary logistic regression, adjusting for potential confounders of age, baseline NIHSS scores, ischemic core volume, occlusion site, and IV thrombolysis, which were known important prognostic variables in previous studies.15,19,20 The adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with $95\%$ CIs were reported. The absolute risk differences (ARDs) were calculated as differences of predicted proportions from regression models. The common odds ratio with $95\%$ CI was analyzed for the ordinal shift in the distribution of mRS scores toward a better outcome after testing of the proportional odds assumption. Another shift analysis was performed with a combination of mRS scores of 5 and 6 into one score because of an unsubstantial improvement of functional status. The heterogeneity of treatment effect across time subgroups was tested by including the multiplicative interaction term into the logistic regression model with adjustment for the abovementioned prognostic variables. To evaluate whether treatment effect varied with extension of OPT (as a linear variable), a multivariable fractional polynomial interaction algorithm was performed, assuming a linear relationship.

We further assessed the associations between variation in care speed and clinical outcomes in the EVT group and MM group, separately, using the multivariable logistic regression models with adjustment for the abovementioned prognostic variables. # Results  



# Patient Characteristics  

A total of 451 patients randomized in the ANGEL-ASPECT trial were included in this study (226 patients receiving EVT plus MM and 225 patients receiving MM alone). The median age was 68 years (interquartile range [IQR] 60–73), $61.4\%$ were men, the median ASPECTS was 3 (IQR 3–4), and the median OPT was 487 minutes (IQR 328–802). There were 139 patients $\left(30.8\%\right)$ with OPT within 6 hours, 180 patients $\left(39.9\%\right)$ within 6–12 hours, and 132 patients $\left(29.3\%\right)$ within 12–24 hours (Figure 1). The frequency distribution of patients at different periods in 2 treatment groups is shown in eFigure 1.  

Baseline characteristics of both treatment modalities in trichotomized OPT subgroups are shown in Table 1. The baseline characteristics were similar between the EVT plus MM and MM alone within each of the 3 periods of OPT. eTable 1 shows details of characteristics among OPT subgroups. The median NIHSS score was 17 [IQR 13–20] in patients treated within 6 hours, 17 [IQR 13–20] within 6–12 hours, and 15 [IQR 12–18] within 12–24 hours $\left(p=0.01\right.$ ). Direct rather than transfer mode of arrival was more common in the early ( $\overline{{\cdot6}}$ hours) vs later windows. Patients in the earlier OPT windows were more often treated with IV thrombolysis ( $42.5\%$ vs $31.1\%$ vs $9.9\%$ ) and had a higher proportion of cardiac embolism ( $57.9\%$ vs $46.7\%$ vs $31.8\%$ ). Patient baseline characteristics according to ORT intervals are also detailed in eTables 2 and 3.

# Benefit of EVT vs MM Alone and Time to Treatment  

Efficacy and safety outcomes in 3 OPT intervals are given in Table 2, and the distribution of mRS scores at 90 days between the EVT and MM groups is shown in Figure 2. Patients who received EVT plus MM had higher rates of favorable outcomes when treated within OPT of 6 hours $(44.4\%$ vs $29.9\%$ ; aOR 2.78 [ $95\%$ CI 1.22–6.32]; ARD, $13.4\%$ $95\%\mathrm{CI}-3.2\%$ to $30.1\%]$ ) and 6–12 hours ( $45.7\%$ vs $29.6\%$ ; aOR 2.39 $95\%$ CI 1.21–4.71]; ARD $17.7\%$ $95\%$ CI $4.1\%-31.2\%],$ ), but not within 12–24 hours ( $51.6\%$ vs $41.4\%$ ; aOR 2.05 [ $95\%$ CI 0.88–4.77]; ARD $13.3\%$ $[95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}-5.9\%$ to $32.5\%]$ ) compared with patients receiving MM alone. Heterogeneity testing of treatment effect by time window was not statistically significant ( $\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}$ interaction $=$ 0.86). There were significant favorable shifts in the distribution of 90-day mRS scores and higher rates of functional independence in the EVT group than in the MM group among all 3 OPT periods. Patients treated with EVT plus MM had higher rates of any ICH than those receiving MM alone, but there was no difference in proportions of sICH or mortality between the EVT and MM groups across all 3 OPT intervals (Table 2).  

In the analyses of OPT as a continuous variable, the benefit of EVT vs MM on favorable outcomes exhibited a nonsignificant log-linear decline with longer OPT. The lower $95\%$ CI for estimated treatment benefit nominally crossed 1.0 at 13 hours and 22 minutes (Figure 3A). The odds ratio for mortality between 2 treatment groups was not statistically significant at any time point (Figure 3B). In sensitivity analyses, the associations of ORT with EVT benefit on clinical outcomes were similar to those observed in the OPT analysis (eTable 4, eFigures 2 and 3). The odds ratio for mRS scores 0–3 at 90 days in the EVT vs MM group showed the time at which the lower $95\%$ CI for estimated treatment benefit first crossed 1.0 at ORT of 13 hours and 4 minutes (eFigure 3A).  



# Associations Between Time Metrics and Clinical Outcomes in Treatment Groups  

Among patients receiving EVT, delay in OPT (aOR 0.99 $95\%$ CI 0.94–1.05]) and longer ED arrival to puncture time (aOR 1.03 $95\%$ CI 0.88–1.19]) were not associated with lower rates of favorable outcomes (Table 3). In the MM group, patients achieved similar rates of 90-day favorable outcomes with delay in expected OPT (aOR 1.03 $[95\%$ CI 0.96–1.09]) and extension in ED arrival to puncture time (aOR 1.04 [ $95\%$ CI 0.88–1.23]). The interaction analyses between treatment modalities and OPT and between treatment modalities and ED arrival to puncture time were not significant. The associations between mRS scores 0–3 and other workflow time metrics were also not significant in both treatment groups (eTable 5).

# Classification of Evidence  

This study provides Class II evidence that EVT is associated with improved functional outcomes for acute large ischemic stroke within 24 hours after last known well, with no interaction by time. This study is Class II because of being a post hoc subgroup analysis of a randomized trial.  



# Discussion  

This post hoc analysis of the ANGEL-ASPECT trial showed that in patients with large ischemic stroke, EVT plus MM was associated with a higher rate of favorable outcomes compared with MM alone when administrated within 13 hours and 22 minutes after onset although the outcome-modifying effect by OPT was not significant. EVT plus MM did not lead to an increased risk of mortality or sICH within a broad time window of 0–24 hours.  

Patients with acute large ischemic stroke suffer from high risk of hemorrhage, edema, and death.5,21-25 Although evidence of randomized trials was reported to favor thrombectomy for this population, neurointerventionists face uncertainty on treating late-presenting patients. In this study, we found that EVT plus MM resulted in better effect on favorable outcomes than MM alone within OPT of 0–6 and 6–12 hours but not in OPT of 12–24 hours in patients with large ischemic stroke. When OPT was analyzed as a continuous variable, the benefit of EVT vs MM alone on favorable outcomes became nonsignificant after 13 hours and 22 minutes, which might represent a more obvious benefit of EVT in the early time window. The potential of EVT vs MM is to salvage penumbra from infarction. A previous study quantifying the penumbral volume related to onset-to-imaging time in large artery occlusive stroke had demonstrated that the penumbral volume decreased in a linear mode as time elapses.26 An additional potential explanation could be increased proportion of atherosclerotic etiology in the later time window. Atherosclerotic thrombosis is often characterized by favorable collaterals,27,28 which could be useful in alleviating the final infarct in patients without recanalization in the MM group and thus decreased relative damage. The absolute increase of favorable outcomes in 12–24 hours of the OPT window in the MM group might support the speculation. However, patients who present later might also benefit from EVT because there was no evidence of treatment interaction across the 3 subgroups. The inconsistent results might be related to the small sample of these patients and merit further verification.

Patients treated with EVT achieved a higher rate of functional independence compared with those treated with MM alone in the OPT 12–24-hour subgroup. This potentially reflected that the critical brain regions remained in penumbra in some patients present later, which could be saved by EVT hence to return to a good state. Indeed, the OPT 12–24-hour subgroup had a lower NIHSS score in case of similar ischemic core at admission. Regarding safety, although the rate of any ICH was higher in the EVT group than in the MM group across the 3 OPT intervals, concordant with the results of the RESCUEJapan LIMIT5 and SELECT 2 trials,6 there was no increased risk of sICH or mortality in the EVT group compared with the MM group with treatment delay. Based on these results, thrombectomy should not be withheld in patients with large ischemic stroke who present later.  

The association between OPT and clinical outcomes in patients receiving MM plus EVT was not significant in this study. The results might arise from imaging selection for treatment, just as findings in patients with small-to-moderate infarct.12,13 Patients who rapidly progress to a large ischemia at an early stage tend to have poor collaterals while patients holding similar infarct at a later stage may have more favorable collaterals to preserve penumbral tissue and slow infarct growth. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with favorable collaterals or slow infarction progression may still achieve good outcomes in the delayed treatment time.29,30 Second, the ASPECTS values were estimated based on noncontrast CT, which could have the tendency to underestimate the degree of early ischemic changes in patients because of its lower sensitivity compared with magnetic resonance imaging.31 This bias might lead to underestimating the efficacy of EVT at an early stage. The association of treatment time with outcomes in large ischemic stroke assessed using magnetic resonance or computerized tomography perfusion imaging remains to be elucidated.  

In contrast to our results, the secondary analysis of the SELECT nonrandomized prospective study showed that functional independence in patients with a baseline ASPECTS of 3–5 or an ischemic core volume of $50{-}100~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ was highly dependent on early revascularization, with a very low likelihood of benefit beyond 12 hours from last seen well to treatment.15 However, as the preponderance of patients in the SELECT study received treatment in an earlier time window, with $75\%$ treated within 5.7 hours, the study was underpowered to well-delineate late treatment effects. Notably, $69\%$ of patients with large ischemic stroke in the SELECT study received IV thrombolysis, which may explain the higher rate of sICH compared with our study ( $13\%$ vs $6.1\%$ ). These findings suggest that for patients with large ischemia who meet ANGEL-ASPECT study inclusion criteria, the dependence of good outcomes on time may not be significant.  

Our results do not imply that patients with large cerebral infarction do not need EVT as soon as possible. Time can be an unreliable indicator of the degree of ischemic injury, particularly in patients who had wake-up or unwitnessed onset of stroke. In this study, patients were evaluated and confirmed to have large ischemic core before treatment. It is unclear what the growth rate of the infarct was before admission. To derive the greatest benefit in patients with large vessel occlusion stroke, it is still essential to go to the hospital for treatment as early as possible after the onset of ischemic stroke because growth of the infarct can be reduced by shortening the time to reperfusion.

Rapid in-hospital care process is important for optimal EVT administration and associated with improved functional outcomes.32 However, the delayed time from ED arrival to arterial puncture was not associated with 90-day functional disability and mortality in this study, and the rate of any ICH within 48 hours decreased with time delay. It is possible that for patients who arrived to the hospital early, especially in the time window of thrombolysis, they tended to have faster speed-of-care process because of urgency of thrombolysis. This bias likely explains the seemingly paradoxical study results because thrombolysis was an independent factor associated with an increased risk of any hemorrhage.33 These results should be interpreted with caution because of the small sample size. Another randomized controlled clinical trial investigating the efficacy and safety of EVT among patients with large ischemic strokes have yet to be published (TESLA34). An individual patient-level meta-analysis of the 6 randomized trials (RESCUE-Japan LIMIT,5 ANGEL-ASPECT,4 SELECT $2,^{6}$ TENSION,7 TESLA,34 and LASTE8) may further inform on the impact of workflow time as related to the potential benefit of EVT in patients with large ischemic stroke.  

There are several limitations in this study. First, although the ANGEL-ASPECT trial was a large-sample randomized clinical trial dedicated to patients with acute large ischemic stroke, the number of patients may have been underpowered for analysis in this study. In addition, the median ischemic core volume of patients in our study was significantly lower than that in other trials; hence, it is uncertain whether our findings can be generalizable for patients who present with larger infarct. Second, postrandomization stratification will not guarantee balance in patients’ characteristics between groups. A multivariable regression analysis has been performed to minimize bias between treatment groups, but we could not eliminate the residual and unmeasured confounders. Third, because our trial excluded patients who were older than 80 years, it is not known whether older patients with large ischemic stroke (ASPECTS 0–5) may derive benefit from EVT because the likelihood of achieving functional independence has been reported to be low.35 Fourth, the proportion of intracranial artery stenosis as the underlying etiology of large vessel occlusion is known to be higher in the Chinese population,36 who tend to present large ischemia in the late window due to collateral development.37 It is unknown whether our results may be applicable in other ethnic populations.  

In patients with large ischemic stroke in the ANGEL-ASPECT trial, EVT plus MM was superior to MM alone in achieving favorable outcomes at 90 days when administrated within 13 hours and 22 minutes of onset. Patients presenting later should not be excluded from EVT, given improved functional disability and acceptable safety outcomes compared with MM alone. Future pooled analysis of larger sample sizes of patients with large ischemic stroke is warranted.
