# ABSTRACT  

Keywords:   
Acute stroke

Hemorrhagic stroke   
Spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage Objective: Decompressive hemicraniectomy (DH) effectively alleviates increased intracranial pressure (ICP) in patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) and malignant middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarction. Its role in the management of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage (SICH) however remains uncertain. This study aims to review the efficacy and safety of DH without clot evacuation in SICH.   
Patients and methods: A systematic literature search of PubMEd, EMBASE, Scopus and Cochrane Library Central Register of Control Trials was performed. Studies were reviewed independently for methodology, inclusion and exclusion criteria and end points. Primary endpoint was overall mortality. Secondary endpoint was functional outcome using modified Rankin scale (mRs) or Glasgow outcome scale (GOS).   
Results: Nine studies with a total of 146 patients who underwent DH without clot evacuation include: 1 RCT, 3 cohort, 2 case series, and 3 case-control studies. Age range was 40–60 years, with majority of patients presenting with a relatively depressed preoperative sensorium (GCS 6–8), large hematoma volumes $(>50\mathrm{mL})$ , and deep locations (basal ganglia and thalamus). Pooled analysis showed a favorable outcome in $53\%$ , a mortality rate of $26~\%$ and a complication rate of $35.8~\%$ .  

Conclusion: DH without clot evacuation may offer functional and mortality benefit in patients with spontaneous ICH, based on limited and heterogeneous studies.  



# 1. Introduction  

Spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage (SICH) remains a major public health problem worldwide. Stroke is the leading cause of disability and ranks second in the leading causes of death after cardiac disease [1]. Hemorrhagic stroke is responsible for $20\%$ of first-ever strokes with a 30-day mortality of $40{-}50~\%$ [2]. The most common cause of SICH is hypertension. Survivors usually are left functionally disabled and require long-term health care.  

To date, the management strategy for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage remains controversial, and the role of surgery equally unclear. The results of two large trials in SICH (STICH I and STICH II) showed that surgery for evacuation of hematoma does not appear to offer any significant advantage over conservative treatment of these patients, except for a small non-significant survival advantage in patients with superficial hemorrhage without IVH [3,4].  

One of the presumed detrimental results of surgery is the additional injury to the brain in the process of evacuating the hematoma. In an attempt to improve surgical outcomes, other operative methods have been proposed ranging from minimally invasive endoscopic techniques ( $\pm$ thrombolytics) to large decompressive hemicraniectomies, all with the goal of decreasing intracranial pressure (ICP) while minimizing iatrogenic brain injury [5].  

Decompressive hemicraniectomy (DH) was first widely used and studied for its role in malignant MCA infarction. Although its impact on functional outcome is still controversial, survival is undoubtedly significantly improved [6]. The use of DH has been expanded to the following conditions when they present with increased ICP refractory to medical treatment: traumatic brain injury (TBI) [7–9], subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) [10–13], and arteriovenous malformation (AVM)

The AHA/ASA guidelines on the management of SICH published in 2015 suggest a favorable outcome with decompressive craniectomy (with or without hematoma evacuation) performed on patients with supratentorial ICH with a midline shift and increased ICP refractory to medical treatment (Class IIB, level C). [15] Since some studies have shown that evacuation of intraparenchymal hematomas, specifically deep seated lesions, led to further tissue damage subsequently contributing to poorer outcomes in surgical groups [16–18], the focus of our study will be on DH alone for SICH. This study therefore aims to do an updated review of the effects of DH without clot evacuation in patients with SICH.  



# 2. Patients and methods  



# 2.1. Literature search  

A systematic literature search of PUBMED, EMBASE, Scopus, and Cochrane Library Central Register of Control Trials up to December 2018 was conducted in order to identify relevant articles for this study. Keywords and MESH terms used were “decompressive hemicraniectomy” with “hemorrhagic stroke” and “intracerebral hemorrhage”. Studies were restricted to published articles in humans but not in any specific language. Additional search and evaluation of the reference list of identified studies were performed for other potential articles. Selection of studies and data extraction were done independently and iteratively by the authors and cross-checked for accuracy.  



# 2.2. Selection  

Studies were considered eligible if they: (1) included adult patients undergoing DH without clot evacuation for CT-proven acute supratentorial hemorrhagic stroke; (2) reported stroke-specific outcomes (GOS, GCS, mRS); and (3) had a minimum follow-up of 1 month. The following were excluded: (1) single case report, review, or commentaries; (2) secondary hemorrhage related to vascular malformations, neoplasm, anticoagulation or amyloid angiopathy; (3) infratentorial hemorrhagic stroke; (4) DH with clot evacuation; and (5) craniotomy with clot evacuation.  



# 2.3. Validity assessment  

Two authors (KMP, AEC) independently assessed selected studies for methodology and endpoints. The PRISMA statement was followed to identify relevant studies. Primary endpoint was mortality. Secondary endpoint was functional outcome using modified Rankin scale (mRS and Glasgow outcome scale (GOS). Favorable outcome was defined in this review as $\mathrm{mRS}0{-}4$ or GOS 4–5. Poor outcome was defined as mRS 5–6 or GOS categories of severely disabled (3), persistently vegetative (2), or dead (1).  



# 2.4. Data collection/statistical analysis  

Relevant data on age, preoperative GCS score, hematoma location and volume, time to surgery, functional outcomes and mortality were extracted. Whenever possible, raw data were used. Measures of central tendency such as mean and median for nominal data were calculated.  

Pooled analysis was done on mortality and functional outcomes to determine whether DH without clot evacuation was associated with better outcomes than other treatment strategies. Individual count data from each study were pooled to obtain unweighted odds ratio and risk ratio while $95~\%$ confidence intervals were computed using Taylor series approach. A p value of $<0.05$ was used as the cut-off value for statistical significance.  



# 3. Results  



# 3.1. Article retrieval  

An initial search from the electronic database yielded 795 studies. Six hundred eighty articles were excluded after reviewing the titles and abstracts. After a full text review of the remaining 115 articles, nine studies were considered eligible for this systematic review (Fig. 1). All patients included in the review underwent DH without clot evacuation as their primary treatment for SICH.  



# 3.2. Study characteristics  

Characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1. A total of 146 patients were derived from the included studies. The 9 studies eligible for review were heterogeneous: 1 randomized controlled trial (RCT), 3 cohort studies, 2 case series and 3 case control studies.  



# 3.3. Patient characteristics  

Mean age was 50 years (range: 40–60 years); Fifty eight percent were males (84 patients). Hematoma location was in the deep nuclei (basal ganglia and thalamus) in $72\%$ and lobar in $28~\%$ . Distance from the cortical surface ranged from $0{-}2\mathrm{cm}$ . Fifty six percent were located in the dominant hemisphere. Data could not be extracted to correlate age, location and laterality to patient outcome.  

The mean or median preoperative hematoma volume was $>50\mathrm{mL}$ in majority of patients (8 out of 9 studies). Hematoma volume was correlated to outcome in 5 studies and found to be inversely related [19,21,23,24,26]. One hundred and three patients with a hematoma volume $>60\mathrm{mL}$ had a favorable outcome in only $15~\%$ , with 88 patients left either severely disabled or dead at 3 months.  

The presence of intraventricular hemorrhage $(\mathtt{p}<0.008)$ was significantly associated with poor outcome in the study done by Satter et al. [26].  



# 3.4. Surgical intervention and complications  

Indications for surgical intervention were mentioned in only 4 studies [20,23,26,27]. The most common criteria were: 1.) $\mathtt{GCS}<15,$ 2.) clinical deterioration from admission status, 3.) worsening focal deficits and 4.) development of ocular nerve dysfunction. The preoperative GCS in 7 studies was 8 and below. [19–23,25,27]. Time to decompressive hemicraniectomy from onset of hemorrhage was reviewed in 7 studies [19–23,25,27]. Ultra early surgery within $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ postictus reported in one study was associated with poor outcomes in 2 out of 3 patients $(66\%)$ [19]. The remaining 20 patients who had surgery more than $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ from ictus had a poor outcome in $40\%$ (8 out of 20 patients). Majority of patients in the remaining studies $(87\%)$ had surgery done within 12−61 hours postictus.  

Surgical technique of DH was described in five studies [20,23,25–27]. The minimum bone flap diameter recommended was at least $12\mathrm{cm}$ in 2 studies [25,26] and at least $15\mathrm{cm}$ in the remaining 3 studies [20,23,27].  

Complications from DH without clot evacuation were reported in only 5 of the 9 studies. [20,22,24,25,27] The morbidity rate was $35.8\%$ (24/67) by pooled analysis. The morbidities reported were intraparenchymal hematoma, subdural hematoma, empyema, subdural hygroma, hydrocephalus, skin necrosis, temporalis muscle swelling and meningitis. (Table 5).

# 3.5. Outcomes  

Mortality data was available for 136 patients in this review. Thirtyfive patients $(26\%)$ who underwent DH without clot evacuation died at 1 month – 2 years from surgery (Table 2). Functional outcome was favorable in $53~\%$ (78/146).  

The three case-control studies compared DH without clot evacuation versus best medical therapy. Pooled analysis showed that patients who underwent DH has a $0.34\mathbf{x}$ lower odds of mortality $(95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}=0.13\$ , 0.92, $\mathbf{p}=0.017,$ ). However, less patients $32\%$ vs $35\%$ ) who had surgery attained functional independence (MRS 0–3). The difference though was not statistically significant $(\mathtt{p}=0.402)$ (Table 3).  

Two out of 3 cohort studies [23,26] compared DH without clot evacuation to conservative/medical treatment. Conservative/medical treatment was standardized by strictly following current AHA/ASA practice guidelines for SICH [15]. The remaining cohort study had patients undergoing craniotomy with clot evacuation as their control group [22]. After pooled analysis, mortality rate was $29\%$ (19/66) for the DH group, and $42\%$ (35/84) for the non-DH group. The difference however was not statistically significant $(\mathbf{p}=0.056)$ . For functional outcomes, patients who underwent DH had a $\boldsymbol{1.41\mathrm{x}}$ higher likelihood of attaining a good functional outcome $95\%{\mathrm{CI}}=1.04$ , 1.89, $\mathbf{p}=0.01$ ) (Table 4).  

Only one randomized controlled trial comparing DH alone to craniotomy with clot evacuation was available for review [27]. This was recently published in 2018. Rasras et al. randomized 30 patients with basal ganglia bleeds measuring at least $40\mathrm{mL}$ to either DH without clot evacuation or craniotomy with clot evacuation. # 4. Discussion  

This systematic review was conducted to evaluate whether DH without clot evacuation is a viable option for SICH patients presenting with persistently elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) despite best medical treatment. Nine studies met the inclusion criteria.

Preoperative GCS, hematoma volume and location have been identified as prognostic factors in patients with SICH. Broderick and his colleagues looked at predictors of 30 day mortality in patients with SICH [28]. The probability of death was $91\%$ in patients with ${\mathrm{GCS}}\leq8$ , while patients with a $\mathbf{GCS}\geq9$ had a significantly lower risk of death at $19~\%$ . Also, their study showed that the most predictive factor of outcome was hematoma volume. The mortality rate for hematoma volumes $<30~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ was $23\%$ ; $64~\%$ for hematoma volumes $30{\cdot}60~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ and $93~\%$ for hematoma volumes $>60~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ .  

A deep location of ICH has been associated with poor outcomes. In the study by Ramnarayan et al., mortality for patients with putaminal hemorrhage was as high as $40{-}50~\%$ while hematomas in the basal ganglia had mortality rates of $50{-}60~\%$ [19]. Superficial location may predict better outcomes because deep grey nuclei are usually spared. In patients who underwent surgical evacuation of hematomas in the STITCH I trial, a small but clinically relevant survival advantage was seen in patients with superficial ( $\leq1~\mathrm{cm}$ from the surface) bleeds.  

Although data could not be extracted from the 9 studies in ou review to determine the relationship of preoperative GCS, hematoma volume and location to patient’s outcomes, the survival and functional outcome advantage seen in the DH alone patients despite the presence of severely depressed preoperative sensorium (GCS 6–8), hematoma volumes $>50\mathrm{ml}$ and deep hematoma locations in majority of the study population lead us to infer that DH alone may provide a benefit in SICH patients despite the presence of these poor prognostic factors.  

The optimal surgical approach to spontaneous ICH remains uncertain. The primary goal in the surgical management of SICH is the control of increased ICP. Elevated ICP in SICH may result from the following factors, namely hematoma volume and the subsequent edema formation that progresses within the first $24\mathrm{h}$ in $75\%$ of patients [29,30], with a secondary peak during the second week from ICH onset [31].  

In the landmark paper by McKissock et al. in 1961, the surgical evacuation of ICH was associated with a higher chance of death or dependency compared with medical management alone [32]. Subsequent studies by Schwartz and Tan also concluded that routine evacuation of SICH could not be recommended [33,34]. In the recent International Surgical Trial in ICH (STITCH I and II) with $>1000$ patients enrolled, craniotomy for surgical evacuation of ICH failed to improve outcomes compared to medical treatment. [3,4]. The subgroup of patients with superficial hematomas $\leq1\mathrm{cm}$ from the surface) without intraventricular hemorrhage showed a small but clinically significant advantage.  

The less favorable outcomes seen among patients who underwent hematoma evacuation (except patients with superficial hematomas) in these studies may suggest that the iatrogenic harm caused by surgery might outweigh any benefit from clot evacuation. This may be from tissue manipulation during surgical evacuation leading to further parenchymal injury and edema formation [21].  

The promising results of DH applied to other pathologies (malignant MCA infarct, TBI, SAH, AVM bleeds) led to neurosurgeons applying it to patients with SICH as well. DH offers the advantage of a relatively fast procedure with no additional brain injury when compared to hematoma evacuation by craniotomy. Preliminary animal studies have shown that DH resulted in a significant time dependent reduction in mortality and poor neurological and behavioral outcome as compared to no craniectomy [35]. Mathai et al. showed that DH with a bone flap diameter of $10\mathrm{cm}$ with durotomy provided an additional intracranial volume of $50\mathrm{ml}$ [36]. Bone removal resulted in $30\%$ of volume expansion seen in DH, with the remaining $70\%$ achieved by dural opening. PET (Positron Emission Tomography) studies have shown that DH contributed to improvements in cerebral compliance, cerebral oxygen delivery and cerebral blood perfusion [37]. Fung and his associates reported that DH in patients with SICH can ameliorate pressure effects for SICH and its associated edema as evidenced by a decrease in postoperative radiographic midline shift [38].  

The benefit of DH in addition to hematoma evacuation is already well established in the literature. Ma and colleagues showed an improvement in 30-day mortality and 6-month functional outcome in patients with decompressive hemicraniectomy with evacuation of spontaneous basal ganglia hemorrhage vs clot evacuation alone [39]. In the most recent RCT by Moussa and his coworkers, decompressive hemicraniectomy with expansile duraplasty was shown to have a statistically significant advantage over evacuation alone in terms of functional outcome ( $70\%$ vs $20\%$ , $\mathrm{~p~}<0.0015;$ [16]. They pointed out that while we commonly find a relaxed, slack brain after evacuation of hypertensive bleeds, DH is still warranted because of (1) postoperative edema and (2) potential delayed edema from residual hematoma and/ or rebleeding.  

The lone RCT in our study did not show a statistical significance in mortality and functional outcomes between patients who underwent DH alone versus craniotomy for clot evacuation [27]. The weight of these results should be tempered by the fact that their sample population was only 30 patients, computed based on a pilot study that was not published nor discussed in detail in their RCT. Our pooled analysis showed a mortality rate of $26~\%$ and a functional independence outcome in $53\%$ . This compares favorably with a recent systematic review by Takeuchi et al. looking at patients with ICH who underwent DH with hematoma evacuation. In their review of 191 patients, mortality rate was $28~\%$ and good functional outcome was seen in $41\%$ [40]. These findings suggest that DH may have a potential mortality/morbidity advantage independent of hematoma evacuation. The SWITCH trial (Swiss Trial of Decompressive Craniectomy versus Best Medical Treatment of Spontaneous Supratentorial Intracerebral Hemorrhage) which commenced in 2014 is still ongoing in Bern, and actively recruiting patients to further look into the efficacy of DH alone in the management of SICH [41]. This is a multi-center randomized controlled trial randomizing patients into group A (DH without clot evacuation) vs group B (medical treatment). It is projected to enroll 300 patients and aims to describe the outcome of these patients using $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{mRS}}0-4$ at 6 mos followup as the definition of good outcome (available online www.switchtrial.ch).  

Timing of surgery has also been considered as a prognostic factor in improving outcomes from surgery for ICH. A recent meta-analysis on surgery for ICH suggested a benefit of early surgery in ICH [42]. This must be weighed against the fact however that a study by Morgestern and colleagues have shown that early surgery for ICH is complicated by rebleeding [43]. In the study by Ramnarayan et al., $13~\%$ (3/23) had surgery within $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ , and these patients had a poorer outcome compared to patients who underwent DH beyond $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ [19]. Whether this arose from early rebleeding could not be proven. STITCH II showed that early surgical intervention (within $^{12\mathrm{h}}$ from ictus) did not increase the rate of death and disability at 6 months. Some authors have raised the issue that DH might actually relieve the tamponade effect exerted by the hematoma, leading to further rebleeding [44,45]. This possibility was not supported by the results in the case control study by Fung et al., where a subgroup analysis showed no significant increase in rebleeding among patients who underwent DH [38].  

Newer studies on surgery for ICH are now focusing on the use of minimally invasive techniques in clot evacuation. Though offering the potential for less tissue injury, a recent meta-analysis on minimally invasive surgery for SICH concluded that patients who are most likely to benefit from this procedure are patients who have GCS scores of 9 or greater and hematoma volumes between $25-40\mathrm{mL}$ [5]. Whether the benefits derived from these procedures are secondary to better patient selection rather than a true effect has yet to be elucidated. Moreover, access to these technologies may not be universally and readily available in resource challenged settings.  

Complications after DH have been described in the literature. Immediate complications include external herniation which can lead to cortical laceration, contusions or vessel damage [46]. Delayed complications are hydrocephalus, subdural hygromas, infections, hemorrhage and syndrome of the trephined. In our review, $35.8~\%$ (24/67) of patients developed complications related to DH, with half of them requiring surgical intervention. Takeuchi in his systematic review of DH with additional hematoma removal reported postoperative hydrocephalus in $20\%$ of their patients [39]. In contrast, hydrocephalus was seen in $<1~\%$ of patients who underwent DH without clot evacuation.  



# 5. Limitations  

There are several limitations to this systematic review. The primary limitation of our study was the absence of significant controlled prospective data. There was only 1 RCT in our review. The rest were case control and cohort studies with varying control groups. Another limitation is the heterogeneity in terms of follow up and the definition of good outcome. Follow up periods ranged from 1 month to as long as 2 years. Because the definition of good outcome in ICH studies still has to be standardized, uncertainty remains and is reflected in the variability of measures used in the studies included in this review: some used mRS  

0–3 as a basis for good outcome while other studies used an mRS of 0–4. GOS score of 4–5 was also used in 3 studies to define good outcome. While this heterogeneity may limit the uniformity of our results, this does not necessarily limit generalizability, as it has been shown that the mRS and GOS scoring systems have a high correlation in these patients [47]. Other limitations include lack of a uniform criteria for surgery in these patients as well as the small number of patients recruited in each study.  



# 6. Conclusion  

Decompressive hemicraniectomy without clot evacuation is safe, and may be an effective surgical option in SICH patients with deep, large hematomas and a depressed sensorium. Pooled analysis showed that DH without clot evacuation resulted in a good functional outcome in $53~\%$ and a mortality rate of $26~\%$ among patients with SICH. Our results are limited by the quality and heterogeneity of the studies available for review; with only a small number of patients enrolled and non-uniform follow up periods and outcome assessments. Better quality studies (i.e. multi center randomized controlled trials) are needed to further confirm our findings. The results of the ongoing SWITCH trial could potentially help resolve the role of DH alone in the management of patients with spontaneous ICH.
