# Abstract  

# Background and Objectives  

The current European Stroke Organisation expedited recommendation on tenecteplase (TNK) for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) advocates that TNK $0.25~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ can be used alternatively to alteplase (tissue plasminogen activator [TPA]) for AIS of ${<}4.5$ hours duration, based on a meta-analytical approach establishing noninferiority. Since the publication of these guidelines, 4 additional randomized controlled clinical trials (RCTs) have provided further insight.  

# Methods  

We conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis including all available RCTs that investigated efficacy and safety of TNK $0.25\mathrm{mg/kg}$ compared with TPA for the treatment of AIS within 4.5 hours of onset. The primary outcome was defined as the excellent functional outcome at 3 months (modified Rankin Scale $\mathrm{[mRS]}$ score 0–1), whereas good functional outcome (mRS score 0–2), reduced disability at 3 months ( ${\bf\tilde{\rho}}_{21}$ -point reduction across all mRS scores), symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), and 3-month mortality were evaluated as secondary outcomes. Pooled estimates were calculated with random-effects model. A prespecified subgroup analysis was performed stratifying for TNK formulation, that is, original TNK vs biocopy: recombinant human TNK tissue-type plasminogen activator that is available in China and has a different production process.  

# Results  

Eleven RCTs were included comprising a total of 3,788 patients treated with TNK vs 3,757 patients treated with TPA. TNK was associated with higher likelihood of excellent functional outcome (risk ratio [RR] 1.05, $95\%$ CI 1.01–1.10; $p=0.012$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ ; risk difference $2.95\%$ ; $95\%$ CI $0.76\%-5.14\%_{.}$ ; $p=0.008$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ ) and reduced disability at 3 months (common odds ratio 1.10, $95\%$ CI 1.01–1.19; $p=0.034$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ ) compared with TPA while good functional outcome (RR 1.03, $95\%$ CI 0.99–1.07; $p=0.142$ ; $I^{2}=28\%$ ) was similar between the groups. Regarding safety outcomes, similar rates of sICH (RR 1.12, $95\%$ CI 0.83–1.53; $p=0.4\dot{5}6$ ; $I^{\bar{2}}=$ $0\%$ ) and 3-month mortality (RR 0.97, $95\%$ CI 0.82–1.15; $p=0.727$ ; $I^{2}=12\%$ ) were observed. When stratified for TNK regimen (original vs biocopy), statistical significance in achieving an excellent functional outcome at 3 months was retained for the original TNK (RR 1.05, $95\%$ CI 1.00–1.10; $p=0.044$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ ).

# Introduction  

Intravenous thrombolysis with tissue plasminogen activator (TPA) alteplase is the only approved systemic reperfusion treatment of acute ischemic stroke (AIS).1-4 Current international recommendations advocate the infusion of TPA at a dose of $0.9~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ as the standard of care,1-3 whereas the expedited European Stroke Organisation (ESO) guidelines and the Australian and New Zealand living guidelines recommend the use of tenecteplase (TNK) at a dose of $0.25\mathrm{mg/}$ $\mathrm{kg}$ as an alternative to TPA for unselected patients with AIS presenting within the first 4.5 hours after symptom onset.3,5  

Given the feasibility of TNK administration (third-generation fibrinolytic with longer half-life) at a single bolus dose,6 there is an expert recommendation suggesting that TNK may even be preferred over TPA (second-generation fibrinolytic) that requires a bolus dose followed by an 1-hour infusion. This recommendation was based on a meta-analysis5 establishing the noninferiority of TNK compared with TPA, using aggregate data from 7 randomized controlled clinical trials (RCTs): the Study of Tenecteplase in Acute Ischaemic Stroke (TNK-S2B),7 the Tenecteplase versus Alteplase for Acute Ischaemic Stroke,8 the Alteplase-Tenecteplase Trial Evaluation for Stroke Thrombolysis (ATTEST),9 the Tenecteplase versus Alteplase before Endovascular Therapy for Ischaemic Stroke,10 the Tenecteplase Reperfusion Therapy in Acute Ischaemic Cerebrovascular Events (TRACE),11 the Tenecteplase Versus Alteplase for Stroke Thrombolysis Evaluation Trial in the Ambulance (TASTE-A),12 and the Alteplase compared to Tenecteplase (AcT).13 However, since the publication of these guidelines, 4 additional RCTs, comparing TNK vs TPA for the treatment of AIS in the standard time window, have been presented.  

In view of the former considerations and because there is no major ongoing RCT comparatively evaluating TNK and TPA in patients with AIS presenting during the first 4.5 hours from symptom onset, we sought to conduct an updated systematic review and meta-analysis by including all available studies so far and assessing the efficacy and safety of TNK vs TPA during intravenous thrombolysis for AIS within the first 4.5 hours after onset. We also performed a trial sequential analysis to determine whether there is evidence to establish a firm conclusion regarding efficacy and safety of TNK.  

# Methods  

# Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents  

The prespecified protocol for this systematic review and metaanalysis has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Ongoing Systematic Reviews PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD42024535995) and adheres to the latest Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses reporting guidelines.14 No ethical board approval or written informed consent from patients was necessary because of the study’s design (systematic review and metaanalysis).  

# Data Sources, Searches, and Study Selection  

A systematic literature search was conducted to identify available RCTs including adult patients with AIS eligible to receive intravenous thrombolysis within 4.5 hours since symptom onset (P: population) with TNK at a dose of 0.25 $\mathrm{mg/kg}$ (I: intervention) vs TPA at a dose of $0.9~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ (C: comparator). Reporting of functional outcomes at follow-up (O: outcome) was required for studies to be considered eligible for inclusion. The literature search was performed independently by 2 reviewers (L.P., and A.H.K.). We searched MEDLINE and Scopus using search strings that included the following terms: “stroke,” “tenecteplase,” “alteplase,” and “intravenous thrombolysis.” No language or other restrictions were applied. Our search spanned from inception of each database to May 16, 2024. Furthermore, the reference lists of published articles and international conference abstracts were manually scrutinized to ensure the completeness of the bibliography.  

Observational cohort studies, noncontrolled studies, case series, and case reports were excluded. Commentaries, editorials, and narrative reviews were also discarded. All retrieved studies were independently assessed by the 3 reviewers (L.P., A.H.K., and A. Safouris), resolving any disagreements through discussion with the corresponding author (G. Tsivgoulis).  

# Quality Control, Bias Assessment, and Data Extraction  

Quality control and bias assessment among eligible studies were performed using the Cochrane Collaboration risk-ofbias (RoB 2) tool for RCTs,15 independently by 2 reviewers (L.P., and A.H.K.), while disagreements were settled by consensus after discussion with the corresponding author (G. Tsivgoulis). Structured forms that included trial names, period of enrollment, patient sample, patients’ characteristics, and outcomes of interest were used for data extraction. In case of studies that were unpublished in peer-reviewed journals, data were extracted from presentations at international conferences.  

# Outcomes  

The primary outcome of interest was excellent functional outcome at 3 months, defined as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ${\leq}1.^{16}$ Good functional outcome at 3 months (defined as $\mathrm{mRS}\leq2$ ), reduced disability at 3 months (defined as ${\geq}1$ -point reduction across all mRS strata), symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) as defined by each study, and all-cause mortality at 3 month were assessed as secondary outcomes.  

# Statistical Analysis  

For the pairwise meta-analysis, we calculated for each dichotomous outcome of interest the corresponding risk ratios (RRs) with $95\%$ CI for the comparison of outcome events among patients receiving TNK vs TPA in intention to treat. The number needed to treat (NNT) was calculated using the formula: NNT = ðRR − 1Þ  x  rate1  in  TPA  group, as previously described.17,18 The risk difference (RD) was also calculated for the primary outcome of interest.5 For the evaluation of reduced disability at 3 months, the unadjusted common odds ratio (cOR) with $95\%$ CI was calculated using generic inverse variance meta-analysis. A prespecified subgroup analysis was performed stratifying for TNK formulation, that is, original TNK vs biocopy: recombinant human TNK tissue-type plasminogen activator for injection that is available in China and has a different production process. Comparison of the baseline characteristics to assess the balance between the 2 arms was performed using odds ratios for dichotomous variables and standardized mean differences for continuous variables. For studies reporting continuous outcomes in median values and corresponding interquartile ranges, we estimated the sample mean and standard deviation using the quantile estimation method.20 The random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird, 1986) was used to calculate the pooled estimates.21 The threshold for statistical significance for the above analyses was set at 2-sided $p$ -value of $_{<0.05}$ . Heterogeneity was assessed with the $I^{2}$ and Cochran $Q$ statistics. For the qualitative interpretation of heterogeneity, $I^{2}$ values $<25\%$ , between $25\%-50\%$ , and ${>}50\%$ were considered to represent low, moderate, and significant heterogeneity, respectively. The significance level for the $Q$ statistic was set at 0.1. Prediction intervals were also calculated for all outcomes of interest. Small-study effects, as a proxy for publication bias, were assessed when at least 10 studies were included in the analysis of the outcomes of interest, using both funnel plot inspection and Egger linear regression test,22 with a 2-sided $p$ - value $<0.1$ being considered statistically significant. The above statistical analyses were performed using R software version 3.5.0 (package: meta).23  

Furthermore, trial sequential analysis was performed for the primary outcome of interest to determine whether there is evidence to establish a firm conclusion regarding efficacy of TNK. Trial sequential analysis is a cumulative meta-analysis in which each trial is considered an interim analysis, controlling for type I and type II errors to address multiplicity issues. Trial sequential analysis provides statistical boundaries for efficacy and futility and offers an estimation of the required information size to attain a predetermined level of power.24 Crossing the futility or efficacy boundaries before reaching this required information size enables the investigators to ascertain whether an intervention (in our case, TNK) is effective even if the required information size has not been attained and avoid the need for further evidence gathering in the future. Conversely, if no conclusive evidence is obtained, additional trials are warranted. The number of patients required for a meta-analysis to come to valid conclusion was influenced by the following factors: (1) type I error risk, set to $5\%$ ; (2) type II error risk, set to $10\%$ ; (3) control event proportion, derived from the accumulated control groups of the included trials; (4) heterogeneity between trials, taken from the empirical distribution of heterogeneity variance estimates as estimated from Cochrane Reviews25; and (5) anticipated intervention effect, set as $\mathrm{RD}=3.7\%$ , as previously shown in the ESO expedited recommendation,5 and the corresponding RR in the proportions of patients in $\mathrm{mRS}0{-}1$ between the 2 treatment groups. To handle zero-event trials, we used the empirical continuity adjustment method.26 We added an adjustment factor of 0.5 to the number of events in the TNK group and in the TPA group in case of zero events in 1 group. The above statistical analyses were performed using R software version 3.5.0 (package: RTSA).27

# Results  

# Literature Search and Included Studies  

The flow diagram for the selection and inclusion of studies in this systematic review is presented in Figure 1. After excluding duplicates, the systematic literature database search yielded a total of 731 records. Following the initial screening process, the full texts of 13 records were retrieved. After reading the full-text articles, 5 records were further excluded, leaving 8 studies eligible for inclusion. In addition, 5 records were identified through the international conference abstracts’ search, 2 of which were excluded being duplicate to studies already identified through databases, leaving 3 studies eligible for inclusion. Finally, we included 11 eligible studies in the systematic review and meta-analysis,7-13,28-31 comprising a total of 3,788 patients treated with TNK (mean age 69 years, $62\%$ men, mean NIH Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score 11, mean onset to treatment time 158 minutes, $12\%$ receiving additional endovascular treatment) vs 3,757 patients treated with TPA (mean age 70 years, $60\%$ men, mean NIHSS score 11, mean onset to treatment time 158 minutes, $11\%$ receiving additional endovascular treatment). The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.  

# Quality Control of Included Studies  

The risk of bias among the included RCTs was assessed by the Cochrane RoB 2 tool and is presented in Figure 2. There was no randomization or reporting bias in any of the studies. Some concerns because of deviations from intended interventions were present in the AcT (16 patients did not receive randomized treatment), ATTEST (1 patient did not receive randomized treatment), ATTEST-2 (3 patients did not receive randomized treatment), Study in Chinese Patients to Compare How Tenecteplase and Alteplase Given After a Stroke Improve Recovering of Physical Activity (ORIGINAL; 1 patient did not receive randomized treatment), TASTE (9 patients did not receive randomized treatment), TNK-S2B (2 patients did not receive randomized treatment), and TRACE-2 (3 patients did not receive randomized treatment) trials that were balanced between the patient groups and were not likely to have affected the outcome. Some concerns because of missing outcome data that exceeded $5\%$ were present in the ATTEST-2 trial. Missing data were also present in the $\mathrm{AcT}$ (10 patients were lost to follow-up) and TRACE-2 (16 patients were lost to follow-up) trials, yet they did not exceed $5\%$ of the included population, thus inducing no significant concerns. Finally, masking to treatment allocation for clinical end points’ assessment could not be guaranteed in the ATTEST trial, leading to some concerns in the outcome measurement.  

# Quantitative Analyses  

Regarding the baseline characteristics (eFigures 1–5), there were no significant differences in age, sex, NIHSS, onset to treatment time, or proportion of patients receiving additional endovascular treatment.

# Discussion  

This systematic review and meta-analysis including all available concluded RCTs so far shows that TNK is superior to TPA in achieving excellent functional outcome and reduced disability at 3 months among patients with AIS treated in the standard time window. Good functional outcome was similar between the 2 groups, although the point estimate favored TNK. There were no safety issues of TNK compared with TPA, presenting similar rates for sICH and all-cause mortality at 3 months. In addition, original TNK was associated with higher rates of excellent functional outcome compared with TPA in the prespecified subgroup analysis. Finally, the trial sequential analysis provides additional evidence in favor of the conclusive results of our study.  

Those present data derived from aggregate data metaanalysis of RCTs complement and confirm previously published data from real-world evidence studies. A previous meta-analysis of exclusively observational data, derived from 6 nonrandomized studies and including a total of 1,820 patients, has shown that TNK was associated with improved functional outcomes at 3 months compared with TPA (adjusted OR 1.60, $95\%$ CI 1.08–2.37).32 Similarly, in 2022, the analysis of more than 9,000 patient data as registered in the Safe Implementation of Treatments in Stroke–International Stroke Thrombolysis Register showed that indeed TNK was associated with better functional outcomes at 3 months (cOR 1.54, $95\%$ CI 1.18–2.00), without raising the risk of sICH (OR 0.72, $95\%$ CI 0.20–2.64).33 More recently, the results of the large multicenter international Comparative Effectiveness of Routine Tenecteplase vs Alteplase in Acute Ischemic Stroke (CERTAIN) collaboration were published, including 9,238 patients and showing that the rate of sICH was actually lower among TNK-treated patients compared with patients receiving TPA (adjusted OR 0.42, $95\%$ CI 0.30–0.58).34 More specifically, it was shown that the rate of sICH in the TNK group was $1.8\%_{,}^{34}$ which is very similar to the one derived from the rate calculated through single-arm meta-analysis of our data: The pooled proportion of sICH among TNK-treated patients was $1.9\%$ . The accordance between RCT and real-world data reassures regarding both the efficacy and effectiveness of TNK administration in patients with AIS treated in either “ideal” or everyday clinical settings.35 Moreover, the results of our study are in line with a previous meta-analysis of RCTs showing that patients with AIS with large-vessel occlusion receiving TNK have significantly better clinical outcomes compared with patients receiving TPA.36

Our study shows that TNK is not only noninferior but also superior to TPA, putting forward the need for modifying the clinical practice and adopting TNK treatment in patients with AIS presenting in the standard time window. Multiple studies have already investigated the transition from TPA to TNK,37-  

39 showing that such a practice is feasible, safe, and effective for patients, while it also reduces process times and facilitates faster interhospital transfers. In addition, TNK has been shown to be cost-effective compared with TPA for the treatment of patients with AIS with or without large-vessel occlusion.40,41 All parties involved in stroke care (including stroke and emergency clinicians, pharmacists, national regulatory bodies, and hospital legal teams) were satisfied with TNK implementation.39 However, there are some potential barriers that need to be considered,42 such as the medicolegal issues in each country, the negotiation procedures between the industry and each country’s medicine agency, the availability of TNK in hospital pharmacies, and the potential of shortage in case of massive implementation. A potential approach to those barriers could be transitioning in a stepwedged fashion, further allowing for better education of the involved personnel to reduce the likelihood of dosing errors and for updating existing protocols and standard operating procedures in each institution.

Although there was no significant heterogeneity in any of our assessed outcomes, it is important to state that not all studies used the same TNK regimen. Nine studies used original TNK while biocopy TNK was used in TRACE and TRACE-II. Biocopies, also known as noninnovator biological products, are not necessarily equivalent products compared with the original products and may differ with regard to the physicochemical and pharmacologic properties.19 Specifically for TNK, previous in vitro studies have shown that the biocopy presents lower clot lysis activity compared with the original TNK while it also contains considerably more amounts of aggregates and foreign proteins .43,44 To account for potential differences, all our outcomes were assessed through stratification for the TNK regimen. No significant subgroup differences emerged, and the effect estimate was similar for both original and biocopy TNK. Statistical significance in achieving an excellent functional outcome at 3 months was retained for the original TNK group (RR 1.05, $95\%$ CI 1.00–1.10; $p=$ 0.044; ${\bf\bar{\calI}}^{2}=0\%$ ), whereas, among the patients receiving biocopy TNK, the difference compared with TPA was not statistically significant. It is expected, though, that the studies using biocopy TNK $(\mathtt{n}=2$ ) do not have enough statistical power to demonstrate superiority.  

Regarding the robustness of our results, not only was the difference for the excellent functional outcome statistically significant between the 2 groups but it also persisted during the assessment of the prediction intervals (1.01–1.10). This finding further supports that no significant variation is expected over different settings when treating future patients.45 Similarly, this is also the case according to the conducted trial sequential analysis showing that the efficacy boundaries were crossed, and the intervention is conclusive when taking account sparse data and multiple testing.  

TNK is being actively investigated in other settings as well, addressing the sustainability of its efficacy and safety in more complex cases. Apart from treating patients presenting in the standard time window, TNK has also been tested among patients with a later or unknown presentation. According to a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, TNK presents increased efficacy compared with standard of care in the treatment of AIS in the extended time window.46 However, this meta-analysis was based on data extracted from only 3 included RCTs, thus its results can be interpreted as hypothesis generating only, further supporting the need for ongoing RCTs in the field. Another important finding of this meta-analysis, though, is again the low rate of sICH in the extended time window, highlighting the safety of TNK.
