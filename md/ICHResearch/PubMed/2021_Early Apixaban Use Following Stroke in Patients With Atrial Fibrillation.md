# Early Apixaban Use Following Stroke in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation  

Results of the AREST Trial  

Arthur J. Labovitz , MD; David Z. Rose , MD; Michael G. Fradley , MD; John N. Meriwether, MD; Swetha Renati , MD; Ryan Martin, MD; Thomas Kasprowicz, MD; Ryan Murtagh , MD; Kevin Kip, PhD; Theresa M. Beckie , PhD, MN, RN; Marcus Stoddard, MD; Andrea C. Bozeman, APRN; Tara McTigue, RN; Bonnie Kirby , RN, MSN; Nhi Tran , MS; W. Scott Burgin , MD; on behalf of the AREST Investigators  

BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: It is unknown when to start anticoagulation after acute ischemic stroke (AIS) from atrial fibrillation (AF). Early anticoagulation may prevent recurrent infarctions but may provoke hemorrhagic transformation as AF strokes are typically larger and hemorrhagic transformation-prone. Later anticoagulation may prevent hemorrhagic transformation but increases risk of secondary stroke in this time frame. Our aim was to compare early anticoagulation with apixaban in AF patients with stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) versus warfarin administration at later intervals.  

METHODS: AREST (Apixaban for Early Prevention of Recurrent Embolic Stroke and Hemorrhagic Transformation) was an open-label, randomized controlled trial comparing the safety of early use of apixaban at day 0 to 3 for TIA, day 3 to 5 for small-sized AIS $(<1.5\mathrm{cm})$ , and day 7 to 9 for medium-sized AIS $(\geq1.5$ cm, excluding full cortical territory), to warfarin, in a 1:1 ratio at 1 week post-TIA, or 2 weeks post-AIS.  

RESULTS: Although AREST ended prematurely after a national guideline focused update recommended direct oral anticoagulants over warfarin for AF, it revealed that apixaban had statistically similar yet generally numerically lower rates of recurrent strokes/TIA ( $14.6\%$ versus $19.2\%_{\cdot}$ , $_{P=0.78,}$ ), death $4.9\%$ versus $8.5\%$ , $_{P=0.68}$ ), fatal strokes $2.4\%$ versus $8.5\%$ , $_{P=0.37}$ ), symptomatic hemorrhages $(0\%$ versus $2.1\%$ ), and the primary composite outcome of fatal stroke, recurrent ischemic stroke, or TIA ( $17.1\%$ versus $25.5\%$ , $\widetilde{P{=}0.44}$ ). One symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage occurred on warfarin, none on apixaban. Five asymptomatic hemorrhagic transformation occurred in each arm.  

CONCLUSIONS: Early initiation of anticoagulation after TIA, small-, or medium-sized AIS from AF does not appear to compromise patient safety. Potential efficacy of early initiation of anticoagulation remains to be determined from larger pivotal trials.

Early DOAC use may result in a favorable outcome for secondary prevention,12–14 if started between 4 and 14 days poststroke, potentially even within 2 to 3 days.9 Currently, the decision on anticoagulation timing is patientcentered, without randomized trials,3 and delay is driven by perception of elevated HT risk influenced by historical data, provider recall bias, knowledge gaps,15,16 and lenient guidelines on the subject (class IIa recommendations for 3–14 days, level of evidence B [United States] and C [Europe]).17,18 Thus, we conducted the investigator-initiated, randomized, open-label AREST study (Apixaban for Early Prevention of Recurrent Embolic Stroke and Hemorrhagic Transformation), to examine early DOAC use at prespecified timepoints based on stroke size, versus conventional VKA at 14 days, or 7 days for TIA.19  

# METHODS  

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.  

# Patient Population  

AREST was approved and regularly reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of the University of South Florida. A data safety monitoring board composed of national experts in vascular (stroke) neurology, cardiology, and epidemiology regularly reviewed enrollment and outcomes data for safety and methodology. All participants provided written informed consent to participate. AREST design details were previously published.19  

The AREST randomization target was 120 patients to provide a detailed assessment of safety of apixaban, and while not powered specifically for efficacy, to have $80\%$ power to detect a potential absolute risk reduction of $16.5\%$ with apixaban compared with warfarin therapy. Eligible subjects included individuals $\geq18$ years, with a new ischemic stroke or a TIA diagnosed within 48 hours of symptom onset, plus historical or newly diagnosed AF by ECG, telemetry, or other cardiac electronic device, such as insertable loop recorders or wearable patch. An arrhythmia adjudication committee confirmed AF in all subjects. Standard-of-care neuroimaging at enrollment was reviewed for the size, acuity, and location of the ischemic stroke as well as for the presence of ICH, HT, or cerebral microbleeds. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was the preferred method for review, but head computed tomography (CT) was allowed for subjects unable to receive MRI. These images constituted the baseline scan for subsequent comparisons.  

In 2019, enrollment was suspended in response to a focused update in the AF management guideline from the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/ Heart Rhythm Society. The update explicitly stated that DOACs were recommended over warfarin in patients with AF for stroke prevention, except for patients with mitral stenosis and mechanical heart valves.15 This new guideline led to the cessation of enrollment at 91 patients, a decision supported by the data safety monitoring board and the University of South Florida Institutional Review Board.  

AREST was a collaboration between the academic vascular neurology and cardiology services at University of South Florida in conjunction with our 1018-bed teaching facility, Tampa General Hospital. During the first year of the study, enrollment was limited to Tampa General Hospital, but subsequently, trial sites expanded to include the University of Louisville in Kentucky, Lundquist Institute for Biomedical Innovation at Harbor-UCLA Medical Center, and Bayfront Hospital in St Petersburg, Florida.  

General exclusion criteria for AREST were as follows: therapeutic anticoagulation upon hospital admission, any absolute indication for anticoagulation other than AF (eg, deep vein thrombosis), need for dual antiplatelet therapy (eg, cardiac stent), persistent hypertension with blood pressure ${}2180/100\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg}.$ , illicit drug or excessive alcohol use per Principal Investigator discretion, moderate (or severe) anemia (hemoglobin ${<}9.0{\mathrm{~}}9/{\mathrm{dL}}, $ ), history of major bleeding within the past 6 months before enrollment, current or history of blood dyscrasias or other bleeding disorders, need for multiple daily non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, liver disease (aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase twice upper limit of normal) or renal disease. Neurological exclusion criteria were as follows: large ischemic stroke (including any brain stem stroke), any other (non-AF) suspected cause for stroke (eg, ipsilateral carotid disease), primary ICH or HT visualized on neuroimaging at the time of randomization, ${>}3$ cerebral microbleeds on gradient recovery echo sequence of brain MRI, and traumatic brain injury associated with ICH within 1 year of randomization. All ICH were classified using the Heidelberg system,20 and cerebral microbleeds were characterized according to the Greenberg criteria.21 Subjects were also excluded if pregnant, or lactating, prisoners (or involuntarily incarcerated), compulsorily detained for treatment of either a psychiatric or physical illness, or participating in another clinical study where an investigational product was used concurrently or within the past 30 days.  

Once enrolled, patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either apixaban or warfarin, following a blocked randomization scheme stratified by the size of the event (TIA, small stroke, or medium stroke). Early use of apixaban was initiated at day 0 to 3 for TIA, day 3 to 5 for small-sized AIS $(<1.5$ cm), and day 7 to 9 for medium-sized AIS $(\geq1.5~\mathrm{cm}$ , excluding full cortical territory), while warfarin was started at 1 week post-TIA, or 2 weeks post-AIS, which was modeled upon the Oxfordshire Community Stroke Project.10 TIA was defined as symptom duration ${<}24$ hours without evidence of stroke on neuroimaging. Small-sized AIS must be $<1.5~\mathrm{cm}$ in the largest dimension and located in the anterior or posterior circulation (brain stem excluded due to precarious location involved if HT were to develop); medium-sized AIS must be $\geq1.5$ cm with the largest lesion less than the full cortical territory of the anterior, middle, or posterior cerebral artery. Large-sized AIS (involving an entire arterial territory) were excluded from AREST due to potentially excessive harm if HT were to develop on anticoagulation, given amount of neuronal bed involved and potential need for neurosurgical decompression. A stroke adjudication committee, comprised a neuroradiologist and multiple vascular (stroke) neurologists, reviewed all neuroimaging to obtain diagnostic consensus.  

In the interest of patient safety, study assignment to warfarin versus apixaban was open-label in AREST; however, the Stroke Adjudication Committee was blinded to assignment when reviewing and verifying recurrent stroke, hemorrhage, and other items/events. All committee members had no knowledge of patient treatment assignment at the time of adjudication.  

Subjects were followed for $\approx180$ days from the time of the initial stroke or TIA and evaluated in clinic on days 14 $(\pm3)$ , 30 $(\pm5)$ , 60 $\left(\pm10\right)$ , 90 $\left(\pm10\right)$ , $120(\pm10)$ , $150(\pm10)$ , and $180(\pm10)$ . Neuroimaging was repeated on or about days 14 and 180 with the same parameters as their baseline scan, if possible. At each clinic visit, an ECG, physical exam including the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and modified Rankin Scale were performed along with a review of the subject’s updated medical history, concomitant medications, investigational medicinal product accountability, and adverse event assessment. If applicable, the warfarin dose was adjusted according to a previously published dosing schedule.16,22 Most subjects also received a transthoracic and a transesophageal echocardiogram at baseline. A post-trial phone call was performed on day 210 $(\pm10)$ with adverse event assessment and medication reconciliation.  

# Statistical Methods  

All data describing the 2 arms of the study was not made available to AREST investigators for analysis until after the database was locked. The primary safety end point was a composite of fatal stroke, recurrent ischemic stroke, or TIA during followup, with the secondary end point of ICH assessed by MRI/CT. Initial baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared by random assignment, by use of Student t tests for continuous variables and Fisher exact test of proportions for categorical variables. To compare the rate of apixaban versus warfarin on the primary and secondary outcomes, univariate (crude)  

comparisons were made by the use of Fisher exact test of proportions. This was followed by fitting for each outcome a general linear mixed model specifying the binomial distribution, log link, and including clinical site as a random effect. This resulted in adjusted risk ratios and $95\%$ CI. All analyses were performed using the intention to treat principle with a 2-sided $P$ value of $_{<0.05}$ considered statistically significant. Given the modest sample size, and in addition to conventional tests of statistical significance, results of the primary and secondary outcomes by random assignment were simply compared numerically (not by hypothesis testing) to judge whether there appeared to be any potential safety concern with the use of apixaban.  

# RESULTS  

# Patient Population  

A total of 91 patients were enrolled, including 3 screenfailures in the study before discontinuation of recruitment in 2019 following the publication of the focused update from the national guidelines recommending DOACs over warfarin for stroke prevention in AF. All randomized patients continued to be followed to study completion. As seen in the CONSORT diagram (Figure), a total of 4287 patients who presented to the hospital with a neurological event were prescreened for the study; however, 2871 patients $(67\%)$ did not present with the main cardiological inclusion criteria: new diagnosis or history of AF, and thus were ineligible. Considering all inclusion/exclusion criteria, a total of 4125 patients were clinically ineligible. Of the 162 patients who were eligible for enrollment, 71 refused informed consent and 91 patients $(56.2\%)$ ) were enrolled. Of these 91 patients enrolled, 3 screen-failed after rereview of inclusion/exclusion criteria before randomization, resulting in a final number of 88 randomized subjects.  

Assessment of medication adherence revealed a high dropout rate in the warfarin arm. A total of 47 patients were randomized to warfarin, but 6 made their own a priori decision to not take warfarin at all, and 6 more waited to confer with their physician, who preferred they instead use DOAC. Additionally, 4 other subjects who agreed to take warfarin met end points before warfarin was scheduled to begin, and hence in total, 16 patients $(34\%)$ did not start the drug. Of those 31 who did begin warfarin, 15 eventually discontinued it at some point during the study: 6 due to a reluctance by the patients’ primary care physician or cardiologist, 6 more because of INR lability and difficulty with monitoring, 2 for patient choice and 1 had a gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Overall, 10 subjects assigned to warfarin experienced end point events: 4 before starting therapy and 6 afterwards. The time in therapeutic range in the warfarin group was $66\%$ .

# Outcome Analyses  

The incidence of the primary composite safety outcome (fatal stroke, recurrent ischemic stroke, or TIA) was similar, yet numerically lower in the apixaban group than in the warfarin group ( $17.1\%$ versus $25.5\%$ , $\widetilde{P{=}0.44}$ ). Respective event rates comparing apixaban to warfarin were as follows: death $4.9\%$ versus $8.5\%$ , $_{P=0.68.}$ ), fatal stroke $(2.4\%$ versus $8.5\%,$ $R{=}0.37$ ) and recurrent stroke/TIA $14.6\%$ versus $19.2\%,$ $_{P=0.78.}$ ). One symptomatic ICH occurred on warfarin, none on apixaban. Five asymptomatic HT occurred in each arm. In the general linear mixed model, random assignment to use of apixaban resulted in an estimated $33\%$ lower risk of the primary composite outcome of fatal stroke, recurrent ischemic stroke, or TIA (rate ratio $[\mathsf{RR}]{=}0.67$ $[95\%$ CI, 0.29–1.54], $P\mathrm{=}0.35$ ; Table 2). Similar nonsignificant lower risk estimates were observed for the outcomes of death $(\mathsf{RR}{=}0.57$ $[95\%$ CI, 0.11–2.97], $\widetilde{P{=}0.51}$ ), fatal stroke $(\mathsf{RR}{=}0.29$ $[95\%$ CI, 0.03–2.46], $_{P=0.26,}$ ), and recurrent stroke/TIA $^{\prime}\mathrm{RR}{=}0.76$ $[95\%$ CI, 0.30–1.96], $_{P=0.58,}$ ). The only outcome that was not numerically lower for apixaban over warfarin was asymptomatic HT ${}^{\prime}R\mathsf{R}{=}1.15$ $[95\%$ CI, $0.36-$ 3.68], $_{P=0.82}$ ). Thus, in aggregate, results indicated no increased risk of safety with the use of apixaban.

# DISCUSSION  

The AREST randomized trial was a multi-year, multicenter study designed to assess whether the early use of the DOAC apixaban did not result in an increase in intracranial bleeds (HT or ICH) for patients with AIS due to AF, while at the same time, yield an initial indication of potential efficacy as compared to use of warfarin. Importantly, this is the first randomized trial to compare the early initiation of apixaban based on stroke size to warfarin.  

Multiple studies over several decades have repeatedly shown that anticoagulation therapy is effective for the long-term prevention of recurrent stroke, which, if due to AF, is associated with significant morbidity and mortality.22 Early recurrence risks range from $5\%$ to $35\%$ , however, previous anticoagulation trials for heparinoids and warfarin have traditionally excluded enrollment in the first weeks after a stroke.14,23 This exclusion was presumably owing to a high perceived risk of early bleeding on anticoagulation, with MRI-evident HT seen in as high as $30\%$ of ischemic strokes.23,24 The initial DOAC vs warfarin randomized controlled trials, published between 2009 and 2013, for stroke prevention in AF, also did not evaluate early use of either the study drug or its comparator, so any benefit of ischemic stroke risk reduction with either anticoagulant (or harm from excess bleeding risk on it) during this early period still remains unknown.7,10,11,16 Despite the focused update in 2019 showing preference for DOAC over warfarin in patients with AF, American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines still do not narrow the time window for starting anticoagulation for AF-related stroke, suggesting that within

DOACs, with their faster onset of action and lower ICH rates compared with warfarin, could potentially reduce early ischemic events without excessive bleeding risk, similar to what has already been proven in long-term data. However, this short-term data remains scant: in 2017, Triple-AXEL, a randomized controlled trial of early DOAC administration (within 5 days, median 2 days) versus warfarin for AIS due to AF showed similar rates of MRIdetected stroke without an increase in ICH, but the DOAC chosen was low-dose rivaroxaban, and the strokes were only mild in severity with median NIHSS score of 2 (interquartile range, 0–4).25 A somewhat larger randomized study, DATAS II (Dabigatran Following Transient Ischemic Attack and Minor Stroke), evaluated initiating the DOAC dabigatran within 3 days of TIA and mostly mild strokes (NIHSS score of 0–9 for entry, but median NIHSS score of 1); there were no primary outcome events (symptomatic ICH) in either group on follow-up MRI at about 5 weeks poststroke, but the comparator arm was aspirin, and because stroke cause was undifferentiated, most subjects did not have AF.26 Interestingly, regardless of this limited data, and despite the lenient guidelines, the clinical standard of practice has been gradual adoption of earlier DOAC use poststroke in the AF population anyway.14  

The results of our AREST trial, designed to focus on stroke size and maturity of infarct in patients with AF, indicate comparable, yet numerically lower rates of stroke and mortality when compared with conventional timing of anticoagulation initiation, and without an increase in bleeding complications. AREST identified nonsignificantly fewer recurrent strokes with early use of apixaban compared with later use of warfarin, without excess HT. Although there was only one symptomatic ICH, it occurred in the warfarin group, not with apixaban. Asymptomatic HT was detected equally, with 5 in each arm, suggesting that some degree of subclinical bleeding may actually be part of the natural history process, or evolution, of embolic stroke—a heretofore underrecognized phenomenon that was only detected because of the AREST trial design to obtain brain MRIs at prespecified intervals, not part of routine clinical practice.  

Limitations of the AREST study included the marked dropout rate of warfarin users, influenced by the 2019 change in American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Rhythm Society guidelines but also from a gradually adopted clinical practice converting VKA to DOACs due to their increasing popularity and affordability. Theoretically, if such warfarin dropout was related to poststroke outcome, this could bias the results either in favor or against outcomes observed with apixaban. We observed physicians and patients themselves decide to switch from warfarin to DOAC for a panoply of reasons. Regardless, in a per-protocol analysis (data not shown), results did not differ materially from the primary intentionto-treat analysis. Moreover, subjects remaining adherent to warfarin had a time in therapeutic range of $66\%$ , which compares well with prior AF clinical trials that top out time in therapeutic range at about $65\%$ .18 AREST was conducted during a time when warfarin use was still prevalent, but its unpopularity became gradually more palpable due to the increasing awareness and acceptance of DOACs as appealing alternatives without frequent blood draws or interactions with multiple medications and foods. The coup de gras for AREST was the 2019 focused update in the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines favoring DOACs over warfarin for AF, which made adherence in the warfarin arm even more difficult, clinical equipoise for enrollment into the trial less balanced, and led to premature closure of the trial.  

Because the change in treatment guidelines for anticoagulation in AF negatively impacted recruitment feasibility, the number of subjects enrolled (91, with 88 randomized due to 3 screen-fails) was not as robust as we had planned (120) and represents a limitation of our trial. Still, the final analysis sample of 88 subjects did permit a meaningful assessment of the safety profile of apixaban, as well as some insight into potential efficacy that may be evaluated in fully powered pivotal clinical trials.  

Last, future research on this topic involves 4 studies (ELAN [Early Versus Late Initiation of Direct Oral Anticoagulants in Post-Ischaemic Stroke Patients With Atrial Fibrillation (ELAN): An International, Multicentre, Randomised-Controlled, Two-Arm, Assessor-Blinded Trial], OPTIMAS [OPTIMAS: Optimal Timing of Anticoagulation After Acute Ischaemic Stroke : A Randomised Controlled Trial], TIMING [Timing of Oral Anticoagulant Therapy in Acute Ischemic Stroke With Atrial Fibrillation], and START [Optimal Delay Time to Initiate Anticoagulation After Ischemic Stroke in Atrial Fibrillation])—all randomized controlled trials like AREST, but instead of early DOAC versus warfarin in recent AF-stroke, these trials plan to investigate early versus later initiation of DOAC at various timepoints based on stroke severity, and mostly without NIHSS restrictions.24 With 30- and 90-day follow-up periods, and expected study completion dates ranging from December 2020 to mid-2022, eventually these analyses may help answer the question of timing, but not for a while: publication of final results may not occur until 2023 or later.24 Until then, AREST provides some direction, with randomized controlled data, of when to start anticoagulation for AF patients with AIS.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

AREST is the first randomized trial to evaluate the early use of safety of apixaban in patients with AF-related small- and medium-sized AIS or TIA. Although a change in guidelines preferring DOAC over warfarin limited the number of patients recruited and studied, results from AREST suggest that depending on stroke size, early use of apixaban does not increase the risk of intracerebral hemorrhagic complications. Potential efficacy of early initiation of anticoagulation remains to be determined from larger pivotal trials.
