# Safety and efficacy of platelet glycoprotein VI inhibition in acute ischaemic stroke (ACTIMIS): a randomised, doubleblind, placebo-controlled, phase 1b/2a trial  

Mikaël Mazighi, Martin Köhrmann, Robin Lemmens, Philippe A Lyrer, Carlos A Molina, Sébastien Richard, Danilo Toni, Yannick Plétan, Anouar Sari, Adeline Meilhoc, Martine Jandrot-Perrus, Sophie Binay, Gilles Avenard, Andrea Comenducci, Jean-Marie Grouin, James C Grotta, on behalf of the ACTIMIS Study Group\*  

# Summary  

Background Antagonists of glycoprotein VI-triggered platelet activation used in combination with recanalisation therapies are a promising therapeutic approach in acute ischaemic stroke. Glenzocimab is an antibody fragment that inhibits the action of platelet glycoprotein VI. We aimed to determine and assess the safety and efficacy of the optimal dose of glenzocimab in patients with acute ischaemic stroke eligible to receive alteplase with or without mechanical thrombectomy.  

Methods This randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study with dose-escalation (1b) and dose-confirmation (2a) phases (ACTIMIS) was done in 26 stroke centres in six European countries. Participants were adults $_{(\geq18}$ years) with disabling acute ischaemic stroke with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 6 or higher before alteplase administration. Patients were randomly assigned treatment using a central electronic procedure. Total administered dose at the end of the intravenous administration was $125\mathrm{mg},250\mathrm{mg},500\mathrm{mg},$ and ${1000~\mathrm{mg}}$ of glenzocimab or placebo in phase 1b and ${\bf1000mg}$ of glenzocimab or placebo in phase 2a. Treatment was initiated $\mathbf{4\cdot5\cdoth}$ or earlier from stroke symptom onset in patients treated with alteplase with or without mechanical thrombectomy. The sponsor, study investigator and study staff, patients, and central laboratories were all masked to study treatment until database lock. Primary endpoints across both phases were safety, mortality, and intracranial haemorrhage (symptomatic, total, and fatal), assessed in all patients who received at least a partial dose of study medication (safety set). The trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03803007, and is complete.  

Findings Between March 6, 2019, and June 27, 2021, 60 recruited patients were randomly assigned to $\mathbf{125mg}$ $250\mathrm{mg}$ , ${500}\mathbf{mg}$ , or ${\bf1000mg}$ glenzocimab, or to placebo in phase 1b $\scriptstyle\left[{\mathbf{n}}=12\right.$ per group) and were included in the safety analysis. Glenzocimab ${\bf1000mg}$ was well tolerated and selected as the phase 2a recommended dose; from Oct 2, 2020, to June 27, 2021, 106 patients were randomly assigned to glenzocimab $1000\mathrm{mg}\left(\mathrm{n}{=}53\right)$ or placebo $(\mathbf{n}{=}53)$ ). One patient in the placebo group received glenzocimab in error and therefore 54 and 52, respectively, were included in the safety set. In phase 2a, the most frequent treatment-emergent adverse event was non-symptomatic haemorrhagic transformation, which occurred in 17 $(31\%)$ of 54 patients treated with glenzocimab and 26 $(50\%)$ of 52 patients treated with placebo. Symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage occurred in no patients treated with glenzocimab compared with five $(10\%)$ patients in the placebo group. All-cause deaths were lower with glenzocimab ${\bf1000mg}$ (four $[7\%]$ patients) than with placebo (11 $[21\%]$ patients).  

Interpretation Glenzocimab ${\bf1000mg}$ in addition to alteplase, with or without mechanical thrombectomy, was well tolerated, and might reduce serious adverse events, intracranial haemorrhage, and mortality. These findings support the need for future research into the potential therapeutic inhibition of glycoprotein VI with glenzocimab plus alteplase in patients with acute ischaemic stroke.

# Evidence before this study  

We searched PubMed for relevant clinical studies of treatment for acute ischaemic stroke with anti-glycoprotein VI, from database inception to June 27, 2021, with no language restrictions. Key search terms were “GPVI stroke” and related synonyms such as “glycoprotein VI” , “GPVI”, “(GP)VI”, “(GP)-VI”, “stroke”, “acute ischemic stroke”, ${}^{\prime\prime}{\sfA}|\mathsf{S}^{\prime\prime}$ , or “cerebral ischaemia”. Glycoprotein VI was proposed as a new therapeutic target for treating the acute phase of ischaemic stroke in 2007, but it was only evaluated in animal models of stroke. The safety of glenzocimab evaluated in phase 1 showed no prolongation of bleeding time and did not cause any dose-related adverse events. The phase 1b/2a clinical trial was initiated in 2019, with a primary objective of evaluating the safety of glenzocimab in patients with acute ischaemic stroke.  

# Added value of this study  

To our knowledge, ACTIMIS was the first randomised controlled trial to assess glenzocimab, or any glycoprotein VI antagonist, for the treatment of acute ischaemic stroke. Glenzocimab was administered as a single dose in addition to intravenous  

thrombolysis with alteplase, with or without mechanical thrombectomy. To the best of our knowledge, ACTIMIS was also the first in-patient trial of any glycoprotein VI antagonist in any indication.  

# Implications of all the available evidence  

Treatment with glenzocimab at a single dose of $1000\mathsf{mg}$ added to standard of care was well tolerated. Patients treated with glenzocimab showed a reduction in the occurrence of intracranial haemorrhage, both symptomatic and nonsymptomatic. We hypothesise that the reduction in intracranial bleeding is a direct consequence of glenzocimab inhibiting glycoprotein VI-mediated thromboinflammation. In animal models, platelet-neutrophil aggregates in the microcirculation have been associated with leakage of the blood– brain barrier and haemorrhagic transformation. The decrease in intracranial bleeding is likely linked to the reduced mortality in patients treated with glenzocimab. These findings support a good safety profile for glenzocimab in the treatment of ischaemic stroke and the need for  phase 2/3 efficacy trials (ACTISAVE, NCT05070260; GREEN, NCT05559398).  

and the monoclonal anti-glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor antibody abciximab, have been assessed for their potential to improve recanalisation; however, randomised controlled trials have shown that a favourable effect on recanalisation is accompanied by an increased risk of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhages.11,12  

Thromboinflammation—a process referring to the complex interplay between thrombotic and inflammatory pathways—has become increasingly recognised as an important contributor to post-stroke damage.13 Platelet glycoprotein VI has received increasing focus as a potential therapeutic target in stroke because its inhibition in acute ischaemic stroke models resulted in reduced infarct volume and reperfusion injury.14,15 Therapeutic inhibition of glycoprotein VI-triggered platelet activation could potentially increase thrombolytic efficiency and inhibit neo-formation of thrombi in the macrocirculation and microcirculation, reduce thromboinflammation, and reduce haemorrhagic risk due to the preservation of physiological haemostasis. Notably, to our knowledge, glycoprotein VI deficiency is not associated with prolonged bleeding in humans or animals.16–18 Glycoprotein VI is, therefore, a clinically meaningful therapeutic target.  

Glenzocimab is a fully humanised antibody fragment that has been shown to inhibit ex-vivo glycoprotein VI function in cynomolgus monkeys without inducing bleeding.19 A phase 1 study20 in healthy volunteers showed a good safety profile of a single intravenous administration of glenzocimab, with no bleeding events or serious adverse events reported. The extent of inhibition was dose-dependent, with complete inhibition of platelet aggregation starting at a dose of $1000~\mathrm{mg}$ .21  

Here, we report the results of the Acute Ischemic Stroke Interventional Study (ACTIMIS; NCT03803007), which was designed to determine and assess the safety and efficacy of the optimal dose of glenzocimab in patients with acute ischaemic stroke diagnosed within $4{\cdot}5{\mathrm{~h~}}$ from stroke symptom onset, who were eligible to receive alteplase with or without mechanical thrombectomy.  

# Methods  

# Study design and participants  

ACTIMIS was a randomised, multicentre, double-blind, placebo-controlled study that comprised a dose-escalation phase (1b) and a dose-confirmation phase (2a). ACTIMIS was conducted in 26 comprehensive and primary stroke centres in six European countries (Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and Switzerland). The trial protocol with the statistical analysis plan is in the appendix (pp 33–366).  

The study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki,22 and national laws and regulations. An independent ethics committee at each site approved the study protocol, if required, and any subsequent amendments. Written informed consent from patients or legal representatives, or emergency consent when necessary, was obtained in every case before study initiation. If patients could not consent themselves at the time of randomisation, re-provide consent was always sought as soon as possible. The study sponsor, Acticor Biotech (Paris, France), provided the trial drug (glenzocimab), monitored the trial, and analysed the data with oversight from the study scientific committee. An independent data safety monitoring board reviewed clinical safety data and CT and MRI scans and made recommendations concerning study continuation. An independent statistician was available to analyse the data for the data safety monitoring board to review.  

Eligible patients were adults (age ${\ge}18$ years), with a diagnosis of potentially disabling acute ischaemic stroke (either in the anterior or posterior circulation within $4\cdot5\mathrm{h}$ of symptom onset), were treated with alteplase, and could receive mechanical thrombectomy as indicated by the treating physician. Patients were also required to have a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 6 or more before thrombolysis. Previous antiplatelet therapy was prohibited in the phase 1b doseescalation part of this study, except if deemed that there was no longer any clinical activity of the antiplatelet agent or the patient was shown to be refractory to these agents using the medical device VerifyNow (Werfen, Barcelona, Spain).23 However, antiplatelet therapy was permitted in the phase 2a dose-confirmation part of this study. The use of effective birth control methods and a negative pregnancy test were required for female patients of child-bearing potential.  

Patients were not eligible if they had: an NIHSS score higher than 25 or were in a coma, previous acute ischaemic stroke within the past 3 months, a pre-stroke modified Rankin scale (mRS) score of 2 or higher, a stroke of haemorrhagic origin, known severe renal impairment (grade ${\geq}3$ ), ongoing anticoagulant therapy, ongoing treatment with a monoclonal antibody, or life expectancy less than 3 months. The complete list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is included in the appendix (pp 5–10).  

# Randomisation and masking  

ACTIMIS was a double-blind study (patients, caregivers, assessors, and investigators were masked to treatment). Staff of the sponsor were also masked to study treatment until database lock. Glenzocimab and placebo treatment were both colourless liquids, with identical viscosity and identical packaging and deemed indistinguishable.  

Participants were randomly assigned using a central electronic procedure to balance the distribution of the various glenzocimab doses and placebo across cohorts, according to the overall study scheme. The central system assigned a treatment number to each participant, corresponded to a unique treatment kit, and the site had to use the exact box assigned to them.  

An interactive web response system was used to provide allocation and drug shipment management (S-Clinica, Ixelles, Belgium). Permuted block randomisation (block size four) was used to create a randomisation list to allocate individuals to cohorts. Permuted block randomisation was also used to create the vial lists, which were used to allocate vials to centres and allow multiple inclusion at each site before re-supply. A random sequence number was included in each vial list to indicate the order in which vials should be shipped and dispensed without unmasking. The strategy to stock vials in centres and re-supply to centres was handled in a manner that ensured all involved study personnel were masked to treatment assignment, but not to dose assignment.  

In the dose-escalation phase (phase 1b), patients were unevenly randomised between active treatment and placebo across five cohorts. After predetermined intervals (appendix p 15), the data safety monitoring board reviewed clinical safety and CT and MRI scans before new patients were randomised into the next cohort. Once escalation was completed, 12 patients would have been assigned at each dose level (ie, $125~\mathrm{mg}$ glenzocimab, $250~\mathrm{mg}$ glenzocimab, $500~\mathrm{mg}$ glenzocimab, $1000~\mathrm{mg}$ glenzocimab, and placebo).  

During the dose-confirmation phase (phase 2a), patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to glenzocimab $1000\mathrm{mg}$ or placebo using a central randomisation system and stratified by type of standard of care administered: alteplase only or alteplase with mechanical thrombectomy. Randomisation occurred as early as possible after the onset of stroke symptoms and early enough to allow for the initiation of treatment within $3\mathrm{~h~}$ of the start of alteplase (appendix p 15).  

# Procedures  

The dose-escalation phase (phase 1b) was designed to assess the dose-related safety of glenzocimab. This drug was administered as an intravenous infusion over $6\mathrm{~h~}$ (a quarter of the dose was administered over $15\mathrm{{min}}$ as a bolus; three-quarters of the dose was administered over $5\cdot75\ensuremath{\mathrm{h}}$ as a slow infusion). The infusion was initiated as soon as possible, and no later than $^3\mathrm{{h}}$ , after the start of thrombolysis with alteplase, which was administered within $4{\cdot}5\mathrm{~h~}$ of acute ischaemic stroke symptom onset. This infusion procedure was identical in phase 1b and phase 2a. Mechanical thrombectomy procedures were not defined by the protocol and were done according to the standard procedures at the centre. Treatment with glenzocimab could begin before, during, or after mechanical thrombectomy. The only time limitation was related to thrombolysis. After treatment, patients had several follow-up visits: at $24\mathrm{h}\left(\pm3\mathrm{h}\right)$ for clinical evaluation and imaging; at the day 7 $(\pm3$ days or at discharge) followup visit, including imaging by MRI for post-treatment infarct zone evaluation; at 30 days ( $\mathbf{\chi}_{\pm3}$ days) for posttreatment evaluation; and a 90 days $(\pm3$ days) for posttreatment evaluation, corresponding to the end-of-study visit. Final mRS score evaluation was done at the end-ofstudy visit for patients in phase 1b and phase 2a.  

# Outcomes  

The primary endpoints were safety (incidence and severity of adverse events, serious adverse events, and treatment-emergent adverse events), deaths, and incidence of intracranial haemorrhage (symptomatic, total, and fatal). Symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage

Non-symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage was defined as any type of haemorrhage seen on the 24-h CT scan in the absence of clinical impairment or death. Intracranial haemorrhage detected by brain imaging was classified according to the Heidelberg classification (appendix p 19).24 Adverse events were graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Changes from baseline in vital signs (systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, body temperature) and electrocardiogram were secondary safety endpoints.  

Secondary efficacy endpoints were neurological recovery assessed by absolute and relative change in NIHSS score from pre-thrombolysis to $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , the mRS score at day 90 with a focus on the binary mRS score 0–2 (vs 3–6), and recanalisation (assessed only in patients who received mechanical thrombectomy using angiography to assess the modified thrombolysis in cerebral ischaemia [mTICI] score2 [mTICI score 3 indicated complete recanalisation, and mTICI score 2b–3 indicated successful recanalisation]).26 All imaging was centrally reviewed by a masked, independent neuroradiologist (Keosys Medical Imaging Software & Services, St Herblain, France).  

For pharmacokinetic analyses, blood samples were taken from all patients in phase 1b and phase 2a at prespecified timepoints. In phase 1b, blood samples were taken in all patients $30~\mathrm{{min}}$ pre-dose and $30~\mathrm{{min}}$ , $^\textrm{\scriptsize9h}$ , and $24\textrm{h}$ post-dose. In phase 2a, blood samples were taken at five timepoints, three of them for all patients (pre-dose, $30\mathrm{min}$ , and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ post-dose); then two groups were randomly selected, the first one after the $5\mathrm{~h~}$ and $^{9\mathrm{h}}$ post-dose timepoints and the second one after the $7{\cdot}5\mathrm{~h~}$ and $12\mathrm{h}$ post-dose timepoints.  

All patients were followed up for at least 90 days. Additional methodological details are in the appendix (pp 5–14).  

# Statistical analysis  

The sample size was prespecified and not determined on the basis of power considerations given that the primary endpoint was safety. As such, there was no adjustment for multiplicity. Therefore, all p values are exploratory.  

Safety outcomes were analysed in the safety population (ie, patients in phase 1b or phase 2a who received at least a partial dose during the infusion) and summarised with usual descriptive statistics by treatment group. All-cause mortality was analysed in phase 2a with the KaplanMeier method, and survival curves were compared between treatment groups with the log-rank test.  

In phase 2a, efficacy outcomes were analysed in the full analysis set (all as-treated patients in phase 2a who received at least a partial dose during the infusion). With regard to phase 2a, both descriptive and model-based approaches (ANCOVA tests and $95\%$ CIs) were performed.  

The analysis of the change from baseline in NIHSS score to $24\mathrm{~h~}$ was performed using an ANCOVA model. Additional details regarding statistical analyses are included in the appendix (pp 13–14).  

Statistical analyses were performed using version 8.3 or later of SAS (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).  

The trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03803007.  

# Role of the funding source  

The funder was involved in study design, data collection, data analysis, and data interpretation, and participated in the writing of the study report and the decision to publish the results.  

# Results  

In phase 1b, 60 patients with acute ischaemic stroke treated with alteplase were randomly assigned treatment from March 6, 2019, to Sept 20, 2020, with 12 patients assigned to each glenzocimab dose ( $125~\mathrm{mg}$ , $250~\mathrm{mg}$ , $500\mathrm{mg}$ , or $1000\mathrm{mg}$ ) or placebo. In phase 2a, 106 patients with acute ischaemic stroke were randomly assigned treatment from Oct 2, 2020, to June 27, 2021, to glenzocimab $1000~\mathrm{mg}$ $(\mathrm{n}{=}53)$ or placebo $(\mathrm{n}{=}53)$ . One patient randomly assigned to placebo received glenzocimab by error and was therefore part of the glenzocimab safety population (figure 1).  

Baseline characteristics were similar between treatment groups for important predictors of outcomes, such as treatment by mechanical thrombectomy, baseline NIHSS score, and time to alteplase. Minor imbalances in age and medical history were observed (table 1). For phase 2a, characteristics for both treatment groups were similar.  

During the phase 1b study, symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage occurred in one patient receiving glenzocimab $125~\mathrm{mg}$ (table 2), and other (ie, non-brain) bleeding-related events were observed in two patients in the glenzocimab $125~\mathrm{mg}$ group, one patient in the glenzocimab $250~\mathrm{mg}$ group, and three patients in the placebo group (appendix pp 21–22). Based on these safety findings and the observed and predicted pharmacokinetic profiles of the glenzocimab $125\mathrm{mg}$ , glenzocimab $250\mathrm{mg}.$ , glenzocimab $500~\mathrm{mg}$ , and glenzocimab $1000~\mathrm{mg}$ groups (appendix p 16), glenzocimab $1000~\mathrm{mg}$ was considered well tolerated and was selected as the recommended dose for the phase 2a study.

The prespecified additional analysis based on the cumulative ordinal logistical regression model showed that the proportional odds assumption did not hold $(\mathrm{p}{=}0\cdot05)$ , meaning that the odds ratio was inconsistent across cutoffs on the mRS and therefore analyses of binary outcomes based on separate cutoffs were relevant. In an exploratory analysis of the ACTIMIS trial, improved outcomes with glenzocimab $1000~\mathrm{mg}$ compared with placebo were found considering a cutoff of $\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}3$ versus 4–6, with mRS 4–6 defined as poor outcome27 (appendix pp 17–18). In further post-hoc analyses, the greatest differences in favour of glenzocimab in preventing unfavourable outcomes were in patients treated by mechanical thrombectomy.  

Glycoprotein VI is a platelet membrane glycoprotein, the expression of which is restricted to megakaryocytes and platelets.28 Glycoprotein VI is a receptor for multiple ligands, most notably vessel wall collagen, and fibrin, the major component of a clot.29–32 Clustering of glycoprotein VI at the platelet surface triggers platelet secretion, aggregation, and pro-coagulant activity, resulting in accumulation of circulating platelets and fibrin and, ultimately, in thrombus growth.28,32,33 Additionally, glycoprotein VI is potentially a key factor in thromboinflam­mation,13 which occurs in vessels subjected to stress, such as stasis, low oxygenation, or inflammatory stimuli.34 In mice, targeting glycoprotein VI during cerebral ischaemia—namely, antibodymediated depletion of platelet glycoprotein VI, or blockade of platelet binding to the vessel walls by a glycoprotein VI-Fc fusion protein competitively inhibiting   glycoprotein  VI—reduced   microvascular thrombosis and infarct volumes after transient middle cerebral artery occlusion.15,35–37 Targeting platelet glycoprotein VI could therefore contribute to treating acute ischaemic stroke by modulating thrombo­ infammation and the downstream microcirculation by a reperfusion effect observed in animal models.38,39 Reduced infarct progression under middle cerebral artery occlusion, along with neutrophil and T-cell reduction within the ischaemic brain during occlusive experimental conditions versus controls, has been shown after targeting glycoprotein VI in a mouse model.14  

The ACTIMIS study was the first clinical trial assessing safety of glenzocimab in which efficacy data were also collected. The limited sample size narrows the scope of the analysis to hypothesis generation for the design of future efficacy trials. Using glenzocimab in addition to recanalisation techniques by alteplase, with or without mechanical thrombectomy, did not show any significant effect of glenzocimab on functional independence at 3 months, defined by an mRS of 0–1 or an mRS of 0–2. Post-hoc analysis showed a trend favouring glenzocimab when using the mRS cutoff of 0–3, particularly in patients receiving mechanical thrombectomy. We speculate, based on these results, that the benefit of glenzocimab might be due to a reduction in bleeding or haemorrhagic transformation related to microcirculation thrombosis, which is more common with large infarcts. This hypothesis warrants further exploration in clinical trials designed for efficacy evaluation.  

In patients receiving best available recanalisation strategies (alteplase with mechanical thrombectomy), a higher rate of complete recanalisation, which was defined as mTICI grade 3, was observed with glenzocimab. This finding is consistent with the hypothesis that glenzocimab increases the efficiency of recanalisation procedures. This finding is also consistent with the pathogenic role of platelets and their role in re-occlusion at the primary occlusion site due to residual embolic fragments or stenosis at the mechanical thrombectomy site.40 Recanalisation in patients receiving thrombolysis only was not evaluated.  

Currently, there are no head-to-head comparisons between glycoprotein inhibitors in clinical development. However, another peptide-based glycoprotein antagonist that targets glycoprotein IIb/IIIa, eptifibatide, was evaluated in the CLEAR-ER phase 2 clinical trial in patients with acute ischaemic stroke and showed a favourable safety profile with a lower rate of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage in the alteplase $(0\cdot6~\mathrm{mg/kg})$ plus eptifibatide group (two $[2\%]$ of 101 patients) versus the alteplase only $(0\cdot9\mathrm{mg/kg})$ only group (three $[12\%]$ of 25 patients; odds ratio 0·15 $[95\%$ CI $0\cdot01\ –1\cdot40];$ $\mathtt{p}{=}0\cdot053^{\prime}$ ).41 More recently, a matched-control analysis from two prospective trials evaluating eptifibatide in patients with acute ischaemic stroke treated with mechanical thrombectomy $(\mathrm{n}{=}81)$ ) reported a higher rate of successful recanalisation ( $91\%$ vs $82\%$ ; $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}043$ ) and favourable 3-month outcomes ( $53\%$ vs $33\%$ ; $\scriptstyle\mathrm{p=0}\cdot016)$ ) in the eptifibatide group versus mechanical thrombectomy alone.42 A larger randomised phase 3 study, MOST (NCT03735979), is currently ongoing to confirm these results.43  

The ACTIMIS study has several limitations. The most important limitation was the small sample size as this phase 1b/2a study was designed to assess safety and was not powered to assess efficacy. Hence, all efficacy outcomes must be considered exploratory and findings from subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously.  

In both phase 1b and phase 2a, there were slight imbalances in baseline characteristics (age and baseline NIHSS [ie, stroke severity]) between groups, which should be considered when interpreting the efficacy findings. The study did not allow for evaluation of infarct growth, and future clinical trials will need to correlate imaging data with intracranial haemorrhage results in a larger sample size. Retrospectively, the imbalance in mortality in phase 2a between the two groups during the first week (ie, six patients in the placebo group vs none in the glenzocimab group) limits the interpretation of imag ing at day 7 for evaluation of glenzocimab effect on ischaemic lesion volume. An evaluation at an earlier timepoint warrants further exploration.  

In summary, this phase 1b/2a study has shown that glenzocimab is safe and might reduce serious adverse events, intracranial haemorrhage, and deaths. Our findings support the potential of therapeutic inhibition of glycoprotein VI with glenzocimab as add-on therapy to alteplase in patients with acute ischaemic stroke, with or without mechanical thrombectomy. Patients with the most severe strokes and at highest risk of intracranial haemorrhage might be the best targets for this treatment. A confirmatory, adaptive, and adequately powered phase $2/3$ study, ACTISAVE (NCT05070260), is currently ongoing.
