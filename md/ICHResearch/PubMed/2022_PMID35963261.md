# Safety and efficacy of prophylactic levetiracetam for prevention of epileptic seizures in the acute phase of intracerebral haemorrhage (PEACH): a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial  

Laure Peter-Derex, Frédéric Philippeau, Pierre Garnier, Nathalie André-Obadia, Sébastien Boulogne, Hélène Catenoix, Philippe Convers, Laure Mazzola, Michel Gouttard, Maud Esteban, Julia Fontaine, Laura Mechtouff, Elodie Ong, Tae-Hee Cho, Norbert Nighoghossian, Nathalie Perreton, Anne Termoz, Julie Haesebaert, Anne-Marie Schott, Muriel Rabilloud, Christine Pivot, Carole Dhelens, Andrea Filip, Yves Berthezène, Sylvain Rheims, Florent Boutitie, Laurent Derex  



# Summary  

Background The incidence of early seizures (occurring within 7 days of stroke onset) after intracerebral haemorrhage reaches $30\%$ when subclinical seizures are diagnosed by continuous EEG. Early seizures might be associated with haematoma expansion and worse neurological outcomes. Current guidelines do not recommend prophylactic antiseizure treatment in this setting. We aimed to assess whether prophylactic levetiracetam would reduce the risk of acute seizures in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage.  

Methods The double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 3 PEACH trial was conducted at three stroke units in France. Patients (aged 18 years or older) who presented with a non-traumatic intracerebral haemorrhage within $^{241}$ after onset were randomly assigned (1:1) to levetiracetam (intravenous ${500}\mathbf{mg}$ every 12 h) or matching placebo. Randomisation was done with a web-based system and stratified by centre and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at baseline. Treatment was continued for 6 weeks. Continuous EEG was started within $24\mathbf{h}$ after inclusion and recorded over $\mathbf{48}\mathbf{h}.$ . The primary endpoint was the occurrence of at least one clinical seizure within $^{721}$ of inclusion or at least one electrographic seizure recorded on continuous EEG, analysed in the modified intention-to-treat population, which comprised all patients who were randomly assigned to treatment and who had a continuous EEG performed. This trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02631759, and is now closed. Recruitment was prematurely stopped after $48\%$ of the recruitment target was reached due to a low recruitment rate and cessation of funding.  

Findings Between June 1, 2017, and April 14, 2020, 50 patients with mild-to-moderate severity intracerebral haemorrhage were included: 24 were assigned to levetiracetam and 26 to placebo. During the first $^{721}$ , a clinical or electrographic seizure was observed in three $(16\%)$ of 19 patients in the levetiracetam group versus ten $(43\%)$ of 23 patients in the placebo group (odds ratio 0·16, $95\%$ CI 0·03–0·94, $\mathbf{p}{=}0\cdot043)$ . All seizures in the first $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ were electrographic seizures only. No difference in depression or anxiety reporting was observed between the groups at 1 month or 3 months. Depression was recorded in three $(13\%)$ patients who received levetiracetam versus four $(15\%)$ patients who received placebo, and anxiety was reported for two $(8\%)$ patients versus one $(4\%)$ patient. The most common treatment-emergent adverse events in the levetiracetam group versus the placebo group were headache (nine $[39\%]$ vs six $[24\%])$ , pain (three $[13\%]$ vs ten $[40\%])$ , and falls (seven $[30\%]$ vs four $[16\%])$ . The most frequent serious adverse events were neurological deterioration due to the intracerebral haemorrhage (one $[4\%]$ vs four $[16\%]]$ and severe pneumonia (two $[9\%]$ vs two $[8\%])$ . No treatment-related death was reported in either group.  

Interpretation Levetiracetam might be effective in preventing acute seizures in intracerebral haemorrhage. Larger studies are needed to determine whether seizure prophylaxis improves functional outcome in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage.

# Research in context  



# Evidence before this study  

We searched PubMed between database inception and Feb 28, 2013, with the term “intracerebral haemorrhage” filtered by “clinical trial” article type, and the terms “epileptic seizures” and “antiseizure drugs”. Only studies published in English were retrieved. Our search identified seven relevant studies (three retrospective cohort studies, two prospective cohort studies, one randomised comparative trial, and one randomised placebo-controlled trial). An updated search in 2022 found two additionnal retrospective cohorts and one prospective cohort study. Evidence was sparse concerning the efficacy of primary antiseizure prophylaxis after spontaneous (non-traumatic) intracerebral haemorrhage. Accordingly, current clinical guidelines do not recommend the use of primary prophylactic antiseizure medication in patients with acute intracerebral haemorrhage in the absence of data from adequately powered randomised clinical trials.  



# Added value of this study  

We did the PEACH trial to assess the safety and efficacy of primary antiseizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam for reducing the risk of early clinical and subclinical seizures at the acute phase of intracerebral haemorrhage. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first  randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial to assess the effects of prophylaxis with levetiracetam versus placebo on the risk of seizures, using continuous EEG monitoring.  



# Implications of all the available evidence  

Levetiracetam started within $48\mathsf{h}$ of onset and maintained over 6 weeks was shown to be safe and might be effective in preventing acute seizures in intracerebral haemorrhage. However, the trial was stopped early because of recruitment difficulties and cessation of funding. Therefore, the possibility that primary prophylactic antiseizure drug might improve neurological outcomes in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage should be further investigated.  

Epileptic seizures are a common complication at the acute phase of intracerebral haemorrhage,5 particularly in cortical and subcortical haemorrhages, which result in higher risk of developing seizures compared with haemorrhages in deeper structures6,7 and possibly reflect the disruptive effect on neuronal networks of the haema­ toma and surrounding oedema.8 Acute sympto­matic seizures, defined by the International League Against Epilepsy as seizures occurring within 7 days of stroke,9 arise in $6-15\%$ of the patients with intracerebral haemorrhage, mostly in the first $72\mathrm{~h~}$ .7 The incidence of seizures reaches $30\%$ when subclinical seizures are diagnosed by continuous EEG.6,10 Indeed, after hospital admission for intracerebral haemorrhage, most seizures are clinically unrecognised and can be diagnosed only with EEG monitoring.10 Some studies suggest that acute symptomatic seizures are associated with worse func­ tional outcome6,11 or increased risk of death in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage, despite accounting for other known prognostic factors such as age and baseline haemorrhage volume.12 By contrast, some studies have shown no association of clinical acute symptomatic seizures with neurological deterioration or long-term mortality and outcome in patients with acute intracerebral haemorrhage.7,13,14 However, the interpretation of these works is subject to bias because almost all studies were based on clinical detection of seizures only, without continuous EEG.  

Randomised controlled trials supporting the use of antiseizure medication as primary prevention among patients with intracerebral haemorrhage are scarce.15,16 Accordingly, clinical guidelines do not recommend the use of prophylactic antiseizure medication in patients with acute intracerebral haemorrhage.17,18 In the context of acute brain injury, primary antiseizure prophylaxis is not recommended because a high risk of spontaneous seizures has not been shown. However, data obtained with continuous EEG suggest that a high risk might occur in the acute phase of intracerebral haemorrhage.10 The efficacy of primary antiseizure prophylaxis in acute intracerebral haemorrhage on the occurrence of clinical and electrographic seizures has never been assessed.  

Here, we report the results of the PEACH (Prevention of Epileptic seizures at the Acute phase of intraCerebral Haemorrhage) trial, a randomised controlled trial com­ paring clinical and subclinical (diagnosed by continuous EEG) seizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam, a prophy­ lactic antiseizure drug, versus placebo within 48 h of intracerebral haemorrhage onset. Our aim was to demon­ strate that the prophylactic antiseizure drug would reduce the risk of early seizures in acute intracerebral haemorrhage.  



# Methods  



# Study design and participants  

PEACH was a parallel-group, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, investigator-led, phase 3 trial done in stroke units of one secondary and two tertiary health-care hospitals in France. Patients were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had a spontaneous supratentorial intracerebral haemorrhage diagnosed by cerebral CT scan or MRI, and presented within 24 h after symptom onset. Exclusion criteria were National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score higher than 25; intracerebral haemorrhage known or suspected by study investigator to be secondary to trauma, vascular malformation,  haemorrhagic  transformation  of ischaemic stroke, or tumour; current use of antiseizure drugs or history of epilepsy; pregnancy or breastfeeding; previous history of severe depression or psychotic disorder; known terminal illness; known allergy or hypersensitivity to levetiracetam; and the occurrence of an epileptic seizure between inclusion and initiation of treatment. The study protocol is available in the appendix (pp 27–73).  

The PEACH trial was performed in accordance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was approved by the institutional review board (CPP Sud-Est, number 2014–000436–41), the National Drug Safety Agency (number 141303A-31), and the National Commission for Data Protection (number DR-2015–539). Investigators sought written informed consent from each participant if they were able to provide it. When participants could not consent, a relative or representative gave proxy consent. When consent was given by a proxy, we informed the participant about the trial as soon as possible and sought their consent. The study was monitored by the Clinical Research Unit of Lyon University Hospital (Lyon, France).  



# Randomisation and masking  

Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive levetiracetam or matching placebo. Randomisation was done using permuted blocks (sizes of two and four) and was stratified by centre and by NIHSS score at admission $\mathord{\left\langle\leq15\right.}$ vs ${>}15$ ). A centralised randomisation process was done online using ClinSight software (version 7.5.30; Ennov Clinical Software, Paris, France).  

Patients and all those involved in patient management or clinical or imaging assessment of adverse events or outcomes were masked to treatment allocation. The investigational drug was distributed to participating centres in externally indistinguishable sealed treatment kits. Preparations of levetiracetam and placebo were matched to ensure masking of participants and investi­ gators, with vials, capsules, packaging, and labelling all identical in appearance. If a clinical epileptic seizure occurred between treatment initiation and the end of the treatment period, unblinding was allowed to adjust the antiseizure treat­ment, but the patient was not excluded from the study nor from the intention-to-treat analyses.  



# Procedures  

Brain imaging was done at admission with either CT or MRI according to routine clinical practice. The webbased randomisation system asked investigators to check eligibility criteria and required investigators to record participant age and stroke severity scored on the NIHSS (ranging 0–42, with higher scores indicating more severe stroke).  

All participants were treated in a stroke unit and received standard care, which was not defined in the protocol but was assumed to be given according to contemporary European guidelines.19 The protocol required levetiracetam or placebo infusion to be initiated within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of randomisation. Levetiracetam （ $\mathrm{^{\prime}500mg}$ every $12\mathrm{~h|~}$ or placebo were administered intravenously for at least $48\mathrm{~h~}$ , then the route of administration transitioned to oral dosing at the same dosage after evaluation of swallowing function. The treatment period was 30 days at full dose, followed by gradual tapering over 2 weeks ( $250\mathrm{mg}$ levetiracetam or placebo every $12\mathrm{h}$ for 7 days, then $250~\mathrm{mg}$ levetiracetam or placebo every $24\mathrm{~h~}$ for 7 days). Compliance was assessed by medication count.

Continuous EEG was done at the patient’s bedside for a total of $^{48\mathrm{h}}$ , beginning as early as possible within $24\mathrm{{h}}$ of randomisation and after treatment initiation. Continuous EEG was recorded digitally using 11 disc electrodes placed on the scalp according to the international 10–20 system20 with the following 10-channel montage: Fp2-C4; C4-T4; T4-O2; Fp1-C3; C3-T3; T3-O1; Fz-Cz; Cz-Pz; C4-Cz; Cz-C3.  A holter device was used so that EEG data were recorded on a memory card without being visible online, and remained unknown to the investi­ gators to avoid changes in antiepileptic treatment in view of the EEG results. Notably, continuous EEG is not routinely carried out in intracerebral haemorrhage patients. EEG recordings were interpreted offline by board-certified neurophysiologists masked to group assignment and clinical data, according to a predefined reading chart (appendix $\mathrm{~p~}~22\$ . All recordings with suspected seizures were reviewed by a consensus board including all the neurophysiologists involved in the study (LP-D, NA-O, HC, SB, LMa, and PC). The following EEG outcomes were collected: presence, timing, duration, number, and EEG pattern of seizures; and presence, location (focal, multifocal, lateralised, or generalised), and pattern of interictal paroxysmal and non-paroxysmal EEG anomalies.  

All investigators were trained and certified to assess the NIHSS and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Neuro­ logical deficit was assessed by a neurologist according to scores on the NIHSS at enrolment, and $^{72\textrm{h}}$ , 1 month, and 3 months after randomisation. Functional outcome was assessed on the mRS (mRS score range 0–6, with 0 indicating no symptoms and 6 indicating death) at 3 months by a face-to-face interview conducted by a neurologist, and at 6 and 12 months by a standardised and validated telephone interview conducted by trained clinical research associates. Anxiety and depression were assessed by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale at day 30 and at 3 months. Disability and health-related quality of life were assessed by the self-report question­ naire Stroke Impact Scale at 3, 6, and 12 months.  

A follow-up CT scan was done at $72\textrm{h}$ . Intracerebral haemorrhage volumes were measured at baseline and $^{72\textrm{h}}$ brain imaging using semiautomated computerised plani­metry. Brain imaging was read centrally by neuro­ radiologists masked to group assignment (appendix p 2). Adverse events were monitored every 6 months, using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 4.0.  



# Outcomes  

The primary outcome measure was the occurrence of at least one clinical seizure within 72 h after inclusion, or of at least one electrographic seizure recorded on contin­ uous EEG. Electrographic seizures were defined as rhythmic discharges or spike and wave patterns with definite evolution in frequency, location, or morphology lasting at least $10~\mathrm{s}$ .21  

Secondary efficacy outcome measures were the number of seizures recorded on continuous EEG; the total duration of seizures recorded on continuous EEG; the occurrence of interictal paroxysmal patterns recorded on continuous EEG; the number of clinical seizures within $72\mathrm{h}$ and early (day 0 to day 30) and late (day 30 to 12 months) clinical seizures; change in NIHSS between inclusion and $^{72\textrm{h}}$ , 1 month, and 3 months; change in $\mathrm{mRS}$ between inclusion and 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months; quality of life assessed by the Stroke Impact Scale at 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months; and change in intracerebral haemorrhage volume and mass effect defined as midline shift at $72\mathrm{h}$ . Secondary safety outcome measures were the frequency of side effects related to treatment, anxiety and depression evaluated with the  

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale at 1 month and 3 months, and all-cause mortality.  



# Statistical analysis  

Assuming a proportion of patients with at least one seizure of $35\%$ in the placebo group6 and of $10\%$ in the levetiracetam group,2 and considering an alpha threshold of $5\%$ , a power of $80\%$ , and an estimated risk of loss to follow-up of $10\%$ , at least 52 patients per group were required per group.  

All analyses were performed according to the modified intention-to-treat principle (all patients who were randomly assigned to treatment and who had a continuous EEG performed), except for the safety analysis, which was performed in the intention-to-treat population (all patients who received at least once either of the treatments, whatever the dose). Analysis of the primary outcome was performed using a logistic regression model with adjustment for the randomisation stratification factors (centre and NIHSS stratum), providing an estimated treatment effect (odds ratio [OR]) with $95\%$ CI, and a Wald test to reject the null hypothesis of no difference between groups. Exploratory analyses were performed with the following adjustment variables: haematoma location (lobar vs deep), haematoma volume, and presence of an intraventricular haemorrhage. The rate of recurrent seizures recorded during EEG monitoring was analysed using a Poisson regression model accounting for overdispersion (negative binomial distribution of residuals), with the duration of EEG monitoring used as an offset. The treatment effect estimate (rate ratio) and its $95\%$ CI were adjusted for the randomisation stratification factors. Other secondary efficacy continuous outcomes based on changes from baseline were analysed using a linear regression model, with adjustment on the baseline level and the random­ isation stratification factors. The modified Rankin score was compared between treatment groups using the Cochran-Mantel Haenszel test for ordinal data. No interim analyses for efficacy or futility were done.

# Results  

Between June 1, 2017, and April 14, 2020, 50 participants were enrolled in the study. Enrolment to the study was slower than expected due to the logistical challenge of initiating continuous EEG recording within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of inclusion and inherent difficulties of obtaining consent and recruiting patients in the setting of acute intracerebral haemorrhage. In April 17, 2020, when recruitment was interrupted due to the COVID-19 pandemic, only 50 patients had been included. At this point, enrolment to the study was prematurely stopped due to cessation of funding. It was not possible to restart the study afterwards, because the treatment and placebo kits had expired and the costs of additional production and control process could not be covered. The investigators and sponsor decided to discontinue the recruitment of patients and remained masked to the results until the last follow-up evaluation of the last included patient.  

Of the 50 patients enrolled, 24 $(48\%)$ patients were assigned to the levetiracetam group and 26 $(52\%)$ to the placebo group (figure 1). One patient assigned leveti­ racetam withdrew consent after enrolment; another four patients either did not have continuous EEG or had a technical problem. One patient assigned placebo was excluded after enrolment because they had a seizure before treatment could be started; another two patients either did not have continuous EEG or had a technical problem. Therefore, 19 $(79\%)$ of 24 patients assigned levetiracetam and 23 $(88\%)$ of 26 patents assigned placebo were included in the modified intention-to-treat population. Comprehensive screening data for all consecutive patients with intracerebral haemorrhage were not available at all sites because study staff availability for screening varied. Participants attended the last evaluation at 12 months after inclusion in the study. Median followup was 366 days (IQR 171–400) in the levetiracetam group and 369 days (98–397) in the placebo group. With respect to compliance with treatment, the proportion of patients who took treatment without interruption for 1 month did not differ between groups (16 $[73\%]$ of 22 in the levetiracetam group vs 17 $[74\%]$ of 23 in the placebo group; $\mathtt{p}{>}0\cdot99!$ ).

During the first $72\mathrm{~h~}$ following inclusion, a clinical or electrographic seizure (or both) was observed in three $(16\%)$ of 19 patients in the levetiracetam group versus ten $(43\%)$ of 23 patients in the placebo group (OR 0·16, $95\%$ CI $0\cdot03{-}0\cdot94_{\cdot}$ ; $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}043$ , after adjustment for randomisation stratification factors [NIHSS stratum and centre]; table 2, figure 2), all of which  were electro­ cardiographic.  One patient in the placebo group had an early clinical seizure (ie, after $72\mathrm{~h~}$ and before day 30), with clinical symptoms of confusion and vigilance impairment related to a focal seizure reported 12 days after randomisation. Regarding late clinical seizures, occurring after day 30 and by 12 months of inclusion in the study, one patient had a focal seizure between the 3-month and the 6-month evaluation in the levetiracetam group. In the placebo group, the patient who had an early focal seizure had a recurrent seizure (secondary generalised seizure) at the 12-month evaluation.  

The median period between intracerebral haemorrhage symptom onset and the first recorded electrographic seizure was $45{\cdot}7\ensuremath{\mathrm{h}}$ [IQR 30·6–55·7] in the levetiracetam group and 49·8 h [32·4–53·9] in the placebo group, with no difference between groups $(\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}87)$ . The median duration of the continuous EEG recording did not differ between the levetiracetam and placebo groups (48·1 h [IQR 45·1–50·3] vs 48·4 h [47·2–49·5]; $\mathtt{p}{=}0{\cdot}69\$ ), but the duration of the continuous EEG that could be interpreted (considering the artefacts or missing signals) was higher in the levetiracetam group (43·9 h [35·4–45·8] vs 35·1 h [23·7–41·5], $\mathtt{p}{=}0{\cdot}035)$ .  

A total of 164 electrographic seizures were recorded in 13 patients during continuous EEG: six seizures in three patients in the levetiracetam group and 158 seizures in ten patients in the placebo group, with three patients in the placebo group having ten seizures or more. The number of electrographic seizures during the 48-h continuous EEG was significantly lower in the leveti­ racetam group than in the placebo group (rate ratio 0·07, $95\%$ CI 0·01–0·38; $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}0021\$ ), and the median duration of seizures was shorter (67 s [IQR 46–300] vs 780 s [380–1980]; $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p}}=0\cdot028$ ; table 2, figure 2). The electrographic seizures presented as low-voltage, fast-activity, rhythmic spikes; or spike and waves; or rhythmic slow waves with a spatiotemporal development (appendix pp 3–4). Two-thirds of patients exhibited epileptic spikes in each group, mostly with a focal or lateralised topography, with 12 $(63\%)$ of 19 patients in the levetiracetam group and 14 $(63\%)$ of 23 in the placebo group showing interictal paroxysmal and non-paroxysmal patterns (table 2). The change in the NIHSS scores at $72\mathrm{h}$ , 1 month, or 3 months did not differ between groups, nor did the change in mRS scores at 3 months, 6 months, or 12 months (table 2).

# Discussion  

To our knowledge, the PEACH trial is the first randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with continuous EEG monitoring investigating the safety and efficacy of primary antiseizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam on the prevention of clinical and subclinical seizures at the acute phase of intracerebral haemorrhage. Unfortunately, only $48\%$ recruitment was reached, meaning that the study was underpowered and any conclusions must be interpreted with caution. Levetiracetam was safe and showed possible efficacy at preventing the occurrence of seizures after intracerebral haemorrhage. The number of seizures during the continuous EEG recording was significantly higher in the placebo group than in the levetiracetam group, and time spent in seizure was longer. Our study corroborates the results of previous studies showing that most early seizures after hospital admission for intracerebral haemorrhage are clinically unrecognised and can only be diagnosed with EEG monitoring.1  

Seizures were commonly recorded in our study, and occurred more frequently in the placebo group. The seizure rate would, however, have been greatly under­ estimated if continuous EEG had not been systematically performed, because all seizures in the first $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ were electrographic seizures, with one clinical seizure recorded at day 12 in the placebo group and one clinical seizure in each group after day 30. Some studies have reported electrographic seizures (ie, seizures without any clinically detected symptoms) in up to $30\%$ of patients with intracerebral haemorrhage and, together with our data, this finding suggests that only with the use of continuous EEG will valid information be gathered on the true incidence of acute seizures associated with intracerebral haemorrhage.6,10 Currently, the American Clinical Neuro­ physiology Society recommends continuous EEG monitoring in acute supratentorial brain injury with altered mental status.23 However, continuous EEG remains underused in stroke and neurointensive care units, potentially because of logistical and financial issues. The assessment of early seizures in acute intracerebral haemorrhage and their effect on neurological outcomes might have been biased in most studies performed up to now because of the absence of systematic EEG monit­ oring.7,24 Indeed, most seizures occurring in intracerebral haemorrhage remain subclinical, either because the observation of additional seizure symptoms or because potential symptoms related to the epileptic discharge cannot be detected with a routine neurological examination. Thus, the reported prevalence of early seizures in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage is probably largely underestimated if based on only clinically detected seizures.7,25

We observed no difference between the levetiracetam group and the placebo group regarding short-term and long-term functional outcomes, or quality of life. The sample size of the trial was powered to compare the effect on seizure prophylaxis and not designed for functional outcomes. It remains unclear whether preventing acute seizures might lead to improved functional outcomes after intracerebral haemorrhage.26 Uncertainty about the risks and benefits of primary seizure prophylaxis in acute intracerebral haemorrhage translates into a wide variation in rates of antiseizure drug prescription after intracerebral haemorrhage, with up to $30\%$ of clinicians reporting use of these drugs in routine clinical practice.11 Seizures could be deleterious in the acute phase of intracerebral haemorrhage because they have been associated with haematoma expansion, a major cause of morbidity and mortality.6 In our study, no difference was reported between the levetiracetam group and the placebo group regarding haematoma expansion and midline shift on secondary brain imaging at $72\mathrm{~h~}$ In other settings, such as spontaneous subarachnoid haemorrhage, non-convulsive seizures are accompanied by elevated heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate.27 Additionally, paroxysmal EEG patterns during seizure have been associated with brain oxygen-concentration decrease without sufficient compensatory increase in cerebral blood flow, thus potentially leading to additional brain damage.28 Some studies have shown that earlyonset status epilepticus within the first 7 days after stroke was associated with a higher mortality rate.29 Simulation model and decision analysis have shown that short-term treat­ment (7-day antiseizure drug course) versus longterm treatment  (indefinite therapy) is associated with the greatest net benefit measured as expected quality-adjusted life-years in patients with spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage.26 Validated tools for predicting early seizures (eg, 2HELPS2B score) could aid clinical decisions on initiation of primary versus secondary early seizure prophylaxis.30 Some antiseizure drugs, such as phenytoin and phenobarbital, might hinder functional recovery,15,31 whereas newer antiseizure drugs, such as levetiracetam and lacosamide, might have a potential neuroprotective effect,32,33 although some controversy surrounds this issue.34 Adverse events with levetiracetam were rare. There was no increase in the frequency of anxiety or depression in the levetiracetam group compared with the placebo group. Overall, the results of the PEACH trial were consistent with the known safety profile of levetiracetam and did not identify new safety concerns in the population of patients with acute intracerebral haemorrhage. Thus, the question of whether it is warranted to preventively treat patients with a short-term antiseizure drug—the efficacy and safety of which has been demonstrated—should be asked.  

A prospective study with a large sample size has shown that patients with early seizures have a worse functional outcome than do those without early seizures (mRS score ${>}3$ in $65\%$ of patients vs $49\%$ at 3 months evaluation).12 Based on our findings, considering an α threshold of $5\%$ , a power of $90\%$ , and a $15\%$ absolute reduction in the rate of poor outcome at 3 months in patients without early seizures,12 240 patients should be included in each group of a future randomised  trial  evaluating  prophylactic  antiseizure therapy versus control. It is, however, important to note that this sample size calculation relies on the assumption that electrographic seizures are associated with a prognosis as poor as that associated with clinical seizures.  

Our study has several limitations. First, recruitment was prematurely interrupted after a total of 50 patients had been included due to cessation of funding. Recruitment on the basis of cEEG recording in a tight time schedule was challenging and led to missingness in continuous EEG data. This sample size has led to some randomisation imbalances between both groups regarding demographic and baseline  characteristics, including  intracerebral haemorrhage location and volume, which might affect the risk of early seizure.5,7,12 The low sample size also limited the power of prespecified exploratory analyses aiming to adjust for most of the factors associated with early seizures. The sample size might also have precluded us from demonstrating a favourable effect of primary seizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam on functional outcomes. Second, although brain imaging was read centrally, the imaging modalities on admission and at $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ follow-up were not standardised (CT scan or MRI were performed at the discretion of the treating clinician). Third, as no com­ bined video monitoring was performed, no data are available about potential subtle clinical manifestations during electrographic seizures. Fourth, continuous EEG was recorded for $48\mathrm{~h~}$ , and we cannot exclude that some subclinical seizures occurred before or after the recording and remained undetected. Fifth, dosage of levetiracetam was not adjusted for age, weight, or additional medication, and serum concentrations of levetiracetam were not analysed. We cannot exclude the possibility that too low treatment doses might have contributed to seizure occurrence in patients with seizures in the levetiracetam group. Finally, our results pertain to patients with mild-tomoderate intracerebral haemor­rhage and might not be generalisable to patients with severe intracerebral haemorrhage.  

Levetiracetam might be effective in preventing acute seizures in patients with spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage. However, given the small sample size and data missingness, the magnitude of the effect remains uncertain. An adequately powered randomised controlled trial is needed to answer whether primary seizure prophylaxis improves functional outcome in this setting.
