# The Safety and Efficacy of Rivaroxaban Compared with Warfarin in Patients with Atrial Fibrillation and Diabetes: A Systematic Review and Meta‑analysis  

Yang Hua1 · Jin‑Yu Sun1 · Yue Su1 · Qiang Qu1 · Hong‑Ye Wang1 · Wei Sun1 $\cdot$ Xiang‑Qing Kong1  

Published online: 8 June 2020   
$\circledcirc$ Springer Nature Switzerland AG 2020  



# Abstract  

Aims  This meta-analysis was conducted to compare the efficacy and safety of rivaroxaban with warfarin in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) and diabetes mellitus.  

Methods  PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases were systematically searched from the establishment of databases up to 15 October 2019. Studies on efficacy and safety outcomes of rivaroxaban and warfarin were included. Efficacy and safety outcomes, including stroke, ischemic stroke, stroke or systemic embolism, myocardial infarction, major adverse cardiac events, major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and major gastrointestinal bleeding were collected for meta-analysis.  

Results  Compared with warfarin, rivaroxaban could significantly reduce stroke (risk ratio [RR] 0.77; $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 0.63–0.95; $P{=}0.01\$ ), ischemic stroke (RR 0.74; $95\%$ C $\mathrm{~\textmu~}0.63\substack{-0.87}$ ; $P{=}0.0004,$ , stroke or systemic embolism (RR 0.73; $95\%$ CI 0.60–0.89; $P{=}0.002,$ , myocardial infarction (RR 0.68; $95\%$ CI 0.56–0.82; $P<0.0001$ ), and major adverse cardiac events (RR 0.71; $95\%$ CI 0.53–0.94; $P{=}0.02,$ ) in patients with AF and diabetes. Moreover, rivaroxaban was associated with a lower risk of major bleeding (RR 0.79; $95\%$ CI 0.65–0.96; $P{=}0.02,$ ), intracranial hemorrhage (RR 0.52; $95\%$ CI 0.39–0.69; $P<0.00001$ ), and major gastrointestinal bleeding (RR 0.74; $95\%$ CI 0.56–0.98; $P{=}0.04\$ ). Similar results were obtained in stratified meta-analysis of cohort studies.  

Conclusion  Our study suggests a favorable risk–benefit profile of rivaroxaban, with superior efficacy and safety over warfarin in patients with AF and diabetes.  



# 1  Introduction  

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent arrhythmia around the world, and it is closely associated with increased morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Several regional clinical studies have attributed the rising prevalence of AF to the aging population, while plenty of other research has demonstrated that the high incidence of AF is caused by a series of cardiovascular risk factors, such as non-cardiovascular comorbidities and lifestyle changes [3–5].  

Diabetes mellitus is a common comorbidity in AF patients, and patients with diabetes are reported to have a $49\%$ higher risk of developing non-valvular AF [6]. Additionally, patients with AF and diabetes are at a higher risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE), including stroke, myocardial infarction, and vascular death, compared with non-AF patients [7]. Importantly, diabetes is an independent risk factor of stroke in AF patients [8], and therefore, prophylactic oral anticoagulation is significant for patients with AF and diabetes to prevent adverse cardiovascular events. Traditionally, vitamin K antagonists (e.g., warfarin) have been demonstrated to show excellent efficacy in patients with AF and diabetes [9–11]. However, frequent drug monitoring and dose adjustment due to food and drug interactions can reduce the compliance of patients, which could eventually result in the underuse of warfarin for stroke prevention [12, 13]. In this case, several non-vitamin-K-dependent oral anticoagulants targeting factor IIa/thrombin or activated factor X have been developed over the past decade.

Rivaroxaban, the first oral factor Xa inhibitor, offers potential advantages over vitamin K antagonists, including rapid onset and offset of action, as well as fewer drug interactions [14]. Besides, it has a dual-mode excretion process; two thirds of rivaroxaban is eliminated by the liver via metabolism to an inactive metabolite, and one third of rivaroxaban is eliminated unchanged by the kidney, which makes rivaroxaban relatively safer for patients, especially for diabetic patients, since they are usually accompanied by renal dysfuction [15]. Several studies have demonstrated that rivaroxaban is at least as safe and effective as warfarin for the prevention of stroke and systemic embolism in patients with AF [16–18]. However, there are few studies on the safety and efficacy of rivaroxaban in patients with AF and diabetes, and no specific conclusion has been reached so far. Therefore, a comprehensive study is urgently needed to summarize the existing data and evaluate the clinical outcomes systematically. In this study, we aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of rivaroxaban compared with warfarin in patients with AF and diabetes.  



# 2  Methods  



# 2.1 Search Strategy  

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in online databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, from the establishment of the databases up to 15 October 2019. The detailed search strategy example of PubMed was as follows: (atrial fibrillation [Title/Abstract] OR AF [Title/Abstract] OR non-valvular atrial fibrillation [Title/Abstract]) AND diabetes [Title/Abstract] AND rivaroxaban [Title/Abstract] AND warfarin [Title/Abstract]. Trials comparing the therapeutic effects between rivaroxaban and warfarin in patients with AF and diabetes were included. Additionally, the reference lists of retrieved eligible articles as well as review articles were hand-searched for potential publications not previously identified.  



# 2.2 Study Design  

Clinical studies were eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort, case–control, and observational studies; (2) studies comparing the outcomes of rivaroxaban and warfarin, such as stroke, stroke or systemic embolism (SSE), MACE, and major bleeding; (3) studies published in English in peerreviewed journals with full-text availability; and (4) for multiple publications from the same trial or cohort, only the article with the most data was included. Animal studies, case reports, review articles, editorials, letters, studies without original data, and studies enrolling fewer than ten patients were excluded. Two researchers (Y. Su and Q. Qu) searched and reviewed the titles, abstracts, and full texts to determine the included studies.  



# 2.3  Data Extraction and Quality Assessment  

Analysis was performed at the study level. Two researchers (Y. Hua and J.-Y. Sun) independently extracted the data, and disagreements were resolved by consensus or discussion with a third author (H.-Y. Wang). We documented the study characteristics, including first author, publication year, study design, drug doses, follow-up duration, and number of patients. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients were also recorded.  

The quality of studies involved was assessed by two researchers (Y. Hua and J.-Y. Sun) independently. The Newcastle-Ottawa Quality (NOS) assessment scale was adopted to evaluate observational studies [19]. The NOS tool involved three domains, with a total of 9 points: the selection of cohorts (0–4 points), the comparability of cohorts (0–2 points), and the assessment of the outcome (0–3 points). An NOS score of $\geq6$ points indicated moderate-to-high quality, while an NOS score of $<6$ points indicated low quality. The Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool was used to assess the quality of one RCT included in our review [20]. Any disagreement was resolved by discussion or consulting a third researcher (Y. Su) until the consensus was reached.  



# 2.4 Statistical Analysis  

We collected the sample size and number of events in each group. If the number of events was unavailable, the expected number of events was calculated based on event rates: event number $=$ (total patient number) $\times$ [event rate (per 100 patient-years)] $\times$ [follow-up time (years)] [21]. We calculated relative risks (RRs) and corresponding $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) for each outcome and trial, respectively. Results were presented as RRs with $95\%$ CI and with twotailed $P$ values. Statistical significance was set at $P<0.05$ .  

$I^{2}$ was used to quantify the proportion of variance due to between-study heterogeneity [22]. $I^{2}$ values of $0\%$ , $<25\%$ , $25{-}49\%$ , and $>50\%$ denoted no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. If the $I^{2}$ value was less than $50\%$ , a fixed effect model would be adopted. Otherwise, a random effect model was used considering the substantial heterogeneity. When possible, we performed stratified meta-analyses based on study design for efficacy and safety outcomes.  

All statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager $\mathrm{v}5.3$ (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark). Our research followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [23].  



# 3  Results  



# 3.1 Study Selection and Quality Assessment  

A total of 257 articles were identified in the initial search. We excluded 47 duplicates and removed 186 studies not meeting inclusion criteria, and 24 full-text studies were evaluated in a closer inspection. After 20 articles (12 review articles, one editorial, two without available results, and one without a control group) were discarded, a total of four studies [24–27] were finally included in the analysis, including one phase III randomized trial and three retrospective cohort studies.

From the four trials, a total of 41,976 patients with AF and diabetes (19,395 treated by rivaroxaban and 22,581 treated by warfarin) were included. Of note, rivaroxaban was used at the dosage of $20~\mathrm{mg}$ once daily or $15~\mathrm{mg}$ once daily. All the included observational studies had a moderate-to-high quality with an NOS score of ${>}6$ points (Table 2), and the only RCT included in our review was a high quality study with a low risk of bias (Fig. 2).  



# 3.2 Efficacy Outcomes Between Rivaroxaban and Warfarin  

Figures 3 and 4 show the overall results of the efficacy outcomes of interest. Compared with warfarin, rivaroxaban was associated with a significantly lower risk of stroke (RR 0.77; $95\%$ CI 0.63–0.95; $P{=}0.01$ ; $I^{2}{=}0\%$ ; Fig. 3a) and ischemic stroke (RR 0.74; $95\%$ CI 0.63–0.87; $P=0.0004$ ; $I^{2}{=}33\%$ ; Fig.  3b). Similarly, rivaroxaban considerably reduced the risk of SSE (RR 0.73; $95\%$ CI 0.60–0.89; $P=0.002$ ; $I^{2}{=}28\%$ ; Fig. 3c). Moreover, rivaroxaban could reduce the risk of myocardial infarction (RR 0.68; $95\%$ CI 0.56–0.82; $P<0.0001$ ; $I^{2}{=}0$ ; Fig. 4a) and MACE (RR 0.71; $95\%$ CI $0.53\substack{-0.94}$ ; $P{=}0.02$ , $I^{2}{=}84\%$ ; Fig. 4b).  

We performed stratified analyses according to types of studies included (shown in Table 3). Similar results in favor of rivaroxaban were obtained in the meta-analysis of propensity-matched cohort studies. Rivaroxaban reduced the risk of ischemic stroke (RR 0.68; $95\%$ CI 0.56–0.83; $P{=}0.0001$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ ) and myocardial infarction (RR 0.64; $95\%$ CI $0.52–0.79$ ; $P<0.0001$ ; $I^{2}=0$ ). Due to insufficient data, stratified meta-analysis of RCTs and assessment of stroke, SSE, and MACE in cohort studies were not performed.  



# 3.3 Safety Outcomes Between Rivaroxaban and Warfarin  

Figure 5 shows the overall results of the safety outcomes of interest. Data regarding the occurrence of major bleeding are available from the four trials. Rates of major bleeding were $4.04\%$ in patients with rivaroxaban versus $5.06\%$ in those with warfarin. In comparison with warfarin, rivaroxaban was associated with decreased risk of major bleeding (RR 0.79; $95\%$ CI 0.65–0.96; $P{=}0.02$ , $I^{2}{=}75\%$ ; Fig. 5a), intracranial hemorrhage (RR 0.52; $95\%$ CI 0.39–0.69; $P<0.00001$ ; $I^{2}{=}0\%$ ; Fig. 5b), and major gastrointestinal bleeding (RR 0.74; $95\%$ CI 0.56–0.98; $P{=}0.04$ ; $I^{2}{=}76\%$ ; Fig. 5c).

Stratified analyses based on study design were also performed (shown in Table 4). Consistent with pooled results, rivaroxaban considerably reduced the risk of major bleeding (RR 0.73; $95\%$ CI $0.59\substack{-0.92}$ ; $P=0.007$ ; $I^{2}=73\%$ ), and intracranial hemorrhage (RR 0.49; $95\%$ CI 0.36–0.68; $P<0.0001$ ; $I^{2}{=}0\%$ ) in the meta-analysis of cohort studies, demonstrating favorable safety performance over warfarin.  



# 4  Discussion  

We comprehensively evaluated 41,976 patients from four published original articles. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to compare the efficacy and safety outcomes between rivaroxaban and warfarin in patients with

AF and diabetes. Moreover, our results demonstrated the advantage of rivaroxaban over warfarin in patients with AF and diabetes. Due to the lack of RCTs, most of the studies enrolled were retrospective and observational studies.  

Diabetes mellitus is closely associated with a higher risk of AF, as well as a worse prognosis. It was reported that the incidence of AF was increased by $35\%$ in diabetic patients, and the AF risk was more significant in those with elevated levels of glycated hemoglobin [28]. Notably, stroke was more likely to occur in patients with AF and diabetes compared with those without diabetes. In the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study, patients with AF and diabetes were associated with an eightfold higher risk of stroke [29], whereas another study [8] indicated that these individuals showed a 1.7-fold increased risk of stroke and commonly experienced worse post-stroke function recovery as well as outcomes [30, 31]. Hypercoagulability may play a critical role in the adverse outcomes of patients with AF and diabetes, and several mechanisms are involved in this situation, including increased tissue plasminogen activator antigen, improved factor VIII activity, decreased fibrin breakdown, and endothelial dysfunction [32].

Stroke is generally divided into two subtypes: ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke. Since patients with AF and diabetes show an increased risk of thromboembolic events, the four included studies took stroke or ischemic stroke as important efficacy outcomes. SSE is a comprehensive endpoint, which has been used to evaluate the hypercoagulable or prothrombotic state of the whole body in recent studies [33]. A recent meta-analysis based on four RCTs indicated that direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) could reduce the risk of SSE compared with warfarin in AF and diabetic patients [34]. Consistent with previous studies, our results suggested that rivaroxaban reduced the risk of stroke, ischemic stroke, and SSE, which supports the efficacy over warfarin in those patients. Moreover, intracranial hemorrhage and major bleeding are severe complications of anticoagulant treatment. Our meta-analysis showed that rivaroxaban was associated with significantly lower risks of intracranial hemorrhage and major bleeding, which indicates a better safety performance over warfarin.  

Diabetic status appeared to be independent of relative efficacy and safety of rivaroxaban versus warfarin. In Bansilal et al.’s study, no statistical interaction was found between the relative efficacy or safety of rivaroxaban and warfarin based on the presence of diabetes ( $\scriptstyle{P=0.53}$ and $P=0.43$ , respectively, for SSE and major bleeding), which was consistent with the research by Patti et al. [34]. Currently, there is no adequate evidence to support the extra benefit of rivaroxaban treatment for diabetic patients.  

Diabetic patients are generally geriatric, and they are probably receiving various drugs against multiple complications. Accordingly, it is essential to select the best anticoagulation therapy [35]. For example, diabetes could affect bone health, which makes osteoporosis-associated fracture a significant complication of diabetes [36, 37]. Warfarin was reported to result in an increased risk of osteoporotic fracture, since warfarin blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase and disturbs normal bone mineralization [38, 39]. Binding et al. [40] found that DOACs could significantly reduce the risk of osteoporotic fracture compared with vitamin K antagonists, which is consistent with previous research [41]. A study-level meta-analysis also demonstrated a lower risk of bone fracture in AF patients receiving rivaroxaban when compared with warfarin in long-term treatment [42]. Despite the lack of subanalysis regarding the presence of diabetes, rivaroxaban seems to be preferable, considering the potential adverse effects of warfarin and diabetes status on bone fracture.

Moreover, comedication plays an important role in anticoagulation selection. For example, metformin, the most used glucose-lowering agent, is generally considered to increase the risk of bleeding in warfarin users. However, Stage et al. [43] reported that the initiation of glucose-lowering agents could cause a decrease in mean international normalized ratio (INR) (from 2.5 to 2.2) and reduce the anticoagulant effect of vitamin K antagonists. Similarly, Wijnen et al. [44] also demonstrated that metformin could decrease the anticoagulant effect of phenprocoumon (a vitamin K antagonist). Differently, rivaroxaban, metabolized via P-gp and CYP3A4, is generally considered to have no clinically relevant interactions with commonly used drugs [45]. Statins (e.g., atorvastatin, fluvastatin, rosuvastatin, and simvastatin) are a class of cholesterol-lowering drugs, reducing the risk of cardiovascular events in diabetic patients. Statins are reported to increase INR values and decrease the required dosage of warfarin, which leads to an increased risk of bleeding in warfarin users [46, 47]. CYP450 inhibition and potential properties of statins on anticoagulation may be the primary causes [48]. Interestingly, some other studies reported no interaction between statin use and rate of bleeding [49] or time in the therapeutic range of warfarin users [50]. Considering the similar metabolism between statin and rivaroxaban (via P-gp and CYP3A4), Kubitza et al. [51] and Wells et al. [52] investigated the interactions between statin and rivaroxaban, and no interaction was found.  

Moreover, antiplatelet medications are frequently coadministered with anticoagulant drugs, owing to their respective established effects on the coagulation system and platelet aggregation. The wide application of comedication is probably driven by comorbidities in geriatric patients, especially those with high risks of acute myocardial infarction and AF. According to the guideline for the Management of Patients With Atrial Fibrillation, for AF patients with high stroke risk $\mathrm{CHA}_{2}\mathrm{DS}_{2}$ -VASc risk score $\geq2$ ) who have undergone percutaneous coronary intervention  with stenting for acute coronary syndrome, dual therapy with a P2Y12 inhibitor and vitamin K antagonist or a P2Y12 inhibitor combined with low-dose rivaroxaban (15 mg/daily) could both reduce the risk of bleeding compared with triple therapy (IIa, B-R) [53, 54]. In this clinical scenario, balancing the risk of stroke and bleeding to develop individualized treatment may outweigh the difference between warfarin and rivaroxaban for anticoagulation. Accordingly, considering the possible comorbidities, fewer adverse drug–drug interactions and direct pharmacologic action make rivaroxaban a more reliable agent for patients with diabetes and AF.  

Multiple clinical studies have shown that warfarin increased the risk of coronary artery calcification [55–57]. Differently, rivaroxaban was reported to exert a protective effect on plaque progression, and therefore, it could reduce the risk of cardiovascular events [58, 59]. Hara et al. [60] assumed that activated factor X might play an important role in the pro-inflammatory response through protease-activated receptors, which could initiate inflammatory activation of endothelial cells, recruit monocytes or macrophages, and lead to the development of atherosclerosis. By inhibiting factor X, rivaroxaban could attenuate plaque progression and atherosclerosis. Considering the converse effects of rivaroxaban and warfarin on atherosclerosis, the lower risk of MACE and myocardial infarction in rivaroxaban users in our meta-analysis could possibly be explained. Additionally, two different dosages of rivaroxaban were applied in the four studies: standard dose $(20~\mathrm{mg/day)}$ and reduced dose $15\mathrm{mg/day)}$ . Numbers of patients receiving the reduced dose varied among studies: $24.1\%$ , $16\%$ , $87.5\%$ , and $20\%$ in Baker et al.’s, Bansilal et al.’s, Hsu et al.’s, and Coleman et al.’s research, respectively. In the study by Coleman et al. [25], rivaroxaban had similar efficacy and safety with warfarin across any-dose and standard-dose analyses. However, the reduced-dose analysis demonstrated that the risk of SSE and ischemic stroke was significantly decreased in the rivaroxaban group. On the contrary, Baker et al.’s study [27] indicated that reduced-dose rivaroxaban had a similar effect on MACE compared with warfarin use (hazard ratio [HR] 1.05; $95\%$ CI 0.73–1.51), while all-dose analysis showed significant protective effect (HR 0.37; $95\%$ CI 0.21–0.65).  

Moreover, the difference in efficacy and safety outcomes between standard and reduced rivaroxaban doses remains unclear. In the study by Jeong et al. [62], the dosage of rivaroxaban $15\mathrm{mg/day}$ or 20 mg/day led to similar efficacy, and both dosages showed a reduced risk in major bleeding compared with warfarin use. Importantly, Lin et al. [63] indicated that low-dose rivaroxaban was associated with a significantly higher risk of myocardial infarction (HR 2.26; $95\%$ CI 1.13–4.52; $P=0.022$ ) as well as systemic embolism (HR 2.04; $95\%$ CI 0.95–4.36; $P{=}0.068,$ ). In conclusion, standard-dose regimens might be an appealing option for those with a high risk of cardiovascular events and normal renal function. In our meta-analysis, the subgroup analysis based on the rivaroxaban dose could not be performed due to the limited data.  

Among the included studies, there was no significant heterogeneity in the parameters of stroke, ischemic stroke, SSE, myocardial infarction, and intracranial hemorrhage. However, obvious heterogeneity was found in composite endpoints, including MACE, major bleeding, and major gastrointestinal bleeding. We conducted a sensitivity analysis by removing studies respectively or performing a stratified meta-analysis of cohort studies alone. Heterogeneity was still significant, and we did not find a single study leading to heterogeneity.  

After examining the four studies thoroughly, we found that the difference in methodology and the diversity in the definition of outcomes might be the main source of heterogeneity. For example, major bleeding was defined as intracranial or gastrointestinal bleeding in Baker et al.’s study, while in Hsu et al.’s study, hematuria was also counted. In addition, major bleeding was determined in Coleman’s study using the validated algorithm for the detection of bleedingrelated hospitalizations, which may also cause heterogeneity. The use of a random effect model and the robustness of the data across subgroups may help mitigate the potential effect of heterogeneity on the validity of our results.  

Our meta-analysis showed that compared with warfarin, rivaroxaban could reduce the risk of stroke events and MACE in patients with AF and diabetes. Furthermore, the risk of major bleeding was also decreased in the rivaroxaban group, especially intracranial hemorrhage. Although our research was limited by the lack of studies, particularly RCTs, our review builds on knowledge garnered from both previous RCTs and real-world studies regarding the optimal approach to the anticoagulant management of patients with AF and diabetes.  



# 5 Limitations  

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, since the number of RCTs was scant in this area, we combined RCTs and cohort studies and performed stratified meta-analyses of cohort studies. Additionally, none of the studies included were primarily designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of rivaroxaban versus warfarin prospectively. Although evidence of heterogeneity was found only in composite endpoints (MACE, major bleeding, and major gastrointestinal bleeding), caution should be exercised when interpreting our results. Second, misclassification and selection bias in retrospective database analysis is unavoidable and may affect the internal validity [64]. Third, the time in the therapeutic range was not available. Mostly, an INR of $2.0{-}3.0\$ is recommended as the optimal therapeutic range for warfarin users. However, poor INR control may affect warfarin treatment and exaggerate the benefit of rivaroxaban and safety endpoints. Last but not least, little information was acquired regarding laboratory data like glycated hemoglobin (only in Hsu et al.’s study), which prevented us from determining the quality of glycemic control and assessing the influence of glycemic levels on clinical outcomes.  



# 6  Conclusion  

In patients with AF and diabetes, rivaroxaban showed a superior efficacy and safety profile over warfarin, with significant reductions of stroke, myocardial infarction, MACEs, and major bleeding. In consideration of multiple medical conditions in those patients, rivaroxaban exhibits a favorable risk–benefit profile. Randomized controlled trials in large cohorts are required to confirm the favorable clinical outcomes with rivaroxaban in patients with AF and diabetes.
