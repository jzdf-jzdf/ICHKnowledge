# Impact of an early mobilization protocol on the reduction of medical complications after surgery for chronic subdural hematoma: the GET-UP Trial  

\*Sérgio Sousa, MD,1 Vasco Pinto, MD,1–3 Filipe Vaz da Silva, MD,1 Tiago Ribeiro da Costa, MD,1 Armindo Fernandes, MD,1 Rodrigo Batata, MD,1 Carolina Noronha, MD,1–3 João Monteiro Silva, MD,1 Sónia Ferreira, RN,1 Salomé Sobral, RN,1 Célia Alves, RN,1 Rui Rangel, MD,1 and Alfredo Calheiros, MD,1,2 on behalf of the GET-UP Trial collaborators  

1Department of Neurosurgery, Centro Hospitalar Universitário de Santo António, Porto, Portugal; 2Department of Anatomy, UMIB–Unit for Multidisciplinary Research in Biomedicine, ICBAS–School of Medicine and Biomedical Sciences, University of Porto, Porto, Portugal; and 3ITR–Laboratory for Integrative and Translational Research in Population Health, Porto, Portugal  

OBJECTIVE  Timing of mobilization after chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) surgery is highly heterogeneous among neurosurgical centers. Past studies have suggested that early mobilization may reduce medical complications without increasing recurrence, but evidence remains scarce. The purpose of this study was to compare an early mobilization protocol with a 48-hour bed rest practice, with a focus on the occurrence of medical complications.  

METHODS  The GET-UP Trial is a prospective, randomized, unicentric, open-label study with an intention-to-treat primary analysis designed to evaluate the impact of an early mobilization protocol after burr hole craniostomy for cSDH on the occurrence of medical complications and functional outcomes. A total of 208 patients were recruited and randomly assigned to either an early mobilization group where they began head-of-bed elevation within the first 12 hours after surgery and proceeded to sedestation, orthostatism, and/or walking as rapidly as tolerated, or to a bed rest group where they remained recumbent with a head-of-bed angle inferior to $30^{\circ}$ for 48 hours after surgery. The primary outcome was the occurrence of a medical complication (defined as either an infection, seizure, or thrombotic event) after surgery and until clinical discharge. Secondary outcomes included length of stay measured from randomization to clinical discharge, surgical hematoma recurrence at clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery, and Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOSE) assessment at clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery.  

RESULTS  A total of 104 patients were randomly assigned to each group. No significant baseline clinical differences were observed before randomization. The primary outcome occurred in 36 $(34.6\%)$ patients included in the bed rest group and 20 $(19.2\%)$ in the early mobilization group $(\mathsf{p}=0.012)$ . At 1 month after surgery, a favorable functional outcome (defined as GOSE $\mathsf{score}\ge5$ ) was observed in 75 $(72.1\%)$ patients in the bed rest group and 85 $(81.7\%)$ in the early mobilization group $(\mathsf{p}=0.100)$ . Surgical recurrence occurred in 5 $(4.8\%)$ patients in the bed rest group and 8 $(7.7\%)$ in the early mobilization group $(\mathsf{p}=0.390)$ .  

CONCLUSIONS  The GET-UP Trial is the first randomized clinical trial to assess the impact of mobilization strategies on medical complications after burr hole craniostomy for cSDH. Early mobilization was associated with a reduction in medical complications without a significant effect on surgical recurrence, compared with a 48-hour bed rest protocol.

KEYWORDS  chronic subdural hematoma; early mobilization; medical complications; functional outcome; recurrence; traumatic brain injury  

hronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) is a major healthcare concern, with incidence reaching 129.5 cases per 100,000 persons per year in patients older than 80 years of age.1 Aging populations and increasing use of antithrombotic medications will expectably increase this number and this pathology’s relevance worldwide.  

Burr hole craniostomy (BHC) with subdural drain placement is one of the most utilized surgical treatment strategies, and results are generally favorable.2 Nevertheless, a recurrence rate of approximately $10\%$ is frequently cited, and as many as $17\%$ of patients fail to reach a good recovery outcome.3,4  

Although adequate postoperative care is essential for optimal surgical results, good-quality evidence-based guidelines are lacking.5 Recently published and ongoing trials are making a valuable contribution to correcting this issue.6 Still, one essential issue remains to be clarified— the impact of postoperative mobilization on medical complications and functional outcomes.  

Studies not primarily focused on postoperative medical complications compared early mobilization protocols to bed rest and reported conflicting results. A group has advocated bed rest to reduce recurrence,7 while others have failed to observe this effect and, conversely, others have postulated that early mobilization may decrease postoperative complications and improve functional outcomes.3,8  

At our institution, we routinely use BHC with subdural drain placement and 48-hour postoperative bed rest with the head of the bed (HOB) less than $30^{\circ}$ after which the subdural drains are removed and the patient initiates mobilization. To investigate the best strategy to reduce postoperative complications and to improve functional outcomes, we conducted a prospective, randomized clinical trial comparing an early mobilization protocol to a 48- hour bed rest approach.  

# Methods  

# Patient Population  

A sample size of 208 patients was precalculated to adhere to a statistical power of $80\%$ , a significance level of $5\%$ , an estimated primary outcome overall prevalence of $25\%$ , and an estimated loss to follow-up of $1\%$ . Patients who were 18 years of age or older and considered for BHC drainage of cSDH met the study’s inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria were previous intracranial procedure, ad initium drainage with craniotomy or intraoperative conversion to craniotomy, concomitant extracranial surgery, need for postoperative sedation exceeding 6 hours, and a previous condition precluding any mobilization. Between January 2019 and August 2021, 224 adult patients considered for BHC drainage of cSDH were screened for study inclusion. Patients who previously underwent cranial surgery or had a physical condition precluding any mobilization were excluded. After these criteria were applied and written informed consent was obtained from the patient or a legal representative, the precalculated total of 208 patients was included (Fig. 1). This study was registered with the ClinicalTrials.gov database (http://clinicaltrials.gov), and its registration no. is NCT03788005.  

# Surgical Procedure and Randomization  

According to the institutional protocol, surgical drainage consisted of trepanation, flush with body temperature saline, opening of the neovascularization membranes, and subdural silicon drain placement if technically possible. Intraoperative conversion to craniotomy or postoperative sedation and ventilation exceeding 6 hours mandated study exclusion, but this was not observed in any case. Patients were randomly allocated to one of the two study groups in a 1:1 ratio and blocks of 4: in total, 104 patients were assigned to the 48 hours postsurgery bed rest group (bed rest group) according to our institutional protocol (Supplementary Table 1); 104 patients were assigned to the early mobilization protocol (early mobilization group).  

# Postoperative Management Protocols  

The patients in the early mobilization group initiated HOB elevation over $30^{\circ}$ within the first 12 hours after surgery and progressed to sedestation, orthostatism, and walking as promptly as clinically tolerated. During mobilization, subdural drains remained closed. A minimum period of 8 hours of drainage per day (attained preferentially during sleeping hours) was mandatory. The patients in the bed rest group remained recumbent with HOB at less than $30^{\circ}$ with drains open for 48 hours. In both groups, subdural drains were removed 48 hours after surgery. Seizure prophylaxis was not routinely administered to patients in either group. Deep venous thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis was accomplished with mechanical compression stockings. Routine pharmacological prophylaxis was not pursued in either group. Of note, the bed rest group represents the standard-of-care postoperative protocol in our institution.  

# Outcome Assessment  

Patients were evaluated daily until clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery in an outpatient setting. The primary outcome consisted of medical complications between the immediate postoperative period and clinical discharge. The primary outcome was defined as any infection resulting in antibiotic treatment, any epileptic seizure resulting in antiepileptic drug initiation or increased dosage, and/or any thrombotic event requiring antithrombotic medication.  

Infections were diagnosed on the basis of clinical symptoms, findings on physical examination and appropriate diagnostic examinations, and the absence of a more adequate alternative diagnosis. For example, urinary tract infection was diagnosed on the basis of clinical symptoms or altered macroscopic urinary characteristics and a suggestive urinalysis. Urine cultures were performed for all patients with suspected urinary tract infection. Respiratory infection was diagnosed with a combination of clinical symptoms and findings on physical examination and chest radiography. Additionally, sputum and blood cultures were performed. Importantly, the clinical decision to initiate antibiotic treatment by the attending physician was the outcome-defining event. As such, negative urine, sputum, or blood cultures did not exclude the event from qualifying as primary outcome achievement. Seizures were diagnosed on the basis of clinical symptoms observed by a healthcare professional that were suggestive of an epileptic event with no alternative etiology. The event was considered a seizure if it motivated the initiation or dosage escalation of an antiepileptic drug, even in the absence of a confirming electroencephalogram. Finally, thrombotic events included DVT, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, or stroke. All were diagnosed with a combination of clinical symptoms and an appropriate diagnostic examination, such as venous echo-Doppler ultrasound for DVT, angio-CT for pulmonary embolism, electrocardiography as well as an elevated troponin level for myocardial infarction, and CT with a perfusion study for stroke. Only events treated with antithrombotic medications were included in the primary outcome. Screening for asymptomatic or subclinical thrombotic events was not routinely implemented.

Secondary outcomes included length of stay measured from randomization to clinical discharge, surgical recurrence for hematoma at clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery, and Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOSE) assessment score at clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery. The results of the GOSE assessments were dichotomized into a favorable outcome group (GOSE score $\geq5$ , indicating lower-level moderate disability) and an unfavorable outcome group (GOSE score $<5$ ). An additional analysis was performed, dichotomizing patients into a good recovery group (GOSE score 7 or 8) and non–good recovery group (GOSE score $<7$ ).  

A subgroup analysis was performed by selecting patients with good preinjury functional status, as defined by a preinjury modified Rankin Scale score equal or inferior to 3. Primary and secondary outcomes were then evaluated as previously described.  

A retrospective outcome assessment review (ROAR) was performed. Five physicians external to the trial and the neurosurgery department blindly reviewed all recorded outcome-defining events and stated whether they agreed or disagreed with the clinical decision (i.e., antibiotic prescription) leading to outcome definition; alternatively, these reviewers did not have sufficient data to decide. Assessments were dichotomized as agreement or no agreement (disagreement or insufficient data available).

# Institutional and Ethics Approval  

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Teaching, Formation and Research Department and the Ethics Commission of Centro Hospitalar Universitário do Porto.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Statistical analysis was performed utilizing the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 26.0 (IBM Corp.). The Pearson’s chi-square test was used to compare primary outcomes, GOSE assessment scores, and ROAR findings between study groups. The Student t-test was applied to compare length of stay. A p value inferior to 0.05 was considered significant. Groups were compared in an intention-to-treat fashion. In the subgroup analysis, logistic regression was performed to control for confounding variables when considering categorical variables and multiple linear regression was performed when considering numerical variables. The results were presented as ORs with $95\%$ CIs.

Thus, the preplanned total of 208 patients was randomly assigned to the study groups, with 104 allocated to each group (Fig. 1).

# Intervention and Follow-Up  

All 104 patients in the early mobilization group followed the early mobilization protocol, as detailed in the Methods. In the bed rest group, 5 $(4.8\%)$ patients did not follow the bed rest protocol: 2 $(1.9\%)$ patients underwent voluntary orthostatism against medical advice on the 1st postoperative day, 2 $(1.9\%)$ patients had the HOB elevated to over $30^{\circ}$ because of dyspnea, and 1 patient underwent full mobilization on the 1st postoperative day after the subdural drains were removed owing to inexistent drainage. The mean time from surgery to mobilization initiation was 42.4 hours in the bed rest group and 5.5 hours in the early mobilization group $(\mathbf{p}<0.001)$ ). Protocol nonadherence occurred in 7 patients, all in the bed rest group: 2 patients due to dyspnea motivating HOB elevation; 2 due to patient noncompliance and orthostatism against medical advice; 2 due to the early spontaneous decision to halt drainage and the subsequent clinical decision to remove the drains and mobilize the patients by the attending physician; and 1 due to the clinical decision to proceed with early mobilization by the attending physician for a patient with no drains. The timing and extent of mobilization achieved by the study participants are shown in Supplementary Table 2. After randomization, no patient was excluded, and outcomes were evaluated at clinical discharge and 1 month after surgery. No patients were lost to follow-up.

# Preinjury Functional Status Subgroup Analysis  

A total of 189 patients had a preinjury modified Rankin Scale score equal or inferior to 3: 95 of these patients were in the bed rest group and 94 were in the early mobilization group. The baseline characteristics are reported in Supplementary Table 3. Obesity was more prevalent in the bed rest group than the early mobilization group: 7 $(7.4\%)$ patients versus 1 $(1.1\%)$ $\mathrm{(p=0.031},$ ). No other differences were observed between groups.  

The primary and secondary outcomes of the preinjury functional status subgroup analysis are described in Table 4. GOSE results are shown in Fig. 3. Medical complications occurred in 31 $(32.6\%)$ patients in the bed rest group and 16 $(17.0\%)$ in the early mobilization group $\mathbf{\tilde{p}}=0.01\boldsymbol{\bar{3}},$ ) (after adjustment for obesity, OR 0.416, $95\%$ CI $0.208-$ 0.833, $\mathrm{p}=0.013$ ). # Retrospective Outcome Assessment Review  

As described in the Methods, we conducted an ROAR analysis to assess the potential impact of biased outcomedefining clinical decisions. Although the primary outcome was observed in 56 patients, a total of 69 (27 in the early mobilization group and 42 in the bed rest group) outcome-defining events were registered (because multiple outcome-defining events were observed in the same patient). Five external physicians retrospectively reviewed all events and produced a total of 345 assessments (dichotomized into an agreement or no agreement). No statistically significant differences were observed between study groups: of the 135 assessments of the early mobilization group events, the reviewers agreed with 100 $(74.1\%)$ of the decisions; of the 210 assessments of the bed rest group events, the reviewers agreed with 153 $(72.9\%)$ of the events $(\mathtt{p}=0.803)$ .

# Discussion  

The GET-UP Trial is, to our knowledge, the largest randomized controlled study to address the topic of postoperative mobilization after cSDH surgery and the first to focus on medical complications as the primary outcome. Our results demonstrated a reduction in medical complications among patients who were allowed to mobilize within a maximum of 12 hours after surgery. These results are mainly driven by the infection rate, which dropped by a factor of nearly 2 in the intervention group.  

We noticed a higher overall infection rate in our study compared with other studies involving larger population samples: $23.6\%$ in GET-UP Trial compared with $3.9\%^{9}$ and $\bar{9}.0\%^{3}$ in other studies. Even when focusing on specific infections, we still observed this discrepancy. For example, pneumonia was diagnosed in $11.0\%$ of the GET-UP Trial population versus $2.2\%^{9}$ and $8.0\%^{3}$ in other studies. These differences may reflect the diagnostic criteria and variable definition inequalities. In particular, we noted the absence of urinary tract infections in these studies.  

Seizures and thromboembolic events rates were the same for both trial groups. Again, we observed a higher seizure rate in the GET-UP Trial compared with larger studies: $9.1\%$ versus $6.2\%^{9}$ and $2.0\%$ .3 This may also have been the result of broader defining criteria. For instance, aphasia (described separately by Gelabert-González et al.9) motivating an antiepileptic drug prescription or dosage escalation would be considered a seizure event in the GET-UP Trial. Conversely, Brennan et al.3 detailed their thromboembolic event rate and presented an overall occurrence rate that was superior to ours ( $15.3\%$ vs $0.5\%$ . We posit that our clinical threshold for investigating thromboembolic events may have been higher than those of our colleagues, as we do not routinely screen asymptomatic patients.  

Patients who mobilized earlier attained better functional outcome 1 month after surgery, as measured by dichotomizing the GOSE scores into good recovery and non–good recovery groups (see the Methods). This dichotomization stems from the intrinsic categories of GOSE, which resulted in the categorization of patients as GOSE score 8 (upper-level good recovery) and GOSE score 7 (lowerlevel good recovery), and highlights those patients who recovered at least $50\%$ of the preinjury level of social and leisure activities. In our opinion, this division adds to the more commonly used dichotomization into favorable and unfavorable functional outcome groups, as the difference between GOSE score 7 and GOSE score 6 (upper-level moderate disability) signals a major change in work capacity and social activity that may be lost or diluted when grouping good recovery patients with moderate disability patients (GOSE score 6 and GOSE score 5, respectively). This becomes more relevant for a pathology such as cSDH compared with more severe traumatic brain injury, which has an expectably worse functional prognosis. For example, utilization of the difference between GOSE score 4 (upper-level severe disability) and GOSE score 5 (lowerlevel moderate disability) as the cutoff for dichotomization in a secondary decompressive craniectomy series may have been more informative than in a cSDH series.  

There is a scarcity of literature that directly focuses on the role of early postoperative mobilization in the medical complication rate and functional outcomes. Kurabe et al.8 designed a single-institution historical control study to compare early mobilization in a prospective cohort with retrospective data of delayed mobilization controls. The authors found a striking reduction in medical complications in the early mobilization group, which had less than half of the occurrences. The GET-UP Trial further validated the conclusions of Kurabe et al., with a nearly identical proportion of patients with reduced medical complications. A large prospective multicenter observational cohort study in the UK also suggested that bed rest was associated with unfavorable outcomes. Contrariwise, a 2007 randomized controlled trial by Abouzari et al.7 observed no significant differences in the medical complication rates between patients who followed an early mobilization protocol and those who followed bed rest. However, this study did not focus on medical complications as its primary outcome and the mobilization protocol was significantly different from ours (as discussed below).  

In the GET-UP Trial, the mean age across groups was 77.4 years. This elderly population is particularly susceptible to inpatient-related medical complications.10–14 Bed rest, especially with the HOB at $0^{\circ}$ , is thought to promote lung stasis, difficulty mobilizing respiratory secretions, and increased risk of aspiration, notably during feeding. This ultimately translates into a higher risk of respiratory infection. Likewise, immobilization promotes urinary retention, one of several risk factors for urinary tract infection. Moreover, it is a known risk factor for deep vein thrombosis. Our trial’s assertive early mobilization protocol may have reduced medical complications chiefly by counteracting these elements. Consistently, the respiratory infection rate was reduced from $14.4\%$ in the bed rest group to $7.7\%$ in the early mobilization group. This is one of our most compelling results and adds strength to this proposed biological mechanism of action.  

Notably, numerous fields in modern medicine are shifting their paradigms toward increasingly vigorous mobilization and rehabilitation programs.15,16 The premise is that sooner and more intensive rehabilitation leads to improved functional outcomes. The better functional outcomes observed in the early mobilization group 1 month after surgery may have stemmed from not only a reduction in medical complications but also from the capability to initiate rehabilitation earlier. Interestingly, we observed that patients with better preinjury functional status benefited significantly from our protocol in terms of reduced medical complications and improved functional performance. This was not observed in patients with worse preinjury functional status. We hypothesize that preinjury functional status signals an increased capability to endure a more intensive mobilization and rehabilitation program that may contribute to future enhanced functional outcomes.  

Historically, the cardinal drawback to early mobilization has been the concern for increased recurrence. The brain re-expansion theory was mentioned in several studies as a supporting argument for delayed mobilization.7 Nevertheless, a prospective, randomized trial directly focusing on this issue did not find any difference in recurrence rates.17 Furthermore, other studies did not report a rise in recurrence rates with early mobilization.8,18,19 Conspicuously, Abouzari et al.7 used radiological criteria to define recurrence. In fact, only 1 of the 9 patients with defined recurrence underwent reoperation. The GET-UP Trial defines recurrence on the basis of reoperation, a criterion that we believe is more clinically significant. No statistically significant differences between study groups regarding hematoma recurrence were observed in the GET-UP Trial.  

The early mobilization group protocol of the GET-UP Trial differs from those of previous randomized clinical trials. In the study by Abouzari et al.,7 patients assumed a sitting position with the HOB elevated to $30^{\circ}{-}40^{\circ}$ for 3 days after surgery. In the study by Nakajima et al.,17 patients assumed a sitting position on the day after the operation. We consider the early mobilization protocol of the GET-UP Trial to be more ambitious and strictly defined. Accordingly, the early mobilization protocol mandated the start of mobilization within the first 12 hours after surgery and progression to orthostatism and walking as clinically tolerated, a denouement to be attained as rapidly as possible. Minimum 8-hour daily drainage was established to take full advantage of the closed drainage system, preferentially during nighttime, without preclusion of the ability to engage in the early mobilization program. The rationale was to promote drainage, which is significantly associated with decreased recurrence,20 and to induce brain re-expansion during the patient’s resting period.  

This study had several limitations. We emphasize its single-center design, which limited the generalizability of the conclusion. Indeed, cSDH treatment practices are highly heterogeneous among neurosurgical centers,21,22 and our results may not apply equally to different surgical or postoperative procedures. However, our institution’s protocol for cSDH management is one of the most adopted strategies worldwide,2,23 limiting the negative impact of the generalizability of our results.  

Another limitation concerns the heterogeneity of the study intervention itself. In the early mobilization group, patients reached strikingly different stages of mobilization, with some tolerating only HOB elevation and many others rapidly progressing to orthostatism and walking. The results of the early mobilization group are average compared with the performance of these diverse patients. Still, we believe this reflects the reality of the future application of any early mobilization protocol in clinical practice.  

Finally, the nonblind nature of the study may have introduced bias regarding outcome definition. To estimate the impact of this potential bias, we performed ROAR, as described in the Methods. The results of ROAR support the idea that the outcome-defining decisions made by the attending neurosurgeons were not significantly biased regarding the randomization group, as the invited physicians similarly agreed with those decisions for each group. However, ROAR can only scrutinize effectively diagnosed events and does not evaluate a potential excess of underdiagnosis in one group compared with the other. Nonetheless, the lack of discrepancy regarding diagnosed events between groups indicates that the use of homogenous clinical criteria throughout the study minimized risk of bias.  

# Conclusions  

The GET-UP Trial is the first randomized clinical trial to compare two mobilization strategies after cSDH surgery. Our results suggest that early mobilization decreases medical complications, particularly infections. Additionally, early mobilization does not seem to impact the recurrence rate significantly and may ameliorate functional outcomes.  

The GET-UP Trial aims to enlighten postoperative care for patients with this prevalent and relevant pathology. Further studies, preferentially those with a multicentric design, are required to confirm our findings and to further advance neurosurgical care.
