Rationale No evidence-based acute therapies exist for intracerebral hemorrhage. Intracerebral hemorrhage growth is an important determinant of patient outcome. Tranexamic acid is known to reduce hemorrhage in other conditions.  

Aim The study aims to test the hypothesis that intracerebral hemorrhage patients selected with computed tomography angiography contrast extravasation ‘spot sign’ will have lower rates of hematoma growth when treated with intravenous tranexamic acid within 4·5-hours of stroke onset compared with placebo.  

Design The Spot sign and Tranexamic acid On Preventing ICH growth – AUStralasia Trial is a multicenter, prospective, 1:1 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, investigatorinitiated, academic Phase II trial. Intracerebral hemorrhage patients fulfilling clinical criteria (e.g. Glasgow Coma Scale ${>}7$ intracerebral hemorrhage volume ${<}70~\mathsf{m}|$ , no identified secondary cause of intracerebral hemorrhage, no thrombotic events within the previous 12 months, no planned surgery) and demonstrating contrast extravasation on computed tomography angiography will receive either intravenous tranexamic acid $19$ 10-min bolus followed by $19$ eight-hour infusion or placebo. A second computed tomography will be performed at ${24\pm3}$ hours to evaluate intracerebral hemorrhage growth and patients followed up for three-months.  

Study outcomes The primary outcome measure is presence of intracerebral hemorrhage growth by ${}_{24\pm3}$ hours, defined as either ${>}33\%$ or ${>}6~\mathsf{ml}$ increase from baseline, and will be adjusted for baseline intracerebral hemorrhage volume. Secondary outcome measures include growth as a continuous measure, thromboembolic events, and the three-month modified Rankin Scale score.  

Discussion This is the first trial to evaluate the efficacy of tranexamic acid in intracerebral hemorrhage patients selected based on an imaging biomarker of high likelihood of hematoma growth. The trial is registered as NCT01702636.

# Introduction and rationale  

The worldwide annual number of incident strokes is 18 million (1). Whereas most strokes are ischemic and can, in selected cases, be treated with thrombolytic therapy within 4·5-hours of onset (2), a significant proportion is due to rupture of an intracerebral artery leading to intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). ICH comprises $12\%$ of all strokes in high-income countries and $23\%$ in low- to middle-income countries (3), presumably due to differences in risk factors and genetics. As about $80\%$ of worldwide strokes occur in low-to-middle income countries (1), the global annual incidence of ICH is approximately 4 million, with one-month mortality rates of around $40\%$ (4). This equates to 1·5 million annual deaths worldwide, equal to 1 in 35 of all deaths (5). Although specialized multidisciplinary treatment in stroke units benefits all stroke patients (6), there are no evidence-based acute therapies specific to ICH (7–9).  

In addition to etiological subtype, the main determinants of clinical outcome in ICH are hematoma volume, age, conscious state, and infratentorial or intraventricular location (10,11). Of these, hematoma volume is the most significant factor. As with ischemic stroke, ICH is frequently a dynamic process, with growth being observed in up to two thirds of patients over the first few hours from symptom onset. This growth has been shown to be independently associated with increased mortality and morbidity, and therefore presents an intervention target (12–14).  

Recombinant activated coagulation factor VII (rFVIIa) consistently reduced hematoma growth in two large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) when administered within four hours of symptom onset (15,16). Despite benefits in the proof of concept trial, no effect on clinical outcome could be shown in the larger Phase III trial that utilized a similar trial design (16). This was presumed to be due, in part, to dilution by a large proportion of patients with no growth in the placebo group. Using CT angiography (CTA), contrast extravasation with the ‘spot sign’ is a recently described biomarker for hematoma growth (17–19), seen in $30\%$ of ICH patients imaged within six hours of onset and associated with a $61\%$ likelihood of hematoma growth (19). Two Phase II trials of rFVIIa in ICH patients using spot sign selection, namely STOP-IT and SPOTLIGHT, are underway in Canada and the United States (20,21). Although rFVIIa may well be shown in the future to be safe and effective, with a cost of around $\mathrm{AU}\S7000$ (US\$7300, €5600) foranaverage $75\mathrm{kg}$ patient (22), itislikelytobetooexpensiveindevelopingcountrieswheremostICHoccurs.Otherhemostaticagents (23) maydisplaysimilarefficacyatmuchlowercost, withtranexamicacidbeingthemostpromising (24,25).

# Summary and conclusions  

The ultimate aim of this study is to define a therapy that will reduce mortality and morbidity associated with ICH. Annually, around four million people are affected by ICH, and the condition causes 1 out of 35 deaths worldwide. There are no evidence-based treatments for ICH, and hence, an urgent unmet need exists. As hematoma growth is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in acute ICH, new safe and effective treatments to stop the hemorrhage are urgently required. Tranexamic acid may provide such a treatment, especially in patients with imaging signs of ongoing hemorrhage. Regardless of outcome, the STOP-AUST study will further our general understanding of ICH growth.
