# New Hope for Adults with Lobar Intracerebral Hemorrhage  

A. David Mendelow, Ph.D., F.R.C.S.(SN)  

The role of surgery in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage has been controversial for more than half a century. The ENRICH (Early Minimally Invasive Removal of Intracerebral Hemorrhage) trial,1 the results of which are published in this issue of the Journal, provides new evidence that surgery to remove the hematoma is effective in adult patients who undergo surgery within 24 hours after the onset of a spontaneous supratentorial lobar hemorrhage. The term “lobar” refers to a lesion superficially located in the main lobes of the brain, typically the parietal, temporal, or frontal lobes. The trial was well conducted; 300 patients were randomly assigned to either minimally invasive surgery through a small craniotomy with the use of a port with imaging guidance in addition to guideline-based medical management or to guideline-based medical management alone. The score on the utility-weighted modified Rankin scale (range, 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating better outcomes) according to patients’ assessment at 180 days was used as the measure of the primary efficacy end point. An adaptive design allowed the investigators to stop enrolling patients who had an anterior basal ganglia hemorrhage after 175 patients had been enrolled because of the futility of surgery for this group. Subsequently, only patients with a lobar hemorrhage were enrolled. The benefits of surgery in patients with lobar hemorrhage were so great that the analysis of the primary end point in the total population (including those with anterior basal ganglia hemorrhage) showed a benefit from surgery. That benefit was not apparent for patients with anterior basal ganglia hemorrhages.  

These findings offer hope for patients with lobar hemorrhage, as the results of the STICH trials (Surgical Trial in Intracerebral Haemorrhage I and $\mathrm{II})^{2,3}$ tentatively suggested; in those trials, conventional methods of evacuation of the hematoma were used at the surgeons’ discretion, but not typically by minimally invasive techniques as in the current trial. Statistical significance for the benefit of surgical evacuation was not quite reached in STICH II; however, there may have been a small but clinically relevant survival advantage among patients with spontaneous superficial intracerebral hemorrhage without intraventricular hemorrhage. The first clinical trial evidence of the benefit of surgery for intracerebral hemorrhage was published in 1989 by Auer et al.; endoscopic surgery was the technique used in that trial.4 More recently, the results of the ICES (Intraoperative Stereotactic Computed Tomography-guided Endoscopic Surgery) trial conducted by Vespa et al.5 also suggested the possibility of a benefit with endoscopic surgery that resulted in small hematoma volumes after completion of surgery. The STICH II trial indicated that the best outcomes were seen when hematoma removal was total and worst when removal was incomplete.6 The association of better outcomes with complete hematoma evacuation had been reinforced in a meta-analysis of high-quality randomized, controlled trials in which disability outcomes, as measured on the modified Rankin scale at 180 days, and hematoma volumes after completion of surgery were known.7 A post hoc analysis of two such trials suggested the same.8,9 The findings of the ENRICH trial published in this issue also suggest in subgroup analyses that were not adjusted for multiplicity that surgery with low final hematoma volumes appeared to be associated with favorable outcomes. This implies that surgery needs to be effective at removing hematomas in order to achieve a good result, a conclusion that perhaps intuitive reasoning would suggest.  

These observations were recently considered by an international consensus panel that has developed a formalized “care bundle” set of recommendations for patients with intracerebral hemorrhage.10 The criteria for surgical treatment were derived with reference to the European Stroke Organization intracerebral hemorrhage guideline (2014) and the American Heart Association–American Stroke Association intracerebral hemorrhage guideline (2022). The care bundle recommends that criteria for the consideration of neurosurgical intervention should be developed within the local neurosurgical unit so that criteria appropriate for that particular unit can be agreed on in advance. Such criteria may vary among units, depending to a large extent on available resources.  

I hope that the encouraging results from the  

ENRICH trial will be confirmed in the near future with the various ongoing trials of surgery in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage: EMINENT-ICH (Early Minimally Invasive Image Guided Endoscopic Evacuation of Intracerebral Haemorrhage; ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT05681988), DIST (Dutch Intracerebral Hemorrhage Surgery Trial; NCT05460793), INVEST (Minimally Invasive Endoscopic Surgical Treatment; NCT02661672), MIND (Minimally Invasive Hematoma Evacuation with the Artemis Neuro Evacuation Device; NCT03342664), EVACUATE (Ultra-Early, Minimally Invasive Intracerebral Haemorrhage Evacuation versus Standard Treatment; NCT04434807), and SWITCH (Swiss Trial of Decompressive Craniectomy versus Best Medical Treatment of Spontaneous Supratentorial Intracerebral Hemorrhage; NCT02258919).

# What Is a CAR T Cell?  

CAR T-cell technology has reshaped our therapeutic approach to combating hematologic cancers. It harnesses the patient’s own T cells, genetically equipping them with a CAR that overrides the native recognition of antigens by T-cell receptors and enables T cells to recognize and bind a cancer antigen on the surface of a cancer cell — and then kill the cancer cell.
