# ORIGINAL RESEARCH  

# Epistaxis Versus Nonepistaxis Bleeding in Anticoagulated Patients With Atrial Fibrillation: Results From the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 Trial  

Robert S. Semco $\circledcirc$ , BSE; Regan W. Bergmark $\circledcirc$ , MD, MPH; Sabina A. Murphy $\circledcirc$ , MPH; Abby L. Cange, BS; Martin Unverdorben $\circledcirc$ , MD, PhD; Cathy Z. L. Chen $\circledcirc$ , MD, MSc; Christian T. Ruff $\circledcirc$ , MD, MPH; Elliott M. Antman $\circledcirc$ , MD; Robert P. Giugliano $\circledcirc$ , MD, SM; Brian A. Bergmark $\circledcirc$ , MD  

BACKGROUND: Epistaxis is common with antithrombotic therapy and is often troublesome to patients, yet its frequency, severity, and outcomes are poorly characterized.  

METHODS AND RESULTS: Effective Anticoagulation with Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation–Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 48 (ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48) randomized 21 105 patients with atrial fibrillation and CHADS2 risk score ${}^{22}$ to higher-dose edoxaban regimen (60 mg daily, dose-reduced to $30~\mathrm{mg},$ , lower-dose edoxaban regimen (30mg, dose reduced to $15~\mathrm{mg}$ , daily), or warfarin. Bleeds were adjudicated using International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria. Patients with intracranial hemorrhage during follow-up were excluded; those with ${>}1$ bleeding event were categorized according to their most severe event. The safety cohort with interval censoring during drug interruption was analyzed. Proportions were compared using Pearson's chi-square test and treatment arms were compared using a Cox proportional hazards model. Among 5247 patients with a bleeding event, 1008 $(19.2\%)$ had epistaxis and 4239 $(80.8\%)$ had nonepistaxis bleeding. Epistaxis events were less severe than nonepistaxis bleeds (International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis major: $3.2\%$ versus $20.7\%$ ; clinically relevant nonmajor: $64.7\%$ versus $60.1\%$ ; minor: $32.1\%$ versus $19.2\%$ ; $P{<}0.001$ ). Permanent drug discontinuation was similar following epistaxis versus nonepistaxis bleeding in patients with major $(59.4\%$ versus $53.6\%$ ; $P{=}0.52.$ ) or clinically relevant nonmajor $(32.5\%$ versus $33.3\%$ ; $P{=}0.70,$ ) bleeding but was significantly higher in patients with minor epistaxis versus other minor bleeds $(33.3\%$ versus $23.9\%$ ; $P{=}0.001$ ). Compared with warfarin, higher-dose edoxaban regimen had similar risk of epistaxis (hazard ratio [HR], 1.09 $[95\%$ Cl, 0.95-1.26]), whereas lower-dose edoxaban regimen conferred reduced risk (HR, 0.73 $[95\%$ CI, 0.62–0.86]).  

CONCLUSIONS: Epistaxis was frequent, and despite being overall less severe than nonepistaxis bleeding, was associated with similar rates of anticoagulant discontinuation. Compared with warfarin, lower-dose edoxaban regimen reduced the risk of epistaxis by $27\%$ whereas higher-dose edoxaban regimen had no effect.

# CLINICAL PERSPECTIVE  

# What Is New?  

Epistaxis is common among patients on anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation, occurring in nearly 1 in 20 patients. •	 Although nearly all patients with epistaxis do not experience severe bleeding, they have a similar rate of anticoagulant discontinuation to patients with extracranial, nonepistaxis bleeding. The lower-dose edoxaban regimen $(30/15\mathrm{mg}$ daily) had a $27\%$ lower risk of epistaxis compared with warfarin.  

# What Are the Clinical Implications?  

Clinicians prescribing anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation must recognize epistaxis as an important adverse event for patients, prepare patients for management of further epistaxis bleeds, and help patients navigate the decision to maintain or modify their anticoagulant regimen.

Despite the frequency of epistaxis in clinical practice and the potential for disruption in medications, these types of bleeding events have received little attention in cardiovascular trials of antithrombotic therapies.11–13 In the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 (Effective Anticoagulation with Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation– Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 48) trial, edoxaban was shown to significantly reduce the risk of multiple types and severities of nongastrointestinal bleeding events relative to warfarin, but the effect of edoxaban on epistaxis has not been explored.14 Here we describe the frequency, severity, and clinical outcomes of epistaxis in anticoagulated patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) randomized to edoxaban or warfarin.15

# ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 Trial Design  

The ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study conducted in 46 countries.15,16 A total of 21 105 patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to a higher-dose edoxaban regimen (HDER), lower-dose edoxaban regimen (LDER), or warfarin (median time-in-therapeutic-range $68.4\%)$ . Eligible patients were adults aged 21 years or older with AF and moderate to high risk of stroke, defined by CHADS2 score ${}^{22}$ . Patients with high risk of bleeding were excluded, including those with a hemorrhagic disorder; a history of intracranial, intraocular, spinal, retroperitoneal, or intraarticular bleeding; gastrointestinal bleeding or gastroduodenal ulcer in the previous year; severe trauma or major surgery in the previous 10 days; uncontrolled hypertension >170/100 mm Hg; dual-antiplatelet therapy or therapy with any medication that increases risk of bleeding; active liver disease or persistently elevated liver enzymes or bilirubin; severe renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance <30 mL/min); hemoglobin $<10\mathsf{g}/\mathsf{dL}$ ; or platelet count <100 000 cells/μL (for microliter). Patients with contraindications to anticoagulants, moderate to severe mitral stenosis, or a mechanical heart valve were also excluded.16  

Patients were randomized using a central computerized response system, with randomization stratified CHADS2 score (2–3 versus 4–6) and qualification for edoxaban dose reduction. Patients assigned to HDER received 60 mg of edoxaban, those assigned to LDER received 30 mg of edoxaban, and those assigned to the warfarin regimen were dose adjusted to achieve an international normalized ratio of 2.0 to 3.0. HDER and LDER doses were reduced by $50\%$ at randomization or at any point during the study for patients with an estimated creatinine clearance of $50~\mathsf{mL}$ per minute or less, a body weight of $60\mathsf{kg}$ or less, or concomitant use of verapamil, quinidine, or dronedarone.  

The primary efficacy end point of the ENGAGE AFTIMI 48 trial was the time to first ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke or systemic embolic event. The principal safety end point was major bleeding during treatment as defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria. All deaths and suspected cerebrovascular, systemic embolic, and bleeding events were adjudicated by an independent clinical end point committee (CEC) whose members were blinded to randomized treatment allocation. As has been previously described, an ISTH scale was used in which major bleeding was defined as a hemoglobin fall of at least 2 g/dL, transfusion of ${>}2$ units of packed red blood cells, symptomatic bleeding in a critical area, or a fatal bleeding event.14–16 Clinically relevant nonmajor (CRNM) bleeding was defined as an event requiring or prolonging hospitalization or resulting in laboratory testing, imaging, compression, a procedure, interruption of the study medication, or a change in concomitant therapies. Minor bleeding was overt bleeding that did not meet criteria for CRNM or major bleeding. Life-threatening bleeds were defined as nonfatal major bleeds that were either intracranial or resulted in hemodynamic compromise that required intervention. Only 1 patient was lost to follow-up.15  

# Statistical Analysis  

Patients in the on-treatment safety cohort, which was used for the primary results of the ENGAGE AF-TIMl 48 trial, were included in the present analyses. This cohort consisted of patients who received 1 or more doses of edoxaban or warfarin, with events included in the analysis if they occurred while the patient was on treatment, defined as up to 3 days after their last dose.15 Interval censoring was used for the at-risk period for study drug interruptions that lasted ${>}3$ days in the analysis of bleeding.  

Two cohorts were retrospectively defined based on the location of bleeding events: an epistaxis cohort, which included all patients who had at least 1 CEC-adjudicated epistaxis event, and a nonepistaxis cohort, which included all patients who had a CECadjudicated bleeding event but no epistaxis events. Patients with an epistaxis event as well as a nonepistaxis bleeding event were analyzed in the epistaxis group. Given the relative severity of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) compared with most other bleeding events (all are considered major by definition; fatality rate was $45.7\%)^{17}$ and the protocol-­mandated permanent discontinuation of the study drug after ICH, patients with ICH during the study were excluded from this analysis, though a sensitivity analysis was performed including patients with ICH. Patients were then classified according to their most severe bleeding event, where patients in the epistaxis cohort were classified according to their most severe epistaxis event and patients in the nonepistaxis cohort were classified according to their most severe non-epistaxis bleeding event.  

Bleeding event severity for the 2 cohorts was compared using a Cochrane–Armitage test for trend. Permanent drug discontinuation and baseline characteristics were compared using a Pearson's chi-square test. Characteristics of patients with epistaxis bleeding, nonepistaxis bleeding, and no bleeding were compared using a multivariable Cox model that included creatinine clearance ${\le}50$ mL/min, age ${}^{275}$ years, vitamin $\mathsf{K}$ antagonist (VKA) naïve, prior coronary artery disease (CAD), prior non-ICH bleeding, prior epistaxis, alcohol use, current smoking, weight ${\le}60\mathsf{kg}$ , region, antiplatelet use at randomization, dose reduction at baseline, and randomized treatment group.  

Time to first epistaxis bleeding was compared between treatment arms using a Cox proportional hazards model. First plus recurrent epistaxis events were compared between treatment arms using a negative binomial model accounting for exposure time. Annualized event rates for all-cause deaths and adverse cardiac events were analyzed in patients with epistaxis bleeds, patients with nonepistaxis bleeds, and patients with no bleeding events during the trial. Adverse cardiac events included cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, or systemic embolic events.  

Statistical analyses were performed by the TIMI Study Group using Stata/SE version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). All tests were 2 sided with a $P$ value $<0.05$ considered statistically significant. There was no adjustment for multiple comparisons in this post hoc analysis. All subjects provided written informed consent. The protocol was approved by the ethics committee at each participating center.  

# RESULTS  

Among 20 792 patients (excluding 234 patients with ICH, who per protocol were mandated to stop the study drug) in the on-treatment safety cohort, there were 5247 $(25.2\%)$ patients with at least 1 bleeding event, including 1008 $(4.8\%)$ with epistaxis and 4239 $(20.4\%)$ with exclusively nonepistaxis bleeding. There were 399 patients who had both an epistaxis bleed and a nonepistaxis bleed of any severity. Baseline characteristics were similar between patients with epistaxis versus nonepistaxis bleeding, with the following exceptions: patients with epistaxis had higher rates of prior epistaxis and prior CAD at baseline, were less likely to be underweight, had a slightly higher creatinine clearance, and were less likely to be VKA naïve (Table). Furthermore, baseline characteristics were similar between patients who had only epistaxis bleeds and those who had both epistaxis and nonepistaxis bleeds, with notable differences being patients with both epistaxis and nonepistaxis bleeds were slightly older, more frequently had CAD, and had higher baseline bleeding risk scores (Table S1).  

After multivariable adjustment, the baseline characteristics associated with more epistaxis bleeding were age ${}^{275}$ years (hazard ratio [HR], 1.35 $[95\%$ CI, 1.18–1.55]), prior CAD (HR, 1.19 $[95\%$ CI, 1.05–1.36]), prior epistaxis (HR, 3.10 $[95\%$ CI, 2.37–4.05]), any prior bleeding (HR, 1.77 $[95\%$ CI, 1.43–2.20]), antiplatelet use at randomization (HR, 1.37 $[95\%$ CI, 1.19–1.57]), VKA experience (HR, 1.20 $[95\%$ CI, 1.04–1.38]), nonsmoking status (HR, 1.72 $[95\%$ CI, 1.28–2.31]), alcohol use (HR, 1.18 $[95\%$ CI, 1.04–1.35]), and North American region (HR, 1.82 $[95\%$ , CI 1.58–2.10]).

# Severity of Epistaxis  

Epistaxis events were overall less severe than nonepistaxis bleeds (ISTH major: $3.2\%$ of epistaxis versus  

$20.7\%$ of nonepistaxis bleeding; CRNM: $64.7\%$ versus $60.1\%$ ; minor: $32.1\%$ versus $19.2\%$ ; $P{<}0.001$ ; Figure  1). There were no fatal epistaxis bleeds, whereas 31 of the patients who had a nonepistaxis bleed had a fatal bleed $(0\%$ versus $0.73\%$ , $P{=}0.006,$ . Two patients in the epistaxis cohort experienced a life-threatening bleed and 91 patients in the cohort without epistaxis experienced a life-threatening or fatal bleed $(0.20\%$ versus $2.15\%$ , $P{<}0.001$ ). The severity of first epistaxis bleeds were similar to the severity of subsequent epistaxis bleeds (major: $3.2\%$ of first events versus $2.8\%$ of subsequent events, CRNM: $64.7\%$ versus $60.3\%$ , minor: $32.1\%$ versus $37.0\%$ .

# Study Drug Discontinuation  

The frequency of study drug discontinuation was overall similar between cohorts, where $33.6\%$ of patients with epistaxis and $35.7\%$ of those with nonepistaxis bleeding permanently discontinued the study drug $(P{=}0.21)$ ), compared with $33.5\%$ of patients without any bleeding events at all (epistaxis versus no bleeding events: $P{=}0.94$ ). Within the cohort with epistaxis, discontinuation was similar between those with only epistaxis bleeds and those with both epistaxis and nonepistaxis bleeds $(35.1\%$ versus $31.3\%$ , $P{=}0.21$ ). Furthermore, patients with only 1 epistaxis event discontinued the drug at similar rates to those who had multiple epistaxis events $(33.7\%$ versus $33.6\%$ , $\scriptstyleP=0.99,$ ).  

When patients were divided into subsets by bleeding severity, study drug discontinuation was similar following epistaxis versus nonepistaxis bleeding in patients with ISTH major $(59.4\%$ versus $53.6\%$ ; $P{=}0.52,$ ) or CRNM bleeding $(32.5\%$ versus $33.3\%$ ; $P{=}0.70\$ ), but was significantly higher after minor epistaxis versus minor nonepistaxis bleeding $(33.3\%$ versus $23.9\%$ ; $P{=}0.001$ ; Figure 2). These results were similar in a sensitivity analysis including ICH (Table  S2). When analyzing rate of permanent discontinuation restricted to within 30 days after major or CRNM bleeds, $11.4\%$ of patients with such epistaxis bleeds discontinued the study drug, compared with $15.7\%$ of patients with nonepistaxis bleeds of the same severity.  

Finally, there were some differences in baseline characteristics between patients with major bleeding who did or did not permanently discontinue the study drug, where those who permanently discontinued the drug were older (median [interquartile range], years: 76 [70–80] versus 73 [66–78], $P{<}0.001$ ), had poorer renal function $(27.8\%$ versus $17.1\%$ had creatinine clearance ${\le}50$ mL/min, $P{<}0.001$ ), had higher bleeding risk $(66.3\%$ versus $56.0\%$ had hypertension, abnormal renal/liver function, stroke, bleeding history or predisposition, labile international normalized ratio, elderly, drugs/alcohol concomitantly score ${\ge}3$ , $P{=}0.001$ ) and more frequently had CAD (4 $1.2\%$ versus $32.6\%$ , $P{=}0.007$ ) (Table S3).  

# Outcomes after Bleeding Events  

The annual rates of death, ischemic stroke, and major bleeding events after epistaxis were similar to the rates following nonepistaxis bleeding. Among patients who experienced epistaxis, there were 94 subsequent deaths ( $5.2\%$ /year), 20 ischemic strokes ( $1.1\%$ /year), and 53 major bleeding events ( $3.9\%$ /year). Meanwhile, after a nonepistaxis bleeding event, the corresponding annualized event rates were $6.6\%$ /year, $1.6\%$ /year, and $4.6\%$ /year, respectively.  

# Treatment Effect  

Among patients randomized to warfarin, 354 $(5.1\%)$ had epistaxis of any severity, as did 387 $(5.6\%)$ of patients on HDER and 267 $(3.8\%)$ of patients on LDER. # DISCUSSION  

In this analysis from the ENGAGE AF-TIMl 48 trial, we found that epistaxis was common and often recurrent in patients with AF on anticoagulation and was associated with anticoagulant discontinuation. Epistaxis was experienced by $4.8\%$ of patients, accounting for nearly 1 in 5 patients with a non-lCH bleeding event. One third of patients with epistaxis permanently discontinued anticoagulant medication, and though this was not significantly different from patients with nonepistaxis bleeding or from patients with no bleeding events, $11.4\%$ of patients with a major or CRNM epistaxis event discontinued the study drug within 30 days of their bleed. Furthermore, although the rate of permanent study drug discontinuation was similar between major epistaxis and nonepistaxis bleeds and additionally between CRNM epistaxis and nonepistaxis bleeds, it was higher after minor epistaxis compared with minor nonepistaxis bleeding. This suggests that these minor epistaxis events may be more important or concerning to patients than minor bleeding at other sites.  

Though epistaxis has received relatively little attention in cardiovascular studies, there are some data on the frequency of epistaxis in other trials comparing direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) to warfarin.In the ROCKET-AF (Rivaroxaban Once Daily Oral Direct Factor Xa Inhibition Compared With Vitamin K Antagonism for Prevention of Stroke and Embolism Trial in Atrial Fibrillation) trial comparing rivaroxaban to warfarin in patients with AF, site-reported epistaxis occurred in $9.3\%$ of patients,11 and in the ARISTOTLE (Apixaban for the Prevention of Stroke in Subjects With Atrial Fibrillation) trial, $6.9\%$ of patients experienced epistaxis.18 To our knowledge there are no other randomized trials reporting rates of antithrombotic therapy discontinuation following epistaxis. However, a postmarketing surveillance study of edoxaban in Japan reported that $0.9\%$ of 6306 patients reported epistaxis over a mean follow-up time of 17.4 months, which is markedly lower than the incidence of epistaxis in this study and the other randomized clinical trials described here.19 This is likely due to the absence of systematic reporting of all bleeding events aS waS required in ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48, differenceS in patient population, shorter follow-up time, and the potential for misclassification of events in the observational postmarket study.  

DOACs are known to reduce bleeding relative to warfarin overall,20 but their effects on epistaxis specifically remain unclear. In ROCKET-AF, which compared bleeding rates based on the first bleeding event to occur for each patient in the safety cohort, sitereported epistaxis was more common among those randomized to rivaroxaban than warfarin $(6.9\%$ versus $5.7\%$ , $P{<}0.001$ ), but there was no difference in adjudicated major or CRNM bleeding events due to epistaxis.21 In the RE-LY (Randomized Evaluation of Long Term Anticoagulant Therapy With Dabigatran Etexilate) trial, major ear, nose, and throat bleeding was less common with $110\mathrm{mg}$ twice per day dabigatran than warfarin $(0.06\%)$ /year versus $0.15\%$ /year), though there was no difference between 150 mg twice per day dabigatran and warfarin.22 In the ARISTOTLE trial, $6.2\%$ of patients randomized to apixaban experienced epistaxis, compared with $7.6\%$ of those on warfarin.18 An analysis of first major bleeds in the ARISTOTLE trial found similar rates of major epistaxis among patients on apixaban and those on warfarin, though there were only 23 events of epistaxis that were first major bleeds.23  

In the present analysis, we found that HDER neither increased nor decreased risk for first epistaxis events overall, whereas LDER reduced epistaxis by $27\%$ compared with warfarin; this parallels the $33\%$ reduction with LDER on nonepistaxis bleeds. HDER had no effect on the rates of major or CRNM epistaxis events, whereas LDER reduced these events by $30\%$ . Finally, both edoxaban regimens had numerically lower rates of major epistaxis compared with warfarin, though these events were rare and the differences were not statistically significant.  

Whether use of a DOAC rather than warfarin might influence the anatomical location of bleeding observed in patients with epistaxis (anterior versus posterior bleeding) is beyond the granularity of data available here. However, in a meta-analysis of observational studies examining patients admitted to the hospital with epistaxis, use of a DOAC rather than a VKA was associated with a lower rate of posterior bleeding (odds ratio [OR], 0.39 $[95\%$ CI, 0.23–0.68]), which is generally more severe, difficult to control, and less common than anterior epistaxis.6,24–29 Indeed, it was also shown that DOACs were associated with shorter lengths of stay when compared with VKAs (standardized mean difference $-0.22$ days $[95\%$ CI, $-0.42$ to $-0.02]$ ).24–28,30  

The findings of this analysis have 2 broad implications. First, there is a need for increased attention to epistaxis in studies of antithrombotic therapies. This analysis suggests that these events, although less likely than bleeding at other anatomic sites to qualify as “major” according to adjudication criteria, recur in nearly 1 in 5 patients and appear to be quite important to patients with a significant impact on treatment adherence. Indeed, minor bleeding events have been previously shown to be associated with increased rates of oral anticoagulant discontinuation,31 bleeding is commonly given as the reason for discontinuing oral coagulation,32 and epistaxis itself has been previously shown to be a leading contributor to antiplatelet discontinuation.33  

Second, there is an opportunity for clinicians to educate patients on how to prevent and care for epistaxis, explain that epistaxis is rarely life threatening, and emphasize how interruption of anticoagulation therapy for patients with AF substantially increases risk of major cardiac and cerebrovascular events.34 As an example, one educational intervention at an academic medical center taught patients how to prevent epistaxis using moisturizing sprays and dehumidifiers, how to distinguish epistaxis that could likely be managed at home versus more severe epistaxis (persisting longer than 30 minutes, pouring down throat and nose, associated with high blood pressure, fast heart rate, chest pain, or lightheadedness), how to manage minor epistaxis at home (remain calm, lean forward, apply pressure, and consider use of oxymetazoline spray) and when to contact their anticoagulation provider.35 In a pre- and postimplementation analysis, the intervention was shown to reduce the risk of visiting the emergency department for epistaxis by $48\%$ (risk ratio [RR], 0.52 $[95\%$ CI, 0.32–0.84]).35  

# Strengths and Novel Aspects of this Analysis  

The strengths of this analysis include the systematic reporting of bleeding events, monitor oversight to minimize underreporting, and the blinded adjudication of events by an independent, centralized committee who were unaware of randomized treatment assignment. The ENGAGE AF-TIMl 48 trial was the largest and longest phase III trial comparing a DOAC with warfarin in patients with AF, following 21 105 participants over a median follow-up of 2.8years thus yielding a relatively large number of epistaxis events relative to prior studies. Additionally, the estimated time in therapeutic range for the warfarin group was $68.4\%$ , which was the highest of any of the large DOAC versus warfarin trials and substantial greater than generally has been reported in clinical practice.15 To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive analysis of a phase 3 trial of a DOAC to report the severity profile of epistaxis among patients, to compare the rates and severity of epistaxis between study arms, to report rates of drug discontinuation following epistaxis, and to describe the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events and mortality following epistaxis.  

# Limitations  

There are several important limitations to note. First, this was not a prespecified analysis and the trial was not powered to show treatment differences; therefore, the findings should be considered hypothesisgenerating.36 Second, a single event of epistaxis was sufficient to assign a participant to the epistaxis cohort, which does not account for the impact of nonepistaxis bleeding events on these participants’ decision to discontinue their medication. However, only $7.6\%$ of those who had any bleeding events had both epistaxis and nonepistaxis bleeding. Third, participants were categorized according to the severity of their most severe bleeding event  within  their  cohort-specific bleeding type. Finally, more granular data concerning the epistaxis events and treatment were not captured. Future studies could include the specific interventions performed, such as anterior versus posterior packing, electrical or chemical cautery, surgical intervention, arterial ligation or embolization, as well as the type and setting of care required for treatment to better characterize the nature and clinical consequences of the epistaxis events.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

In this large study of patients with AF randomized to edoxaban or warfarin, epistaxis was common and was associated with anticoagulant discontinuation in 1 in 3 patients despite overall lower severity compared with nonepistaxis bleeding. Compared with warfarin, LDER significantly reduced the risk of epistaxis overall by $27\%$ , whereas HDER had similar rates to warfarin. This analysis suggests that among patients with AF treated with an oral anticoagulant, epistaxis is symptomatically important, frequently recurs, may cause disproportionate interruption in antithrombotic therapy, and deserves increased attention in cardiovascular studies.
