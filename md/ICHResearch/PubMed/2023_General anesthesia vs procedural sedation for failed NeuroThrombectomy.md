# Abstract  

Background  There is little data available to guide optimal anesthesia management during rescue intracranial angioplasty and stenting (ICAS) for failed mechanical thrombectomy (MT). We sought to compare the procedural safety and functional outcomes of patients undergoing rescue ICAS for failed MT under general anesthesia (GA) vs non-general anesthesia (non-GA).  

Methods  We searched the data from the Stenting and Angioplasty In Neuro Thrombectomy (SAINT) study. In our review we included patients if they had anterior circulation large vessel occlusion strokes due to intracranial internal carotid artery (ICA) or middle cerebral artery (MCA-­M1/M2) segments, failed MT, and underwent rescue ICAS. The cohort was divided into two groups: GA and non-GA. We used propensity score matching to balance the two groups. The primary outcome was the shift in the degree of disability as measured by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included functional independence (90-day mRS0-2) and successful reperfusion defined as mTICI2B-3. Safety measures included symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) and 90-day mortality. Results  Among 253 patients who underwent rescue ICAS, 156 qualified for the matching analysis at a 1:1 ratio. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were balanced between both groups. Non-GA patients had comparable outcomes to GA patients both in terms of the overall degree of disability (mRS ordinal shift; adjusted common odds ratio 1.29, $95\%$ CI [0.69 to 2.43], $\mathsf{P{=}}0.43\$ and rates of functional independence $(33.3\%$ vs $28.6\%.$ , adjusted odds ratio 1.32, $95\%$ CI [0.51 to 3.41], $\mathsf{P}{=}0.56,$ ) at 90 days. Likewise, there were no significant differences in rates of successful reperfusion, sICH, procedural complications or 90-day mortality among both groups. # Introduction  

anesthesia strategy for patients undergoing rescue ICAS  

Previous studies have suggested improved functional outcomes after rescue stenting for failed mechanical thrombectomy (MT) in large vessel occlusion strokes (LVO).1–4 The optimal anesthesia choice during MT remains controversial. An individual-patient level meta-analysis from seven randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing MT with best medical therapy has suggested worse outcomes after MT with general anesthesia (GA) compared with conscious sedation (Cs). Conversely, a meta-analysis of three single center RCTs specifically comparing anesthesia modality in MT (ie, GA vs CS) has shown that GA was significantly associated with less disability at 90-days.° Notably, these studies were limited to patients undergoing standard MT without rescue intracranial angioplasty and stenting (ICAS), and functional outcomes were among several secondary endpoints. ICAS is considered a more complex and risky procedure than MT alone and has typically been performed under GA.7 8 The potential advantages of CS are clinical monitoring and more stable hemodynamics. However, it carries the risk of procedural complications secondary to patient movement, especially in patients with altered mentation due to stroke. On the other hand, GA has the advantage of patient immobilization and airway protection, but it might delay initiation of the procedure and is more prone to cause disturbances in cerebral hemodynamics. Intraprocedural drop in blood pressure might be an even more relevant problem in cases of MT failure given the inherent reperfusion delay and the high prevalence of intracranial atherosclerosis in this population.9 10  

Currently, there are no data to guide optimal anesthetic management in rescue ICAS during MT. Our study aims to compare the procedural safety and functional outcomes of patients undergoing rescue ICAS for failed anterior circulation LVO MT under GA vs non-GA.  

# Methods  

# Study population  

Data regarding anesthesia management during rescue stenting for failed stroke thrombectomy was derived from the Stenting and Angioplasty In NeuroThrombectomy (SAINT) study. 11 We reviewed patients’ charts from 14 comprehensive stroke centers in the United States (US) dating from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2020. Consecutive adult patients were included if they had anterior circulation LVO due to intracranial internal carotid artery (ICA) and proximal middle cerebral artery (MCA-­M1 and/ or M2- segments), failed MT, defined as modified thrombolysis in cerebral infarction (mTICI) 0–1 after multiple attempts to clot retrieval with stent-retriever and/or aspiration catheters, and underwent rescue ICAS.  

We divided the cohort into two groups, GA and non-GA. Patients who converted to GA during the procedure were included in the non-GA arm based on the intention to treat principle.  

# Treatments and rescue ICAS procedure  

All patients were administered intravenous thrombolysis if they presented within the first 4.5 hours of symptom onset in the absence of contraindications. The decision to perform MT was based on multidisciplinary discussions between the vascular neurology and neuroendovascular teams in every participating center. The choice and the method of the anesthetic strategy followed individual site protocols and their operators’ preferences. The decision to perform rescue ICAS as well as the type of stent (balloon mounted stent (BMS) vs self- expandable stent (SES)) was at the operators’ discretion.  

# Anesthesia protocol  

Among the 14 centers included in the study, the GA was the default modality in four centers. The remaining centers used the non-GA (monitored anesthesia care (MAC), $\scriptstyle\mathtt{n}{=}6$ and CS, $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n}=4$ ) as the default for all patients who were non-intubated on arrival at hospital with GA reserved for patients who presented with acute respiratory distress and/or hypoxemia, active vomiting, uncontrollable cough, severe and refractory agitations as well as those who were unable to protect their airways. In the majority of centers where non-GA was the default modality, the anesthesia care team included an attending anesthesiologist supervising a nurse anesthetist or anesthesiologist’s assistant. In three centers where CS was the default modality, there was no anesthesia team, the angiosuite nursing team was welltrained to manage sedated patients. Moreover, the anesthesia staff was available when needed. There were no formal protocols specifying anesthetic/sedating agents or blood pressure targets.  

# Outcome measures  

The primary outcome was the ordinal shift in the degree of disability as measured by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 90 days (which was reported by an experienced nurse practitioner/ clinician either in person or through a telephone interview). The mRS is a 7-point scale which ranges from 0 to 6, with a score of 0 indicating no symptoms, higher scores indicating more disability, and a score of 6 indicating death. Secondary outcomes included functional independence defined as 90-day $\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2$ and successful reperfusion defined as mTICI score of 2B-3 at the end of the procedure as reported by the operators. Safety measures included rates of procedural complications, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) which was defined as parenchymal hematoma leading to neurologic deterioration, as reflected by the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score worsening of ${\geq}4$ points, and 90-day mortality. The institutional review board in each contributing center approved the study.  

# Matching procedure  

We used propensity score matching to balance the two groups at a 1:1 ratio, without replacement, by the nearest neighbor algorithm with a caliper width equal to 0.2. Propensity scores were calculated for each patient based on the probability for undergoing GA using a multivariable logistic regression model including baseline NIHSS score and the presence of a tandem lesion.  

# Subgroup analyses  

We tested the impact of the anesthesia modality on functional independence and successful reperfusion in different subgroups of variables in the matched cohort. These variables included age $(\leq70\mathrm{vs}>70$ years old), pre-procedure use of intravenous thrombolysis, the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS, $\leq7\mathrm{vs}>7.$ ), last known well (LKW) to puncture time $\stackrel{\prime}{=}6$ hours $\mathbf{vs}{>}6$ hours), baseline NIHSS score $(<15\mathrm{vs}\ge15\$ ), type of stent (BMS vs SES), and site of occlusion (ICA vs MCA-­M1/M2 segments).  

# Sensitivity analysis  

We compared the baseline characteristics and outcomes between the GA and non-GA groups using an as-treated analysis (where converted patients were assigned to the GA group).  

# Statistical analysis  

Continuous variables were expressed as mean $\pm$ standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) according to the normality of distributions as assessed by the Shapiro-­Wilk test, and compared with the Mann-Whitney U test or Student t-test as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and were compared using Pearson $\mathrm{X}^{2}$ , Fisher exact, or the McNemar test for discordant pairs as appropriate. To evaluate the association of non-GA with the outcome, all variables with $\mathrm{P}{<}0.10$ in the univariate analysis related to the outcome of interest were added to the model. The model’s goodness of fit was assessed by a Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Similarly, ordinal regression analysis was performed to identify the association of GA with 1-point mRS improvement using the same variables in the model for functional independence. The effect size of anesthesia modality on functional independence (adjusted for age, baseline NIHSS score, initial blood glucose levels) and successful reperfusion (adjusted for volume of stroke center, stroke etiology and type of stent) was analyzed according to binary logistic regression across different subgroups. Significance was set at $\mathrm{P}{<}0.05$ , and all P values were 2-sided. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27 software (IBM Armonk, NY, USA) and the University Edition of SAS statistical software (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA).  

# Results  

A total of 253 patients with failed MT underwent rescue ICAS during the study period (online supplemental figure 1). For the overall cohort, the GA group $\mathrm{(n}\mathrm{=}80\$ ) had a lower frequency of intracranial atherosclerosis disease as stroke etiology $(38.8\%$ vs $58.4\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.007,$ ), higher proportions of tandem lesion $(13.8\%$ vs $5.8\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.03$ ), and higher mean baseline NIHSS scores (16 vs 14, $\mathrm{P}{=}0.005$ ) compared with the non-GA group $(\mathrm{n}{=}173)$ . The GA group also had higher rates of intraprocedural intraarterial tissue plasminogen activator (IA-tPA) $10\%$ vs $1.7\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.003^{\cdot}$ ） and IV-Aggrastat $(33.8\%$ vs $17.34\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.004^{\cdot}$ ) use compared with the non-GA group. Other baseline demographic clinical and procedural characteristics were comparable among both groups (online supplemental table 1).  

Eleven patients $(6.4\%)$ were converted to GA during the procedure secondary to agitation $(\mathrm{n}{=}3)$ , airway protection $(\mathrm{n}{=}3)$ ), occurrence of complications/clinical deterioration $(\mathrm{n}{=}2)$ , decision made to perform ICAS $(\mathrm{n}{=}1)$ ) and unknown reasons $(\mathrm{n}{=}2)$ ).  

# Outcome measures in the overall population  

The overall degree of disability at 90 days was comparable between the non-GA and the GA groups (online supplemental figure 2) (mRS ordinal shift, adjusted common OR 1.42, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.78 to 2.59], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.26)$ . Similarly, there were no significant differences in the rates of successful reperfusion $(88.4\%$ vs $78.8\%$ , adjusted l odds ratio (aOR) 1.49, $95\%$ CI [0.64 to 3.46], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.36)$ and functional independence $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2\$ at 90-days: $38.2\%$ vs $27.7\%$ , aOR 1.61, $95\%$ CI [0.69 to 3.73], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.27;$ across the two groups. In terms of safety, non-GA had similar rates of procedural complications $1.7\%$ vs $1.3\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{>}0.99\$ ), sICH $(6.9\%$ vs $7.5\%$ , aOR 0.55, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.17 to 1.81], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.33$ ), and 90-day mortality $(22.5\%$ vs $33.3\%$ , aOR 0.91, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.42 to 1.96], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.81$ ) compared with the GA group. The predictors of clinical outcomes and safety measures in the overall population are shown in online supplemental tables 2 and 3.  

# Outcome measures in the matched cohort  

A total of 156 patients (78 matched pairs) qualified for the matched analysis. Baseline demographic, clinical and procedural characteristics were well balanced among both groups except for higher utilization rates of intraprocedural IA-tPA ( $10.3\%$ vs $0\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.006)$ and IV-Aggrastat $(33.3\%$ vs $14.1\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.005^{\mathrm{}}$ ) in the GA compared with the non-GA group (table 1).  

As observed in the overall cohort, the outcome measures were comparable between the non-GA and GA groups as follows: overall disability at 90 days (mRS ordinal shift, adjusted common OR 1.29, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.69 to 2.43], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.43\$ ) (figure  1), rates of functional independence at 90 days ( $33.3\%$ vs $28.6\%$ , aOR 1.32, $95\%$ CI [0.51 to 3.41], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.56,$ ), successful reperfusion $(85.9\%$ vs $78.2\%$ , aOR 1.33, $95\%$ CI [0.50 to 3.58], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.57$ ), sICH $(7.7\%$ vs $7.7\%$ , aOR 0.57, $95\%$ CI [0.11 to 3.00], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.50\$ ), and 90-day mortality ( $23\%$ vs $32.9\%$ , aOR 0.80, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.32 to 2.05], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.65$ ) (table 2).  

# Subgroup analysis  

There was no evidence of heterogeneity in the effect of anesthesia modality across any of the pre-specified subgroups in terms of either successful reperfusion (figure 2A) or functional independence at 90 days (figure 2B).  

# Sensitivity analysis  

The results of the as-treated analysis were similar to the intention to treat analysis. Specifically, the overall degree of disability at 90 days was comparable between the non-GA and the GA groups (mRS ordinal shift, adjusted common OR 1.61, $95\%$ CI [0.92 to 2.82], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.10\$ ). Likewise, secondary outcomes and safety measures were comparable between both groups (online supplemental tables 4 and 5).  

Notably, among 11 patients who were converted to GA, nine patients $(81.8\%)$ had successful reperfusion at the end of the procedure, and there were no reported cases of sICH. Additionally, five patients $(45.5\%)$ had functional independence (90-day mRS0-2), six patients $(54.5\%)$ had 90-day mRS0-3 and 90-day mortality was reported in two patients $(18.2\%)$ .  

# Discussion  

Our study suggests that non-GA seems to be as safe and effective as GA in failed MT patients requiring rescue ICAS. This is an important finding since there is a consensus toward the adoption of GA for more complex neuroendovascular interventions, including ICAS, aneurysm treatment, and arteriovenous malformation embolization. During these interventions, it is essential to acquire a high-quality roadmap to accurately navigate and deploy devices to the intended targets. The ability to fully paralyze the patient also averts the risks associated with lesion access in case of unexpected patient motion. Indeed, previous studies have reported that, as compared with GA, local anesthesia is associated with significantly higher rates of intraoperative aneurysm rupture ( $(12.9\%$ vs $2.5{-}5\%\rangle$ ).12 In this context, and given the relatively high procedural complexity of ICAS, previous RCTs have protocolized GA to ensure procedural safety.7 8  

On the other hand, CS may still be a reasonable option for properly selected patients undergoing aneurysm treatment or ICAS. In fact, a prior study supported the safety and feasibility of CS with local anesthetics for endovascular treatment of ruptured intracranial aneurysms in patients with low-grade subarachnoid hemorrhage (symptomatic procedural complication rate: $2.4\%$ with CS group vs $2.7\%$ with GA, $\mathrm{P}{=}0.89$ ). Interestingly among the 14 interventions with intraprocedural perforation, 11 were performed under GA $(9.8\%)$ while 3 $\left(3.8\%\right)$ were performed under CS.13 Additionally, previous studies suggested the feasibility of elective intracranial stenting under CS.14 15 The present study shows comparable safety between non-GA and GA in terms of procedural complications ( $1.7\%$ vs $1.3\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{>}0.99$ in the overall cohort and $2.6\%$ vs $1.3\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{>}0.99$ in the matched cohort), sICH ( $6.9\%$ vs $7.5\%$ , aOR 0.55, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.17 to 1.81], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.33$ in the overall cohort, and $7.7\%$ vs $7.7\%$ , aOR 0.57, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.11 to 3.00], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.50$ in the matched cohort) and 90-day mortality.

In our study, 11 out of 173 patients $(6.4\%)$ converted to GA of whom three patients $(1.7\%)$ were secondary to agitation or patient movement. This rate is comparable to a previously reported rate of $1.6\%$ , with patient agitation being the most common reason.16 Several factors could cause patient movement and poor cooperation, for instance discomfort at the puncture site, a hot sensation due to injection of contrast material into the cerebral arteries and the pain associated with the traction of the blood vessels during catheter navigation, balloon inflation or stent deployment. The implications of these factors become more evident in acute stroke patients with cortical signs such as neglect.17 It is worth mentioning that the need for GA to assure patient immobility should not prolong time to reperfusion. In the HERMES collaboration, GA was associated with a delay in reperfusion.5 Conversely, in the three RCTs,18–20 the delay to start the procedure in patients who had GA $(\sim6\mathrm{min})$ appeared to be counterbalanced by the shorter procedural time $-19$ (-31 to -4) min.6 Our results show no significant difference in procedure duration between GA and non-GA (78 vs $73\mathrm{min}$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.24$ in the overall cohort and 74 vs $79.5\mathrm{min}$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.78$ in the matched cohort). That said, not all non-GA modalities are similar and differences in performance might exist across local anesthesia only, conscious sedation, and MAC where deep sedation can be achieved without the need for intubation.21 Also, it is worth mentioning the importance of the presence of the anesthesia team during the procedure for proper and safe management of patients with fast conversion to GA if needed.

The optimal anesthesia modality in MT remains controversial with the current evidence favoring individualization in care rather than the prescription of any given modality. The HERMES meta-analysis suggested that patients receiving GA have worse outcomes compared with patients receiving CS. However, since patients were not randomized to GA vs CS, the anesthesia modality followed local institutional protocols which might have resulted in selection bias where patients who were more severely ill may have been more likely to receive GA. In addition, it has been suggested that there are factors inherent to the GA technique itself, such as hypotension, hyperventilation, and impaired cerebral autoregulation, all of which may affect collateral circulation of the penumbra.22 23 On the contrary, a meta-analysis of three single-center trials evaluating the impact of anesthesia modality on MT outcomes reported better outcomes in terms of 90-day mRS shift (cOR 1.58, $95\%$ CI [1.09 to 2.29], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.02\$ ) and higher rates of functional independence 90-day $\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2$ $(49.2\%$ vs $35.1\%$ , aOR 2.16, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [1.31 to 3.54], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.003^{\cdot}$ ) with GA compared with procedural sedation. The better outcome in the GA group could be explained by the significantly higher rates of successful reperfusion with GA compared with sedation (mTICI 2b-3: $85.2\%$ vs $75.7\%$ ; aOR 2.02, $95\%$ CI [1.16 to 3.53], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.01$ ). The present study shows no significant differences between GA vs non-GA either in mRS shift to lower degree of disability (adjusted common OR 1.29, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.69 to 2.43], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.43\$ ) or the rates of functional independence (aOR 1.32, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ [0.51 to 3.41], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.56.$ ) at 90-days. Additionally, there were no significant differences in the rates of successful reperfusion among both groups $(85.9\%$ vs $78.2\%$ , aOR 1.33, $95\%$ CI [0.50 to 3.58], $\mathrm{P}{=}0.57^{\mathrm{'}},$ ).

For instance, patients who were intubated in the emergency department or outside hospital for airway protection secondary to severe stroke. To overcome baseline imbalances, we used two different methods of adjustment: multivariable regression analysis and propensity-score matching, which gave consistent results. While we cannot completely exclude the influence of the group differences in the utilization of IA-tPA (GA: $10.3\%$ vs non-GA, $0\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.006,$ ) and IV-Aggrastat (GA: $33.3\%$ vs non-­ GA, $14.1\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.005^{\circ}.$ ) in our results, there is recent evidence to support that, if anything, these strategies would have more likely favored the GA rather than the non-GA group. Specifically, there is reasonable data on the safety of IA lytics in MT and a recent phase-2 randomized trial suggested a benefit.24 25 Likewise, a randomized trial of IV-Aggrastat prior to MT suggested a potential benefit in the subgroup of patients with large artery atherosclerosis (despite the lack of benefit in the overall population).26 Finally, the anesthesia management was at the discretion of the treating center without a standardized protocol in terms of anesthetic medications and blood pressure parameters. However, considering the multicentric nature of our study, this might represent a strength as results are likely to be generalizable to real-world practice.  

# Conclusion  

In this multicenter cohort of patients with anterior circulation LVO undergoing rescue ICAS for failed MT, non-GA yielded similar results to GA in terms of both safety and functional outcomes. Larger prospective studies are warranted to identify which patient groups may experience greater benefit from a particular anesthesia modality.
