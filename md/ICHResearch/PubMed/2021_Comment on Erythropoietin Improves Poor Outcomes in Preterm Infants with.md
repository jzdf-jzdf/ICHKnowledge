# Comment on “Erythropoietin Improves Poor Outcomes in Preterm Infants with Intraventricular Hemorrhage”  

Anita Chevle1 · Anish Pillai1 $\circledcirc$ · Nandkishor Kabra1  

Accepted: 12 August 2021 / Published online: 4 September 2021   
$\circledcirc$ The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature Switzerland AG 2021  

We read with interest the report on the prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the impact of recombinant human erythropoietin (rhEPO) in premature infants with intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH) by Song et al. [1]. The authors reported a significant improvement in their primary outcome (death or neurological disability at 18 months) with the use of rhEPO. The study adds to the literature indicating that low-dose erythropoietin is safe and has the potential to improve long-term neurological outcomes in premature infants.  

However, we observed certain limitations while reading the paper that we would like to highlight. The primary outcome of the study, as stated by the authors, was death or neurological disability. The sample size calculation was performed using only one component: neurological disability. For the sample size calculation, the authors assumed the baseline incidence of neurological disability in preterm infants with IVH to be $47\%$ , based on data previously published by Patra et al. [2]. However, the study by Patra et al. [2] included infants of extremely low birth weight (ELBW), whereas, in the present study, less than $15\%$ of the study population comprised infants of ELBW. The authors found a much lower incidence of neurological disability ( $17\%$ in the placebo group and $9\%$ in the rhEPO group) than their prior assumption, which limited the power of the study. This is likely to be the reason that the clinically meaningful reduction of $8\%$ (from 17 to $9\%$ ) in the neurological disability rate did not reach statistical significance. Also, the present study had a moderately high attrition rate ( $11.94\%$ in placebo vs. $14.64\%$ in rhEPO group), and it is unclear whether any attempt was made to contact families lost to follow-up, either by phone call or with questionnaires. A sensitivity analysis assuming best-case and worst-case scenario outcomes of the infants lost to follow-up would have been a worthwhile exercise. It is known from previous long-term neonatal follow-up study literature that infants who are lost to follow-up are likely to be those who are not performing well.  

Certain outcome measures such as the number of blood transfusions and post hemorrhagic ventricular dilation were mentioned as baseline characteristics. Since the median time of IVH onset was 2 days, it would have been prudent to account for these as secondary outcomes. Previous RCTs showed a reduction in the number of red blood cell transfusions in preterm infants receiving rhEPO during their stay in the neonatal intensive care unit [3, 4].  

Standard care practices such as the use of antenatal steroids, magnesium sulphate, and delayed cord clamping have been shown to reduce the incidence and severity of IVH in premature infants. Since the primary outcome involved neurodevelopment at 18 months, it would have been ideal to include these factors, which have a direct impact on this outcome.  

We congratulate the authors for conducting an RCT and following babies up to 18 months’ corrected age. It would be interesting to see the long-term follow-up of this cohort at 5–7 years of age.
