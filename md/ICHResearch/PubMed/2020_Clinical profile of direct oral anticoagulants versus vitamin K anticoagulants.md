# Clinical profile of direct oral anticoagulants versus vitamin K anticoagulants in octogenarians with atrial fibrillation: a multicentre propensity score matched real‑world cohort study

# Abstract  

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia in clinical practice and its prevalence increases with age. Few data are available about the clinical performance of direct oral anticoagulant (DOACs) in patients aged $\geq80$ years with AF. The aim of our propensity score matched cohort study was to compare the safety and efficacy of DOACs versus well-controlled VKA therapy among octogenarians with AF in real life setting. Data for this study were sourced from the multicenter prospectively maintained Atrial Fibrillation Research Database (NCT03760874), which includes all AF patients followed by the participating centers, through outpatient visits every 3 to 6 months. The database was queried for AF patients aged $\geq80$ years who received DOACs or VKAs treatment. The primary effectiveness endpoint was the occurrence of thromboembolic events (a composite of stroke, transient ischemic attack, systemic embolism); the primary safety endpoint was the occurrence of major bleeding; the secondary endpoint was all-cause mortality. The database query identified 774 AF patients aged $\ge80$ years treated with VKAs and 279 with DOACs. Propensity score (2:1) matching selected 252 DOAC and 504 VKA recipients. The mean follow-up was $31.07\pm14.09$ months. The incidence rate of thromboembolic events was 13.79 per 1000 person-years [14.80 in DOAC vs 13.34 in VKA group, Hazard Ratio 1.10; $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) 0.49 to 2.45; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.823.$ . The incidence rate of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) was 8.06 per 1000 person-years (3.25 in DOAC vs 10.23 in VKA group, HR 0.33; $95\%$ CI 0.07 to 1.45; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.600;$ . Through these incidence rates, we found a positive net clinical benefit (NCB) of DOACs over VKAs, equal $_{\mathrm{to}+9.01}$ . The incidence rate of all-cause mortality was 105.05 per 1000 person-years (74.67 in DOAC vs 118.67 in VKA group, Hazard Ratio 0.65; $95\%$ CI 0.47 to 0.90; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.010$ ). The concomitant use of antiinflammatory drugs (HR 7.90; $\mathrm{P}<0.001\$ ) were found to be independent predictor of major bleeding. Moreover, age (HR 1.17; $\mathrm{P}<0.002;$ ) and chronic kidney disease (HR 0.34; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.019$ ) were found to be independently associated with thromboembolic events. In our study no significant difference in terms of both thromboembolic and major bleeding events, but a significant lower incidence of all-cause mortality, was detected in AF patients aged $\ge80$ years treated with DOACs vs VKAs.  

Keywords  Atrial fibrillation $\cdot$ Direct oral anticoagulant $\cdot$ Octogenarians $^{\ast}$ Net clinical benefit $\cdot$ Effectiveness $\cdot$ Safety $^{\ast}$ Major bleeding $\cdot$ Thromboembolic events  

# Highlights  

Elderly patients with AF are more likely to benefit from oral anticoagulation therapy than younger patients. Few real-world data are available about the clinical performance of DOACs in octogenarians with AF. Our results showed a high incidence rate of major bleedings and a relatively low incidence rate of thromboembolic events among octogenarians treated with oral anticoagulant therapy. Our data suggest a safe and effective use of DOACs in this population, mainly justified by a reduction in overall mortality over VKAs.  

# Introduction  

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia in clinical practice and its prevalence increases with age, ranging from $0.1\%$ in patients younger than 55 years to more than $9\%$ in octogenarian patients [1]. As a result of increased life expectancy, the number of octogenarians with AF is expected to significantly increase in the next few years. Elderly patients are at higher risk of both ischemic and bleeding events compared to younger patients and age is an overlapping factor in both the CHA2DS2-VASc score for stroke risk [2] and the HASBLED score for bleeding risk [3]. Over the age of 80 years, the annual risk of stroke increases to up to $23.5\%$ in high-risk patients [4]. According to the current guidelines [5], a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) is preferred over a vitamin K antagonist (VKA), when oral anticoagulation is initiated in eligible patients with AF. In a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials comparing DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, and dabigatran; insufficient data was available for edoxaban) with VKAs therapy in patients aged $\geq75$ years, DOACs were associated with equal or greater efficacy than conventional therapy, without causing excessive bleeding [6-8]; however, few data are available about the clinical performance of DOACs in octogenarians with AF [9]. The aim of our propensity scorematched cohort study was to compare the safety and effectiveness of DOACs versus well-controlled VKAs therapy among octogenarians with AF in real life setting.  

# Materials and methods  

# Database  

Data for this study were sourced from the prospectively maintained Atrial Fibrillation Research Database (NCT03760874), shared by three Italian Cardiologic Centers (Monaldi Hospital, Naples; University of Campania “Luigi Vanvitelli”, Naples; Maggiore Hospital, Trieste), which includes all AF patients followed by these centers. We selected all AF patients aged $\geq80$ years who received a treatment with DOACs or VKAs. We excluded patients with a follow-up $\leq360$ days from the first qualifying anticoagulant prescription or VKA patients with time in therapeutic range $<70\%$ . Potentially eligible DOAC and VKA patients were propensity score-matched to generate an analysis cohort with minimal differences in baseline characteristics. International normalized ratio was not included in propensity score because it would be inherently higher in the VKA group. Follow-up data were obtained through outpatient visits every 3 to 6 months. During the followup visits the clinical status, occurrence of stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), systemic embolism (SE), major bleeding (MB) events or other side effects were assessed.  

# Definitions  

Ischemic stroke was defined as a focal neurologic deficit lasting for at least $^{24\mathrm{~h~}}$ with no signs of hemorrhage on cerebral imaging and was verified radiologically. TIA was defined as an acute focal neurologic deficit lasting less than $^{24\mathrm{~h~}}$ . SE was defined as an acute vascular insufficiency associated with clinical or radiographic evidence of arterial occlusion and not associated with another likely cause. MB was defined as fatal bleeding or symptomatic bleeding in a critical area or organ, or bleeding causing a fall in hemoglobin level of $\geq2~\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{dL}$ or more or leading to transfusion of two or more units of whole blood or red cells. Minor bleeding was defined as overt bleeding not meeting the criteria for major bleeding but requiring medical intervention, unscheduled contact (visit or telephone) with a physician, temporary interruption of study drug (i.e., delayed dosing), pain, or impairment of daily activities.  

# Patient population  

We identified 1053 AF patients aged $\geq80$ years who received DOACs (n: 279) or VKAs treatment (n: 774). We excluded patients with a follow-up $\leq360$ -days from the first qualifying anticoagulant prescription (n: 26) or VKA patients with time in therapeutic range $<70\%$ (n: 69).Potentially eligible DOAC $(\mathtt{n}=253\$ ) and VKA ${\bf\zeta}_{\bfn=705}$ ) patients were propensity scorematched to generate an analysis cohort with minimal differences in baseline characteristics.  

# Endpoints  

The primary safety endpoint was the occurrence of major bleeding events. The primary effectiveness endpoint was the occurrence of thromboembolic events (a composite of stroke, transient ischemic attack, systemic embolism). The secondary endpoint was the occurrence of all-cause mortality.  

# Statistical analysis  

Descriptive statistics of patient characteristics were carried out; in particular, frequency and percentage were reported for the categorical variables, and mean and the standard deviation were used to summarize continuous variables. The incidence of bleeding was calculated both as incidence rate (the ratio between the number of new events occurred during the follow-up and the person-time accrued from the study members) every 1000 patient-years and as cumulative incidence. Continuous variables were compared using t-tests, and categorical variables were compared using $\chi^{2}$ tests. Propensity score matching was used to balance the differences in baseline characteristics between patients receiving DOACs versus VKAs. The model included all pretreatment variables that could possibly affect treatment assignment and/or outcome, according firstly to clinical judgment and/or previous evidence in literature and, only in third instance, in case of doubt, considering the results of our exploratory regression analysis for treatment assignment and study endpoints (less reliable due to risk of overfitting). We performed nearest neighbor matching method without replacement and without using of caliper. This method provided the best compromise between the efficacy of the confounder adjustment, the precision of the estimation and performance in terms of bias. The ratio of matching was 2:1, because the sample size in VKA group exceeded more than 2 times the number of patients in DOAC group. A Cox proportional hazard analysis was performed to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) with $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) for developing the primary and secondary endpoints between the matched cohorts. All relevant baseline variables were tested at univariate and multivariate analysis (choosing the model by Akaike Information Criterion in a stepwise algorithm) for the primary and secondary endpoints by using Cox regressions. A Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank test were used to compare the event rates of primary endpoint over time. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered significant for all tests. The Net Clinical Benefit (NCB) was calculated in order to obtain an integrated assessment of the anti-thrombotic and pro-hemorrhagic effects of DOACs vs VKAs with the following formula: ${\mathrm{NCB}}=$ (thromboembolic events incidence rate with VKAs − thromboembolic events incidence rate with DOACs) − weighting factor $\times$ (intracranial hemorrhage incidence (ICH) rate with DOACs − ICH incidence rate with VKAs), where we used a weighting factor of 1.5 for ICH, as previously described [10]. All statistical analyses were performed using Rstudio (RStudio Team (2016). RStudio: Integrated Development for R. RStudio, Inc., Boston, MA URL https://www.rstudio.com/).

# Results  

Propensity score logit matching identified 252 DOAC and 504 VKA recipients who were comparable with respect to demographic and clinical characteristics. Baseline characteristics of the study population before and after propensity score matching are summarized in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. The mean follow-up was $31.07\pm14.09$ months.  

Among DOAC patients, 121 $(48\%)$ were on rivaroxaban, 65 $(27\%)$ on dabigatran and 64 $(25\%)$ on apixaban therapy. An appropriate dosage of DOAC, based on the summary of product characteristics $({\mathrm{SmPC}})$ , was prescribed in $67.9\%$ of study population. The prevalence of underdosing and overdosing were $23.4\%$ and $8.3\%$ respectively.  

A total of 27 patients (9 in DOAC vs 18 in VKA group) experienced thromboembolic events during the follow-up. The incidence rate of thromboembolic events was 13.79 per 1000 person-years (14.80 in DOAC vs 13.34 in VKA group, HR: 1.10; $95\%$ CI: 0.49 to 2.45; $P{=}0.823.$ ). A total of 69 patients (20 in DOAC vs 49 in VKA group) had major bleeding events. The incidence rate of major bleeding was 36.30 per 1000 person-years (34.25 in DOAC vs 37.22 in VKA group, HR 0.89; $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI}0.53\$ to 1.50; $P=0.664.$ ). Among major bleedings, 16 were ICH (2 in DOAC group, 14 in VKA group). The incidence rate of ICH was 8.06 per 1000 person-years (3.25 in DOAC vs 10.23 in VKA group, HR 0.33; $95\%$ CI 0.07 to 1.45; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.600$ ). Through these incidence rates we found a positive NCB of DOACs over VKAs, equal to $+9.01$ (Fig. 1). Figures 2 and 3 show the Kaplan–Meier cumulative probability of major bleeding (log rank P-value $=0.970^{}.$ ) and thromboembolic (log rank P-value $=0.820^{}.$ ) events -free survival respectively, in DOAC and VKA treatment groups.  

A total of 209 patients (46 in DOAC vs 163 in VKA group) died during the follow-up. The incidence rate of allcause mortality was 105.05 per 1000 person-years (74.67 in DOAC vs 118.67 in VKA group, HR 0.65; $95\%$ CI 0.47 to 0.90; $P{=}0.0IO,$ . Figure 3 shows the Kaplan–Meier cumulative probability of overall (P value $=0.004,$ survival in DOAC and VKA treatment groups, respectively.  

Univariate and multivariate analyses results for the variables determining the development of major bleeding and thromboembolic events are shown in Tables 3, 4 and 5, respectively. According to these analysis, chronic kidney disease (HR 1.52; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.073.$ ) and previous thromboembolic events (HR 1.73; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.061$ ) seemed to be slightly (not statistically significant) associated with a numerically higher incidence of major bleeding; but only the concomitant use of antinflammatory drugs (HR: 7.90; $\mathrm{P}<0.001\$ ) and anemia (HR 2.41; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.001$ ) were found to be independent predictors of major bleeding. Moreover, age (HR 1.17; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.002;$ resulted an independent predictor of thromboembolic events, whereas an inverse association between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and thromboembolic events was found (HR: 0.34; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.019,$ ). Finally, age (HR 1.07; $\mathrm{P}<0.001$ ), weight $\mathrm{~HR~}0.99\$ ; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.023.$ ), the presence of paroxysmal (instead of persistent/permanent) AF (HR 0.62; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.012$ ), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, HR 1.65; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.003,$ ), heart failure (HF, HR: 1.98; $\mathrm{P}<0.001\$ ), resulted independent predictors of all-cause mortality (Fig. 4).

# Discussion  

Age is one of the strongest risk factors for ischemic stroke and elderly patients with AF are more likely to benefit from oral anticoagulation therapy than younger patients [11- 14]. Warfarin is more effective than aspirin in prevention of stroke in elderly patients with AF [15] and its related bleeding risk only mildly increases after the age of 80 years, while there is a sharp increase in the risk of thrombosis in the same age group [16]. There are few data, emerged from randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and real-world experiences (RWEs), about the clinical performance of DOACs in octogenarians with AF [9].  

The present study investigated the safety and effectiveness of DOACs versus VKAs among a large cohort of octogenarians and older patients with AF in real life setting. Our results showed a high incidence rate of major bleedings and a relatively low incidence rate of thromboembolic among octogenarians treated with oral anticoagulant therapy. As we expected, the advanced age were associated with an increased risk for bleeding during anticoagulant treatment [17]; however, this risk is balanced by the clinical benefit of the thromboembolic events reduction in elderly patients on oral anticoagulant treatment [18] Among AF patients aged $\geq80$ years, the annual incidence rates of major bleedings and thromboembolic events did not significantly differ between DOACs and VKAs therapy; in particular, octogenarians on DOACs showed a numerically lower incidence of major bleedings and a numerically higher incidence of thromboembolic events compared with those on VKAs. These results could support the hypothesis of a safer profile of DOACs vs VKAs in octogenarians with AF, but the numeric decrease of overall major bleedings was lower than previously showed in a pooled analysis from two European prospective real-world registries including 3835 AF patients $\operatorname{aged}\geq75$ years (658 patients aged $\geq85$ years) [19] and in a multicenter observational study including 546 patients aged 90 years or older receiving VKAs and DOACs [20]. The numerically higher, but not statistically significant, incidence of thromboembolic events in AF octogenarians on DOACs vs VKAs may be driven by high percentage of inappropriate reduction of DOACs dosage among our study population $(23.3\%)$ ; however, it was lower than those emerged from large European real-world registries [21-23]. The association between DOACs and inappropriate low dosing has been explained by the physicians’ fear of bleeding events or by the therapeutic inertia in the follow-up [21]. Moreover, in the $8.3\%$ of our AF octogenarians an inappropriate high dosing was prescribed, probably related to the different indications for reducing dosing between EHRA recommendations and the summaries of product characteristics (SmPC). Our results suggest that caution should be adopted in considering DOACs more effective than VKAs in preventing thromboembolic events among AF octogenarians in real world setting.

There are few and conflicting data about the incidence of thromboembolic events among very elderly patients on DOACs vs VKAs in real world setting [19, 20, 24, 25]. In a retrospective Asian study including 293 consecutive AF patients aged $\geq80$ years on DOACs $(50.5\%)$ or warfarin $(49.5\%)$ , the annual incidences of stroke/systemic embolic events were low in both group and with no significantly different among the study population ( $1.16\%$ vs $2.98\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.46$ ), however a numerically decrease of ischemic events was noted in DOACs group [24]. These results are in line with those by Patti et al. who showed a numerically decrease in vascular events ( $2.5\%$ vs $3\%$ , $\mathrm{P}{=}0.46)$ in AF elderly patients on DOACs vs VKAs [19]. In contrast, in a multicenter observational study including 546 patients aged 90 years or older receiving VKAs and DOACs, the annual incidence rate of ischemic stroke/TIA/SE was numerically higher in DOACs vs VKAs group $2.4\%$ vs $2.3\%$ ) [20]; these data were confirmed by a Taiwanese cohort study including 1746 AF patients aged $\geq90$ years which showed a non-significant increase in ischemic stroke (37/978, $4.07\%$ year versus 51/768, $4.59\%$ /yesr; adjusted HR 1.16; $95\%$ CI 0.61–2.22; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.654,$ ) between DOACs vs VKAs [25].  

As expected, we observed a high rate of death among our study population, this data confirms the frailty of the octogenarians with AF on anticoagulant therapy [26]. In our study population, there was a statistically significant difference in annual incidence rate of all-cause mortality between AF octogenarians on DOACs versus VKAs therapy; in particular, DOAC group showed a lower incidence of all-cause mortality, confirming the evidence about death emerged from randomized clinical trials (RCTs) on DOACs in AF patients [27].  

The lower mortality rate of DOACs vs VKAs has been explained by a decrease in anticoagulation related intracranial bleeding among AF patients on DOACs [28] and by a low time in therapeutic range (TTR) among VKA users, which ranged from 55 to $65\%$ in RCTs [29] and was expected to be higher than those in general practice. However, in our study population excluding VKA patients with TTR $<70\%$ , the difference in mortality rate might be partially driven by a decrease in anticoagulant-related major bleedings (particularly intracranial bleeding).

The concomitant use of non-steroidal antinflammatory drugs (HR 7.90; $\mathrm{P}<0.001,$ ) were found to be a strong independent predictor of major bleedings in AF octogenarians on oral anticoagulation therapy, confirming that the combination of NSAIDs with both DOACs or VKAs poses a serious risk of bleeding [30, 31]. Furthermore, we found that the increased age (HR 1.17; $\mathrm{P}<0.002,$ is the only independent predictor of thromboembolic events, confirming that elderly patients with AF are more likely to benefit from oral anticoagulation therapy than younger patients. The presence of CKD in AF octogenarians on oral anticoagulation therapy seems to have a protective effect against thromboembolic events (HR 1.17; $P<0.002,$ ). This data appears in contrast with the evidence that AF patients with renal impairment are at increased risk for thromboembolic events relative to those without chronic kidney disease [32]; however, it might be explained considering that all DOACs reduced systemic embolic event risk to a greater extent than warfarin in patients with moderate renal impairment in RCTs [33]; and considering that warfarin decreased the risk of stroke or systemic thromboembolism in patients with renal disease than in those without renal disease (HR 0.76; $95\%$ CI 0.64 to 0.91; $\mathrm{P}{=}0.003$ ) [34].

# Limitations  

The Atrial Fibrillation Research Database is a prospectively maintained registry; however, the limitation of the present study is the retrospective nature of the analysis. Due to his relatively low sample size, this study was not sufficiently powered for the endpoints analysed. The small size of this study population and the relatively low incidence of primary endpoints do not allow a subgroup analysis according to DOACs type. No specific information on adherence and persistence with therapy was available, although compliance was assessed during follow-up visits and those few patients who had definitely stopped OAC were excluded.  

# Conclusions  

There are few real-world data on clinical profile of direct oral anticoagulants among octogenarians with AF. Our study provides the evidence of a safe and effective use of DOACs in this population, mainly justified by a reduction in allcause mortality over VKAs. Further larger prospective studies are necessary to confirm our observations.
