# Effects of acupuncture treatment on motor function in patients with subacute hemorrhagic stroke: A randomized controlled study

# ABSTRACT  

Keywords:   
Acupuncture   
Stroke   
Motor function   
Randomized controlled trial  

Objective: The aim of this study was to evaluate the additional effects of acupuncture treatment on motor function in patients with subacute hemorrhagic stroke.  

Design: One hundred and thirty-four patients with subacute hemorrhagic stroke were randomized to receive acupuncture treatment plus conventional treatment (treatment group) or conventional treatment only (control group). Acupuncture treatments were given in 24 sessions over 4 weeks, with 3-month follow-up period. Blinded evaluation was based on Fugl-Meyer Assessment, Barthel Index with an intention-to-treat analysis. For those patients who were able to walk, a three-dimensional gait analysis system was employed to objectively record spatiotemporal and kinematic parameters.  

Results: Compared with control group, the treatment group showed a significantly greater over-time improvement in total Fugl-Meyer, lower-limb Fugl-Meyer, but not in upper-limb Fugl-Meyer and Barthel Index. The spatiotemporal parameters of velocity, step length, cadence, step width all showed significant difference between the 2 groups. The velocity in treatment group decreased unexpectedly at day 14, then increased sharply and overcame control group at day 28. The treatment group also showed a significantly greater increase in peak circumduction, peak hip hiking, hip range of motion, knee range of motion and a tendency for the ankle range of motion.  

Conclusions: Acupuncture may promote the motor function recovery of hemorrhagic stroke patients in subacute phase mainly by enhancing the lower limb ability. It probably diminishes the compensation strategies earlier to correct the abnormal gait pattern. Although this adjustment may result in a compromise in the improvement of gait velocity temporarily, patients would benefit from it in a long run.  

# 1. Introduction  

Stroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, which amounts to an enormous burden for society. One of the most momentous challenges for stroke survivors is regaining basic motor function such as walking.2 Acupuncture has been historically used in stroke therapy in China for more than 2000 years and is recommended by WHO nowadays.3 However, Clinical studies investigating efficacy of acupuncture on the motor function of stroke survivors presented varied results,4–8 leaving it as a controversial and hot topic. Moreover, the majority of the previous studies included both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke patients,6,7 which increased the heterogeneity of subjects. For this reason, our study focused on the hemorrhagic stroke, the most devastating subtype, despite it took a long period to recruit all the patients.  

In our previous publication,9 we found a better hematoma absorption and a greater improved neurological deficit score for stroke patients in the acute phase (0–1 month from onset). This article reports on the additional effects of acupuncture on the motor function for hemorrhagic stroke patients in the subacute phase (1–3 months from onset). In this part, we added a three-dimensional gait analysis at different time points to assess the effects of acupuncture for hemorrhagic stroke patients in subacute phase, which has never been adopted by other studies. Hopefully, we could measure and analyze the acupuncture effects specifically and objectively. We hypothesized that stroke participants treated with acupuncture would gain additional benefits on motor function.  

# 2. Methods  

# 2.1. Study design and ethical considerations  

This study was a prospective, randomized, controlled trial and carried out according to the CONSORT statement.10 The study protocol was approved by Ethics Committee of Shanghai Yueyang Hospital and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR-TRC08000225). All patients were given clear explanations of the research, including the purpose and procedure of the trial, potential benefits and risks they may experience, their rights and duties. Moreover, all patients were given enough time to fully understand the trial and determine if they will participate. The patients were also free to withdraw from the trial any time. Written consent was obtained from all the patients. The treatment began only after the acquisition of the written consent.  

# 2.2. Participants  

A total of 569 post-stroke participants were recruited from acupuncture department and rehabilitation department of Shanghai Yueyang Hospital. The recruitment was performed by a research assistant who was not involved in any treatment and assessment. Inclusion criteria were: (1) first-time stroke; (2) hemorrhagic stroke diagnosed by CT scan based on the standard of the Fourth National Cerebrovascular Academic Conference11; (3) 30–90 days after stroke onset; (4) Fugl-Meyer scores $<70$ and (5) conscious and with stable vital signs. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior treatment with surgery; (2) serious primary cardiovascular, hematopoietic, liver, or kidney diseases; (3) pregnancy and (4) cognitive injury or communicative disorders hampering assessment.  

# 2.3. Randomization and blindness  

Each patient was randomly allocated to treatment group (TG) or control group (CG) according to a random number produced by the analyst using SPSS software version 13.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The research assistant who was not involved in any treatment and assessment unsealed the envelope containing the random number for each patient enrolled and informed the corresponding treatment without telling the allocation information. She also reminded the participants not to leak information regarding whether they received acupuncture treatment. The physicians, physiotherapists, assessor and analyst were all blinded to the group division throughout the trial.  

# 2.4. Intervention  

# 2.4.1. Conventional treatment  

After randomization, patients in both the TG and the CG received standard routine pharmacologic treatment, such as control of blood pressure and treatment for complications. Physiotherapies for patients were carried out based on the hemiplegia phase. Program of exercises for atonic phase (Brunnstrom stages 1–2): (1) optimal limb positioning; (2) passive joint movement; (3) lie-to-sit transfer; (4) bend-the-crab and (5) neuromuscular facilitation. Program of exercises for spasm stage (Brunnstrom stages 3–5): (1) antispasm limb positioning; (2) trunk muscle distraction; (3) sit-to-stand transfer; (4) sitting and standing balance; (5) gait training and (6) upper-limb control training. The above exercises were performed once a day for 45 minutes. The program took place 6 days a week for 4 weeks. The physicians for medicine care and therapists performing the program of exercises were blinded to the group division.  

# 2.4.2. Acupuncture treatment  

The TG received acupuncture treatment beside the conventional therapy. The main points chosen were Baihui (GV20) and Taiyang (EXHN5). Two other optimum reaction points identified by motor-evoked potential tests12 were chosen, which were located at the one-third and two-third of the distance between Baihui and Taiyang. These two intermediate points and Baihui were punctured in the direction of Taiyang, while Taiyang itself was punctured backwards and downwards as we described in the previous publication.9 The limb points were selected based on hemiplegia phase. For atonic phase, the following points were chosen: Jianyu(LI15), Jianzhen(SI9), Quchi(LI11), Waiguan(TE5), Hegu(LI4), Huantiao(GB30), Fengshi(GB31), Xiyangguan (GB33), Yanglingquan(GB34), Xuanzhong(GB39), Kunlun(BL60); For spasm stage, the following limb points were selected: Jiquan(HT1), Chize(LU5), Neiguan(PC7), Daling(PC7), Hegu(LI4), Yinmen(BL37), Xiyangguan(GB33), Fuxi(BL38), Yanglingquan(GB34), Tiaokou(ST38), Zusanli(ST36), Taichong(LR3).  

In this study, needles of $0.25\mathrm{-mm}$ diameter and $40{\cdot}\mathrm{mm}$ to $70{\cdot}\mathrm{mm}$ long were applied (Suzhou Hua Tuo Medical Instruments, Suzhou, China). In the treatment, the needle was initially inserted at a 90-degree angle to puncture the galea aponeurotica or skin, then pushed forward $30\mathrm{mm}$ obliquely or vertically as appropriate. Then, the needle was twirled for $5\mathrm{min}$ , 3 times, with two intervals of $5\mathrm{min}$ . The rate of twirling was more than $200~\mathrm{rev/min}$ . During the manipulation, patient achieved a sensation of de qi, which was described as soreness, numbness, distention, or an electric shock. The acupuncture treatment protocol was performed six consecutive sessions per week for 4 weeks by 2 acupuncturists, each with a doctor's degree in acupuncture and more than 5 years’ experience. They had been trained in advance to apply the same protocols.  

# 2.5. Measurements  

Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA), a well-validated instrument for motor function, was employed and its change from baseline to day 90 was taken as the primary outcomes. We used only the motor domain of the FMA scale to evaluate the motor function of patients’ limbs, including two subscales for upper limbs and lower limbs respectively. FMA was assessed at day 7, 14, 28, 60, and 90 after enrollment. Barthel Index (BI) were used to assess activities of daily living $\mathrm{(ADL)^{14}}$ and was assessed at day 28, 60, and 90.  

In addition, for those patients who were able to walk, we employed a three-dimensional gait analysis system (Eagle 4, MotionAnalysis, USA) to measure spatiotemporal and kinematic parameters as we did in previous study.15 Furthermore, to assess the compensation strategies of the paretic-limb, circumduction and hip hiking were computed referring to Tyrell et al.16 Circumduction was computed as the maximum difference of bottom heel of the paretic-limb between stance phase and the subsequent swing phase in the frontal plane. Hip hiking was computed as the angle of pelvis between its position of static standing and its maximal deviation from that position during the swing phase of the paretic-limb in the frontal plane. The gait analysis was performed at day 14 and 28. The assessor applying all outcome measures was qualified and blinded to group assignments.  

# 2.6. Statistical Analysis  

Based on the primary outcome measure FMA scale, a sample size of 114 people (57 per group) was required to achieve $90\%$ power to detect a difference of 4.0 points between groups with 5 repeated measurements when the standard deviation is 11.0. We included 134 patients to allow for dropout. The baseline demographic characteristics were evaluated by t-test, or $\chi^{2}$ test. Repeated-measure analysis of variance model was applied to test differences of changes over time between groups, with “Time” as a within-group factor and “Group” as betweengroup factor. Post hoc comparisons were performed by using an independent-samples t-test to evaluate the significance of the differences between groups for each assessment. For the clinical measurements, we carried out an intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses and contained all patients including the withdrawers. Missing data were treated via multiple imputation. The significance level was set at $5\%$ (2-tailed). All analyses were implemented using SPSS software for Windows, version 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

# 3. Results  

A total of 134 Chinese patients, aged 40-70 years old, met the criteria and were randomized to TG $(\mathrm{n~=~67})$ ) and CG $(\mathrm{n~=~67})$ in the present study. Ten $(7\%)$ patients dropped out during the trial (1 at postintervention, 4 at month 2, and 5 at month 3). Subjects tolerated treatment well, and there were no adverse effects reported in TG, and no deaths occurred during the trial. Sixty-seven patients who were able to walk received the three-dimensional gait analysis, 9 patients were not able to complete the analysis (Fig. 1). No statistically significant differences were found in the baseline values between groups (Table 1), suggesting that they were homogenous in these measurements at  

baseline.  

Changes in FMA and BI over the course of treatment are demonstrated in Table 2. No significant difference was found at baseline between TG and CG $(\mathbf{P}>0.05)$ . Repeated measurements analysis showed a significant effect of time $(\mathbf{P}=0.000)$ and a group $\times$ time interaction for FMA $\mathrm{~\ensuremath~{~(~P~}~=~0.000)~}$ . The TG showed a significantly greater improvement in total FMA than CG over time and sustained at the 3- month follow-up $(\mathbf{P}=0.010)$ . The FMA subscale showed a significant difference between groups for lower limb $\mathrm{~\ensuremath~{~(~P~=~0.000)}~}$ , but not for upper limb $({\bf{P}}=0.397)$ , indicating that the additional improvement of the total FMA was caused by lower limb, rather than upper limb. For the BI scores, no significant group $\times$ time interaction $(\mathrm{P}=0.270)$ was found.  

Changes in spatiotemporal parameters are presented in Table 3. No significant differences were found at baseline between the TG and CG $(\mathbf{P}>0.05)$ . A group $\times$ time interaction was found for all the spatiotemporal parameters, including velocity, step length, cadence, step width. Worth noting was that the velocity in TG at day 14 was significantly lower compared with CG. However, it increased sharply and became higher than CG at day 28. For the kinematic parameters, the hip range of motion (ROM) and knee ROM presented a group $\times$ time interaction. Post hoc comparisons showed that the hip ROM of patients in TG obtained a significantly greater increase compared with CG from day 14 onwards (Fig. 2A), while the knee ROM in TG showed a significantly greater increase only at day 28 (Fig. 2B). No significantly differences were found in the ankle ROM between groups $(\mathrm{P}=0.054)$ (Fig. 2C). However, there was a tendency towards greater increase at day 28 for TG $({\bfP}~=~0.055)$ . The peak circumduction and peak hip hiking both showed a group $\times$ time interaction and a significantly greater improvement for the TG from day 14 onwards than CT.

# 4. Discussion  

The present study found patients treated by acupuncture obtained a greater improvement in FMA, but not in BI. BI involves a greater percentage of questions for upper limb tasks. Therefore, the upper-limb dysfunction has a substantial impact on ADL measurements. As presented by the FMA subscore in this study, lower-limb ability enhancement mainly accounts for the improvement in whole motor function. Hence, the negative result presented by BI is not surprising. In addition, some other researchers also found that studies using ADL to evaluate the comprehensive capacity of patient with stroke were more likely to result in negative or contradictory outcomes, since BI probably involves the subjectivity of the testers, the reliability of the outcomes is low, and the differences between grades are wide.17,18  

The significance of gait analysis has continually increased over recent years and has turned out to be complementary to the clinical measurement for motor dysfunctions, as it can accurately detect the angular deviation between the segments, computing and elucidating what the human eye, even with great clinical experience, can not to do.19 Restoration of walking ability is a top goal for many stroke survivors since gait dysfunction not only limits daily activities and the functional independence, but also causes further health problems such as serious metabolic and cardiovascular deconditioning.20 Interestingly, in present study, patients in TG presented a decreased gait velocity in day 14, whereas that in CG increased significantly, which is worth exploring deeply. Gait speed is always regarded as a key indicator of functional rehabilitation. However, it has disadvantages as an outcome indicator owing to the lack of information on movement pattern improvement. Due to the damage of central nervous system causing weakness, deviant muscle tone, and stereotypical movement synergies that collectively restricted functional reach, post-stroke patients often rely on various compensatory movement strategies to ensure sufficient foot-clearance, including circumduction and hip hiking.21 However, compensatory strategies are believed to be maladaptive and are often harmful to regaining necessary movement abilities.22 Owing to the restricted joint excursion of lower limbs, survivors of stroke are inclined to increase gait cadence to get a faster walking velocity rather than increasing step length.23 Therefore, increased walking speed unless together with kinematic correctness may, in turn, exacerbate kinematic deviation.24 In this study, acupuncture corrected the abnormal gait pattern by diminishing the compensatory strategies from the start of the assessment, as shown in circumduction and hip hiking. However, since a adequate flexion of the knee in the swing phase was not achieved in the beginning of the treatment, patients could not get a sufficient footclearance. To avoid the risk of injurious fall, patients in TG could have been forced to additionally use the knee flexors and ankle dorsiflexors to achieve a joint excursion although with a slower movement, rather than achieving a fast speed by compensatory strategies. Research has revealed that neuronal plasticity is a use-dependent course.25 These forced practice may enhanced the joints ROM, which, in turn, promoted the gait motion towards the normal pattern. Although the gait velocity compromised temporarily, patients would benefit from it in a long run. Consequently, when the joints ROM in TG were greatly improved at day 28, gait velocity progressed sharply and overcame the CG.  

Due to the disruption of central nervous system, post-stroke patients present muscle weakness, exaggerated reflex activity, proprioceptive deficits, and impaired coordination. Previous studies found that scalp acupuncture could cause motor cortex excitability and plastic brain reorganization.26 In the present study, the scalp points related neuroanatomically to the sensory and motor cortices were chosen. An previous experimental research has showed that acupuncture could facilitate the restoration of extremity muscle strength in individuals post-stroke by enhancing expression of gelsolin.27 Thus, the hip extensor and knee flexor force of patients in the present study might be augmented after acupuncture. In addition, cocontraction of spastic antagonists of the hip extensor and knee flexor might be reduced by acupuncture as well, given that previous studies have showed the antispasticity effect of acupuncture for stroke patients.8 In general, muscle coordination involved in the accomplishment of gait could be improved. Furthermore, body acupuncture on lower limbs may promote hip extensor and knee flexor through proper sensory facilitation of the proprioceptive receptors by activating the neurotrophin-3/tropomyosin receptor kinase C signaling pathway.28

The first limitation of this trial is that we did not take sham acupuncture as the control, therefore expectation effects may exist. However, the sham acupuncture methods employed in some previous studies are not practical in China. These methods include inserting the needle shallowly, touching the skin without inserting or inserting at different points. However, most Chinese patients are well familiar with acupuncture therapy and can easily recognize the distinctive sensation of de qi caused by real acupuncture. Moreover, inserting at different points is also a sort of acupuncture, which could induce potential physiological effects,29 and thus confounds the interpretation of the outcomes. Therefore, whether adopting sham acupuncture in the clinical study of acupuncture is disputable. Some scholars hold that sham acupuncture is not a proper control.30,31 Given all that, we chose the conventional treatment as the control. Another limitation is that not all patients were able to walk independently or walk long enough to accomplish the gait analysis. Eventually only 67 patients completed the gait analysis. Thus, it was potentially underpowered for the gait measurements, and hence a larger sample size is needed to confirm these findings.

# 5. Conclusions  

Acupuncture may promote the motor function recovery of hemorrhagic stroke patients in subacute phase mainly by enhancing the lower-limb ability. It probably corrected the abnormal gait pattern at an earlier stage of treatment by diminishing the compensation strategies, as shown in circumduction and hip hiking. Although this adjustment may lead to a compromise in the improvement of gait velocity momentarily, patients would benefit from it in a long run.
