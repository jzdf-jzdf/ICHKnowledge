# Cerebral Microbleeds are Associated With Post-stroke Dysphagia in Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage Patients

Shu-Mei Yang1,2 , Yen-Heng Lin3 , Ting-Ju Lai 4 , Ya-Chu Hsu5 , You-Lin $\pmb { L } \pmb { u } ^ { 2 } .$ , Chueh-Hung $\boldsymbol { { w } _ { u } } \boldsymbol { \imath } , 2$ , Tyng-Guey Wang1 , Meng-Ting $Lin ^ { 1 , 2 }$

OBJECTIVE: Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) are markers of small vessel disease in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and may impact recovery. Post-stroke dysphagia (PSD) is a significant complication of ICH. This study investigates the association between CMBs and PSD in patients with spontaneous ICH.   
METHODS: This retrospective cohort study included patients with acute spontaneous ICH who underwent magnetic resonance imaging for lobar CMB assessment between June 2019 and June 2023. Lobar CMBs were defined based on susceptibility-weighted MRI interpreted by a board-certificated neuroradiologist. PSD was assessed with NG tube retention and Functional Oral Intake Scale levels at the first, fourth, and ${ \bar { 1 } } 2 ^ { \mathrm { th } }$ week after ICH. Logistic regression was performed to evaluate the association between lobar CMBs and PSD.   
RESULTS: A total of 187 patients were included, of whom $61.5 \%$ presented with lobar CMBs. The NG tube retention rate was significantly higher in ICH patients with lobar CMB compared to those without at 4 $( 47.8 \%$ vs. $22.2 \% ,$

$P ~ = ~ 0.0004 )$ and 12 weeks $( 42.6 \%$ vs. $9.7 \% ,$ $P { < } 0.0001 )$ . Functional Oral Intake Scale levels were significantly lower in ICH patients with lobar CMBs at 4 weeks (5.0 vs. 5.7, $P = 0.0449 )$ . ICH patients with lobar CMBs showed a significant association with prolonged NG tube retention at 4 and 12 weeks.

CONCLUSIONS: Lobar CMBs were associated with prolonged PSD in patients with spontaneous ICH. CMBs may serve as predictive markers for post-ICH dysphagia outcomes. Early identification of CMBs may aid in personalizing rehabilitation strategies to improve swallowing recovery in ICH patients.

Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) have gained significant attention as important markers in stroke research, particularly those related to small vessel disease.10,11 CMBs, derived from the rupture of small arteries, arterioles, or capillaries, present as small ( $< \mathrm { { ro } ~ mm }$ in diameter) hypointense lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).12-14 CMBs can occur in lobar (cortical/subcortical areas), deep (basal ganglia/thalamus), or infratentorial (the brainstem/ cerebellum) regions.14,15 Several factors, including advanced age, hypertension, smoking, and vascular lesions, are linked to CMBs, with their prevalence increasing from $6.5 \%$ in people aged $45 ^ { - } 50$ years to $35 { \cdot } 7 \%$ in those aged 80 years.16,17 The distribution of CMBs is also associated with different underlying risk factors. Deep or infratentorial CMBs are associated with hypertensive vasculopathy, while lobar CMBs are linked to cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and APOE ϵ4 allele.14,18 Lobar ICH in older adults contributes to vascular fragility, increasing the susceptibility of individuals to recurrent hemorrhages and small vessel disease.19 In addition to lobar CMBs, various MRI features indicative of CAA-related small vessel damage include the presence of centrum semiovale perivascular spaces (CSO-PVSs), basal ganglia perivascular spaces (BG-PVSs), and multifocal white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), which collectively represent the disease burden of CAA.20

Despite the clinical relevance of PSD in stroke management, predicting swallowing recovery in ICH patients remains challenging. Several prognostic models, including the functional outcome in patients with primary intracerebral hemorrhage (FUNC) score and the ICH score, are commonly applied to predict functional recovery and mortality in ICH patients.21,22 Previous studies also identified multiple factors as potential predictors of PSD.23-25 Moreover, the anatomical location of the hemorrhage, whether cortical, subcortical, or infratentorial, was proposed to influence dysphagia development. Thus, PSD likely results from a complex interplay of factors rather than a single cause.26 In patients with hemorrhagic stroke, particularly those with lobar ICH, CMBs were linked to poorer outcomes, including a higher risk of recurrent hemorrhage and unfavorable functional recovery.27,28 A greater number of CMBs further increases the risk of future ICH, which, in turn, is associated with high mortality and severe disability.29 Previous research found that ICH in patients with or without CMBs was not related to PSD, although bilateral CMBs might contribute to the abnormal bedside swallowing assessment.30 Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which CMBs influence dysphagia recovery remain unclear.

Given the potential relationship between lobar CMBs and dysphagia, this study aims to explore the association between lobar CMBs and PSD in patients with spontaneous ICH. Accordingly, we hypothesized that patients with spontaneous ICH

and lobar CMBs present with a higher PSD rate compared with those without lobar CMBs.

# METHODS

# Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a universityaffiliated medical center, following ethical approval from the hospital’s research ethics committee while adhering to the principles in the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria for this study were (1) patients diagnosed with acute spontaneous ICH, as confirmed by brain computed tomography (CT) imaging; (2) patients who underwent brain MRI; (3) patients admitted to a rehabilitation ward between June 2019 and June 2023. Patients were excluded if their ICH was secondary to other pathological conditions, such as hemorrhagic transformation following an ischemic stroke, cerebral aneurysm rupture, arteriovenous malformations, traumatic intracranial hemorrhage, other secondary causes of ICH, or brain tumors. This research utilized previously published cohort data for the initial recruitment and excluded ICH patients without brain MRI to enable an extended analysis.24 The design and reporting of this study adhered strictly to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.31

# Data Collection

Demographic and baseline clinical data, including sex, age, body mass index, initial laboratory data at emergency room arrival, and comorbidities, such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus, were carefully extracted from patient medical records. Additionally, information on prior medication use was collected. The NIH Stroke Scale/Score (NIHSS) scores that were recorded at multiple time points following the patient’s admission were collected retrospectively. Consciousness levels were assessed using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores, and blood pressure upon arrival at the hospital was documented. Additional data included information about the patient’s surgical intervention for ICH, such as hematoma evacuation or decompressive craniectomy.

Imaging data from non-contrast CT obtained upon hospital admission were used to assess hematoma characteristics. Hematoma volume was calculated using the $\operatorname { ABC } / 2$ method, a wellvalidated technique for estimating the volume of ICH based on axial CT images.32 Furthermore, the presence of intraventricular hemorrhage and the specific location of the hematoma were evaluated. Hematoma locations were categorized into distinct regions: the lobar cortex, putamen, caudate nucleus, thalamus, brainstem, and cerebellum.

All patients underwent MRI, including T2-weighted images (slice thickness: 5 mm, gap: 1 mm, field of view [FOV]: 220 mm, echo time/repetition time [TE/TR]: 93/5790 ms), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences (slice thickness: 5 mm, gap: 1 mm, FOV: 220 mm, TE/TR: 93/9000 ms), and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) sequences (slice thickness: $\mathbf { I } .9 \ \mathrm { mm }$ , gap: 1 mm, FOV: 240 mm, TE/TR: 20/27 ms). MRI was performed using 1 of 3 systems: Siemens Verio 3T, Siemens Biograph mMR 3T, or Siemens Aera 1.5 T. A board-certified neuroradiologist, blinded to all clinical data, reviewed the

CMBs were defined following the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging 2 (STRIVE 2) guidelines, which provide clear criteria for the classification and reporting of vascular changes in the brain.33 Lobar CMBs were defined as homogenous, round, hypointense lesions smaller than 10 mm, confined to the cortical and subcortical white matter, as visualized on SWI sequences (Figure 1).18,34

We further analyzed the number and severity of lobar CMBs, graded on an ordinal scale in which a score of 0 indicated the absence of CMBs, a score of 1 denoted the presence of fewer than 5 CMBs, a score of 2 indicated a range between 5 and 50 CMBs, and a score of 3 represented the presence of more than $50$ CMBs.20 CSO-PVSs and BG-PVSs were assessed on T2-weighted images according to the STRIVE 2 guidelines.33 These spaces were defined as fluid-filled areas measuring less than $3 ~ \mathrm { mm }$ in diameter, following the course of medullary vessels, and were rated on a four-point scale: a score of 1 indicated fewer than 10 spaces, a score of 2 corresponded to 11—20 spaces, a score of 3 denoted 21—40 spaces, and a score of $4$ indicated more than 40 spaces.35,36 WMHs were classified as periventricular hyperintensities (PVWMH) and deep white matter hyperintensities (DWMH) and scored using the Fazekas scale.37 For PVWMHs, a score of 0 denoted no lesions, 1 described caps or a pencil-thin lining, 2 represented a smooth halo, and 3 referred to irregular extensions. For DWMHs, a score of 0 denoted no lesions, 1 represented punctate foci, 2 indicated early confluence, and 3 represented large confluent areas. Lesions located in the subcortical and brainstem regions were excluded from the above

assessment. The presence or absence of lacunes was also recorded on MRI, with lacunes defined as subcortical lesions measuring 3 to 15 mm in diameter and exhibiting CSF-like signal characteristics.18

# Outcome Measurements

The assessment of PSD was performed at 1, 4, and 12 weeks after ICH. PSD was defined using 2 objective outcome measures: the presence of a nasogastric (NG) tube and the Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS) level, as documented in the medical records. The FOIS is a clinician-administered tool that evaluates a patient’s ability to consume food and liquids. Scores of 1 to 3 indicate dependence on tube feeding, ranging from no oral intake to minimal oral intake with supplemental tube feeding. Scores of 4 to 6 reflect partial oral intake with varying dietary restrictions, while a score of 7 represents full oral intake without restrictions and is considered functionally normal. In this study, patients with FOIS scores below 7 were considered to have some degree of dysphagia.38

# Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for ordinal variables, and percentages for categorical variables. Groups were compared using t-tests. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between MRI parameters, including lobar CMB presence and burden, CSO-PVSs, BG-PVSs, WMHs, and lacunes, and NG tube retention at 1, 4, and 12 weeks. Additionally, ordinal logistic regression was employed to examine the relationship between the same MRI parameters and FOIS levels (ordinal variable, $\mathbf { I } ^ { - } \mathbf { 7 } ,$ . Further subgroup analyses were performed, stratified by different main ICH locations (the lobar region, cerebellum/brainstem, and basal ganglia) to explore potential variations in the association between CMBs and dysphagia outcomes. Odds ratios (ORs) with $95 \%$ confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A P-value ${ < } 0.05$ indicated statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (Version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., USA).

# MRI Parameters of Cerebral Small Vessel Disease and Swallowing Outcomes

As shown in Table 4, a greater number of lobar CMBs was associated with higher odds of NG tube retention at 12 weeks (OR: 1.82, $95 \%$ CI: 1.19—2.77, $\mathrm { P } = \mathbf { 0.006 }$ ), but not at 1 or 4 weeks. # DISCUSSION

# Association Between Lobar CMBs and PSD

This study provides new insights into the association between CMBs and PSD in patients with ICH. Specifically, our study focused on the impact of lobar CMBs on PSD duration, as indicated by prolonged NG tube retention and lower FOIS levels. Our findings suggested that lobar CMBs were significantly associated with higher NG tube retention rates at 4 and 12 weeks and with lower FOIS scores at 4 weeks after ICH, contributing to the growing understanding of CMBs as prognostic markers in PSD recovery.

Research indicated that CMBs may serve as an early imaging biomarker for ICH in asymptomatic individuals, carrying significant clinical implications.39 CMB prevalence increases with age, especially in patients with hypertension, with $6 \mathrm { { r } .8 \% }$ of patients with stage 2 hypertension exhibiting these lesions.40 These factors underscore the significance of CMBs in predicting hemorrhagic events and the need for targeted preventive strategies, including hypertension management.39 In ICH patients, CMBs contribute to worse outcomes, further emphasizing the importance of their identification and management.27,28

# Pathophysiological Mechanisms Underlying Swallowing Dysfunction

CMBs are markers of cerebral small vessel disease, particularly in cases associated with CAA.11 Lobar CMBs are frequently associated with CAA, a condition marked by the accumulation of amyloid deposits in the walls of small cortical blood vessels, causing vessel fragility and increasing the risk of hemorrhage.43 In this context, CMBs in the lobar regions may reflect ongoing vascular injury weakening the cerebral vasculature, increasing the risk of both symptomatic and asymptomatic hemorrhages.44 These microvascular changes can disrupt key neural networks that control swallowing, causing prolonged dysphagia. Given that swallowing involves a complex interaction between cortical, subcortical, and brainstem structures,45-47 lobar CMBs may indicate more extensive and diffuse damage to these critical brain regions, thereby hindering the recovery of swallowing function.

Our results demonstrated that a greater lobar CMB count was significantly associated with NG tube dependence at 12 weeks, but not at 1 or $4$ weeks, and showed no association with FOIS levels at any time point. This temporal pattern suggests that the impact of CMB burden on swallowing recovery may become more apparent in the chronic phase after ICH. In the acute and subacute stages $( \tau - 4$ weeks), swallowing impairment is likely dominated by direct effects of hematoma mass effect,

perihematomal edema, and acute neurological deficits,48 which may mask the influence of underlying microangiopathic damage. Extensive CMBs may reflect diffuse cortical and subcortical microvascular injury,49 which can compromise neuroplasticity and adaptive reorganization required for swallowing recovery, thereby prolonging tube dependence. The absence of an association with FOIS scores likely reflects fundamental differences between these outcome measures. NG tube dependence represents a binary threshold for safe and sufficient oral intake, and thus may be more sensitive to subtle deficits or safety concerns that prevent tube removal. In contrast, FOIS levels quantify the degree of oral intake among patients who are already able to eat by mouth; thus, once minimal oral feeding is achieved, CMB burden may exert less influence on further functional progression. Taken together, these findings suggest that lobar CMB count may be a useful marker for predicting prolonged NG tube dependence after ICH, particularly in the chronic phase.

# Interaction Between Hemorrhage Location and CMBs

In exploring the relationship between ICH location, CMBs, and PSD, our findings offer several noteworthy insights. The association between ICH location and PSD was examined in our previous publication,50 which found that hematoma location was not an independent predictor of PSD. Although prior studies have implicated regions such as the insular cortex and corticobulbar tract in swallowing impairment,51,52 our multivariate analysis did not reveal significant associations between specific ICH locations and dysphagia outcomes.50 This may reflect the multifactorial nature of PSD, in which ICH severity outweighs anatomical considerations. In the current study, subgroup analysis stratified by ICH location showed that only patients with basal ganglia hemorrhage exhibited a significant association between CMBs and dysphagia. Specifically, the presence of CMBs in basal

ganglia ICH was associated with higher odds of NG tube dependence at both 4 and 12 weeks, suggesting a synergistic effect between deep hemorrhagic damage and small vessel pathology. The basal ganglia are essential for modulating the motor aspects of swallowing through their connections with cortical and brainstem centers.53,54 Damage to the basal ganglia can impair both voluntary and reflexive components of swallowing, causing longer delays in oropharyngeal transit and increasing the risk of aspiration.54,55

# CMB Distribution and Implications for Dysphagia Risk

Additionally, analysis of CMB distribution (lobar vs. deep) in Tables 4 and 5 revealed no significant differences in PSD outcomes, indicating that the presence of CMBs may not be relevant to their anatomical distribution. It is worth noting that Maeshima et al. reported that in acute supratentorial ICH, the overall presence of CMBs was not significantly associated with dysphagia, although bilateral CMBs might be related.30 This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences: their study used only bedside swallowing assessments, whereas we used both NG tube retention rate and FOIS level, offering a more comprehensive evaluation. Moreover, we performed subgroup analyses by both ICH location and CMB location, allowing the detection of location-specific associations. Taken together, these findings suggest that although ICH location alone is not an independent predictor of PSD, the presence of CMBs in patients with basal ganglia ICH may confer a higher risk of persistent dysphagia, irrespective of whether the CMBs are lobar or deep in distribution.

# Small Vessel Disease Markers and Swallowing Outcomes

In our study, higher NG tube retention rates in patients with lobar CMBs might have occurred due to multiple factors. Lobar CMBs may also indicate more widespread cortical and subcortical damage, as CAA and other small vessel diseases often cause diffuse microvascular changes affecting multiple brain regions.56,57 In addition, further analysis of MRI markers of cerebral small vessel disease showed that several markers affecting subcortical and periventricular regions, including BG-PVSs, PVWMHs, DWMHs, and lacunes, were significantly associated with prolonged NG tube dependence and lower FOIS scores at various time points. These subcortical and periventricular regions contain important white matter tracts.58-60 such as the corticobulbar pathway and cortico-subcortical connections, that are involved in motor execution and sensory integration required for safe and coordinated swallowing.53,61 Lesions in these tracts may limit neuroplasticity and delay functional recovery of oral feeding.

Although lobar CMBs were associated with a higher likelihood of NG tube retention at both 4 and 12 weeks, their relationship with FOIS levels was less clear. Specifically, despite a significant difference in FOIS scores at 4 weeks, the difference at 12 weeks was not statistically significant. Therefore, while lobar CMBs contribute to delayed recovery in early rehabilitation, their impact on swallowing function may diminish over time as compensatory mechanisms and neuroplasticity take effect. This finding aligns with the concept that recovery after stroke, including swallowing function recovery, can continue for several months and is

influenced by the extent of initial brain damage and the effectiveness of rehabilitation interventions.62

# Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The relationship between CMBs and PSD underscores the importance of advanced neuroimaging techniques, such as SWI, for assessing ICH patients. SWI is particularly sensitive in detecting CMBs and could provide valuable information about the number, location, and distribution of these lesions.63 This information could guide prognostic evaluations, allowing clinicians to develop more personalized rehabilitation plans. Additionally, since CMBs may indicate the presence of an underlying chronic cerebrovascular condition, such as CAA or hypertensive vasculopathy, these patients may require closer monitoring for recurrent hemorrhages and other long-term complications. 14,18

Our study has important clinical implications. Identifying CMBs, particularly in lobar regions, may help clinicians better stratify patients according to their risk of prolonged dysphagia, guiding early interventions to improve swallowing outcomes. Patients with lobar CMBs and those with deep brain hemorrhages, such as that in the basal ganglia, may require more intensive dysphagia management and longer-term rehabilitation to facilitate oral intake recovery. Moreover, these patients would likely benefit from a comprehensive and multidisciplinary rehabilitation approach addressing multiple aspects of recovery given the association between CMBs and poor functional outcomes in other domains, such as cognition and motor function.

Our findings also raise important questions about the mechanisms through which CMBs contribute to prolonged dysphagia. While our study focused on lobar CMBs, other CMB types, such as those in the deep or infratentorial regions, could also play a role in swallowing dysfunction. Our analyses showed that the anatomical distribution of CMBs (lobar vs. deep or mixed) was not significantly associated with dysphagia outcomes. However, in patients with basal ganglia ICH, the presence of CMBs was associated with prolonged NG tube dependence, suggesting a possible interaction between hemorrhagic injury and small vessel pathology. Further research is needed to explore how the number, size, and distribution of CMBs influence swallowing outcomes. Additionally, bilateral CMBs, particularly when involving lobar regions, could have a more pronounced impact on swallowing function given the bilateral cortical control of swallowing.64,65 Understanding the interplay between CMBs and the anatomy of hemorrhages could provide deeper insights into PSD pathophysiology and help identify patients who may benefit from targeted interventions.

# Study Limitations

There are several limitations to our study that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design limits our ability to establish causal relationships between CMBs and PSD. Although our findings suggest a strong association between lobar CMBs and prolonged dysphagia, further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up are necessary to validate these results and explore the underlying mechanisms. Additionally, the lack of regular instrumental swallowing assessments, including fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing or videofluoroscopic swallow study, restricted our ability to thoroughly

evaluate the full extent of PSD severity. Thus, future studies should incorporate these assessments to evaluate dysphagia in ICH patients more comprehensively. Second, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the single-center design and the focus on an Asian population. Since the prevalence and clinical impact of CMBs may vary across different populations, multicenter studies involving diverse patient cohorts are warranted to validate our findings. Furthermore, we acknowledge that diffusely distributed CMBs are associated with a broad spectrum of neurological and cognitive impairments beyond swallowing, including deficits in cognition, executive functioning, gait, and mood, which may indirectly influence swallowing through mechanisms such as reduced attention, impaired sensorimotor integration, or diminished cognitive control over volitional swallowing. However, due to the retrospective design and the absence of comprehensive neuropsychological testing, we were unable to evaluate these domains or their potential interaction with PSD. Finally, future research should explore the influence of other factors, such as pre-existing cognitive impairment, comorbidities, and rehabilitation interventions, on the relationship between CMBs and PSD.

# CONCLUSION

CMBs, particularly in lobar regions, were significantly associated with prolonged PSD in ICH patients. CMBs may serve as crucial

prognostic markers for identifying patients at higher risk of persistent dysphagia, guiding early interventions, and optimizing rehabilitation strategies to improve swallowing outcomes.
