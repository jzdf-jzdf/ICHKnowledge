# ABSTRACT  

Introduction: Uncertainty exists over the prognostic significance of pyrexia in acute intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH). We aimed to determine the association of elevated body temperature with clinical and imaging outcomes among participants of the main Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Haemorrhage Trial (INTERACT2).  

Methods: Post-hoc analyses of INTERACT2, an international open, blinded outcome assessed, randomised trial of 2839 patients with spontaneous ICH ( $_{\cdot<6}$ h of onset) and elevated systolic blood pressure (SBP, $150{-}220\mathrm{mmHg},$ randomly assigned to intensive (SBP target ${<}140\mathrm{mmHg)}$ or guideline-recommended (SBP target $<180\mathrm{mmHg}.$ BP management. Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine associations of elevated baseline body temperature $.<37.5$ vs. ${\geq}37.5~^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ and 90-day clinical outcome defined on the modified Rankin scale (mRS). Analysis of covariance determined relations of body temperature and haematoma and perihaematomal oedema (PHE) volumes, at baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ post-randomisation.  

Results: Of 2792 participants with data available at admission, 39 $(1.4\%)$ patients had elevated body temperature $\geq37.5~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . Elevated body temperature was significantly associated with 90-day mortality (adjusted odds ratio 2.44; $95\%$ confidence interval 1.02–5.82; $\mathbf{P}=.044,$ but not with major disability alone (mRS scores 3–5) and combination death or major disability (mRS scores 3–6). Elevated body temperature was also associated with larger PHE volume at baseline (10.89 vs. $3.14~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ , $\mathbf{P}<.001;$ ) and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ (12.43 vs $5.76~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ , $\mathbf{P}=.018\$ but not with haematoma volumes at these time points.  

Conclusion: Early pyrexia in mild to moderate ICH is associated with greater mortality and larger PHE volume, suggesting an early inflammatory-mediated reaction.  

Clinical trial registration: www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT00716079).  

# 1. Introduction  

Acute spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) is the most serious type of stroke, with high mortality and disability [1]. Pyrexia occurs in $30{-}40\%$ of ICH patients, in relation to infection and central mechanisms [2]. Preclinical studies in acute ischaemic stroke have shown that high body temperature is associated with large infarcts and higher mortality [3–5], while in ICH it predicts high mortality, greater haematoma growth and residual disability [6–9]. A retrospective study of 251 patients with spontaneous ICH admitted within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of onset and body temperature measured on admission and during the first $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ found that elevated body temperature independently predicted poor functional outcome [6], which lends support for an immuneinflammatory reaction to perihaematomal oedema [10]. We aimed to determine associations of early elevation in body temperature on key clinical and imaging outcomes in ICH patients who participated in the main phase of the international Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Haemorrhage Trial (INTERACT2) [11].

# 2. Methods  

# 2.1. Study design and participants  

The INTERACT2 study was a multicentre, prospective, open, blinded outcome assessed, randomised controlled trial in which the efficacy of intensive (target systolic blood pressure (SBP) ${<}140\mathrm{mmHg}$ within 1 h and maintained for 7 days) was compared with contemporaneous guideline-recommended $\scriptstyle(<180~{\mathrm{mmHg}})$ ) BP management in patients with spontaneous ICH $(<6\mathrm{h})$ with elevated SBP ( $\mathbf{150}\mathbf{-220}\mathbf{mmHg})$ [11]. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their legal surrogate. The study protocol was approved by the ethics com­ mittee at each hospital and registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT00716079).  

# 2.2. Procedures  

Demographic and clinical characteristics, including the body tem­ perature of patients, were recorded at the time of enrolment (baseline). Severity of the neurological deficit was assessed using the Glasgow coma scale (GCS) and the National Institutes of Health stroke scale (NIHSS) at baseline, $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , and at Day 7 (or earlier upon discharge from hospital). Patients were followed up in-person or by telephone at 90 days, for assessment of functional status on the modified Rankin scale (mRS), by trained researchers who were blind to treatment allocation. All patients received standardised, guideline recommended, management for ICH during the study treatment and follow-up period.  

Uncompressed digital images of CT scans, performed according to standardised techniques at baseline (diagnosis) and $24\pm3\mathrm{~h~}$ , were collected in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format on a CD-ROM (identified by unique patient study number) and assessed centrally for haematoma and PHE volumes, by trained neurologists blind to other data using computer-assisted multi-slice planimetric and voxel threshold techniques.  

# 2.3. Outcomes  

The primary outcome for these analyses was death at 90 days. Sec­ ondary outcomes were death or major disability (primary outcome for the INTERACT2 trial, defined by mRS scores 3–6) and separately for major disability (mRS scores 3–5) [12] at 90 days. Imaging outcomes were haematoma volume (with and without intraventricular haemor­ rhage [IVH]) and perihaematomal oedema (PHE) volume at baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ .  

# 2.4. Statistical analysis  

Participants were divided into two groups according to baseline body temperature ( $_{<37.5}$ vs. ${\ge}37.5^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ [6,13]. Baseline characteristics of patients in pre-defined groups were summarized as mean $\pm$ standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables, and as number $(\%)$ for categorical variables, with compari­ sons made using Wilcoxon or chi-square tests. Associations of body temperature and 90-day clinical and imaging outcomes were examined in logistic regression models and ANCOVA, respectively. Multivariable models were adjusted for confounders selected based upon prior liter­ ature and knowledge of baseline prognostic factors including age, sex, enrolment in China, history of diabetes mellitus, antithrombotics, and randomised allocation to intensive BP lowering treatment, logtransformed time from onset to randomisation, SBP, heart rate, leuco­ cyte count, high NIHSS score $(\ge14)$ , GCS score lobar location of hae­ matoma, IVH, and haematoma volume. Sensitivity analyses were performed using body temperature as a continuous variable and with an alternative elevation cut-point $\phantom{0}{<}37.0$ vs. ${\ge}37.0~^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ , and using pro­ pensity score with inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) [14]. Statistical tests were 2-sided with a standard significance level of P $<.05.$ . All analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.2 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).  

# 3. Results  

In 2829 INTERACT2 participants, there were 2792 $(98.7\%)$ with admission body temperature and 90-day clinical outcomes data, including 966 $(34.1\%)$ with baseline and 24-h CT scans, available for analyses (Supplemental fig. A.1, online only). Overall, there were 39 $(1.4\%)$ patients with baseline elevated body temperature within $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ of ICH onset. Table 1 shows that patients with baseline elevated body temperature had higher heart rate, leucocyte count, NIHSS scores, lower GCS scores, and more IVH and larger baseline haematoma and PHE volume, compared to other patients. The median times from onset to death were 7 (IQR 2–16 days) and 3 days (IQR 2–10 days) for patients with and without pyrexia, respectively.  

Table 2 shows that compared to patients with normal body tem­ perature, those with elevated body temperature had higher 90-day mortality (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.44, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 1.02–5.82; $\mathrm{~P~}=\mathrm{~.044~}$ . Comparable results were observed after adjustment for NIHSS instead of GCS score in the multivariable model ([aOR 2.62, $95\%$ CI 1.08–6.33; $\mathbf{P}=.031\$ ) (supplemental Table A.1, online only). Although, pyrexia was associated with the outcome of death and major disability in the crude analysis $({\bfP}=.027)$ , the associ­ ation was no longer significant after adjustment for other confounders (OR 1.38, $95\%$ CI $0.54-3.51$ ; $\mathrm{P}=.467)$ . No association was found be­ tween elevated body temperature and major disability alone in both the crude and multivariable analysis. In sensitivity analysis, a lower threshold value $(\ge37^{\mathrm{~o~}}\mathrm{C})$ for elevated body temperature, and treating body temperature as a continuous variable, were not associated with any of the 90-day clinical outcomes (supplemental Tables A.2 and A.3, on­ line only). Although IPTW produced a similar magnitude of association for death (OR 2.17, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 1.75–2.71; $\textbf{P}<.0001)$ , the results were inconsistent for major disability (OR 0.73, 95%CI 0.62–0.86; $\mathbf{P}<.0002\$ ) and death and major disability (OR 1.14, $95\%\mathrm{CI}$ 0.97–1.34; P 0.105) (supplemental Table A.4).

# 4. Discussion  

In these post-hoc analyses of the INTERACT2 dataset, we have shown that an early elevation in body temperature after the onset of sponta­ neous ICH was associated with higher mortality, and larger PHE volume at baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ .  

Our results are consistent with other studies showing associations between pyrexia and poor outcomes in ischaemic and mixed stroke patients [7, 13], and with exacerbated neuronal damage and cerebral oedema in all forms of brain injury induced by temperature elevation [6,15]. As our analyses were confined to ICH patients who presented within $^{6\mathrm{~h~}}$ after the onset of symptoms and included adjustment for leucocyte count, a marker of inflammation [16], the relation of pyrexia and mortality in ICH appears independent of early infection. Other studies have linked inflammatory markers, in particular elevated creactive protein, and mortality after ICH [17], while others have shown that elevated body temperature is associated with poor outcomes in acute stroke regardless of aetiology [8,18,19]. Studies suggest the location of the brain injury is important in influencing body tempera­ ture: fever being more likely with haemorrhage in the brainstem or thalamus, or in association with IVH [18]. A recent study in 95 patients with primary ICH found central fever was more frequent $(32\%)$ compared to infectious fever $(9\%)$ , and ICH volume and IVH were not predictors of central fever [20].  

The mechanisms by which elevated body temperature influences ICH outcome is not completely understood. It has been suggested that inflammation, glutamate excitotoxicity, and processes in relation to haematoma growth, which induces early pathophysiologic changes in surrounding brain tissue, such as breakdown of the brain-blood barrier and development of oedema, could be involved in the deleterious con­ sequences of pyrexia [21].  

Additional factors such as anticoagulant use has been reported in the pathogenesis of brain oedema after ICH. Studies have described an associated of warfarin use with less oedema [22], indicating a possibly causal role of the thrombin in the PHE formation or the retraction of the clot induced by the warfarin [23]. Our results were not adjusted for anticoagulant use due to the reported low frequency (Table 1).

Furthermore, a recent study by Campos et al., [24] showed that ICH patients presenting with elevated body temperature within the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ had higher levels of active matrix metallo-peptidase-9 (MMP-9), a molecule involved in the pathogenesis of haematoma growth [25] and secondary brain injury [26]. Experimental [27] and clinical studies suggest that therapeutic hypothermia $(32–34~^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ may reduce PHE vol­ ume in ICH [15,28] and ischaemic stroke [29], but randomised clinical trials of pharmacological or physical temperature-lowering therapy in ICH have shown inconsistent results [30]. On the basis of these data the American Heart Association guidelines for the management of ICH recommend avoidance and early management of elevated body tem­ perature, albeit without a specific treatment strategy being defined [31].  

Contrary to prior investigations of fever and outcomes in ICH [6,32], we found no associations with the outcomes of major disability alone or in combination with death. While this may have been to chance due to the small number of events, the magnitude of association for death was consistent in the IPTW analysis and suggests early pyrexia has prog­ nostic significance over and above the degree of mass effect and neuronal damage from the haematoma but has less influence on func­ tional outcome in survivors. Inconsistency between the primary multi­ variable and IPTW analyses for major disability, with the latter showing an association of early fever and good function, suggests these results are less reliable. While detailed diagnostics could be done on the different statistical approaches, including the adequacy of specification of the propensity score and covariate adjustment [33], these estimates are likely prone to random error and bias from low statistical power and residual confounding.  

Although our study used a large and well-characterised cohort of ICH patients, the population was comprised mainly of those with mildmoderate levels of severity, defined clinically and by the size of hae­ matoma, by virtue of their inclusion in a clinical trial of early intensive BP lowering. Moreover, we were limited to having only a single baseline measure of body temperature recorded within $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ of symptom onset, which likely explains the low frequency of pyrexia $(1.4\%)$ as compared to higher frequencies reported in studies up to $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ after ICH [6,32]. Taken together with the absence of details on the methods of tempera­ ture measurement or management of pyrexia, and only a subset of pa­ tients with repeat brain imaging, our results are further complicated by selection and measurement bias despite studies reporting only small differences in temperature measurements across different parts of the body [34,35]. Our primary analysis was not supported by sensitivity analyses using a lower threshold for pyrexia and body temperature as a continuous measure, but these approaches may not be appropriate biological measures of inflammation.  

In summary, our study suggests that an early elevation in body temperature has prognostic significance in acute ICH and provides further support for PHE having an inflammatory reaction to this illness.
