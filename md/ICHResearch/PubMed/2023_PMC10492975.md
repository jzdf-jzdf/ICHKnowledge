# ORIGINAL RESEARCH  

# Cigarette Smoking as a Risk Factor for Hematoma Expansion in Primary Intracerebral Hemorrhage: Analysis From a Randomized Clinical Trial  

Alexander J. Schupper $\circledcirc$ , MD; Mirhojjat Khorasanizadeh, MD; Christina P. Rossitto $\circledcirc$ , BS; Lydia D. Foster $\circledcirc$ , MS; Christopher P. Kellner $\circledcirc$ , MD; Jose I. Suarez, MD; Adnan I. Qureshi $\circledcirc$ , MD; Shahram Majidi $\circledcirc$ , MD; on behalf of ATACH-2 trial investigators\*  

BACKGRouND: Cigarette smoking is a well-known risk factor for ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. We evaluated the impact of smoking status on hematoma expansion and clinical outcome in patients with primary intracerebral hemorrhage.  

METHODS AND RESULTS: This is a post hoc exploratory analysis of the ATACH (Antihypertensive Treatment at Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage)-2 trial. Patients with intracerebral hemorrhage were randomized into intensive blood pressure lowering (systolic blood pressure, ${<}139\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg)}$ ) versus standard blood pressure lowering (systolic blood pressure, $140\mathrm{-}179\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg})$ in this study. We compared the demographic characteristics; hematoma size, location, and expansion rate; and clinical outcome based on subjects’ smoking status. Of a total of 914 patients in the trial with known smoking status, 439 $(48\%)$ patients were ever smokers (264 current smokers and 175 former smokers). Current and former smokers were younger and more likely to be men. Baseline Glasgow Coma Scale score and initial hematoma size did not vary based on smoking status. Ever smokers had higher rates of thalamic hemorrhage ( $42\%$ versus $34\%$ ) and intraventricular hemorrhage $29\%$ versus $23\%$ ; this rate was highest among former smokers versus current smokers ( $49\%$ versus $35\%$ , respectively). Ever smokers had a higher rate of hematoma expansion in 24 hours (adjusted relative risk [RR] $[95\%$ Cl], 1.46 [1.08-1.96]) compared with nonsmokers on multivariate analysis. There was no significant difference in the rate of death and disability at 90 days between the 2 groups (adjusted RR $[95\%$ Cl], 1.18 [0.998-1.40]).  

CONCLUSIONS: Our analysis demonstrates cigarette smoking as an independent predictor for hematoma expansion. There was no significant difference in death and disability based on smoking status.

# METHODS  

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. We conducted a post hoc exploratory analysis of ATACH-2 trial,15 which was an international, randomized, open-label clinical trial to determine the efficacy of rapid lowering of systolic blood pressure in patients with acute spontaneous ICH. Institutional review board approval and participant consent were waived for the current study. A detailed protocol of the trial has been previously published. Briefly, patients with initial ICH volume ${<}60\mathsf{m}L$ , Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of ${>}4$ , and systolic blood pressure (SBP) ${\geq}180\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either intensive SBP lowering (target SBP, 110-139 mm Hg) or standard SBP lowering (target SBP, 140-179mmHg) Deidentified ATACH-2 trial data were analyzed via an internally derived data set from the original trial data archive. Patient demographic factors, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, and smoking status (current versus former versus never smoker), were collected. Current smokers were defined as subjects who were actively smoking during the time of enrollment, former smokers had previously regularly smoked but were not presently during enrollment, and never smokers had no history of smoking. Ever smokers were defined as either current or former smokers. Clinical parameters, such as initial GCS score and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, were included. Radiographic factors, such as ICH volume and location, perihematomal edema volume, intraventricular hemorrhage, and presence of a spot sign, were recorded. Outcome measures, including modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at 90 days, rates of hematoma expansion, and neurologic deterioration, and adverse events, including in-hospital mortality, were extracted. The following binary outcome variables were analyzed: 90-day mRS score of 0 to 3 versus 4 to 6, 90-day mRS score of 0 to 1 versus 2 to 6, hematoma expansion, neurologic deterioration, presence of any serious adverse event within 72 hours after randomization that was considered by the site investigator to be related to treatment, and death within 3 months after randomization. Hematoma expansion was defined as expansion of $23\%$ in the volume of the hematoma on the computed tomography scan obtained at 24 hours after randomization, compared with the entry scan. Neurologic deterioration was defined as a decrease from baseline of ${}^{22}$ points in the GCS score or an increase of ${\ge}4$ points in the score on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale that was not related to sedation or hypnotic agent use and was sustained for at least 8 hours within the 24 hours after randomization.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Patient parameters15 were compared on the basis of subjects’ smoking status. Baseline demographic variables were compared using $\chi^{2}$ test, Fisher exact test, Wilcoxon test, or $t$ test, as appropriate. Outcome measures were compared by using a generalized linear model with log-link function, Poisson distribution, and robust error estimate, adjusting for covariates included in the ATACH-2 trial primary analysis (age, randomized treatment, GCS category [3-11, 12-14, 0r 15], and presence of intraventricular hemorrhage) or associated with smoking status (sex and hemorrhage location [lobar/nonlobar]).  

# RESULTS  

# Patient Demographics and Smoking Status  

A total of 914 patients with known smoking status were enrolled in the trial. Male subjects were more likely to have ever smoked compared with female subjects $(80.6\%$ versus $19.4\%$ ; $P{<}0.001$ ). As a cohort, 439 $(48\%)$ patients were ever smokers (264 current smokers and 175 former smokers). Current smokers were younger than former or ever smokers (Tables 1 and 2).  

# ICH Characteristics and Subject Presentation  

There was no difference in the baseline GCS score or National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score based on smoking status. Initial hematoma size was also not different based on smoking status (9.9 versus $9.9\mathsf{mL}$ ; ${\calP}{=}0.5167^{}$ ). Ever smokers had a higher rate of thalamic hemorrhage $(42.2\%$ versus $33.8\%$ ), but no difference in rates of lobar hemorrhage ( $10.8\%$ versus $10.9\%$ . Ever smokers had higher rates of intraventricular hemorrhage $(29\%$ versus $23\%$ ; $P_{=0.0458}$ ) (Table  1). Ever smokers also had a higher rate of hematoma expansion in 24 hours (adjusted relative risk [RR] $[95\%$ CI], 1.46 [1.08– 1.96]) (Tables 3 and 4) compared with nonsmokers after adjusting for confounding factors (Table 5).  

# Patient Outcomes and Smoking Status  

There were no differences between rates of good outcome measures (mRS score 1-2; adjusted RR $[95\%$ Cl], 0.89 [0.72-1.11]) between ever and never smokers. Despite ever smokers having higher rates of hematoma expansion (adjusted RR $[95\%$ Cl], 1.46 [1.08-1.96])] rates of neurologic deterioration within 24 hours did not depend on smoking status (adjusted RR $[95\%$ CI], 1.23 [0.77-1.96]). There was no significant difference in the rate of death at 90 days between the 2 groups (adjusted RR $[95\%$ Cl], 1.24 [0.68-2.27]) (Table 5).  

# DISCUSSION  

The effects of cigarette smoking on clinical outcomes after spontaneous ICH are poorly understood. Few clinical studies have explored this effect, with conflicting results. Preclinical studies point toward a potential paradoxical neuroprotective effect of nicotine exposure. Herein, we investigated the effect of smoking history on radiologic progression, functional morbidity, and mortality of patients with ICH through a post hoc analysis of the ATACH-2 trial. Results showed that a history of current or past smoking is associated with significantly higher rates of hematoma expansion, but does not affect mortality, 90-day disability, 24-hour neurologic deterioration, or serious adverse event rates.

Our analysis indicated that ever smokers had a significantly higher rate of hematoma expansion at 24 hours compared with never smokers after adjusting for age, blood pressure goal randomization group, GCS score, intraventricular hemorrhage, sex, and hemorrhage location. Smoking can precipitate expansion of the hematoma through several mechanisms.18,19 Nicotine is known to cause transient increases in blood pressure,20 which can trigger hematoma expansion. Smoking is also known to have deleterious effects on structural integrity of the vessel wall. Both human21 and animal22 studies have documented increased numbers of dead anuclear endothelial cells in circulating blood of smokers, and exposure to cigarette smoke has a dose-dependent negative effect on cerebral vascular endothelial cell viability.23 This effect on vascular wall integrity is evidenced by the fact that smoking is a wellknown risk factor for formation, growth, and rupture of cerebral aneurysms.24 Another mechanism could be increased blood-brain barrier permeability in individuals chronically exposed to tobacco. This effect is thought to be mediated by upregulated expression of proinflammatory cytokines,25 particularly tumor necrosis factor-α,26 which, in turn, impair the barrier properties of the blood-brain barrier.27-29 Also, NO, which is found abundantly in cigarette smoke, is known to enhance blood-brain barrier permeability.19 Finally, smoking can exacerbate the ICH-induced secondary neurovascular injury30 through inducing the release of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species31 and propagating an inflammatory milieu via activation of microglial and astrocytic cells,32 matrix metalloproteinases, and release of proinflammatory cytokines.25  

Several preclinical studies have indicated a potential neuroprotective effect of nicotine in animal models of ICH. The α7-nicotinic acetylcholine receptor is widely expressed in neuronal, glial, and immune cells in the mammalian brain.33 Hijioka et $a|^{34}$ found a dosedependent reduction in neuron loss and improvements in sensorimotor function and survival in collagenaseinduced ICH rat models injected with intraperitoneal nicotine. Similar results were later achieved in an autologous whole blood murine ICH model.9 Such findings hint toward a potential “smoker’s paradox” effect in the outcomes of patients with ICH, a phenomenon that has been previously described in several vascular conditions.35  

However, the hypothesis that smokers have improved outcomes after ICH compared with nonsmokers is not supported by the findings of the current study. We did not observe any significant differences in 90-day mortality or disability rates, the rate of serious adverse events, or 24-hour neurologic deterioration. This is in concert with the findings of previous studies in Western populations. Chen et al10 did not find any difference in 90-day mortality, mRS score, or Barthel index between the ever-smoker and never-smoker ICH groups in an exploratory analysis of the ERICH (Ethnic/Racial Variations of Intracerebral Hemorrhage) study. Similarly, Ironside et $a^{|36}$ did not find any differences in 90-day mortality, mRS score, or Barthel index after adjustment for baseline variables between the recent smoker group and either the nonrecent smoker group or the never smoker group in a retrospective review of the ICH Outcomes Project database. Moreover, Sembil et al11 did not find significant differences in 1-year mortality or favorable outcomes (mRS score, 0-3) in a propensity-matched comparison of smoker and nonsmoker ICH groups. Several explanations may clarify a discrepancy of the neuroprotective effect of nicotine in preclinical studies. There is a markedly lower nicotine dose exposure per weight in smoking humans compared with what was achieved in the animal models injected with nicotine.37,38 In addition, on the basis of the terminal half-life of nicotine in humans, circulating nicotine levels 48 hours after having the last smoke would be insignificant.38 As no human study has yet quantitatively documented circulating nicotine levels in patients with ICH who are labeled as “ever smokers” or “current smokers,” it is unclear what proportion of such subgroups would in fact have nicotine levels sufficient to exert potential neuroprotective effects in the time period after ICH.

Although some studies, including ours, have not demonstrated worse survival and functional outcomes in smoker patients with ICH compared with nonsmoker patients with ICH, this may be attributable to a short duration of follow-up. In a cohort of patients with ICH, Xu et al4 demonstrated significantly higher mortality rate in current smokers versus never smokers over 10.9years of follow-up (11.5 versus 6.2 per 10000 person-years, respectively; $P{<}0.001$ ). Similar results were achieved by Saloheimo et $a1^{39}$ in a 7-year study of prognostic factors in ICH survivors. Several population-based long-term prospective studies designed to study risk factors of stroke have also found smoking to be associated with higher mortality in patients with ICH.5,40-42  

In our study, ever-smoker patients with ICH tended to be significantly younger than never smokers, in line with prior studies.11,36 This potentially highlights detrimental effects of smoking on cerebrovascular health. Furthermore, the odds of intraventricular extension of the ICH were significantly higher in ever smokers. This could be attributable to chronic microangiopathy and blood-brain barrier disruption caused by smoking, which can facilitate small-vessel bleeding and extension.  

We acknowledge several limitations of the current study. First, information about smoking was provided by patients and/or their family members, which is prone to recall, reporting, and missing data bias. Data about duration and intensity of smoking, or the exposure levels at the time of ICH or afterwards, were not collected. Pooling current and former smokers into 1 "ever-smoker” group is also associated with limitations, especially given that differential short- and long-term effects of each tobacco smoke component are not entirely understood. We have provided separate analyses for current, former, and never smoker groups to address this limitation. The study was not equipped to explore long-term effects of smoking on ICH outcomes given the 90-day duration of follow-up. Last, this was a post hoc analysis of the ATACH-2 clinical trial data, so the study protocol was not specifically designed to compare outcomes of ICH based on smoking status, and this post hoc analysis is limited to the variables included in the initial study design. Strengths of the study include large study size, use of rigorous clinical trial data, and adequately accounting for confounding factors other than smoking (eg, age, hematoma location, GCS score, and intraventricular hemorrhage) that can affect ICH outcomes through use of multivariate statistical models.

# CONCLUSIONS  

Our analysis in a large group of hypertensive patients with ICH demonstrates that cigarette smoking is an independent predictor for hematoma expansion. There was no significant difference in death and disability based on smoking status. These findings do not support the “smoker’s paradox” hypothesis, and they indicate that neuroprotective effects of nicotine, if any, are likely offset by detrimental microangiopathic and inflammatory effects of smoking, which result in a higher risk of hematoma expansion. Further studies are warranted to fully understand the impact of smoking on clinical outcome in patients with hypertensive ICH.
