Background and aim: There is a current debate on the best approach for blood pressure (BP) reduction in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). Through this scoping review, we aimed to examine how research on reducing BP in ICH patients has been conducted and to clarify the evidence on which approach is the best (intensive vs. standard BP reductions).  

Methods: We performed a scoping review and overview of reviews of the literature. We systematically searched clinical practice guidelines (CPGs), systematic reviews (SRs), and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that compared intensive versus standard BP reduction. We searched in three databases from inception until March 2020. Two independent authors conducted the study selection, data extraction, quality assessment, and over­ lapping analysis of SRs. We performed a description and critical appraisal of the current body of evidence.  

Results: We included three CPGs (with moderate to high quality); all of them recommended intensive reduction in specific clinical settings. We included eight SRs (with high overlap and critically low quality): two supported intensive reduction and four supported its safety, but not effectiveness. One SR reported that patients with intensive reduction had a significant risk of renal adverse events. We included seven RCTs (with limitations in randomization process); trials with large population did not found significant differences in mortality and disability. One RCT reported a significantly higher number of renal adverse events.  

Conclusions: CPGs support the use of intensive BP reduction; however, most recent SRs partially supported or did not support it due to the association with renal events. It seems the range goal between 140 and $180\mathrm{mmHg}$ could be safe and equally effective than intensive reduction. We recommend further research in serious and non-serious events promoted by intensive reduction and outcomes homogenization across studies to ensure correct comparison.  

# 1. Introduction  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a severe cerebrovascular disorder leading to functional and disabling conse­ quences [1,2]. It represents up to $20\%$ of all cerebrovascular disorders [3]; besides its incidence is approximately 24.6 per 100 000 person-years [4], being higher in developing countries [1,2]. More than $80~\%$ of ICH are related to hypertension or amyloid angiopathy [1,5].  

The relationship between systolic blood pressure (SBP) and clinical outcomes has been represented as a U or J-shaped curve in people with acute stroke or ICH. In ICH, there is a reduction in mortality or volume of bleeding when SBP is between 120 and $180\mathrm{mmHg}$ [6]. However, the SBP intensive reduction could cause ischemia in the perihematomal region [7].  

Because more than half of people with cerebrovascular disorders have high SBP [8,9], blood pressure (BP) reduction is essential. Indeed, the most recent clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) have recommended intensive BP reduction (desired $\mathrm{SBP~<~140~mmHg}$ over standard reduction (desired SBP: $140{-}180\mathrm{mmHg}$ ) in specific settings, especially when SBP is between 150 and $220\mathrm{mmHg}$ [10–12]. Nevertheless, their methodology and the certainty of their body of evidence may not fully support the recommendations. It is worth saying that intensive reduc­ tion may be quite high from neurosurgeons and neuroanesthesiologists, it is a goal that several studies have assessed.  

Several systematic reviews (SRs) and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been published after those CPGs. SRs have issued different conclusions in comparison to CPGs’ recommendations, maintaining the controversy. Through this scoping review, we aimed to examine how research on reducing BP in ICH patients has been conducted and to clarify the evidence on which approach is the best (intensive vs. stan­ dard BP reductions).  

# 2. Material and methods  

We performed a scoping review and overview of reviews of the literature. The protocol is available at https://bit.ly/3kdvqjX. We fol­ lowed the PRISMA guidelines for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [13].  

We performed a scoping review because it is a way of qualitative synthesis to provide direction to research priorities [14]. Moreover, in comparison to SRs, scoping reviews do not have a delimitated question, but map certain evidence characteristics, and identify knowledge gaps [15]. In this review, our discussion was based on CPGs’ recommenda­ tions, and conclusions from SRs and RCTs. Moreover, meta-analysis was not undertaken as our aim was not to refine overall estimated of the treatment effects.

# 2.1. Eligibility criteria  

We included all CPGs that met the following criteria: 1) emission of recommendations on BP reduction in ICH; 2) being published or updated in the last five years (January 2014 – January 2019); 3) used SRs to guide its recommendations; and 4) full-text was available in any lan­ guage on the internet. We included SRs and RCTs that assessed efficacy and safety of intensive compared to standard BP reduction in ICH pa­ tients. We did not make any restrictions of language or risk of bias assessment.  

# 2.2. Search strategy  

We performed a systematic search in three databases: Medline/ PubMed, The Cochrane Controlled Register of Trials, and Scopus. Search strategy is appended in Supplementary Material 1. Search was per­ formed by two independent authors (DCM and ABC), and it was completed in January 2019. We updated the search in March 2020, but no more papers were included.  

# 2.3. Study selection  

Two independent authors (DCM and ABC) assessed the eligibility of CPGs, SRs, and RCTs. When there were discrepancies, a consensus was executed with a third author (CAD). The flowchart of selection is pre­ sented in Fig. 1.  

# 2.4. Data extraction  

Two independent authors (DCM and ABC) extracted the following items from the CPGs: recommendations, level, recommendation system, publication year, design, and year of search. The extracted items from included SRs were first author, year of publication, design of primary studies, assessed outcomes, participants, results, and main conclusions. For RCTs, were the year of publication, design, inclusion criteria, BP target, pharmacological agents, sample size, follow-up duration, and outcomes results.  

# 2.5. Overlapping analysis of systematic reviews  

We created a matrix of included studies of each SR for overlapping analysis (Supplementary Material 3). The overlap among SR was eval­ uated with the corrected covered area (CCA) postulated by Pieper et al. [16]. This measurement evaluates the overlap of included studies on SRs that addressed the same clinical question, considering the number of SRs, the total number, and repetition of included studies. We used the following formula: $\mathrm{CCA}{=}$ (Sum of the included studies in all the SRs – individual studies)/[(individual studies\*number of SRs) – individual studies].  

Values ranges from 0 to $100\%$ . We interpreted the results following these categories: slight $(0-5~\%)$ , moderate $(6-10~\%)$ , high $(11-15\%)$ , and very high overlap $(>15~\%)$ [16].  

# 2.6. Quality appraisal  

# 2.6.1. Clinical practice guidelines  

Two independent authors (DCM and ABC) assessed the quality of CPGs quantitatively with the Appraisal of Guidelines Research and Evaluation II (AGREE-II) [17], which has six domains with 23 item­ s/questions. The guideline is considered “recommended for use” when it has a score greater than $60\%$ in most domains [18]. When there were discrepancies in two or more points of the items, there was a consensus with a third author (CAD).  

# 2.6.2. Systematic reviews  

The authors (DCM and ABC) assessed the quality of SRs using “A  

MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews – version ${\mathbf{2}}^{*}$ (AMSTAR 2) [19]. This tool has 16 questions; all questions with a "yes" answer received a point. According to the AMSTAR 2 methodology, one or any “no” answer in non-critical questions (non-critical weakness) means high confidence in the results; more than one non-critical weakness means moderate confidence; a critical weakness ("no" answer in critical questions) means low confidence; and more than one critical weakness means critically low confidence. The critical questions are #2, #4, #7, $\#9$ , $\#11$ , $\#13$ , and $\#15$ .  

# 2.6.3. Randomized controlled trials  

RCTs were assessed by two authors (DCM and ABC) following the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool version 2.0 (RoB 2) [20]. Each RoB 2 domain has a subtotal risk according to the judgment of signaling questions, and there is an overall risk score.  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Clinical practice guidelines  

In the systematic search of CPGs, 903 records were screened, and we included three CPGs (Fig. 1) [10–12]. One CPG used the “Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation” (GRADE) methodology as recommendation system [11], and the other two CPGs used their system [10,12]. Two CPGs had at least one recommendation with a high level of evidence [10,12]. There were eight recommenda­ tions, and only two had grade A of evidence [10,12]. Regarding the content, KSCVS/CRCS and AHA/ASA recommended the aggressive reduction when SBP was ${>}200\mathrm{mmHg}$ or ${>}220\mathrm{mmHg}$ , respectively. AHA/ASA reported that it could be safe and effective for improving functional outcome. ESO recommended the intensive reduction within 6 h of onset (Table 1). The publication year ranged from 2014 to 2015. The mean delay from search to publication was 1.3 years (range: 1–2). References ranged from 80 to 328 studies (ESO: 154, AHA: 328, KSCVS/CRCS: 80; mean: 187), only one GPC included non-randomized studies [12]. Regarding the AGREE-II assessment, the overall quality ranged from 50 to $83.3~\%$ (mean: $66.6~\%)$ , and ESO and AHA/ASA guidelines had scores of $83.3\%$ and $66.7\%$ , respectively. Finally, scores for rigor of development ranged from 51 to $80.25\%$ (mean: $68\%$ , and two CPGs had a score $\geq60~\%$ [10,11] (Table 2).  

# 3.2. Systematic reviews  

In the systematic search of SRs, 158 records were screened, and we included eight SRs (Fig. 1) [21–28]. None of the cited SRs used the GRADE method to evaluate certainty of evidence. All the SRs performed meta-analyses of RCTs. Sample size varied between outcomes, the intervention group size ranged from 686 to 2145, and the control group size ranged from 701 to 2170. All SRs assessed 3-month mortality and hematoma growth, seven SRs assessed 3-month mortality or disability [23,25–30], five SRs assessed adverse events (hypotension, cardiovas­ cular events, renal failure, among others) [22,25,22–28], and one SR assessed 3-month favorable clinical outcome [22] (Table 3).  

Regarding conclusions, we observed that two SRs supported inten­ sive instead of standard reduction, moreover they were the oldest (2014 and 2015, respectively) [21,22]. One SR concluded that evidence was insufficient [23], and four fully supported intensive reduction’s safety, but not its effectiveness [24–26,28]. One SR stated that intensive reduction could reduce hematoma growth in patients who aged $\leq62$ years and presented an ICH volume of less than $15~\mathrm{mL}$ [28]. The most recent SR mentioned that there was no evidence to support superiority of intensive reduction [27]. Among adverse events, in one ${\mathrm{SR}},$ renal failure was the only outcome with a statistically significant higher relative risk in the intensive BP reduction group $(\mathrm{{RR}}~=~2.18$ (1.08 4.41), fixed, $\mathrm{I}2=0\%$ ) [26]. No significant differences were found in hypotension, cardiovascular events, neurological impairment, or severe adverse events [22–28]. Finally, regarding the AMSTAR 2 assessment, three SRs had a score ${\geq}11$ , but all SRs had critically low confidence [21–23] (Supplementary Material 2). We found a CCA (64.7 $\%$ indicative of a high overlap among SRs (Supplementary Material 3).

# 3.3. Randomized controlled trials  

In the systematic search of RCTs, 590 records were screened, and we included seven RCTs (Fig. 1) [31–37].  

Four RCTs were open-label studies [34–37]. One RCT included only patients with basal ganglia ICH [31], and five RCTs included patients within ${\le}8$ h of onset [8,31,33–35]. All studies included patients with $\mathrm{SBP}\geq200\mathrm{mmHg}$ . Regarding type of ICH, five RCTs included primary  

ICH [31–34,36] and two RCT did not detail this information [35,37]. Five RCTs used any available BP-lowering agents [31,33–36], one RCT used nicardipine and other agents (hydrochloride, labetalol, diltiazem, or urapidil) [37], and one RCT used lisinopril and labetalol [32]. Follow-up was up to three months in six RCTs, and 14 days in one RCT [31].  

Regarding RCTs with a larger population, the Intensive Blood Pres­ sure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage Trial (INTERACT) $\mathbf{(n\mu=}$ 404) found no significant differences in mortality, disability, and adverse events; however, the evolution of the hematoma growth was unfavorable for the standard reduction (p-value $=0.05\$ ) [34]. The INTERACT 2 $({\bfn}=2389)$ found a slight tendency to favorable results in mortality or disability (Odds ratio $=~0.87$ ; $95\%$ CI 0.75–1.01), functional results (Odds ratio $=0.87$ ; $95\%$ CI 0.77–1.00), and quality of life $0.60\pm0.39$ vs. $0.55\pm0.40$ , p-value $=0.002\$ ) for the intensive reduction group [35]. In contrast, the Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage 2 (ATACH 2) $(\mathtt{n}=1000)$ ) evidenced that there were no differences in several outcomes; nevertheless, intensive reduction tended to increase the risk of any serious and renal adverse events [37].

# 4. Discussion  

# 4.1. Main findings  

We aimed to discuss BP reduction by reviewing CPGs, SRs, and RCTs. All CPGs based their recommendations on RCTs [12]. There were eight recommendations, and only two had grade A of evidence [10,12]. Regarding the AGREE-II instrument, two CPGs had a score ${\geq}70\%$ in the rigor of development domain [10,11].  

Regarding SRs, the majority assessed mortality, disability, and he­ matoma growth, and five SRs assessed adverse events. Only two out of seven SRs supported the effectiveness of intensive reduction. Regarding AMSTAR 2 assessment, three SRs had a score ${\geq}11$ (optimum quality), but all SRs had critically low confidence [21–23]. Interestingly, we found a higher overlap among SRs, this could be explained by the small range of time from the first SR and the most updated one (4 years, from  

2014 to 2018); also the most updated SRs [26–28] did not include all the identified RCTs. Heterogeneous inclusion criteria and low methodo­ logical quality could be potential explanations.  

Regarding RCTs, four of them used any available antihypertensive drugs. Nicardipine and nitroprusside were common drugs [10] that could explain the adverse events. They are associated with increased intracranial pressure and new onset of hypertension [38,39]. Nicardipine-related vasoconstriction could explain kidney damage by glomerular hypertension [40]. Also, different methods of pharmaco­ logical administration were used. For instance, INTERACT 2 used intravenous bolus or continuous infusion [35], and ATACH 2 used an intravenous infusion of nicardipine [37]. Three RCT had high risk of bias arising from the randomization process [35–37,41].  

# 4.2. Quality of CPGs  

CPGs received a moderate score in the patient-involvement domain (AGREE-II). Currently, US CPGs continue to downplay this domain even when patient participation has been established as a national mandatory standard [42]. Patients can help to identify the main problems to solve, helping to establish the scope, objectives, and the final revision of CPG [43]. Additionally, they contribute to a better and wider diffusion of the CPG [44].  

CPGs received a moderate score in the rigor of the development domain (AGREE-II). To be considered a reliable CPG, it is essential to detail the methodological process, limitations, and strengths of the ev­ idence [45]. CPGs received a low score in editorial independence; however, KSCVC/CRCS did not declare a conflict of interest (COI) [12]. Authors must avoid this mistake, because it could lead to biased rec­ ommendations owing to unknown reasons, which can be misunder­ stood, such as choosing a treatment to favor a pharmaceutical company [46].

# 4.3. Recommendations of CPGs and main results of SRs  

Recommendations favor intensive BP reduction when SBP or MAP is above a cut-off value or within a specific range. All CPGs indicated that intensive reduction is safe [10–12]. All guidelines supported intensive BP reduction; additionally, KSCVC/CRCS and AHA/ASA established a cut-off to use this management (SBP is ${>}180\mathrm{mmHg}$ or ${>}220\mathrm{mmHg}.$ , respectively). AHA/ASA recommended a standard BP reduction when SBP ranged from $150\mathrm{mmHg}$ to $220\mathrm{mmHg}$ .  

Most SRs suggested that intensive reduction is safe; however, one SR [26] concluded that it could lead to a significant risk of renal adverse events. These findings are aligned with a previous cohort study report­ ing that a maximum reduction in $\mathrm{SBP}>90\mathrm{mmHg}$ was associated with an increased risk of acute kidney injury (AKI) in patients with ICH; however, it should be noted that patients with AKI received significantly nephrotoxic agents during the first 4 days after admission [47]. It is essential to mention that these CPGs’ recommendations (based on evi­ dence until 2013) were published before these evidences were carried out.  

Regarding hematoma growth, intensive reduction showed a nonsignificant reduction according to SRs [22–28].-. BP reduction through the administration of antihypertensive drugs does not vary significantly compared to the initial BP in ICH or with the magnitude of the pressure change of the antihypertensive therapy [48]. Even the cerebral blood flow remained stable after BP reduction [48,49]. This supports the re­ sults found in the SRs and it would rule out the need to suggest one treatment.  

The intensive reduction could be more harmful compared to stan­ dard reduction regarding adverse renal events. ATACH 2 was the only RCT included in SRs that reported a tendency to have a significant dif­ ference in renal events [37]. Moreover, readers must take into account that the renal events were not only based on renal failure, but other serious outcome, such as hematuria, and more non-serious outcomes were reported, such as renal injury and abnormal renal function test [37]. Furthermore, Moullaali et al. [50] performed an analysis combining the individual data from the two largest trials (ATACH 2 and INTERACT 2) in which they concluded intensive, standard management of SBP, appears to be safe and to be associated with favorable clinical outcomes in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage, although they report that $1\%$ and $3\%$ had renal and cardiac adverse events, respectively.  

# 4.4. Methodological aspects of RCTs  

There was a low risk of bias arising from the randomization process in five RCTs [32,34–37]; however, Koch et al. [33] had uncertain risk in the randomization process. According to evidence-based medicine, RCTs provide the highest degree of evidence for interventions when they are properly developed [51]. This implies having an adequate randomiza­ tion sequence generating two groups of people with similar character­ istics (demographic, clinical, and prognostic factors) to assess the effect of the intervention without influencing any other characteristic that could generate bias [52,53]. If this is not done, the truthfulness of the results could not be relied upon.  

Regarding the RCTs, some of them included patients within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of onset. If there is an increase in time, there will be an increase in BP fluctuation that is associated with unfavorable outcomes [54]. Furthermore, the expansion of hematoma is more frequently between the first 6–12 hours; therefore, some BP management outside of that time interval could negatively influence more bleeding [55]. Finally, ATACH 2 was meta-analyzed in the last SRs (2017–2018, see Supple­ mentary Material 3) [25–28], but its results could be biased because the patients received antihypertensive treatment before randomization.  

A critical consideration of the current larger RCTs is that they used slightly different BP reduction strategies (INTERACT 2: target ${<}140\mathrm{mm}$ $\mathrm{Hg}$ within one h, cessation of treatment at $<130\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg};$ ATACH 2:  

target $110–139\mathrm{mmHg}$ within two h), so the participants’ selection and the slightly intensive intervention in the ATACH 2 could explain the different results. Besides, both studies used a categorical outcome based on the target SBP level without considering other magnitudes of the BP change, such as, mean achieved SBP, magnitude of reduction in the first hour after randomization, and variability (SD). One recent study [50] reports that some degree of BP reduction is beneficial but more vari­ ability in SBP could be deleterious, and large reductions in SBP over the first hour after onset of symptoms might also be harmful. Given the complex nature of the BP physiology, additional rigorous evaluations for BP changes as a continuous variable are needed in future trials.  

Because most of the included RCTs excluded patients with large hematomas and high clinical severity (i.e., a low score on the Glasgow Coma Scale or an intracerebral hemorrhage volume of ${>}60\mathrm{mL}$ , asso­ ciations between magnitude of BP reduction and outcomes of mass effect or elevated intracranial pressure, were unlikely to be identified in the current body evidence. For that, the external validity of the efficacy and safety outcomes of the current evidence is limited to patients with acute intracerebral hemorrhage for small- and medium-size lesions. These results suggest that physicians should consider an individualized approach to BP reduction that avoids abrupt, uneven, and large drops in SBP.  

Future well-designed and well-powered RCTs are needed to confirm the non-difference of the intensive and standard BP reduction approach in ICH, including the magnitude and variability of BP reduction as outcomes, and also to identify subgroups that could benefit from the intensive reduction and possible co-interventions that could improve the acute ICH management together with the BP reduction. It should be noted that a current pragmatic cluster RCT (INTERACT 3) [56] is eval­ uating the effectiveness of the intensive care bundle including BP reduction in ICH, this will be the largest trial (sample size of 8360 participants) that could give us a final answer on this topic. The esti­ mated completion date is on May 2022.  

# 4.5. Limitations and strengths  

We excluded CPGs that did not provide recommendations on BP management in ICH; however, it did not influence our discussion. Regarding quality, the CPGs varied greatly in the domain score and this could have biased the results. There were different definitions of metaanalyzed outcomes for each SR, so the results varied among them. Be­ sides, we excluded observational studies to obtain higher quality evi­ dence through RCTs.  

Our strengths are that we used a systematic search strategy for the three types of publications, and we assessed them with validated methodological instruments.  

# 5. Conclusions  

We did not intend to add new information to the debate on which reduction treatment is the best, but organize the evidence on which treatment is the best. CPGs, only two with an overall score more than 60 $\%$ and grade A of evidence, supported the use of intensive BP reduction. Current SRs, all with critically low confidence and only three SRs with optimum quality, partially supported or did not support intensive reduction treatment, moreover, a SR supported an association between this reduction and adverse renal events from included RCTs with low heterogeneity and risk of bias. However, this outcome was defined as any renal alteration, therefore we cannot assume a clinically relevant adverse event. We recommend further research in serious and nonserious events causes by intensive reduction, and outcomes homogeni­ zation across studies to ensure correct comparison. These elements are key to decide which management is the best based on a balance between risks and benefits.
