# Thrombectomy for anterior circulation stroke beyond 6 h from time last known well (AURORA): a systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis  

Tudor G Jovin\*, Raul G Nogueira\*, Maarten G Lansberg, Andrew M Demchuk, Sheila O Martins, J Mocco, Marc Ribo, Ashutosh P Jadhav, Santiago Ortega-Gutierrez, Michael D Hill, Fabricio O Lima, Diogo C Haussen, Scott Brown, Mayank Goyal, Adnan H Siddiqui, Jeremy J Heit, Bijoy K Menon, Stephanie Kemp, Ron Budzik, Xabier Urra, Michael P Marks, Vincent Costalat, David S Liebeskind, Gregory W Albers  

# Summary  

Background Trials examining the benefit of thrombectomy in anterior circulation proximal large vessel occlusion stroke have enrolled patients considered to have salvageable brain tissue, who were randomly assigned beyond $\mathbf{6~h~}$ and (depending on study protocol) up to $24\textbf{h}$ from time last seen well. We aimed to estimate the benefit of thrombectomy overall and in prespecified subgroups through individual patient data meta-analysis.  

Methods We did a systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis between Jan 1, 2010, and March 1, 2021, of randomised controlled trials of endovascular stroke therapy. In the Analysis Of Pooled Data From Randomized Studies Of Thrombectomy More Than 6 Hours After Last Known Well (AURORA) collaboration, the primary outcome was disability on the modified Rankin Scale $({\bfmRS})$ at 90 days, analysed by ordinal logistic regression. Key safety outcomes were symptomatic intracerebral haemorrhage and mortality within 90 days.  

Findings Patient level data from 505 individuals $\scriptstyle(\mathbf{n}=266$ intervention, $\scriptstyle\mathbf{n}=239$ control; mean age 68·6 years [SD 13·7], 259 $[51\cdot3\%]$ women) were included from six trials that met inclusion criteria of 17 screened published randomised trials. Primary outcome analysis showed a benefit of thrombectomy with an unadjusted common odds ratio (OR) of $2\cdot42$ ( $95\%$ CI 1·76–3·33; $\mathbf{p}{<}0\cdot0001)$ and an adjusted common OR (for age, gender, baseline stroke severity, extent of infarction on baseline head CT, and time from onset to random assignment) of $2\cdot54$ (1·83–3·54; $\mathbf{p}{<}0{\cdot}0001)$ . Thrombectomy was associated with higher rates of independence in activities of daily living $(\mathbf{m}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\delta}0{-}2)$ than best medical therapy alone (122 $[45\cdot9\%]$ of 266 vs 46 $[19\cdot3\%]$ of 238; $\mathbf{p}{<}0{\cdot}0001)$ . No significant difference between intervention and control groups was found when analysing either 90-day mortality (4 $4\%$ of 266 vs 46 [19·3%] of 238) or symptomatic intracerebral haemorrhage (14 $[5\cdot3\%]$ of 266 vs eight $[3\cdot3\%]$ of 239). No heterogeneity of treatment effect was noted across subgroups defined by age, gender, baseline stroke severity, vessel occlusion site, baseline Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score, and mode of presentation; treatment effect was stronger in patients randomly assigned within $\scriptstyle12-24\mathbf{h}$ (common OR 5·86 $[95\%$ CI 3·14–10·94]) than those randomly assigned within 6–12 h (1·76 [1·18–2·62]; $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{interaction}}{=}0\cdot0087)$ ).  

Interpretation These findings strengthen the evidence for benefit of endovascular thrombectomy in patients with evidence of reversible cerebral ischaemia across the ${\bf6}{-}24{\bfh}$ time window and are relevant to clinical practice. Our findings suggest that in these patients, thrombectomy should not be withheld on the basis of mode of presentation or of the point in time of presentation within the ${\bf6}{-}24{\bfh}$ time window.

# Introduction  

The first generation of prospective randomised trials that showed clinical benefit of mechanical thrombectomy in acute stroke due to anterior circulation large vessel occlusion (LVO) enrolled most patients within $6\mathrm{h}$ of time last seen well and selected patients primarily based on imaging studies aiming to exclude haemorrhage, confirm the presence of LVO, and exclude a large baseline infarct (core).1–7 In these trials, CT perfusion or MRI, considered more accurate imaging methods than non-contrast CT for assessment of salvageable brain tissue were not required for patient enrolment. Furthermore, benefit of thrombectomy was shown to be time dependent as the lower boundary of the $95\%$ CI for demonstrable benefit was found to cross the line representing the common odds ratio (OR) of 1 at 7 h from time last seen well to procedure initiation.8 Two subsequent trials (DAWN9 and DEFUSE $3^{10}$ ) have shown that in selected patients with acute stroke due to anterior circulation LVO randomly assigned within $6{-}24\mathrm{~h~}$ and 6–16 h of time last seen well respectively, the benefit of thrombectomy is no less than that shown in the early time window. # Evidence before this study  

Time is an essential determinant of patient eligibility for thrombectomy in acute stroke. In 2015, several randomised trials established that thrombectomy—when performed predominantly within $6\mathfrak{h}$ of time last seen well with newer generation devices and more efficient workflow— significantly reduces disability rates after acute stroke due to proximal large vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation. Some of these studies allowed inclusion of patients who could be randomly assigned beyond 6 h from time last seen well but few such patients were enrolled. As such, data supporting thrombectomy in patients treated beyond 6 h from time last seen well were scarce until the publication in 2018 of two trials that enrolled only patients who could be randomly assigned between 6 h and up to $24\mathsf{h}$ from time last seen well. These studies required proof of substantial mismatch between extent of infarcted brain tissue and extent of at-risk brain tissue on the basis of advanced imaging studies (CT perfusion or MRI) for patient inclusion. They were stopped for efficacy based on prespecified criteria after enrolment of a relatively small number of patients. As such, all individual trials were underpowered to provide convincing evidence of efficacy across some of the subgroups of great relevance to clinical practice. Between Jan 1, 2018, and March 1, 2021, on several occasions we searched PubMed, Embase, MEDLINE, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases without language restrictions for any patient-level systematic reviews and meta-analyses reporting on data from randomised controlled trials testing the efficacy of endovascular treatment using modern thrombectomy devices in patients presenting beyond $^{6\mathfrak{h}}$ from time last seen well. The search focused on articles published between Jan 1, 2000, to March 1, 2021, using the search terms “stroke”, “endovascular”, “large vessel occlusion” and $^{\prime\prime}6$ hours”. Although meta-analyses of observational studies and study level meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials have been reported, we did not identify any patient-level meta-analyses of such data in our search.  

# Added value of this study  

Our study is an individual patient meta-analysis of all individuals enrolled in published randomised endovascular stroke trials in which randomisation occurred beyond 6 h of time last seen well and thrombectomy for large vessel anterior circulation occlusion was conducted according to current clinical practice. Treatment was with modern generation thrombectomy devices leading to highly effective reperfusion rates, proof of vessel occlusion before enrolment, exclusion of patients with large infarcts at baseline, and evidence of reversible cerebral ischaemia especially when ascertained by advanced imaging (MRI or CT perfusion). These data will enable a better understanding of the degree of precision of effect size estimates and safety outcome estimates in the overall population and of effect size estimates in some clinically relevant subgroups. We have shown that the clinical benefit derived from thrombectomy extends across a wide range of ages and initial stroke severity or extent of baseline infarct. Furthermore, our analysis shows that thrombectomy is beneficial regardless of presentation mode (stroke symptoms discovered upon awakening, witnessed onset, or unwitnessed onset) and that the treatment effect is stronger in the later (12–24 h) time window than the earlier (12–24 h) time window.  

# Implications of all the available evidence  

Our study has several implications for health policy considerations and for clinical practice, which might lead to changes in current guidelines. First, by showing consistent results across different patient populations with respect to geography, our study suggests that benefit from thrombectomy in patients presenting within $6\ensuremath{-}24\ensuremath{\mathrm{h}}$ from time last seen well is generalisable to a broad range of patients with large vessel ischaemic stroke with imaging evidence of reversible cerebral ischaemia. Second, by providing a more precise treatment effect estimate than each individual trial, the cost effectiveness of this intervention at a societal level can be assessed with higher precision. Third, our study provides evidence that, in clinical practice, endovascular therapy for stroke in patients with proximal large vessel anterior circulation occlusion should not be withheld on the basis of advanced age, moderate size infarcts on baseline CT, moderate or severe clinical deficit, mode of presentation, or the point in time of presentation within the $6{-}24\mathsf{h}$ time window.

Although the time of symptom discovery is typically when medical attention is sought, for the purposes of establishing eligibility for thrombectomy, the universally accepted reference point is the time last seen well. There are three distinct modes by which patient stroke symptoms first come to attention: wake-up stroke, unwitnessed onset stroke, and witnessed onset stroke (appendix p 7).  

The distinction between the $0{-}6\mathrm{~h~}$ and $6{-}24\mathrm{~h~}$ time periods is important in clinical practice; current guidelines15,16 recommend demonstration of mismatch as defined in DAWN9 or DEFUSE $3^{10}$ as a prerequisite for selection for endovascular therapy in patients who are late-presenting but not for those who are early-presenting.  

Uncertainties remain for the benefits of endovascular therapy in patient subpopulations that were underrepresented in individual trials, including those who present in the latest time period, who are not selected on the basis of volumetric mismatch assessment, and those whose symptoms are witnessed to have occurred for longer than $6\mathrm{~h~}$ . Moreover, since these trials were either individually moderate in size or their contributing dataset entailed a small number of patients, data pooling can provide more precise treatment effect estimation. Herein, we seek to address questions about the risks and benefits of endovascular therapy with second-generation thrombectomy devices in patients with LVO stroke presenting beyond 6 h from time last seen well by performing an individual patient data pooled metaanalysis of randomised controlled trials of thromb­ ectomy for acute ischaemic stroke involving this patient population.

# Methods  

# Search strategy and selection criteria  

In this systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis, we searched MEDLINE, PubMed, Embase, and ClincalTrials.gov, using the search terms “stroke”, “endovascular”, “large vessel occlusion”, and “6 hours” to identify randomised controlled trials of endovascular stroke therapy published between Jan 1, 2000, and March 1, 2021, with no language restrictions, which recruited patients with anterior circulation ischaemic stroke who were randomly assigned beyond $6\mathrm{~h~}$ of time last seen well to treatment with either second-generation thrombectomy devices (stent-retrievers or large bore aspiration catheters) plus standard medical treatment versus standard medical treatment alone. Six studies met these criteria: the DAWN,9 DEFUSE 3,10 ESCAPE,4 REVASCAT,5 POSITIVE,11 and RESILIENT12 trials. We established the AURORA (Analysis Of Pooled Data From Randomized Studies Of Thrombectomy More Than 6 Hours After Last Known Well) collaboration to pool patient level data from all six trials. Design and imaging selection features of the six included trials are described in tables 1 and 2. Literature searches were performed by TJ and RN. Data extraction from all individual datasets was performed by SB. There were no conflicts regarding inclusion given the small number of randomised controlled trials identified.

# Outcomes  

The prespecified primary outcome was the degree of disability on the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 90 days, which ranges from normal (0) to death (6), with scores of 5 (severe disability) and 6 collapsed into one category. The key prespecified secondary efficacy outcomes are outlined in table 3. Technical efficacy was assessed by revascular­ isation at the end of the endovascular procedure defined using a modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction scale score of 2b or 3 corresponding to reperfusion of at least $50\%$ of the affected vascular territory as adjudicated by the core laboratory of each individual trial.17 Alberta  

Stroke Program Early CT Scores (ASPECTS) on the baseline non-contrast CT scans and presence and type of haemorrhage on follow-up imaging studies (CT or MRI) were also adjudicated by individual core laboratories. Mode of presentation as exclusively recorded in the DAWN9 and DEFUSE $3^{10}$ trials was categorised as wakeup, witnessed onset, or unwitnessed onset (appendix p 7). Key safety outcomes (table 3) were mortality within 90 days and the occurrence of symptomatic intracerebral haemorrhage as defined by each trial. Most trials defined symptomatic intracerebral haemorrhage as neurological worsening of four points or more on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) from $22–24\mathrm{~h~}$ to $36\mathrm{~h~}$ after random assignment, associated with either any intracranial haemorrhage (DAWN, DEFUSE 3, ESCAPE, and POSITIVE) or a parenchymal haematoma type $2^{18}$ (RESILIENT, REVASCAT; appendix p 3).  

# Data analysis  

Details of the statistical analysis plan are available in the appendix (pp 11–17). To account for between-trial differences, we used mixed-effects modelling for all analyses, with fixed effects for parameters of interest such as treatment assignment. With this approach, treatment effects for each trial (t1, t2, t3, t4, t5, and t6) are not assumed to be deterministically equal, but rather drawn from a common distribution centred on the overall treatment effect (T) across trials. This structure is captured by including trial and the interaction term “trial\*treatment” as random effects variables in all mixed models. We report the overall treatment effect (T) and all other effects using this model, which ensures that between-trial variance is incorporated when estimating all parameters, their SEs, and associated CIs.  

The primary analyses used mixed-effects ordinal logistic regression to answer the following research question: “do patients with acute ischaemic stroke and proximal anterior circulation occlusions randomized beyond 6 hours of time last seen well have reduced disability at 90 days with additional mechanical thrombectomy compared with standard medical care?” Unadjusted and adjusted treatment effects for the primary endpoint are reported using common OR (indicating the odds of improvement of one or more points on the mRS in a shift analysis) along with $95\%$ CIs. The analyses were adjusted for the following prespecified covariates: age (as continuous integer in years), gender (binary as men or women), baseline NIHSS score (as continuous integer from 0–42), baseline ASPECTS (as continuous integer from 0–10), location of occlusion (as binary intracranial internal carotid artery or middle cerebral artery), and time from onset to random assignment. Overall treatment effect is also reported as number needed to treat by calculating the geometric mean of the values derived by the algorithmic joint outcome table method and the permutation test.19,20 In secondary analyses, differential treatment effect for prespecified efficacy and safety outcomes along with $95\%$ CIs (unadjusted and adjusted for the above prespecified covariates) are reported using either mixed effects regression (logistic or linear, as appropriate). Heterogeneity of treatment effect by prespecified clinically relevant variables on the primary outcome (mRS distribution at 90 days) and two secondary outcomes (mRS 0–2 at 90 days and death at 90 days) was tested using a multiplicative interaction term (treatment\*prespecified variable). Stratum specific treatment effects along with the p value for the interaction term are reported graphically using forest plots. Main effects are reported in the text if no significant interaction was noted. All secondary analyses are reported as unadjusted and adjusted effects (adjusted for the same prespecified covariates as in the primary analyses). When appropriate, treatment effects are also reported using rate ratios.  

We did not exclude any studies that met inclusion criteria. This factor, along with the individual patient level nature of our analysis allowed us to conclude that the risk of bias was low and that  sensitivity analyses were not required.  

An independent statistician (SB) collated all data from the individual trials and cross-checked them against previous publications. For outcomes modelling requiring use of baseline covariates, missing data in those fields were imputed singly using mean values to permit analysis of the full set of outcome responses. As the quantity of missing data for model-required covariates was minimal, differences between these analysis results and completecase analysis (ie, not including participants with incomplete baseline data) were minimal (see appendix pp 10–18 for protocol). # Discussion  

This pooled analysis of patient-level data from all published randomised controlled trials that enrolled patients with anterior circulation LVO stroke, randomly assigned between $6{-}24\mathrm{h}$ from time last seen well and given secondgeneration thrombectomy devices, confirms the benefit of endovascular thrombectomy across a broad range of subgroups, including in those presenting more than $12\mathrm{~h~}$ from stroke symptom onset and those with witnessed onset deficit. Our study provides a more precise estimate of treatment effect than each individual trial. We found a 2·5-times improvement in outcomes with thrombectomy over best medical therapy alone. For every 100 patients given endovascular therapy, 33 will be less disabled than if they had received best medical therapy alone including 27 more patients who will reach functional independence $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2)$ at 90 days. We did not detect significant differences in the rates of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage and 90-day mortality between patients given thrombectomy versus best medical therapy alone. However, because symptomatic intracerebral haemorrhage rates were numerically more frequent in the thrombectomy group than the control group and given the observed wide CIs around the point estimate, our findings do not exclude the possibility that thrombectomy might be associated with an increased risk of symptomatic intracerebral haemorrhage.  

Similar to studies conducted in the early time window, our results indicate consistent benefit of thrombectomy across the entire spectrum of baseline stroke severity beyond an NIHSS of more than five on presentation. Treatment of patients with proximal LVO and NIHSS of five or less is an important research question that awaits clarification from ongoing randomised trials using time windows of up to $24\textrm{h}$ (NCT04167527, NCT03796468). Contrary to the individual level meta-analysis of early treated patients (HERMES),7 in which most patients enrolled across all five trials were eligible for and received intravenous alteplase, our dataset includes mostly patients who were not given intravenous thrombolysis, mainly due to presentation outside the eligibility time window (ie, ${>}4\cdot5$ h from time last seen well). Despite treatment in later time windows, the rate of functional independence $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2)$ in the thrombectomy arm of our study $(45\cdot9\%)$ was similar to that reported in HERMES $(46\cdot0\%)$ . By contrast, the rate of functional independence in the control group of our study $(19\cdot3\%)$ was lower than in the control group of HERMES $(26\cdot5\%)$ which might in part be explained by the lower rate of alteplase used in AURORA controls than HERMES.

The use of mismatch as a prerequisite for enrolment in most patients from our dataset (those enrolled in DAWN and DEFUSE 3) might have identified a proximal LVO patient population with a small baseline core volume but a large volume of at-risk tissue that progresses to infarction over a protracted period due to slow collateral failure. On the basis of the findings of our study, these patients, previously identified as so-called slow progressors21 benefit from reperfusion even in the prolonged time window (6–24 h).  

Extent of pretreatment infarction on baseline imaging has been recognised as an essential determinant of clinical outcome in patients given reperfusion therapies,22,23 which explains why all trials included in this analysis have excluded patients presenting with large, established infarcts. Although each individual trial included in our analysis used different methods for imaging definition of core volume, the baseline ASPECTS on CT represents an element common to all trials except in the minority of cases in which MRI was performed in lieu of CT. Our analysis suggests that while lower baseline ASPECTS (implying more extensive irreversible injury) are strongly associated with lower rates of favourable outcomes, similar benefit is conferred in patients with small or absent baseline infarcts (ASPECTS 8–10) compared with patients with moderate sized infarcts (ASPECTS 6–7). Owing mainly to discrepancies between site investigator and core laboratory adjudicated ASPECTS, our study included patients with core laboratory adjudicated ASPECTS 0–5. However, this proportion was small $(<10\%)$ and because all trials excluded patients with ASPECTS of five or less by design, the effect of endovascular thrombectomy in this category of patients cannot be well established by this study. The question of whether there is an ASPECTS threshold below which little benefit (or harm) from thrombectomy occurs remains a matter of active investigation in both early and later time windows after stroke.  

We noted no evidence of treatment effect heterogeneity between patients selected in DAWN and DEFUSE 3 by volumetric mismatch criteria versus patients selected in the other trials that used different criteria obtained through visual interpretation of baseline imaging studies by site investigators. This finding could be partly confounded both by the small sample size and by the fact that many patients not in the DAWN or DEFUSE 3 trials underwent imaging beyond non-contrast CT. Whether benefit from thrombectomy in the extended time window exists when patients are selected only based on non-contrast CT awaits further clarification from randomised trials (NCT04256096).  

A surprising finding emerging from our study is that, although the overall treatment effect favoured thromb­ ectomy both in patients randomly assigned in the $6{-}12\mathrm{~h~}$ period and in the 12–24 h period, the patients in the $12{-}24\mathrm{~h~}$ group derived more benefit from thrombectomy than the patients in the $6{-}12\mathrm{h}$ group. This finding should not be interpreted as an inference that later treated patients have better outcomes than earlier treated patients. In fact, in our study, earlier treated patients had numerically higher rates of favourable outcomes $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2)$ than patients who were treated later. However, the treatment effect was larger in those randomly assigned later, because control patients randomly assigned later had worse outcomes than those randomly assigned earlier. The reasons for this observation are unclear. It is possible that patients with LVO not given thrombectomy who improve neurologically do so predominantly because of spontaneous recanalisation and that the later in time they present with a proximal LVO the lower the chance that spontaneous recanalisation will subsequently occur. Furthermore, in the $6{-}12\mathrm{~h~}$ time window, a significantly higher proportion of patients received alteplase than in the $12{-}24\mathrm{h}$ time window (appendix p 4), which could also have contributed to higher recanalisation rates in the patients who were randomly assigned earlier. We do not have data for the rate of spontaneous or alteplase-induced recanalisation in our control patients and thus the above explanation remains speculative. Finally, it is possible that some imbalances in factors known to affect outcomes, including age and proportion of internal carotid artery occlusion, between patients who were randomly assigned early versus those randomly assigned late might have contributed to the observed differences in outcomes between the two control groups. Nonetheless, given the small sample size and because the true time of stroke onset is unknown in our patients with wake-up and unwitnessed stroke onset, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility that our findings are due to chance. However, on the basis of the results of our analysis, there is no reason to withhold thrombectomy in otherwise eligible patients who present at any point in time between $12{-}24\mathrm{h}$ from time last seen well. In addition, this finding leaves open the possibility of benefit from thrombectomy beyond $24\textrm{h}$ from time last seen well in patients with anterior circulation LVO and mismatch, which should be investigated in future randomised trials.  

Our study reveals that patients who present with witnessed stroke onset more than $6\mathrm{{h}}$ from time last seen well might benefit from thrombectomy, providing reassurance that the positive results of late-onset trials are not primarily driven by the high proportion of patients presenting with wake-up stroke or stroke of unknown onset time who are presumed to have had their stroke onset close to the timepoint of symptom discovery. This finding supports the efficacy of thrombectomy in some patients with ongoing cerebral ischaemia of longer than 6 h duration and confirms previous reports from single randomised trials showing that in the presence of salvageable brain tissue, benefit of thrombectomy in the extended time window exists regardless of symptom discovery mode.24  

A strength of this study was that although the individual trial populations were similar in many respects, they varied in some entry criteria and in the diversity of the patient population with respect to geography across the trials. The consistency of results noted across all six trials suggests that our findings are generalisable to a broad range of late presenting patients with ischaemic stroke with LVO who have imaging evidence of salvageable brain tissue.  

This study has some limitations. Participating trials were conducted at comprehensive stroke centres, considered to be experienced; registry studies in larger group of hospitals are needed to confirm applicability to less experienced centres. Although ascertainment of procedural, imaging, and clinical outcome measures (eg, modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction, ASPECTS, symptomatic intracerebral haemorrhage, mRS) was done in a blinded manner, different core laboratories and operationalised approaches were involved across all six trials (tables 1, 2; appendix p 3). Finally, the potential for bias was minimised by the prespecification of analysis objectives and methods while the variance in resulting estimates was modelled appropriately by the inclusion of between-trial random effects in the statistical models. Some patient populations, particularly those with the largest infarcts at baseline, posterior circulation occlusions, presenting beyond $24\mathrm{~h},$ presenting with mild deficits $\mathrm{(NIHSS~}<6\mathrm{)}$ , and with substantial pre-stroke disability $(\mathrm{mRS}\geq2)$ were excluded from all participating trials. Consequently, our results cannot be extrapolated to these patient populations. Finally, since the meta-analysis included some trials that were stopped early (DEFUSE 3, ESCAPE, POSITIVE, and REVASCAT), the possibility of overestimation of treatment effect exists. Establishing broad applicability will require careful systematic collection of registry data.
