# Abstract  

Background The aim of the present meta-analysis was to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) versus vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) in atrial fibrillation (AF) patients with polypharmacy.  

# Keywords  

atrial fibrillation ► non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants vitamin K antagonists ► polypharmacy ► meta-analysis  

Methods and Results Randomized controlled trials or observational studies reporting the data of NOACs versus VKAs among AF patients with polypharmacy were included. The search was performed in the PubMed and Embase databases up to November 2022. A total of 12 studies involving 767,544 AF patients were included. For the primary outcomes, the use of NOACs compared with VKAs was significantly associated with a reduced risk of stroke or systemic embolism in AF patients with moderate polypharmacy (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.77 $95\%$ confidence interval [CI]: 0.69– 0.86]) and severe polypharmacy (HR: 0.76 $[95\%$ CI: 0.69–0.82]), but there was no significant difference in major bleeding (moderate polypharmacy: HR: 0.87 $95\%$ CI: 0.74–1.01]; severe polypharmacy: HR: 0.91 $\left[95\%~\mathsf{C}\right]\colon0.79\ –1.06];$ between the two groups. In secondary outcomes, there were no differences in the rates of ischemic stroke, all-cause death, and gastrointestinal bleeding between the NOAC- and VKA- users, but NOAC users had a reduced risk of any bleeding compared with VKA- users. Compared with VKAs, the risk of intracranial hemorrhage was reduced in NOAC- users with moderate polypharmacy but not severe polypharmacy.

# Introduction  

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a cardiac arrhythmia that is more common in the elderly, particularly those aged between 65 and 85 years, who make up nearly $70\%$ of AF patients.1,2 Elderly AF patients often have a heavy comorbidity burden and simultaneously use multiple drugs, a condition known as polypharmacy. However, there is no international consensus on the threshold and measurement methods of drug use.3  

In patients with AF, oral anticoagulant (OAC) therapy is necessary to prevent thrombotic events. Current evidence has indicated that the use of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) is a safer and more effective alternative to vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) for nonvalvular AF patients. They have fewer drug–food interactions and a rapid onset of action.4–7 However, when encountering polypharmacy, a critical treatment issue is presented. In this setting, anticoagulated patients with polypharmacy frequently exhibit an unexpected dose–response relationship to OAC therapy, in which polypharmacy has been demonstrated to be a risk factor for both anticoagulation-related events such as bleeding and thromboembolism.8–10  

Despite the guideline recommendations from the European Society of Cardiology and the Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society,11,12 only approximately $50\%$ of elderly AF patients received OAC therapy. On the one hand, owing to the multiple comorbidities and polypharmacy, these patients take the risks of pill burden, drug–drug interaction, nonadherence, and adverse drug events.13 On the other hand, the increased risk of bleeding in elderly patients with AF due to their advanced age necessitates greater caution when considering the use of anticoagulants.14 In this context, it is necessary to explore safe and effective OAC treatments for AF patients with polypharmacy.  

Several post-hoc analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in patients with AF polypharmacy have been conducted. Jaspers Focks et al conducted a post-hoc analysis of the ARISTOTLE trial, showing that apixaban was more effective than warfarin, but they had similar risks of safe outcomes.9 In addition, numerous real-world studies, such as the ARISTOPHANES study, have demonstrated that the effectiveness and safety profiles are more favorable for NOACs compared with warfarin.  

Therefore, we performed a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis by including high-quality studies to determine the effectiveness and safety of NOACs versus VKAs in AF patients with polypharmacy. The aims of this meta-analysis were as follows: (1) comparing the risks of adverse outcomes in AF patients with and without polypharmacy; (2) assessing the effectiveness and safety outcomes of NOACs versus VKAs in patients with AF and polypharmacy; and (3) assessing the effects of NOACs versus VKAs in AF patients with and without polypharmacy.  

# Methods  

We conducted this meta-analysis based on the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 6.3) criteria. The results were presented according to the preferred reporting items for the systematic review and meta-analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement. Ethical approval was not required, as this study only included articles of published data in the public domain.  

# Literature Search  

Two reviewers performed the literature search, systematically searching the PubMed and Embase database sources until November 2022 for studies exploring the effect of NOACs in AF patients with polypharmacy. The following search terms were used: (1) “atrial fibrillation,” (2) “dabigatran” OR “rivaroxaban” OR “apixaban” OR “edoxaban” OR “non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant” OR “direct oral anticoagulant” OR “novel oral anticoagulant” OR “NOAC” OR “DOAC,” (3) “polypharmacy” OR “polymedication,” (4) “Vitamin K antagonists” OR “VKA” OR “warfarin” OR “dicoumarol” OR “acenocoumarol” OR “coumadin.” The above four categories of search terms were combined using the Boolean operator “and.” The detailed search strategies are shown in ►Supplementary Table S1 (available in the online version). In addition, the reference lists of the retrieved articles and prior reviews were manually checked for additional eligible studies. We applied no restrictions on the language of publication.  

# Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria  

RCTs, post-hoc analyses, or observational (prospective or retrospective cohort) studies focusing on AF patients with polypharmacy who received VKAs or NOACs (dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, or edoxaban) were all enrolled. Included studies reported quantitative estimates of the hazard ratios (HRs) and $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) for clinical outcomes.  

Criteria for exclusion were as follows: (1) certain publication types (e.g., reviews, comments, case reports, case series, letters, editorials, and meeting abstracts); (2) data were unable to be obtained or insufficient data; (3) studies that did not report the relevant outcomes or classification of polypharmacy. If there were overlapping data among two or more studies, we included the one with the largest sample size.  

# Clinical Outcomes  

To assess the association of polypharmacy with adverse outcomes in AF patients, we included the outcomes of stroke or systemic embolism (SSE), all-cause death, major bleeding, and intracranial hemorrhage. To assess the effectiveness and safety of NOACs versus VKAs in patients with AF and polypharmacy, we included the primary effectiveness outcome of a composite of SSE and the primary safety outcome of major bleeding. In addition, the secondary effectiveness outcomes included ischemic stroke and all-cause death, whereas the secondary safety outcomes were any bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and gastrointestinal bleeding.  

# Study Selection and Data Abstraction  

The titles and abstracts of the studies acquired from the electronic databases were examined separately by two reviewers. Subsequently, we selected the eligible studies after the full-text screenings based on the predefined inclusion criteria. Conflicts were settled by the conversation between the two reviewers or communication with the relevant authors. The following data of the included studies were abstracted: study characteristics (first author, year of publication, study design), study population and baseline characteristics (study period, demographic, sample size, age, female ratio, the definition of polypharmacy, follow-up period), effectiveness and safety outcomes, confounders, and outcome data (sample size and the number of events between groups, adjusted HRs).  

# Study Quality Assessment  

The revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB2) was used to assess the risk of bias for post-hoc analyses of RCTs, which mainly includes five aspects: randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. The levels of the bias risk in each domain and overall were scored as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high” risk. We assessed the quality of observational cohorts by using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) tool. In this metaanalysis, the NOS values of ${\ge}6$ and ${<}6$ points were moderateto-high quality and low quality, respectively.  

# Statistical Analysis  

The statistical analyses were conducted using the Review Manager version 5.4 software (the Cochrane Collaboration 2014, Nordic Cochrane Centre Copenhagen, Denmark;  

https://community.cochrane.org/). In this study, significant heterogeneity was indicated by a $p$ -value of ${<}0.10$ in the Cochrane Q test or an $I^{2}$ value of $>50\%$ . Due to the different types or doses of NOACs and the inconsistent classification of polypharmacy, the clinical heterogeneity among the included studies could not be ignored. Regardless of the heterogeneity, we combined HRs according to the random-effects model to obtain a relatively conservative conclusion. In the pooled analysis, the adjusted HRs and $95\%$ CIs were converted to the natural logarithms (Ln [HR]) and their corresponding standard errors (Ln [upper CI]  Ln [lower CI])/ 3.92), which were pooled by a DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model with an inverse variance method. The sensitivity analysis was performed to examine the influence of each study on the pooled results. The subgroup analysis was performed based on the type of studies (observational studies vs. post-hoc analyses of RCTs). The statistical significance threshold was set at $p<0.05$ .

# Polypharmacy Definition  

There was a slight variation in the definition of polypharmacy used across the studies included in our meta-analysis. We defined polypharmacy as a discrete definition. When dividing theboundaries of nonpolypharmacy, moderate polypharmacy, and severe polypharmacy, the threshold used by the study author was applied. Specifically, 5 studies10,15,23–25 included articles that defined moderate polypharmacy as the use of 5 to 9 drugs, 3 studies8,28,30 included articles that defined moderate polypharmacy use as the use of 5 to 8 drugs, and 2 studies9,29 included articles defined moderate polypharmacy as use of 6 to 8 drugs, and 1 article27 defined moderate polydrug as use of 4 to 8 drugs. Correspondingly, six articles8,9,27–30 defined severe polypharmacy as the use of ${\ge}9$ drugs, and six articles10,15,23–26 defined severe polypharmacy as the use of $\geq10$ drugs. It should be noted that the article by Martinez et $\mathsf{al}^{26}$ defines polypharmacy as the use of five or more drugs and explores the grouping of 10 or more drugs in the secondary analysis. Therefore, we did not classify its data into the moderate polypharmacy group but used its secondary analysis data as the severe polypharmacy group. Detailed classification information is shown in ►Table 1.

# Discussion  

The main findings of our meta-analysis were summarized as follows: (1) there were no differences in SSE between polypharmacy and no polypharmacy, whereas moderate and severe polypharmacy were associated with increased risks of all-cause death and major bleeding compared with no polypharmacy; (2) NOACs were associated with a significant reduction in SSE and any bleeding compared with VKAs in AF  

Polypharmacy is common in the elderly, often accompanied by AF.1,2 The meta-analysis by Harskamp et al16 enrolling two post-hoc analyses of RCTs showed that the frequencies of bleeding events and mortality, but not SSE, were positively associated with the number of concomitant drugs. Our study yielded similar findings after including more high-quality retrospective studies and post-hoc analyses of RCTs. Since most of the included articles were extensively adjusted for covariates, our conclusions showed increased risks of bleeding events and mortality with greater confidence. Our results showed that polypharmacy was associated with poor clinical outcomes in AF patients receiving OAC maintenance therapy, suggesting that clinicians should minimize the risk of bleeding during the treatment of such patients, including during discontinued concomitant antiplatelet therapy as appropriate.

Before the advent of NOACs, VKAs had been the first choice for anticoagulation in AF patients. As research progresses, several studies have confirmed that NOACs may have benefits in terms of efficacy, and most of the safety outcomes are also comparable to VKAs.4–7,31 The current guidelines only recommend the use of NOACs as first-line anticoagulants in general-population AF patients. Our results showed that NOACs had advantages over VKAs in SSE and any bleeding and were comparable to VKAs for other effectiveness and safety outcomes in those AF patients with polypharmacy. Until RCTs specifically examining the safety and efficacy of NOACs are available in patients with AF and polypharmacy, our meta-analysis provided some evidence to give clinicians some confidence in selecting NOACs for the population requiring anticoagulation. The intuitive effect of polypharmacy is an increase in drug–drug interactions, which may lead to a decrease in the effectiveness of given drugs or an increase in the level of toxicity. While NOACs can target a single coagulation factor such as factor Xa and factor IIa, the anticoagulant effect is independent of antithrombin, has a rapid onset of oral administration, and has less interaction with food and drugs.32 This property of NOACs gives them an advantage in a polypharmacy population with an increased risk of drug interactions.  

Existing evidence suggests that NOACs may interact pharmacokinetically with strong Cytochrome P450 (CYP3A4) and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) inhibitors or inducers, thereby affecting their absorption, distribution, or clearance levels.33 P-gp inhibitors or CYP3A4 inducers (such as verapamil, amiodarone, and rifampin) are drugs that are being used in many patients with AF, although our study did not focus on those drugs at a time when guidelines recommend avoiding or very cautiously combining NOACs with them.34  

Another effect of polypharmacy on adverse outcomes is a decrease in drug adherence. Claxton et al35 showed that drug adherence decreased with increasing frequency of dosing, with adherence of $79\%$ for once-daily medication and only $51\%$ for four-times-daily medication. This result might be attributable to a patient’s loss of trust in medical care or drug intolerance. In any case, decreased drug adherence in patients with AF is associated with increased risks of thromboembolic and bleeding events. There has been no consensus on whether NOAC or VKA drug adherence is higher. On one hand, VKAs require frequent monitoring and dose adjustment, which has been proven associated with higher adherence.36 On the other hand, NOACs (especially rivaroxaban and dabigatran) have higher adherence in AF patients.26,28,37 The latter might partly explain the demonstrated advantages of NOACs over VKAs in terms of effectiveness and safety outcomes in AF patients with polypharmacy.  

# Limitation  

Our meta-analysis had several limitations. First, the thresholds for defining polypharmacy status differed in each included study, and subjects with different baseline characteristics might have significant bias despite statistical adjustments. Second, the included studies only assessed the extent of patients’ polypharmacy at baseline and were unable to adjust drug use for subsequent prescription changes. Fortunately, the burden of co-morbidity in polypharmacy populations is often chronic, and the number of drugs rarely changes significantly. In addition, the study did not take into account nonprescription drugs and health products that patients were taking, which could lead to a potential influence on the results. Third, drug compliance largely determines how patients actually take their medication. Unfortunately, the frequent dose changes of VKAs prevented the study from further evaluating the compliance of the two classes of OACs. Meanwhile, the international normalized ratio monitoring of AF patients determines the stability of OAC therapy, which will directly affect the effectiveness and safety of VKA-related anticoagulant therapy and thus affect the results. Finally, due to the limited amount of data, we were unable to perform head-to-head comparisons between NOAC versus NOAC, and further studies might reveal which NOAC would be more suitable for this population.  

# Conclusion  

This meta-analysis suggested that in patients with AF and polypharmacy, NOACs showed advantages over VKAs in SSE and any bleeding. Additionally, NOACs were found to be comparable to VKAs in terms of major bleeding, ischemic stroke, all-cause death, intracranial hemorrhage, and gastrointestinal bleeding.  

# What is known about this topic?  

• Elderly atrial fibrillation (AF) patients are often accompanied by a huge comorbidity burden and polypharmacy use. • Non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) served as a safer and more effective alternative to warfarin for nonvalvular AF patients. • Several observational studies and post-hoc analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in patients with AF and polypharmacy have been conducted.  

# What does this paper add?  

• In this meta-analysis, we found that patients with moderate and severe polypharmacy were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and major bleeding.   
• Importantly, in patients with AF and polypharmacy, NOACs showed advantages over VKAs in SSE and any bleeding, and noninferiority in major bleeding, ischemic stroke, all-cause death, intracranial hemorrhage, and gastrointestinal bleeding.
