Tirofiban Introduction: Tirofiban is a fast-acting glycoprotein IIb-IIIa inhibitor that inhibits the final common pathway to platelet aggregation and has been studied as adjuvant therapy for acute ischemic stroke (AIS). Since the prior meta-analysis new randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been published. This meta-analysis aimed to update the current knowledge on the efficacy of tirofiban for patients with AIS not submitted to reperfusion therapies. Methods: We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials for RCTs reporting the use of tirofiban in AIS. The efficacy outcomes were favorable functional outcome, functional disability, modified Rankin Scale change at 90 days, and changes in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score after 24 hours and 7 days of the symptom onset. The safety outcomes include symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), any intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and all-cause mortality.   
Results: A higher rate of favorable functional outcome was associated with tirofiban administration $(\mathrm{RR}=1.09\$ ; $95\%$ CI 1.04–1.14; $\mathbf{p}{<}0.001\$ ). The mRS after 90 days was significantly lower in the tirofiban group $(\mathrm{MD}=-0.55\$ $95~\%$ CI −0.90 – [-0.20]; $\mathbf{p}{<}0.01,$ . Tirofiban administration was not significantly associated with higher rates of sICH in AIS patients $\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{RR}=0.85\$ ; $95~\%$ CI $0.26{-}2.81$ ; $\mathbf{p}=0.79\$ ) or any ICH compared to the control group $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{RR}}=$ 1.01; $95~\%$ CI 0.42–2.39; $\mathbf{p}=0.98,$ . All-cause mortality was similar between groups $(\mathrm{RR}=0.64\$ ; $95~\%$ CI 0.34–1.23; $\mathbf{p}=0.18)$ ).  

Conclusion: Tirofiban increases the number of patients achieving a favorable functional outcome in patients. There was no improvement in NIHSS after 24 hours and 7 days. Tirofiban did not increase the risk of sICH or any ICH, and mortality was similar between groups.  

# 1. Introduction  

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is a condition associated with significant morbidity and mortality, becoming the leading cause of death in many countries [1–4]. Although reperfusion therapies with intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy have provided favorable results, the brief time window and procedure-related contraindications may be a limitation in some cases [5].  

Tirofiban is a fast-acting glycoprotein IIb-IIIa inhibitor with a short half-life that may inhibit the final common pathway to platelet aggre­ gation and allow bleeding time to revert to normal within 3 hours approximately after its administration [6,7]. Several clinical studies have tested tirofiban in patients with AIS and obtained promising re­ sults, associated with reperfusion therapies or not [8–11]. The use of intravenous tirofiban was associated with a greater likelihood of an excellent outcome (mRS of 0–1) in patients with recent-onset stroke or progression of stroke symptoms and non-occluded large- and medium-sized cerebral vessels [11].  

Since the latest meta-analysis [8], new randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been published [11-13]. Recently, The Safety and Efficacy of Tirofiban for the Prevention of Neurological Deterioration in Acute Ischemic Stroke (TREND) evaluated the tirofiban use in patients with acute noncardioembolic stroke and a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 4 or more, with promising results [13]. This meta-analysis aimed to update the current knowledge on the efficacy of tirofiban for patients with AIS not submitted to reperfusion therapies.  

# 2. Material and methods  

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook of Systematic Review of Interventions and the Preferred Reporting Items for the Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement guidelines [14,15]. The protocol was preregistered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42024541156).  

# 2.1. Search strategy  

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception to May 2024. The complete search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Two authors (M. L. and B.A.) independently screened titles and abstracts and fully eval­ uated the studies for eligibility. To identify additional literature, we conducted reference mining in the included studies. Discrepancies were settled by discussion with a third author (G.A.M.).  

# 2.2. Eligibility criteria  

We restricted inclusion in this study to articles that met all the following eligibility criteria: (1) RCTs; (2) enrolled participants diag­ nosed with AIS; (3) who underwent the standard medical treatment (SMT); (3) tirofiban use within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ after symptom onset; and (4) re­ ported at least one of the following outcomes of interest: (4.1) favorable functional outcome; (4.2) functional disability; (4.3) change in modified Rankin Scale (mRS); (4.4) change in NIHSS after $24\mathrm{~h~}$ or 7 days after symptoms onset; (4.5) any intracranial hemorrhage (ICH); and (4.6) allcause mortality. We considered SMT the early antiplatelet therapy ac­ cording to the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) recommendations [16]. The studies were excluded if they were published only as conference abstracts, observational studies, re­ views, or studies not written in English. The detailed eligibility criteria of the included studies are described in Supplementary Table S2.  

# 2.3. Outcomes  

The efficacy outcomes are favorable functional outcomes at 90 days, functional disability at 90 days, mRS change at 90 days, and changes in NIHSS score after $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and 7 days of the symptom onset. We defined a favorable functional outcome as a mRS of 0–2 points. Meanwhile, functional disability was defined as mRS of 3–5 points. The safety out­ comes include symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), any intra­ cranial ICH, and all-cause mortality.  

# 2.4. Data extraction  

Two authors (A.M. and G.A.M.) independently extracted data, following predefined search criteria and quality assessment. The data extracted included article characteristics (publication year and authors), population characteristics (age, risk factors, intervention used), inter­ vention characteristics (drug, dosage of intervention, and duration of treatment), and outcomes (mRS, NIHSS, sICH, any ICH, and mortality).  

# 2.5. Quality assessment  

The risk of bias and quality assessment in randomized studies were evaluated with the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment tool version 2 (RoB 2) [17]. Two independent authors (M.L. and G.A.M.) completed the risk of bias assessment. Disagreements were resolved through a consensus after discussing the reasons for the discrepancy.  

# 2.6. Statistical analysis  

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We employed risk ratio (RR) with $95~\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) as the measure of effect size to report binary outcomes. Mean Differences (MD) with $95\%$ confidence intervals were used for continuous outcomes. Heterogeneity was assessed with the Cochran Q test and $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ statistics. P values inferior to 0.10 and $\mathrm{I}^{2}\ge25\%$ were considered significant for heterogeneity. We used the Restricted Maximum Likelihood random-effects model. Sensi­ tivity analyses, employing the leave-one-out approach, were conducted when significant heterogeneity was observed.  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Study selection and characteristics  

A total of 1120 potentially relevant publications were systematically identified. After removing duplicate records and screening titles and abstracts, 18 articles were selected for full-text reading. Ultimately, 8 studies comprising 2784 patients were included (Fig. 1) [9,7,13,18–22]. The detailed characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1.  

# 3.2. Pooled analysis of all studies  

# 3.2.1. Efficacy outcomes  

Seven studies, encompassing 2563 patients reported data for favor­ able functional outcomes [9,11,7,13,18,19,21]. The addition of intra­ venous tirofiban to the SMT was significantly associated with a higher rate of favorable functional outcome $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq2\$ ) than the SMT alone, with a low heterogeneity in the results $(\mathrm{RR}=1.09\$ ; $95~\%$ CI 1.04–1.14; $\mathbf{p}{<}0.001$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=8\%$ ; Fig. 2). Mean change of the mRS after 90 days was reported by four studies and 2082 patients [9,11,13,18]. The mRS after 90 days was significantly lower in the tirofiban group $\mathbf{\left(MD=\right)}$ $-0.55$ ; $95~\%$ CI −0.90 – [-0.20]; $\mathbf{p}{<}0.01$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=83\%$ ; Fig. 4).

# 3.2.2. Safety outcomes  

Tirofiban administration was not significantly associated with higher rates of sICH in AIS patients $\mathbf{\partial}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}{=}0.85$ ; $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.26{-}2.81$ ; $\mathbf{p}=0.79$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ $=15\%$ ; Fig. 6A). The tirofiban group presented similar results regarding the mortality rates ( $\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{R}\mathrm{R}=0.64$ ; $95~\%$ CI 0.34–1.23; $\mathbf{p}=0.18;\mathbf{l}^{2}$ $=57\%$ ; Fig. 7).  

# 3.2.3. Sensitivity analysis  

The functional disability endpoint presented a high heterogeneity. The heterogeneity was resolved after excluding Hu et al. $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=6\%)$ [19], with a significant result favoring tirofiban (Supplementary Figure S1). Mean change in mRS after 90 days also presented a high heterogeneity. Following the sensitivity analysis, after excluding The Efficacy and Safety of Tirofiban in Clinical Patients with Acute Ischemic Stroke (ESCAPIST) trial, the results presented a low heterogeneity $(\mathtt{I}^{2}=10\%)$ (Supplementary Figure S2) [9]. This outcome continued to show a benefit with tirofiban use. NIHSS endpoints also presented a significant heterogeneity which was not possible to resolve with the sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figure S3). Any ICH endpoint presented a significant heterogeneity. After excluding Hu et al. the heterogeneity reduced substantially $(\mathtt{I}^{2}=12\%)$ (Supplementary Figure S4) [19]. Finally, following the leave-one-out approach all-cause mortality was significantly reduced after excluding the Efficacy and Safety of Tirofiban Compared with Aspirin in the Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke trial (RESCUE BT 2), but continued with a significant heterogeneity $(\mathtt{I}^{2}=$ $26\%$ ) (Supplementary Figure S5) [11].

# 3.2.4. Quality assessment  

The individual assessment of each included study is presented in Supplementary Figure S6. Overall, most included studies were consid­ ered as low risk of bias [9,11,7,13,20,21]. Two RCTs raised some bias concerns, mostly due to the selection of the reported results [18,19].  

# 4. Discussion  

In this updated systematic review and meta-analysis of eight RCTs and more than 2000 patients, we assessed the efficacy and safety of intravenous tirofiban for the treatment of AIS. Overall, the tirofiban group was significantly associated with higher favorable functional outcomes. Initially, functional disability was similar between groups, but after the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, tirofiban improved the risk of functional disability. NIHSS score was not significantly altered with the initiation of tirofiban. Tirofiban did not increase the risk of bleeding outcomes. Finally, mortality was similar between groups.  

The administration of tirofiban in AIS patients has been controversial over the years since some trials found no benefit in its administration $[7,$ 23], while others presented contrary results [9,11,13,21]. The Study of Efficacy of Tirofiban in Acute Ischemic Stroke (SETIS) trial evaluated the effectiveness of tirofiban compared to aspirin within a 6-hour treatment window in a multicenter double-blind study [7]. They found no supe­ riority of tirofiban over aspirin in improving neurological outcomes, and the trial was discontinued due to the lack of efficacy.  

According to the last revised guidelines from the AHA/ASA, the ef­ ficacy of the intravenous glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors tirofiban and eptifibatide in the treatment of AIS is not well established [16]. A recent meta-analysis of observational studies evaluated the efficacy of intra­ venous tirofiban in patients who underwent endovascular treatment and presented benefits in improving the rates of favorable functional out­ comes in the experimental group [10]. Although the most recent guideline is cautious regarding tirofiban recommendations, the latest evidence favoring the intravenous glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor should be considered in the near future.  

Several RCTs have shown benefits in the use of tirofiban in AIS [9,11, 13,20]. The recent RESCUE BT 2 trial observed a higher likelihood of excellent outcomes (mRS of 0–1) after 90 days for patients with non-occluded large- and medium-sized cerebral vessels, although nonsignificant in favorable outcome (mRS of 0–2) [11]. The TREND trial similarly observed no significant difference in 90-day mRS, albeit with a lower proportion of patients with worsening NIHSS scores after $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ [13]. Although functional improvement was inconsistent among the included RCTs, tirofiban was significantly associated with a higher rate of favorable functional outcomes in patients in our meta-analysis. Otherwise, tirofiban showed benefits in improving functional disability only after our sensitivity analysis excluding Hu et al., which included only patients with large hemispheric infarction [19].  

Following a previous meta-analysis [8], no immediate improvement in NIHSS scores was associated with tirofiban administration after 24 hours or 7 days in our pooled analysis. Nevertheless, these findings lack robustness due to the persistent high heterogeneity of the results even after the sensitivity analysis, which might be explained by the extremely large variability in baseline NIHSS between studies. There were also significant differences in terms of study design: some studies included patients without large vessel occlusion, while others included large vessel occlusion. These differences should raise caution in inter­ preting the results of the analysis of NIHSS variations.  

Regarding ICH outcomes, the TREND trial observed that a 72-hour treatment of intravenous tirofiban did not increase the risk of ICH or systemic bleeding [13]. However, the RESCUE BT 2 trial observed a higher incidence of sICH after tirofiban therapy. Of note, distinct from the ESCAPIST trial, RESCUE BT 2 enrolled AIS patients with more severe strokes, which could explain the higher rates of any ICH [9,11]. Our meta-analysis did not encounter a significant difference in sICH and any ICH associated with tirofiban therapy. Over the years, tirofiban’s safety profile showed favorable results in AIS patients ineligible for reperfusion therapy and should be taken as a viable option in further recommendations.  

The Safety of Tirofiban in Acute Ischemic Stroke trial (SaTIS) trial was a multicenter study of tirofiban’s safety profile in AIS [20]. Their findings showed promising results, with a significantly lower mortality rate after a 5-month follow-up associated with tirofiban [20]. However, several subsequent trials showed no significant difference in mortality rates related to tirofiban use [9,11,13]. In the previous meta-analysis of randomized studies, mortality was not significantly different between groups [8]. Our pooled analysis follows the same pattern, showing no significant difference in mortality rates.  

In terms of clinical practice, this evidence summary helps the clini­ cian to use tirofiban with greater certainty, especially in patients without large vessel occlusion and of Asian descent. Furthermore, it appears effective in patients who underwent standard medical treatment without reperfusion therapy. Although the most recent guideline is cautious regarding tirofiban recommendations, we believe these rec­ ommendations will be reconsidered soon.  

# 4.1. Limitations  

Some limitations are worth mentioning in this study: (1) most studies were conducted in a single country (China), which has a higher pro­ portion of intracranial artery stenosis. Therefore, these results may not be generalizable to other populations; (2) NIHSS score outcomes lack robustness since they presented few trials included and a high hetero­ geneity which could not be resolved with our leave-one-out sensitivity analysis; (3) the broad inclusion criteria among the included studies did not allow us to perform subgroup analyses based on the stroke severity or etiology; (4) the inclusion of SETIS trial might introduce some bias since they did not exclude patients with large vessel occlusion eligible for reperfusion therapy.  

# 5. Conclusion  

Tirofiban increases the number of patients achieving a favorable functional outcome in patients. There was no improvement in NIHSS after 24 hours and 7 days. Tirofiban did not increase the risk of sICH and any ICH in patients. Mortality was similar between groups.
