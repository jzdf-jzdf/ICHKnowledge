# Abstract  

Objective: To compare the efficacy of intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) and continuous veno-venous hemofiltration (CVVH) in patients with chronic renal failure complicated by massive intracerebral hemorrhage. Methods: Sixty-two patients were randomly and equally divided into IHD and CVVH groups. The clinical variables were compared, including National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score as the primary indicator, cerebral edema volume, hospitalacquired pneumonia (HAP) incidence, acute heart failure (AHF) incidence, rehemorrhage incidence, hospital stay length, and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score. Results: The CVVH group had lower NIHSS scores and edema volumes than the IHD group on postoperative days 7 and 14. Moreover, in the CVVH group, (i) the NIHSS scores on postoperative days 3 and 7 were higher than those on postoperative day 1; (ii) there was no significant difference in NIHSS scores between days 14 and 1; and (iii) no significant difference in cerebral edema volume was found between postoperative days 1 and 3, 7, and 14. In the IHD group, the NIHSS scores and cerebral edema volume on postoperative days 7 and 14 were significantly higher than those on postoperative day 1. The CVVH group had a lower incidence of HAP, AHF, and adverse events and shorter hospital stay length than the IHD group. The proportions of patients with mRS scores of 1 and 2 in the CVVH group were higher than those in the IHD group on day 30 after discharge. Interpretation: CVVH is more effective than IHD in the treatment of patients with chronic renal failure complicated by massive intracerebral hemorrhage.  

# Introduction  

Chronic renal failure (CRF) is a global public health problem and a significant risk factor for cardiovascular events.1,2 Clinical evidence indicates that the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke in patients with chronic kidney disease increases as the glomerular filtration rate decreases.3 Moreover, chronic kidney disease, especially end-stage renal disease, is associated with severe stroke, a baseline volume of cerebral hemorrhage, ventricular involvement, risk of cerebral hemorrhage recurrence, and poor prognosis.4–7 In fact, chronic kidney disease is an independent risk factor for stroke, including the hemorrhagic and ischemic subtypes, and patients with end-stage renal disease who undergo renal replacement therapy have a fourfold to tenfold higher risk of stroke than the general population, including a seven-fold higher risk during the first year of dialysis. Cerebral hemorrhage is a common complication and cause of death in CRF patients undergoing continuous hemodialysis (HD), and its mortality rate is significantly higher than that of cerebral infarction.12 In addition, a high prevalence of cerebral microbleeds has been reported in HD patients.13 The mortality rate of patients with advanced CRF-induced intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) who are undergoing HD is high and increasing every year.14 Notably, CRF complicated by massive ICH poses a challenge in relation to renal replacement HD therapy. As a consequence, there is an urgent clinical need to identify an appropriate HD method for renal replacement therapy in patients with CRF complicated by acute massive ICH as well as to reduce the adverse effects of HD when it comes to the treatment of CRF complicated by ICH.  

HD is intended to normalize the fluid and electrolyte environment within the body, transport urea and other toxic solutes from the blood to the dialysis solutions, and remove excess body water by means of ultrafiltration.15 However, the physiological changes brought about by HD can result in changes in the osmotic pressure, which may lead to a sharp increase in cerebral edema.16,17 Indeed, several studies have found that HD aggravates cerebral edema in patients with cerebral hemorrhage.18 The findings of the previous study also revealed that patients with CRF complicated by large-volume ICH experienced worsening neurological deficits and even cerebral herniation.19 Renal replacement therapy intended to restore renal function and improve survival can be divided into two main types: HD and peritoneal dialysis (PD).20 Globally, HD remains the most common form of dialysis treatment, accounting for around $80\%$ of cases.21 The three most common HD modalities are intermittent hemodialysis (IHD), sustained low-efficient dialysis (SLED), and continuous veno-venous hemofiltration (CVVH).22 Among these modalities, IHD and CVVH have frequently used forms of renal replacement therapy in intensive care unit (ICU) patients.23,24 IHD and CVVH are both associated with certain advantages. For instance, IHD can quickly remove solutes, control the liquid balance, and adjust electrolyte acid–base balance, and it is characterized by a short dialysis time, low medical expenses, and low equipment cost.22 For its part, CVVH is a slow and smooth form of continuous extracorporeal HD that provides hemodynamic stability in patients with severe conditions.25,26 There is currently no clinical evidence concerning which of these two modalities is most beneficial for renal replacement therapy in patients with CRF complicated by massive ICH.  

In the present study, the clinical therapeutic effects of CVVH and IHD in patients with CRF complicated by massive ICH were compared in terms of the level of neurological dysfunction, cerebral edema volume, and complications so as to provide a reference for selecting the appropriate HD mode for such patients.  

# Materials and Methods  

# Ethics statement  

This study was approved by the hospital ethics committee of the Second People’s Hospital of Huai’an (HEYLL201864) and Huai’an First People’s Hospital (KY2023-074-01). Either the patient or their authorized representative signed the treatment consent form prior to treatment.  

# Sample size calculation  

The primary outcome indicator was the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score on day 14 after surgery. The predicted mean NIHSS score in the IHD group was $17\pm3$ , while the CVVH group was expected to have a reduction of 3 points. Based on the two-sided test $(\mathtt{a}=0.05)$ ), the sample size ratio of 1:1, and the power of the test $(1-\upbeta)$ of $90\%$ , the sample size of the two groups was calculated by PASS11 software as $\mathrm{N}1=\mathrm{N}2=22$ cases. Considering the rate of loss to follow-up of $10\%$ , the required sample size was $\mathrm{N}1=\mathrm{N}2=22/0.9=25$ Our sample size of 30 in each group met the requirements. The estimated effect size was the NIHSS score, and the endpoint was the NIHSS score on day 14 after surgery.  

# Patient characteristics  

This randomized, blinded, and prospective study involved 62 patients with CRF complicated by massive ICH who underwent minimally invasive puncture and drainage (MIPD) between May 2018 and June 2020. The patients were randomly divided into the CVVH treatment group and the IHD treatment group at a 1:1 ratio according to the computer randomization method (i.e., there were 31 cases in each group). The randomization was achieved via the use of a web-based system. More specifically, the randomization sequence was computer-generated, while module randomization involving a module size of four or six was used to ensure the masking of the randomization status. Sixty-two out of 160 trial volunteers in the Second People’s Hospital of Huai’an and Huai’an First People’s Hospital were enrolled in this study in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria.  

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients who met the diagnostic criteria for CRF and underwent maintenance HD; (b) patients who met the diagnostic criteria for massive-volume $\mathrm{ICH}^{19}$ ; (c) patients who were admitted to our hospital within $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ of onset; (d) patients with supratentorial bleeding and blood loss of 30–60 mL19,27; and (e) patients with first stroke or a history of stroke without any significant neurological deficits.  

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) rupture and/ or hemorrhage of a cerebral aneurysm or vascular malformation, or cerebral hemorrhage secondary to other systemic diseases (e.g., tumor, blood disease); (b) severe coagulopathy; (c) brainstem function failure at admission (cerebral hernia or deep coma); (d) traumatic intracranial hematoma; (e) severe cardiopulmonary insufficiency; or (f) refusal of further treatment by patients or their family members.  

One patient from each group dropped out of the study. More specifically, one patient in the CVVH group was transferred to the cardiac ICU for further treatment due to a sudden right ventricular myocardial infarction, whereas one patient in the IHD group had to cease participation due to a severe intolerance to dialysis. All the study procedures are summarized in the participant flow diagram presented in Figure 1.  

# General care  

After admission, the patients in the two groups were given a series of medical treatments, including quiet bed rest, blood pressure control, temperature control, blood glucose control, intracranial pressure reduction, nutritional support, and airway management. When the patient’s blood pressure was high, oral or intravenous antihypertensive drugs were used for the purpose of blood pressure control (the goal systolic blood pressure around $120{-}150\mathrm{mmHg}.$ , the goal diastolic blood pressure around $70{-}90\mathrm{mmHg}$ ). # MIPD  

The MIPD procedure was performed within $6{-}24\mathrm{~h~}$ of admission. In this regard, a disposable intracranial hematoma removal device (YL-1, Beijing Wantefu Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used for the electro-driven puncture and drainage under an electric drive, before urokinase was utilized to dissolve and liquefy the hematoma so as to facilitate drainage. Subsequently, the drainage tube was removed after the hematoma was largely cleared and there was no any obvious midline displacement or brain compression. The MIDP procedure was conducted by a neurologist with rich surgical experience, and the neurology intensive care unit nursing team was responsible for the related care. All the nursing staff were well trained and had rich experience in the postoperative nursing of patients who have undergone MIDP.  

# Hemodialysis  

HD was performed on the first day following the MIPD procedure (the second day after admission). Both groups were treated with bicarbonate HD via a femoral vein or arteriovenous vascular access, which was established by puncturing the internal fistula. The patients in the IHD group were treated with the IHD mode using a Fresenius $4008s$ hemofiltration machine (Fresenius Kabi France, Louviers, France). The treatment parameters were as follows: the blood flow rate was $200{-}250\mathrm{mL/min}$ , the dialysate flow rate was $500\mathrm{mL/min}$ , and the ultrafiltration rate was $500{-}600\mathrm{ml/h}$ . Moreover, the duration of the IHD treatment was $^{3\textrm{h}}$ , once a day, although the frequency was changed to once every 2 days at 2 weeks after the MIPD procedure. The patients in the CVVH group were treated with the CVVH mode using Prismaflex dialysis equipment (Gambro, Stockholm, Sweden) and an $\mathrm{M}100$ integrated filter pipeline. The treatment parameters were as follows: the blood flow rate was $150{-}220\mathrm{mL/min}$ , the replacement fluid flow rate was $2000\mathrm{mL/h}$ , the proportion of pre-replacement was $75\%$ , and the ultrafiltration rate was $100{-}300\mathrm{mL/h}$ . In addition, the duration of the CVVH treatment was $12{-}16\mathrm{~h~}$ , once every 2 days, although the treatment method was changed to the IHD mode once every 2 days at 2 weeks after the MIPD procedure. In terms of the anticoagulation treatment, both groups received heparin-free hemodialysis within 2 weeks after the MIPD procedure. Two weeks later, low-molecular-weight heparin anticoagulation was adopted in both groups.  

# Clinical variables  

The primary outcome was the NIHSS score on day 14 after surgery. The following variables at admission were recorded and compared between the two groups: NIHSS score, edema volume, hematoma volume, underlying diseases (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease [CHD]), blood pressure, glucose, ICH score, and location of hemorrhage. On 1, 3, 7, and 14 days after the MIPD procedure, the following clinical variables were recorded and compared between the two groups: postoperative NIHSS score, edema volume, complications (hospital-acquired pneumonia [HAP], and acute heart failure [AHF], rehemorrhage, adverse events). Length of hospital stay and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score on day 30 after discharge were also collected and compared between the two groups. The NIHSS score was used to evaluate the degree of neurological dysfunction. The edema volume was determined in the low-density area around the hematoma, and it was calculated using 3D Slicer software (3D-Slicer4.9.0) according to the coniglobus formula ABC/2 in the cranial computed tomography (CT) scan. More specifically, the DICOM format data concerning each patient’s intracranial CT were guided into the 3D Slicer software, and then the Editor module was selected to complete the 3D reconstruction of the perihematomal edema. The edema area was automatically identified and marked by setting the threshold (20–33 HU), and subsequently, the volume was calculated. HAP refers to pneumonia that occurs in patients who do not have an infection or are not in the incubation period upon admission, but rather develops after $^{48\mathrm{~h~}}$ of hospitalization, including pneumonia that occurs within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ after discharge as a result of hospital-acquired infection. The diagnosis criteria of HAP was based on the guidelines for the management of adults with hospital-acquired, ventilator-associated, and healthcare-associated pneumonia by the American Thoracic Society in 2005, which were as follows: new or progressive infiltrative shadows on Xray chest radiographs, combined with two of the three clinical symptoms (body temperature ${>}38$ , increased or decreased white blood cell count, or purulent sputum).28 The diagnostic criteria for AHF were based on the 2016 ESC Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure.29 The adverse events recorded included hypothermia, hypotension (systolic blood pressure ${<}90\mathrm{mmHg}$ and/or diastolic blood pressure ${<}60\mathrm{mmHg}$ ), bloodstream infection, pipeline clotting, bleeding, and electrolyte disturbance. The patients’ clinical variables, including the NIHSS score, edema volume, HAP, AHF, rehemorrhage, length of stay, and mRS score on day 30 after discharge, were measured by neurologists and radiologists from other hospitals who were unaware of our study. All the evaluators received standard training.  

# Statistical analysis  

The measurement data with a normal distribution were expressed as the mean $\pm$ standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for the normality testing. For the inter-group comparison, the $F$ -test was used to determine the homogeneity of variance, while the t-test was performed to analyze the difference between two independent samples. For the intra-group comparison, Levene’s test was used to determine the homogeneity of variance, while a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed to compare the differences among multiple groups. The count data were expressed as percentages, and the comparisons between groups were performed using the Pearson chi-square test or the continuity correction chisquare test. A $p<0.05$ was considered statistically significant. The utilized statistical software was R 4.05 (http:// www.r-project.org/).

As shown in Table 3, the incidences of both HAP $(13.3\%)$ and AHF $(13.3\%)$ in the CVVH group were significantly lower than those in the IHD group (HAP: $50\%$ , AHF: $40\%$ ) $(p<0.01\$ and $p<0.05$ , respectively). # Comparison of adverse events between the two groups  

Six patients in the IHD group experienced hypotension, with the patients’ hypotensive symptoms being relieved by reducing the blood flow and ultrafiltration (Table 3). No hypotension occurred in the CVVH group. There were no other adverse events associated with HD, such as hypothermia, bloodstream infection, pipeline coagulation, bleeding, or electrolyte disorder, in either of the two groups. Therefore, the incidence of adverse events in the CVVH group was significantly lower than that in the IHD group $(p<0.05)$ (Table 3).  

# Comparison of the mRS scores between the two groups on day 30 after discharge  

The functional outcomes in the two groups were evaluated on the basis of the mRS score, which serves as a global outcome rating scale after stroke.30,31 Our data showed that the proportions of patients with mRS scores of 1 and 2 in the CVVH group were significantly higher than those in the IHD group on day 30 after discharge $(p<0.05)$ (Fig. 8), which suggests CVVH treatment to be advantageous in terms of reducing disability rates.  

# Discussion  

The optimal mode of dialysis for patients with CRF complicated by spontaneous ICH remains controversial. The present study found CVVH to be an advantageous HD method for treating CRF complicated by massive ICH due to it both decreasing the probability of early neurological worsening and improving the functional outcomes.  

Our results showed that, when compared with IHD, the CVVH treatment reduced the effects of HD on the neurological deficit symptoms and cerebral edema volumes in Asian patients with CRF complicated by acute massive ICH. We considered the main factors likely to

Prior studies have shown that the incidence of HAP among ICU patients with acute spontaneous ICH is as high as $60\%$ .37 Our results revealed that the CVVH treatment reduced the incidence of HAP in patients with CRF complicated by massive ICH when compared with the IHD treatment $13.3\%$ vs $50\%$ ). The main reason may be that CVVH can reduce the effects of HD on cerebral edema and neurological deficits, thus reducing the disturbance of consciousness and dysphagia. Disturbance of consciousness and dysphagia are risk factors for HAP.38–40 Patients with severe disturbance of consciousness exhibit weakened cough reflex, decreased sputum capacity, easy aspiration, dysphagia accompanied by nasal feeding, and easy reflux, all of which can lead to an increased incidence of HAP. As common types of HAP, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and healthcare-associated pneumonia are mainly attributed to pneumonia, pulmonary edema, respiratory distress syndrome, and atelectasis, which indicates that HAP may be associated with pulmonary edema.41 On that basis, the question of whether CVVH affects pulmonary edema needs to be addressed in further studies. In addition, this study determined that the incidence of AHF in the CVVH group was $13.3\%$ , which was significantly lower than the incidence of $40\%$ found in the IHD group. This may be mainly due to the better control of the fluid balance by CVVH. Consistent with this, the ultrafiltration rates and volumes in the CVVH group were noticeably higher than those in the IHD group.  

In the present study, low-molecular-weight heparin anticoagulant therapy was started for the patients in the two groups at 2 weeks after the MIPD procedure. The results showed that the incidence of rehemorrhage in the IHD group was $10\%$ , while in the CVVH group, it was $6.67\%$ . The difference in prevalence between the two groups was not statistically significant, which was similar to the findings concerning the incidence of rehemorrhage after the minimally invasive removal of cerebral hemorrhage.42 This suggests that CVVH treatment does not significantly increase the incidence of rehemorrhage.

Due to the long duration of CVVH treatment, the large amount of replacement fluid, and the frequent need to rinse the dialysis machine and pipeline during heparinfree HD, the adverse events of HD are theoretically more likely to occur and affect the efficacy of CVVH treatment. However, the adverse event monitoring in this study showed that no HD-related adverse events such as hypothermia, hypotension, bloodstream infection, pipeline coagulation, bleeding, and electrolyte disturbance occurred in the CVVH group. In the IHD group, 6 patients experienced hypotension, which was mainly related to the large blood flow and the large ultrafiltration volumes per hour. The symptoms of hypotension were relieved by reducing the blood flow and ultrafiltration volumes. The lengths of hospital stay in the CVVH group were significantly lower than in the IHD group, while the proportions of patients with mRS scores of 1 and 2 were significantly higher than in the IHD group on day 30 after discharge. These results further confirm that CVVH treatment can decrease the probability of early neurological worsening, reduce the disability rates. and improve patients’ overall prognosis.  

It must be acknowledged that this study had several limitations. First, the sample size was small, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power. Second, the short follow-up period in this study did not adequately reflect the patients’ long-term prognosis. Third, the study population was limited to Asians. Fourth, the study design was not comprehensive with regard to the assessment of adverse events and complications. Given these limitations, a larger sample size and study population; a multicenter, randomized, blinded, controlled trial; and a more comprehensive experimental design are required for further validation of the present results.  

In conclusion, Compared with IHD treatment, CVVH treatment reduced the effects of HD on neurological dysfunction and cerebral edema, decreased the incidence of HAP and AHF, shortened the lengths of hospital stay, reduced the incidence of adverse events, reduced the disability rates, and improved the patients’ prognosis. Therefore, CVVH should be the preferred treatment mode for Asian HD patients with CRF complicated by acute massive ICH.
