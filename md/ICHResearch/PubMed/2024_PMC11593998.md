# Anti-Inflammatory Thrombolytic JX10 (TMS-007) in Late Presentation of Acute Ischemic Stroke  

Kuniyasu Niizuma , MD\*; Naoko Nishimura, PhD\*; Keiko Hasegawa, BSc; Takashi Moritoyo , MD; Kohsuke Kudo , MD; Josh Bell, MD; Michael Wald , PhD; Yoshifumi Umeda, MSc; Kazuhiko Kuribayashi , PhD; Yasuo Toda , MD; Teiji Tominaga , MD; Keiji Hasumi , PhD  

BACKGROUND: Contemporary thrombolytics in acute ischemic stroke are limited to administration within 4.5 hours of last known normal. JX10 (formerly TMS-007), a Stachybotrys microspora triprenyl phenol family member, may extend this therapeutic window.  

METHODS: In this multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation phase 2a study, JX10 or placebo was administered as a single intravenous infusion to Japanese patients with acute ischemic stroke who were unable to receive tissue-type plasminogen activator or thrombectomy within 12 hours of last known normal. Primary end point was incidence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage with a worsening National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of ${\ge}4$ points within 24 hours of drug administration (symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage incidence).  

RESULTS: Ninety patients received either placebo $\scriptstyle\left({\mathtt{n}}=38\right)$ ; female $26.3\%$ ) or $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ at 1, 3, or $6{\mathrm{~mg}}/{\mathsf{kg}}$ $\scriptstyle\bigcap=\varnothing,$ 18, 28; female $0\%$ , $33.3\%$ and $42.9\%$ , respectively). Median age (range) and baseline median (range) National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores were respectively 76.5 (42–87) and 8 (6–21) for the combined JX10 cohort (JX10 Cohorts) and 75.0 (34–85) and 8 (6–22) for placebo. Median (range) dosing time since last known normal was 9.5 (5.0–12.1) and 10.0 (3.7–12.0) hours for JX10 Cohorts and placebo, respectively. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage incidence was $0\%$ (0/52 $[95\%$ CI, 0.0–5.6]) for JX10 Cohorts versus $2.6\%$ (1/38 $[95\%$ CI, 0.1–13.8]) for placebo $:P{=}0.42,$ ). Vessel patency at 24 hours (secondary end point) in patients with baseline arterial occlusive lesion score ${<}3$ (39/90) improved in $58.3\%$ (14/24) of patients in $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ Cohorts versus $26.7\%$ (4/15) for placebo (odds ratio, 4.23 $[95\%$ CI, 0.99–18.07]). In $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ Cohorts, a significantly higher proportion of patients had modified Rankin Scale scores of 0 to 1 on day 90 (secondary end point) versus placebo (JX10: 21/52, $40.4\%$ versus placebo: 7/38, $18.4\%$ ; $\widetilde{\calP{=}}0.03$ ).  

CONCLUSIONS: JX10 was well tolerated and may expand the acute ischemic stroke therapeutic window as a novel thrombolytic agent.

Rendovascular thrombectomy, is the current stan(AIS).1,2 However, the currently approved thrombolytic, tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA), is only administered to a minority $(\approx10\%{-}20\%)$ of patients with AIS because of the limited treatment window and excess intracerebral hemorrhage risk.1 tPA must be started within 4.5 hours after stroke symptom onset and after radiographic exclusion of intracranial hemorrhage; later administration risks excess bleeding in the absence of meaningful benefits.3,4 Other studies have shown that advanced neuroimaging approaches may identify subpopulations who could benefit from thrombolysis beyond the 4.5-hour window.5,6 However, these imaging procedures and equipment are not readily available at many hospitals. The risk of intracerebral hemorrhage remains high for those receiving tPA.

Tenecteplase, an alteplase (tPA) variant originally approved for treatment of acute myocardial infarction, is increasingly being used for AIS due to its practical administration advantages, despite not being approved for use in AIS and the need for more studies to prove its efficacy.7,8 In the recently reported TIMELESS trial (Thrombolysis in Imaging Eligible, Late Window Patients to Assess the Efficacy and Safety of Tenecteplase) of patients with AIS presenting between 4.5 and 24 hours after last known normal (LKN) with imaging evidence of salvageable tissue, tenecteplase failed to demonstrate superiority over placebo.9 Given the limitations of tPAbased approaches, an improved thrombolytic therapy for use in a broad AIS patient population with an extended time window is urgently needed.  

The small molecule JX10 (formerly TMS-007) is a member of the Stachybotrys microspora triprenyl phenol family, derived from the fungus S microspora.10 Preclinical studies indicated that JX10 has a novel dual mode of action (MOA) distinct from tPA and tenecteplase. JX10 promotes physiological fibrinolysis by inducing a conformational change in plasminogen (from closed to open), facilitating its binding to fibrin, and increasing the action of endogenous plasminogen activators to activate fibrinbound plasminogen to plasmin.10–13 JX10 also inhibits soluble epoxide hydrolase, believed to suppress inflammation at the site of thrombosis.10,14,15  

Results from preclinical studies in various embolic and thrombotic stroke models indicated lower bleeding risk with JX10 than tPA owing to its promotion of physiological fibrinolysis and anti-inflammatory activity.10 Based on this nonclinical pharmacological evidence, the novel dual MOA of $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ may extend the treatment time window for patients with AIS.16,17  

In a phase 1 first-in-human study (jRCT2080222603) of healthy male volunteers, JX10 showed favorable safety and pharmacokinetic profiles at doses of 3, 15, 60, 180, and 360 mg (corresponding to dose-body weight ratios of 0.045, 0.25, 0.91, 2.87, and $5.86\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg},$ respectively).18 Here, we present findings from a phase 2a dose-escalation trial (jRCT2080223786; formerly JapicCTI-183842) of $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ in Japanese patients with AIS unable to receive tPA or thrombectomy who received study treatment within 12 hours of LKN.  

# METHODS  

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.  

# Study Design  

This phase 2a, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation trial in Japanese patients with acute cerebral infarction evaluated the safety, efficacy, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic profiles of $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ versus placebo within 12 hours of LKN. Forty-one Japanese sites participated (Table S1). See Supplemental Material for protocol amendments.  

Eligible patients enrolled into 1 of 3 consecutive cohorts and received 1, 3, or $6~\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg}$ (cohorts 1, 2, and 3, respectively) $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ (maximum dose of $360~\mathrm{mg},$ ) or placebo. The first patients to be enrolled were treated in cohort 1. After enrollment of 9 patients $(\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ : $_{\mathsf{n}=\mathsf{6}}$ ; placebo: $\scriptstyle\mathsf{n}=3$ ) in cohort 1 and 27 (JX10: $_{\mathsf{n}=18}$ ; placebo: $\scriptstyle\ngeq9$ ) in cohort 2, the Independent Data Monitoring Committee (Table S2) evaluated safety data to confirm progression of enrollment to the $6{\mathrm{~mg}}/{\mathsf{kg}}$ dose. If safety concerns were detected, a dose reduction or suspension of drug administration for the next cohort was considered. Cohort 3 was to enroll 54 patients $(\mathsf{J}\times10\colon\mathsf{n}{=}27$ ; placebo: $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{n}}=27$ ).  

The trial was conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Act No. 145, and the Standards for the Implementation of Clinical Trials on Pharmaceutical Products, with oversight by each site’s institutional review board and the Independent Data Monitoring Committee. The sponsors and academic authors were involved in study design and execution, data collection, analysis, and interpretation, and the decision to submit for publication, and had full access to all data. This article follows CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) reporting guidelines19 (Supplemental Material).  

# Patients  

Patients were Japanese males aged 20 to 88 years or females aged 60 to 88 years (excluding those of child-bearing potential, based on the current unavailability of dedicated nonclinical reproductive and developmental toxicity testing with JX10) with clinical signs and symptoms of AIS as identified by the site investigators based on findings from magnetic resonance angiography and diffusion-weighted imaging (mostly in combination with computed tomography findings) together with neurological examination (post hoc analysis confirmed that patients with stroke mimics were excluded). Patients aged ${>}88$ years were excluded due to concerns that baseline functional impairment due to aging might not be distinguished from that occurring as sequelae of AIS. Enrolled patients were unable to receive tPA or endovascular thrombectomy (see Table S3 for reasons), as determined by the treating physician, and able to start treatment within 12 hours after LKN.  

Patients had a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 6 to 23 points and a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) diffusion-weighted imaging-Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (11-point method) of ${\ttz5}$ at screening. Visible occlusion was not required. In cohort 3, enrollment of patients with suspected lacunar infarction was limited to $50\%$ , ensuring inclusion of patients with cardioembolic and atherothrombotic strokes. Infarction subtype diagnosis, including lacunar infarction, was determined by investigators based on clinical and imaging data obtained at screening20; perfusion imaging or diffusion-weighted imaging/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery mismatch was not used for patient selection. Patients with active systemic or intracranial hemorrhage, suspected subarachnoid hemorrhage, or a baseline international normalized ratio of prothrombin time ${\ge}2.6$ were excluded. See Supplemental Material for all exclusion criteria. Written informed consent was provided by the patient or their legal representative.  

# Randomization and Masking  

Eligible patients were randomly allocated in a ratio of 2:1 (cohorts 1 and 2) and 1:1 (cohort 3) to $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ or placebo by the Interactive Web Response System. $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ was evaluated under double-blind conditions (Supplemental Material).  

# Procedures  

$\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ or placebo was administered intravenously by 1-minute rapid infusion ( $10\%$ of the dose), followed by 30-minute continuous infusion (remaining $90\%$ ), starting within 12 hours after LKN. See Supplemental Material for details of treatment administration and permitted concomitant medications/treatments.  

All adverse events (AEs) observed or reported by patients during the study period were assessed by the investigator.  

Patients underwent assessment for modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at screening (before symptom onset) and day 90, and NIHSS score at screening and as needed until 24 hours after the start of study drug administration, then at 48 hours, and 7, 14, and 90 days. Infarct volume, occurrence of hemorrhage, and vessel patency were assessed by MRI at screening, day 2, and day 14, although perfusion MRI at screening was not mandatory. Japanese-version mRS score assessment standards were used.  

For pharmacokinetic analyses, blood samples were collected at 31 minutes and 2 hours after study drug administration. For pharmacodynamic blood marker analysis, blood samples were collected before study drug administration and 2 or 24 hours afterward. See Supplemental Material for further details.  

# Outcomes  

Primary end point was the incidence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH; type 1 or 2 parenchymal hematoma)  

with a deterioration of ${\ge}4$ points in the NIHSS, an end point used in evaluating hemorrhagic transformation after ischemic stroke,21–23 up to 24 hours after $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ administration.  

Secondary end points included occurrence of symptomatic and asymptomatic intracranial hemorrhage,24 occurrence of extracranial hemorrhage and major bleeding, change in cerebral infarct volume, recanalization rate on magnetic resonance angiography, patients with mRS score 0 to 1 and 0 to 2 on day 90, mRS outcome on day 90, improvement from baseline in NIHSS by ${\ge}4$ points within 24 hours and 14 and 90 days, AEs, deaths occurring during the study period, and blood pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic markers. Details of the assessments are described in the Supplemental Material.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Since this study was exploratory, $P$ values of the hypothesis tests and CIs were intended to help understand and estimate clinically significant changes; significance levels were set to $5\%$ for 2-sided tests. No adjustments for multiplicity or imputation for missing data were considered. Given the small sample sizes, all $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ cohorts were combined to obtain stable estimates when appropriate. See Supplemental Material for additional details on the sample size and statistical analysis.  

# RESULTS  

# Participant Characteristics  

Between February 21, 2018, and November 11, 2020, informed consent was obtained from 91 eligible patients. Of these, 90 were enrolled and received study treatment (Figure 1). Three patients discontinued owing to AEs not related to treatment resulting in death $(\mathrm{J}\times103\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{kg}$ : cardiac/renal failure $[\mathfrak{n}{=}1$ ]; placebo: secondary cerebral infarction and brain stem hemorrhage $\scriptstyle[\ n=2]$ ). All 90 patients $(\mathsf{J})\times10\colon\mathsf{n}{=}52$ ; placebo: $_{\mathsf{n}=38}$ ) were included in the safety and full analysis sets. Patient baseline characteristics were similar between groups (Table 1; Tables S4 and S5), with median dosing time since LKN of 9.5 (5.0–12.1) and 10.0 (3.7–12.0) hours for JX10 and placebo, respectively.  

# Safety  

No sICH events were observed within 24 hours after treatment administration with JX10; 2 were observed with placebo (Table 2). Incidence of sICH with a deterioration of ${\ge}4$ points in NIHSS score within 24 hours after treatment administration (primary end point) was $0\%$ (0/52 $[95\%$ CI, 0.0–5.6]) with $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ versus $2.6\%$ (1/38 $[95\%$ CI, 0.1–13.8]) with placebo $ \mathrm{\'}P{=}0.42\$ ). Intracranial hemorrhage events (both symptomatic and asymptomatic) within 24 hours after treatment administration were reported in both groups (JX10: $11.5\%$ [6/52]; placebo: $13.2\%$ [5/38]). Occurrence of extracranial bleeding up to day 7 was $21.2\%$ (11/52 $[95\%$ CI, 11.1–34.7]) for JX10 versus $13.2\%$ (5/38 $[95\%$ CI, 4.4–28.1]) for placebo $\mathrm{\Delta}P{=}0.41$ ); the most common site was hematuria (including a traumatic hematuria). No major bleeding events (as assessed by site investigators) occurred in either group up to day 7.

AEs totaled 207 (47/52 patients) in the combined JX10 cohort and 162 (34/38 patients) in the placebo group; AEs related to treatment were reported by 10 of 52 patients and by 11 of 38 patients in the combined JX10 and placebo cohorts, respectively (Table S6). Constipation was the most common reported AE $38.5\%$ [20/52] and $31.6\%$ [12/38], respectively) but was not considered related to JX10 (Table S7).  

# Efficacy  

In the JX10 combined group, $40.4\%$ (21/52) of patients achieved an mRS score of 0 to 1 on day 90 versus $18.4\%$ (7/38) in the placebo group (adjusted OR, 3.34 $[95\%$ CI, 1.11–10.07]; $\widetilde{P{=}0.03}$ ; Table 3; Figure 2). mRS score of 0 to 2 on day 90 was achieved by $53.8\%$ (28/52) of patients in the combined JX10 cohort versus $36.8\%$ (14/38) in the placebo group (OR, 2.01 $[95\%$ CI, 0.83–4.86]; $_{P=0.12,}$ ).  

NIHSS scores decreased from baseline in all groups over time, with an increase over time in the proportion of patients with an improvement from baseline of ${\ge}4$ points (Table S8). On days 14 and 90 after administration, the improvement rate of ${\ge}4$ points from baseline in the NIHSS score was higher in the $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ cohort than in the placebo group (Table S8). The median change in cerebral infarct volume from baseline to day 14 by MRI did not differ between groups (Table S8).  

In patients with baseline arterial occlusive lesion score ${<}3$ $43.3\%$ [39/90]), recanalization (any improvement in arterial occlusive lesion score), as evaluated by a central neuroradiologist (blinded to treatment assignment), occurred in $58.3\%$ (14/24) of patients in the combined JX10 cohort versus $26.7\%$ (4/15) in the placebo group (OR, 4.23 $[95\%$ CI, 0.99–18.07]; Figure 3A). See Figure 3B and 3C for representative cases. Assessment of recanalization at 24 hours per site investigator in patients with visible occlusions at baseline $38.9\%$ [35/90]) is shown in Table S9.  

# Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics  

Plasma JX10 levels increased with dose; plasma concentrations at 31 minutes after JX10 administration confirmed linearity across a dose range of 1 to $6~\mathrm{mg}/\AA$ kg (Figure S1).  

Pharmacodynamic analyses of blood markers of thrombolysis and inflammation 24 hours after treatment administration indicated that plasma levels of fibrinogen, ${\bf{a}}_{2}$ -antiplasmin, and dihydroxyeicosatrienoic acid were not affected by $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ treatment (Figure S2).  

# DISCUSSION  

In this phase 2a dose-escalation study of patients with AIS unable to receive tPA or endovascular thrombectomy, JX10 was well tolerated with encouraging efficacy signals. Specifically, JX10-treated patients showed improved functional outcomes and vessel recanalization without any increase in sICH, despite a median treatment time of 9.5 hours since LKN.  

This study enrolled a heterogeneous AIS patient population, including a mix of stroke subtypes; since patients were unable to receive tPA or thrombectomy, JX10 treatment efficacy assessment was unbiased by standard recanalization therapies. Thus, the results reported here may potentially be applicable to a wide range of patients with AIS.

tPA-based therapy is only recommended for use up to 4.5 hours since LKN,23 with the new modified version, tenecteplase, also failing to extend the treatment window.7,9 Here, our data suggest potential of JX10 as a late-window thrombolytic, with observed improvements in both recanalization and functional outcomes, with no excess hemorrhage (0/52 events of sICH with deterioration of ${\ge}4$ points in NIHSS score within 24 hours with JX10, versus 1/38 events with placebo). This contrasts with results from studies with tPA.25,26 There was no notable increase in hemorrhagic incidences compared with placebo in a moderately severe AIS population (median baseline NIHSS score 8)—a population not considered to be at particularly low risk for sICH. The putative antiinflammatory effects of JX10 may have contributed to this reduced hemorrhage risk.14,15 Additionally, administration of concomitant medications, including directacting oral anticoagulants, was allowed. This contrasts with clinical studies with tPA, which excluded patients taking oral anticoagulants.5,6,27 Although based on a relatively small, predominantly male population, these results are encouraging in a study population considered difficult to treat. These results also suggest the potential use of $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ to reduce stroke-related disability in regions without access to the more advanced hospital facilities or to be given alongside thrombectomy to provide earlier reperfusion or improve the degree of reperfusion.28

therapies.30 However, tenecteplase with or without thrombectomy failed to definitively demonstrate superiority over placebo over the extended time window despite the use of this imaging approach.9 Our efficacy results with JX10 within a 12-hour window suggest a potential use of JX10 in the treatment of patients in this context.  

JX10 suggested improvements in vessel patency on MRI in the subset of patients with visible occlusions at baseline. Although no statistically significant differences were observed, likely due to the small sample of evaluable lesions, a higher recanalization rate was observed in the combined $\mathsf{J}\mathsf{X}10$ cohort versus placebo. Based on the proposed dual MOA of JX10, visible occlusions were not required for enrollment. Notably, JX10 is expected to be more efficacious in small vessel occlusions due to their relatively larger endothelial surface area to release endogenous plasminogen activators (promoting profibrinolytic action).  

In this study, the functional outcome (mRS score, $0{-}1$ ) rate at 90 days of $40.4\%$ in the combined JX10 cohort was significantly higher than with placebo $(18.4\%)$ , with no distinct increase in hemorrhage. This functional independence was achieved in patients unable to receive tPA or thrombectomy who may have a higher risk for poor functional outcome.  

Consistent with the previous phase 1 study,18 JX10 was generally well tolerated up to 12 hours since LKN across this broad AIS population, and AEs were reported for ${\approx}90\%$ of patients in both the JX10 and placebo groups. These findings suggest that JX10 may have a tolerable safety profile as a late-window thrombolytic agent for AIS treatment.  

tPA promotes plasminogen activation not only in fibrin clots but also by systemically inducing a decrease in key hemostatic proteins, including plasminogen, ${\bf{a}}_{2}^{.}$ - antiplasmin, and fibrinogen, which in turn increases bleeding risk.10 No noteworthy variations in pharmacodynamic markers (fibrinolytic-coagulation system parameters and inflammatory parameters) were observed after JX10 treatment, consistent with the phase 1 study.10,18 One possible explanation is that JX10 exerts only a minor effect on the systemic fibrinolytic system and mostly promotes localization of endogenous plasminogen to fibrin to enhance thrombolysis.  

# Limitations  

A broad Japanese patient population was enrolled, limiting generalizability especially to Western populations. However, we enrolled a high-risk population, particularly as Asian individuals are at high risk of bleeding after thrombolytic therapies.31 The insufficient number of patients with internal carotid artery or M1 occlusions limited any conclusions on efficacy in large vessel occlusion, and larger trials including patients eligible for endovascular thrombectomy are needed. Predominance of male patients (potentially exacerbated by the exclusion of female patients aged ${<}60$ years), exclusion of patients aged ${>}88$ years, and the cap of $50\%$ lacunar infarcts at enrollment may have introduced biases. Larger studies are, therefore, required to confirm these findings. However, baseline demographics and outcomes for the study population generally align with the literature.5 An NIHSS upper limit of 23 was set for safety reasons but may also have introduced some bias. Safety and efficacy conclusions are limited by the small sample size, which might also explain the low frequency of sICH in this trial. The 3 dosing cohorts were appropriately combined, but a lack of randomization among them challenged dosing comparisons. No dose-dependent effects were detected, including in the pharmacodynamic biomarkers, and sample timing and measuring sensitivity, combined with the limited number of patients and heterogeneity of pathology among patients, may have contributed to this.  

Due to ethics, a direct comparison of JX10 with tPA treatment was not possible.  

# Conclusions  

The present study supports further investigation of JX10 to confirm benefits and risks in larger and broader populations. JX10 has a novel dual MOA that may be beneficial in a wide AIS patient population while extending the time window for thrombolytic therapy.
