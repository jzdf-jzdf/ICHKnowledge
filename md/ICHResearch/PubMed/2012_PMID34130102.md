# Aspirin Use and Risk of Subdural Hematoma: Updated Meta-Analysis of Randomized Trials  

Robert G. Hart, MD,a Lesly A. Pearce, MS,b Philip B. Gorelick, MD, MPH,c Ben J. Connolly, BS,d and Luciana Catanese, MDe  

Background and Purpose: Subdural hematomas are an uncommon, but a serious, bleeding complication of antithrombotic therapies. We update our previous inconclusive meta-analysis to better estimate the risk of subdural hematoma associated with aspirin use. Methods: For the initial meta-analysis, nine randomized trials published between1980 and 2012 comparing aspirin with placebo/control were considered. Additional data from four large primary prevention trials were added. Two reviewers independently extracted data on subdural hematomas, with differences resolved by joint review and consensus. Results: Numbers of subdural hematoma were available from thirteen randomized trials involving 155,554 participants comparing aspirin (dosage range $25~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily to $325~\mathrm{mg}$ daily) to placebo (ten double-blind trials) or no aspirin (three trials). Participants included healthy healthcare providers, older people with vascular risk factors without manifest vascular disease, and those with atrial fibrillation or chronic angina. Pooling all trials, subdural hematomas were identified in 93 of 77,698 participants assigned to aspirin versus 62 of 77,856 participants assigned to placebo/no aspirin. By meta-analysis, the relative risk ratio $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{meta}}$ of subdural hematoma associated with assignment to aspirin was 1.5 $(95\%{\mathrm{CI~}}1.1,2.0,p=0.01;p=0.9$ for heterogeneity, $\mathrm{I}^{2}\mathrm{index}=0\%$ ). Based on recent primary prevention trials, subdural hematoma diagnosis averaged 1 per 3,125 people per year without aspirin use; the absolute increase associated with aspirin use was estimated as one additional subdural hematoma per 6,500 patients annually. Conclusions: This meta-analysis confirms that aspirin use increases the relative risk of subdural hematoma, but the absolute increased rate associated with aspirin therapy is very low for most people.

# Introduction  

Subdural hematomas are collections of blood between the dura mater and the surface of the brain usually resulting from bleeding from bridging leptomeningeal veins. Subdural hematomas are not benign, associated with a 15 to $20\%$ mortality and often requiring neurosurgical treatment and prompting discontinuation of antithrombotic therapy that can lead to thrombotic events.13 The frequency of subdural hematoma in the general population is increasing.2  

Our previous meta-analysis of the effect of aspirin on subdural hematoma was inconclusive.4 It included nine randomized trials involving heterogeneous patient populations published through 2012 that reported a total of 21 subdural hematomas, not permitting aspirin’s effect to be reliably estimated. (Table)(4) Since then, four additional large primary prevention trials (i.e. involving participants without clinically manifest vascular events) comparing aspirin with placebo or no antithrombotic therapy have reported subdural hematoma as an outcome, increasing the available data by more than seven-fold. Here, we update our earlier meta-analysis to better define the risk of subdural hematoma associated with aspirin use.  



# Methods  

For our earlier meta-analysis, randomized clinical trials comparing long-term ( ${\bf\zeta}_{>1}$ month) aspirin with placebo or control (i.e. no antithrombotic therapy) were sought by a computerized search of the Stroke group trials Register (a subset of the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) from 1980 to 2012 (the era of modern neuroimaging required to reliably diagnose subdural hematoma), not restricted by language.4 In addition, the reference lists of the Antithrombotic Trialists’ Collaboration5,6 were reviewed. For this update, we reviewed primary prevention trials subsequently published that compared aspirin with placebo or control that were identified by five highquality systematic reviews.7-11 For trials that did not report the occurrence of subdural hematoma, we contacted the investigators to request unpublished data.  

Three reviewers (RGH, LC, BC) independently reviewed the published extracted data, with disagreements resolved by joint review. All subdural hematomas, acute and chronic, traumatic and atraumatic, based on intention-to-treat analysis were included.  



# Data Synthesis  

Meta-analyses of the trial results are presented as relative risks $\left(\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{meta}}\right)$ and their $95\%$ intervals (CI). Estimates of $\mathrm{RR}_{\mathrm{meta}}$ were computed assuming a fixed effects model with the assumption of statistical homogeneity of the treatment effect (across trials) tested using the $\mathrm{Q}$ statistic. The fixed effects model was chosen due to the infrequency of events, and if the number of subdural hematoma observed for exactly one of the treatment arms of a trial was 0, then 0.5 was added to each of the four cells in the corresponding 2 by 2 table.12 Heterogeneity across trials was also evaluated using the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ index (percentage of the total variability in a set of effect sizes due to betweenstudies variability). A sensitivity analysis restricted to results of double-blind trials was done. An estimate of the total years of follow-up in each treatment arm across trials was made by first multiplying the mean follow-up of participants by the number of participants in each trial, and then summing the total years of follow-up across trials. A pooled estimate of the annualized incidence rate of subdural hematoma in each treatment arm was then computed by dividing the total number of subdural hematomas reported across relevant trials for a treatment by the estimate of the total years of follow-up for that treatment. The $95\%C\mathrm{I}$ for the rate was computed assuming a Poisson distribution. All CIs and $p$ -values are twosided; alpha of ${\le}0.05$ was accepted as statistically significant. Software used for the meta-analysis was MedCalc for Windows, version 19.6 (MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium).  



# Results  

The thirteen included randomized trials (summarized in the Supplemental Appendix) involved 155,554 total participants with 155 subdural hematomas reported. (Table) Nine (including the largest) were primary prevention trials, mainly involving those with vascular risk factors (e.g. diabetes, diastolic hypertension) or advanced age.(Table) Ten had double-blinded designs. Among the primary prevention trials, the mean age of the study cohorts ranged from 53 to 74 years, while mean cohort ages ranged from 65 to 67 years in trials involving patients with atrial fibrillation (two trials), chronic stable angina, or recent ischemic stroke. Aspirin dosages ranged from $25~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily to $325~\mathrm{mg}$ daily, with most participants given $100\mathrm{mg}$ daily.(Table) Mean ages, treatments, or outcomes of participants with subdural hematoma not were available.

# Discussion  

Subdural hematoma is an uncommon, but serious, complication of antithrombotic therapies. The best available estimate of the increased risk of subdural hematoma associated with aspirin use is based on meta-analysis of the 13 randomized trials included in our analysis: a $50\%$ increase. Importantly, this substantial relative risk increase translates into a very low absolute risk increase for most people.  

Observational studies are conflicting regarding whether aspirin use is associated with an increased risk of subdural hematoma. Diagnosis of subdural hematoma during life requires neuroimaging with computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, usually prompted by headache, cognitive/personality changes, or focal neurological deficits. Occasionally small subdural hematomas are diagnosed fortuitously when neuroimaging is performed for unrelated reasons. Symptoms can resolve spontaneously. Hence, detection of subdural hematoma was probably incomplete in these trials because systematic neuroimaging was not undertaken, and particularly in earlier trials when the threshold of symptoms and signs prompting neuroimaging was generally higher than in later trials.  

Intracerebral (intraparenchymal) hemorrhage, another type of intracranial bleeding, is more common and is often more devastating than subdural hematoma The increased risk of subdural hematoma associated with aspirin use is consistent with the aspirin’s effect to increase intracerebral hemorrhage, with odds ratios ranging from 1.23 to 1.39.6,7,27 Hence, aspirin appears to increase similarly the relative risk of major types of intracranial bleeding that occur in different anatomic compartments.  

The absolute rate of subdural hematoma is relevant when considering the increased risk with aspirin use. Two population-based studies from Scandinavia reported annualized rates of subdural hematoma of 0.15 and 0.20 per 1000 patients years.2,27 Because of wider access to neuroimaging in recent decades and larger numbers of participants, the estimated rates of subdural hematoma are likely more reliable in the four recent primary prevention trials compared with earlier trials, in our view. The average annualized rates of subdural hematoma estimated by pooling data from the placebo/control arms of the four recent primary prevention trials (mean participant age 71 years) is 0.32 per 1000 patient-years and 0.47 per 1000 patient-years for aspirin-assigned participants.2226 (Table) This excess of 0.15 subdural hematomas per 1000 patient-years translates into one additional subdural hematoma for approximately every 6500 elderly people using aspirin. The absolute risk may be larger in some populations, such as East Asians who have relatively higher rates of intracranial hemorrhage.7  

Limitations of this analysis include that subdural hematomas were identified as a subset of major hemorrhage outcomes or as an adverse event and not as a prespecified outcome event in the included trials. If subdural hematomas among those taking aspirin is associated with larger subdural hematomas accompanied by more severe neurological deficits, this could result in biased detection. However, this caveat also applies to diagnosis of subdural hematomas in clinical medicine. The reliability of diagnosis of subdural hematomas is uncertain in most trials, and no specific diagnostic criteria were provided. The availability and use of brain imaging (CT or MRI) is likely the determining factor whether a subdural hematoma is diagnosed or not.

In conclusion, the best available evidence based on meta-analysis of randomized trials supports that aspirin use increases the risk of subdural hematoma. However, the absolute increase is very low for most people. These observations add a small piece to the larger puzzle of the safety and efficacy of antithrombotic therapy.
