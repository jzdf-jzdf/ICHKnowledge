# Network meta-analysis and trial sequential analysis for atrial fibrillation patients receiving PCI or with ACS  

Shu-Mei Yanga, Chi-Jung Huanga, Chen-Huan Chenb,c,d,e, Wen-Chung $\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}}^{\mathbf{\mathsf{c}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{d}}}}$ , Shih-Hsien Sungc,d,e, Chao-Yu Guoe, Shao-Yuan Chuangf, Hao-Min Chenga,b,d,\*, Chern-En Chiangg,d,\*

# Abstract  

Background: In patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) and acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), choosing the most appropriate antithrombotic treatment remains a dilemma. We aimed to compare the relative efficacy and safety outcomes of antithrombotic drugs in patients with AF after undergoing PCI or ACS.  

Methods: Randomized controlled trials were systematically searched on PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library. Five studies (11,532 patients) were included in the network meta-analysis. Trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed to assess the reliability and conclusiveness of the meta-analysis comparing the dual antithrombotic therapy strategies with the triple antithrombotic therapy strategy.  

Results: Compared with vitamin K antagonist $^+$ dual antiplatelet therapy, novel oral anticoagulant $(\mathsf{NOAC})+\mathsf{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor was associated with a significantly better trial-defined primary safety outcome (odds ratio: 0.53; $95\%$ CI, 0.31-0.90) and the lowest probability of thrombolysis in myocardial infarction major bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage using the cumulative ranking technique. In patients omitting aspirin, TSA demonstrated conclusive evidence with significant decreases in all safety outcomes and inconclusive evidence with a nonsignificant increase in in-stent thrombosis (risk ratio: 1.32; TSA-adjusted $95\%$ CI, 0.54-3.24) and myocardial infarction (risk ratio: 1.19; TSA-adjusted $95\%$ CI, 0.84-1.68).  

Conclusions: In patients with AF receiving PCI or with ACS, ${\mathsf{NOAC}}+{\mathsf{P}}2\mathsf{Y}_{12}$ inhibitor was associated with the lowest bleeding risk but resulted in a statistically nonsignificant, numerically greater risk for stent thrombosis and myocardial infarction, suggesting that triple antithrombotic therapy should still be an option for certain patients at a high risk of stent thrombosis or myocardial infarction.  

Keywords:  Antiplatelet; Antithrombotic; Atrial fibrillation; Non-vitamin $\mathsf{K}$ oral anticoagulant; Trial sequential analysis  



# 1. INTRODUCTION  

Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) and a $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ antagonist is recommended for patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), mainly to prevent stent thrombosis, and for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients, mostly to prevent recurrent events.1 For patients with atrial fibrillation (AF), oral anticoagulation therapy is required to decrease the risk of stroke or systemic embolism.2 In daily practice, patients with concomitant coronary artery disease and AF are frequently encountered, since $30\%$ of patients with AF also have coronary artery disease, $10\%{-}15\%$ of patients with AF receive PCI, and up to $15\%$ of patients with stable coronary artery disease have concomitant AF.3 This raises concerns regarding the cotreatment with various antithrombotic and antiplatelet drugs, given that a high risk of bleeding is associated with DAPT in combination with oral anticoagulation therapy (triple antithrombotic therapy [TAT]), especially in patients with AF who receive PCI or who have ACS.4  

Several systematic reviews with meta-analysis based on evidence from randomized controlled trials $(\mathrm{RCTs})^{5-8}$ have been conducted to address this issue.3,9–11 All these systematic reviews have led to similar conclusions: that omitting aspirin resulted in fewer major bleeds, while preserving the efficacy of therapy, compared with TAT (combining a vitamin K antagonist [VKA] or novel oral anticoagulant [NOAC] with DAPT). Moreover, the use of $\mathrm{NOAC}+\mathrm{P}2\check{\mathrm{Y}_{12}}$ inhibitor was recommended as the preferred regimen after PCI for these high-risk patients with AF.9 Although the summarized bleeding risk of these meta-analyses is sensible, considering that the dual antithrombotic therapy (DAT) (combining VKA or NOAC with a $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor) should carry a reasonably lower bleeding risk than TAT, the key assessment of this issue should rely on the comparison of the efficacy endpoints between these strategies. In particular, stent thrombosis is an ominous complication and is associated with serious clinical consequences in patients receiving PCI.12 In fact, in the latest meta-analysis,9 a nonsignificant increase in the occurrence of ischemic events (myocardial infarction, stent thrombosis, and cardiovascular death) was noted in patients in whom aspirin was omitted.9 The consistent trend toward a higher number of myocardial infarction and stent thrombosis events in patients who had withdrawn from aspirin therapy among all NOAC AF PCI trials inevitably raises concerns.9,13 Actually, the sample size of approximately 10,000 patients for meta-analysis is probably insufficient to detect the differences in the less frequent, but important, complications, such as stent thrombosis.9 Trial sequential analysis (TSA), which is a novel method developed to reduce the spurious inference from a conventional meta-analysis by avoiding random errors due to sparse data and repeated significance testing when updating a meta-analysis with new trials, is an ideal meta-analytic tool to address this limitation.14  

Recently, ENTRUST-AF PCI,13 a randomized, open-label, phase 3b trial was made available. A previous meta-analysis using TSA,15–17 which addressed only the primary safety outcome (bleeding) and the primary efficacy outcome (major adverse cardiovascular events), showed that DAT reduced the risk of bleeding with adequate sample size. However, the evidence regarding major adverse cardiovascular events remained inclusive and the sample size was insufficient.15–17 Moreover, no study has analyzed the summarized evidence regarding secondary outcomes. With the recently published study,13 we used TSA to further investigate whether the evidence regarding major adverse cardiovascular events is robust. Furthermore, we analyzed the individual safety (thrombolysis in myocardial infarction [TIMI] major or minor bleeding, trial-defined major bleeding events, and intracranial hemorrhage) and efficacy outcomes (cardiovascular causes, all-cause death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and stent thrombosis) to make informed antithrombotic decisions in this high-risk group of patients.  



# 2. METHODS  



# 2.1. Study Selection  

This research was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Project for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.18 PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library databases were systematically searched to identify all relevant studies from inception to September 4, 2019. The following keywords were used in the search queries: PCI, ACS, VKA, NOACs, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulant agents, and AF (Supplementary Table S1, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A114). The inclusion criteria for eligible studies were as follows: (1) RCTs comparing the effects of an anticoagulation and antiplatelet combination therapy versus VKA $^+$ DAPT for AF patients with ACS or undergoing PCI; (2) RCTs reporting the outcomes of interest with a follow-up of, at least, 6 months.  



# 2.2. Outcome Measures  

The primary safety outcome of interest was defined in accordance with the TIMI definition. The secondary safety outcomes were TIMI major or minor bleeding, trial-defined primary bleeding events, and intracranial hemorrhage. The primary efficacy outcome was trial-defined major adverse cardiovascular events, a composite endpoint that consisted of death from cardiovascular causes, all-cause death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and stent thrombosis. The secondary efficacy outcomes were cardiovascular causes, all-cause death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and stent thrombosis.  



# 2.3. Data Extraction and Quality Assessment  

Data were independently extracted by two investigators (S.M.Y. and C.J.H.). The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration tool.19 The included studies were independently evaluated by two authors (S.M.Y. and C.J.H.) using the methodological quality assessment system in RevMan 5.3. Egger’s test and funnel plot asymmetry were used to assess the potential publication bias.20 The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system was used to assess the quality of evidence.21 Evaluation discrepancies were resolved by a third author (H.M.C.).  



# 2.4. Statistical Analysis  

The Bayesian network meta-analysis (NMA) was performed using the Markov chain Monte Carlo engine R 3.6.1 by using the “gemtc” package (V0.8-2).22 All Bayesian network analyses were performed using four chains and, after discarding the results of a burn-in period of 20,000 iterations, each chain had a sample of 50,000 iterations. The relative effect results of the consistency model were reported as odds ratios with a corresponding $95\%$ confidence interval $95\%$ CI). We estimated the ranking probability of each antithrombotic strategy, that is, the most effective drug, the second best, the third best, and so on. The overall rankings were interpreted using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) technique and the ranked probabilities sum to one.23 Rank 1 is considered the preferred strategy for the specific outcome. In addition, the consistency between direct and indirect evidence was assessed using node-splitting plots. We also performed Cochran’s $\mathrm{Q}$ statistical analysis and conducted an inconsistency $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ test to evaluate the heterogeneity.19  

TSA was conducted to assess the reliability and conclusiveness of the available evidence in our pairwise meta-analysis.24,25 The unadjusted risk ratios were derived from the number of patients with each outcome in the DAT and TAT groups. To address the risk of random errors resulting from sparse data and repetitive testing on accumulated data,26 TSA, which combines the estimation of optimum sample size (required information size), for statistical inference, with an adjusted threshold (trial sequential monitoring boundaries), for statistical significance, was used.14,27 We calculated the required information size with the heterogeneity adjustment based on the control event rate, the anticipated relative risk increase or reduction, and the model-based diversity regarding the outcome of interest. The control event rate was the median proportion of events in the control groups, and the diversity was derived from the random-effects model. All TSAs were performed at the level of an overall type-I error of $5\%$ (two-sided) and a power of $80\%$ . TSA software (version 0.9.5.10 Beta) was used for these analyses. Sensitivity analysis was performed by omitting the WOEST trial due to inherent differences from the other four trials that were conducted in the NOAC era. Pairwise meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3.  



# 3. RESULTS  

In the literature search, 1,961 articles were identified and their titles and abstracts were reviewed. After an initial screening, the full text of 39 articles was retrieved for detailed assessment (Supplementary Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/  

A114). Five studies met the inclusion criteria and were chosen for the NMA.  



# 3.1. Study Characteristicss  

The five selected RCTs enrolled a total of 11,532 patients with AF after PCI with a mean age of 70.3 years. The baseline characteristics of the trials are presented in Table 1. The follow-up duration ranged from 6 to 14 months. Most patients were randomized to receive $\mathrm{NOAC}+\mathrm{P}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}$ inhibitor $[{\mathrm{n}}=4,357$ ; $37.8\%$ ), followed by VKA $^+$ DAPT $\mathrm{~\textmu~n~}=~3,\bar{3}80$ ; $33.6\%$ ), NOAC $^+$ DAPT $(\mathrm{n}=\mathrm{1},862$ ; $16.1\%,$ ), and VKA $+\mathrm{P}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}$ inhibitor $\mathrm{~(~n~=~}1{,}433$ ; $12.4\%$ ). The construction of network comparisons between the different treatment strategies is shown in Fig. 1. We hypothesized that the four NOAC drugs (apixaban; dabigatran, $110\mathrm{mg}$ and $150\mathrm{mg};$ ; edoxaban; and rivaroxaban) would be comparable in terms of safety and efficacy based on this network analysis. All these studies had a low risk of bias, except for performance bias (Supplementary Figures S2 and S3, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A114).  



# 3.2. NMA Results for the Safety and Efficacy Outcomes  

Regarding the safety outcomes, $\mathrm{NOAC}+\mathrm{P}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}$ inhibitor and VKA $^+$ $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor regimens, which belong to the DAT strategy, were associated with decreased risks of trial-defined primary bleeding versus VKA $^+$ DAPT (Fig.  2). Moreover, $\mathrm{\bar{NOAC}}+\mathrm{P}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}$ inhibitor was associated with a reduced risk of TIMI major bleeding (odds ratio: 0.52; $95\%$ CI, 0.34-0.78), TIMI major or minor bleeding (odds ratio: 0.53; $95\%$ CI, 0.30- 0.90), and intracranial hemorrhage (odds ratio: 0.31; $95\%$ CI, 0.12-0.72) compared with VKA $^+$ DAPT. However, there were no significant differences in the efficacy outcomes between the antithrombotic regimens. The corresponding NMA results are listed in Supplementary Table S2, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/ A114. # 3.3. Ranking of Treatment Strategies  

The distribution of probability and SUCRA value for the safety and efficacy outcomes are shown in Supplementary Figure S5A,B, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A114. The combination of $\mathrm{NOAC}+\bar{\mathrm{P}}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}$ inhibitor had the highest SUCRA value; that is, it was the most beneficial antithrombotic strategy for preventing TIMI major bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage. The $\mathrm{VKA}+$ $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor had the highest SUCRA value regarding TIMI major or minor bleeding and a trial-defined primary safety outcome. For the efficacy outcomes, NOAC $^+$ DAPT ranked first (SUCRA: $69.3\%$ ), followed by VKA $^+$ DAPT (SUCRA: $63.5\%$ ), regarding stent thrombosis. Similarly, NOAC $^+$ DAPT and VKA $^+$ DAPT ranked first and second for myocardial infarction (SUCRA: $76.7\%$ and $66.2\%$ , respectively).  



# 3.4. TSA and GRADE Findings  

We found a significant decrease in the safety outcomes, and a nonsignificant increase in ischemic events (stent thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and cardiovascular death) was noted in patients who did not receive aspirin. We further conducted TSA for five trials to evaluate the effects of TAT against DAT on the risk of bleeding and the risk of safety outcome, stent thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and cardiovascular death in patients with AF undergoing PCI. As shown in Fig. 3, for all outcomes, except for TIMI major bleeding and TIMI major or minor bleeding, the cumulative $Z$ curve did not cross the conventional boundary and any of the monitoring boundaries, and the calculated optimum sample size was not exceeded. In other words, only for safety outcomes, including TIMI major bleeding (risk ratio: 0.59; TSA-adjusted $95\%$ CI, 0.44-0.77) and TIMI major or minor bleeding (risk ratio: 0.61; TSA-adjusted $95\%$ CI, 0.44-0.84), the TSA provided conclusive evidence (Fig. 3). TSA analysis showed similar results for the trial-defined primary safety outcomes (Fig.  4). The evidence regarding the efficacy outcomes, including cardiovascular death (risk ratio: 1.06; TSAadjusted $95\%$ CI, 0.84-1.34), myocardial infarction (risk ratio: 1.19; TSA-adjusted $95\%$ CI, 0.84-1.34), and stent thrombosis (risk ratio: 1.32; TSA-adjusted $95\%$ CI, 0.54-3.24) were all inconclusive (Fig. 3). Egger’s test $(\boldsymbol{p}>0.100)$ suggested that publication bias was not found in terms of safety and efficacy outcomes (Supplementary Figure S6, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A114). Based on GRADE assessment, the quality of the current evidence was moderate to high for the safety and efficacy outcomes (Supplementary Table S3, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A114).

# 3.5. Results of Sensitivity Analyses  

We further conducted sensitivity analyses by comparing the NOAC-based DAT and NOAC-based TAT strategies. In the sensitivity analyses that included the NOAC-based studies, the NMA (Supplementary Figure S7, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/ A114) and TSA (Supplementary Figure S8, http://links.lww.com/ JCMA/A114) showed similar findings to most outcomes presented in the main analysis. Compared with TAT, DAT regimens were associated with a significantly lower risk of trial-defined primary safety outcomes (odds ratio: 0.67; $95\%$ CI, 0.55-0.82) (Supplementary Figure S9A, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A114). NOAC-based DAT versus NOAC-based TAT revealed no significant differences in the risk of efficacy outcome, but there was a trend toward an increased risk of myocardial infarction and stent thrombosis with DAT (Supplementary Figure S9B, http:// links.lww.com/JCMA/A114).  



# 4. DISCUSSION  

The major finding of the present systematic review (Fig. 4), in which NMA and TSA were employed, is that, compared with  

VKA $^+$ DAPT (TAT), for patients with AF receiving PCI or with ACS, an antithrombotic regimen of an anticoagulant with NOAC or VKA plus $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor (DAT) results in less bleeding and a non-significantly increased risk of stent thrombosis and myocardial infarction. Among the antithrombotic strategies, omitting aspirin caused less bleeding compared with the aspirin treatment. However, preventing stent thrombosis or recurrent myocardial infarction is a major indication of taking DAPT for patients with AF receiving PCI or for patients with ACS. Therefore, we should carefully investigate whether there is genuinely no increased risk of stent thrombosis in this high-risk population, considering its catastrophic consequences.12 Compared with the previous meta-analyses, the present systematic review represents the most updated systematic review after the publication of ENTRUST-AF PCI13 on the safety and efficacy outcomes in AF patients after PCI or ACS. In addition, we performed TSA to investigate whether the safety and efficacy outcomes achieved the required information size. We also used the GRADE system to evaluate the quality of the body of evidence. By conducting TSA analysis, we suggest that this strategy may trend toward increase the risk of stent thrombosis (GRADE: moderate) or recurrent myocardial infarction (GRADE: moderate). Our study is clinically important because previous meta-analyses failed to address this important issue due to the intrinsic limitations of the conventional meta-analysis technique.  

In general, the risk of bleeding increases with the increasing number of antithrombotic therapies, given that these medications are all designed to prevent clot formation and may increase the bleeding hazards. Therefore, previous systematic reviews have all reached similar conclusions: that omitting aspirin resulted in fewer major bleeds compared with VKA $^+$ DAPT.3,9,10 We included five RCTs with 11,532 patients for analysis, whereas the recent meta-analysis included four RCTs with 10,969 patients.17 Therefore, the larger sample size of the present systematic review may have reduced type II errors.  

A systematic review and meta-analysis of previously published RCTs is helpful to explain the conflicting information and provide the highest level of evidence regarding the benefits and risks of different treatment strategies. However, a meta-analysis may result in an increased random error caused by sparse data and repeated testing of accumulating data. Therefore, TSA, a new meta-analysis technique, has been developed to reduce the risk of random errors and the spurious inference resulting from traditional meta-analyses.14  

In our TSA for stent thrombosis, myocardial infarction, and cardiovascular death, we found that the current information (sample) size is still too small to make a firm conclusion that an anticoagulant plus $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor is not associated with an increased risk of stent thrombosis or myocardial infarction. Further studies should be conducted to answer this question.  

Our study findings are basically in agreement with those of a previous meta-analysis; that is, omitting aspirin in patients with AF who receive PCI or with ACS can unequivocally reduce the bleeding risk.9 In patients who undergo PCI, balancing between the risk of bleeding and the risk of ischemic events is a difficult task for clinicians. Based on previous evidence, the use of fewer antithrombotic therapies is expected to result in less overall bleeding hazards. However, without the reassurance that the risk of ischemic events does not increase because of the reduction of the antithrombotic effects, the general recommendation that “less is better” should be followed cautiously. The signals of an increased risk of stent thrombosis or myocardial infarction in DAT vs. VKA $^+$ DAPT suggests that “one size probably cannot fit all.” In the era of precision medicine, more effort should be made to investigate which phenotypes of patients can benefit from TAT.

Previous studies have shown that oral anticoagulants could be more effective in preventing reinfarction and stroke than aspirin plus clopidogrel in patients with AF.28 Moreover, patients with AF undergoing PCI had an increased risk of ischemic events without having a reduced risk of bleeding complications after temporary cessation of oral anticoagulants or omission of a $\mathrm{P2}\mathrm{\bar{Y}}_{12}$ inhibitor.29 Previous research also suggested that, compared with VKA monotherapy, adding aspirin to VKA did not clearly demonstrate an additional benefit in patients, following myocardial infarction, while it increased the risk of bleeding.30 The use of oral anticoagulants, including NOACs, can exert antiplatelet effects in the prevention of stroke and recurrent myocardial infarction, which can be further synergized by $\mathrm{P}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}^{\dot{}{}}$ inhibitors.31–34 Thus, it is a reasonable recommendation for most patients with AF and ACS or PCI to use a dual pathway of platelet inhibition therapy with oral anticoagulants plus $\mathrm{P}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}$ inhibitor, rather than TAT. However, from our TSA results, until the signals of an increased risk of stent thrombosis or myocardial infarction can be confidently disapproved, TAT should still be an antithrombotic strategy for patients at high risk of in-stent thrombosis or recurrent myocardial infarction.

For AF patients with ACS or undergoing PCI who also developed stent thrombosis or myocardial infarction events, personalized antithrombotic regimens are the direction of future research, which may include mechanistic studies on the role of platelet function, genetic testing in patients treated with VKA or NOAC and antiplatelet therapy, and genome-wide pharmacogenomics data with polymorphisms affecting the response to VKA or NOACs.35  

The present systematic review and meta-analysis has several limitations. It is worth noting that, given the important differences in trial design of the included trials, it is impossible to conclude whether the reduction in the risk of bleeding that was seen with the NOAC was due to the use of the new drug, the reduced dose, or the discontinuation of aspirin. The NMA results were obtained by assuming homogeneity, consistency, and similarity.36,37 Therefore, the findings obtained from NMA should be less credible than direct pairwise comparisons. Regarding the NMA of our study, between-study heterogeneity apparently results from the design, indication, type, dose, duration of antithrombotic therapy, the timing of aspirin discontinuation, and the duration of the follow-up, which may potentially affect the interpretation of our results. The differences in the definition of stent thrombosis between studies is another limitation of this study.  

In conclusion, in patients with AF with ACS or receiving PCI, $\mathrm{NOAC}+\mathrm{P}2\mathrm{Y}_{12}$ inhibitor was associated with the lowest bleeding risk, including intracranial bleeding. However, based on the TSA results, the increased risk of stent thrombosis or myocardial infarction cannot be confidently excluded, which suggests that TAT should still be an option for certain patients at high risk of stent thrombosis or recurrent myocardial infarction.
