# Comparison of craniotomy and decompressive craniectomy in large supratentorial intracerebral hemorrhage

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is devastating disease with high mortality and morbidity rates. Most ICH is evacuated by either craniotomy (CR) or decompressive craniectomy (DC) although optimal treatment has not been established yet. The objective of this study was to compare clinical outcomes of spontaneous ICH patients between CR and DC groups and determine clinical factors affecting clinical prognosis. We retrospectively analyzed our single-center experience with large supratentorial ICH. From January 2011 to December 2016, 286 consecutive supratentorial large ICH patients underwent surgery in our institute. We compared CR group and DC group with regard to age, sex, GCS score, hematoma volume, midline shift, ICH score, and time from ictus to surgery. Statistical analysis was done using the t-test or $x^{2}$ test, and odds ratio was calculated. During study period, CR was performed in 139 patients while DC was performed in 125 patients. There were no significant difference in 30-day mortality between the CR group and the DC group $13.7\%$ vs $15.2\%$ , $p=0.729.$ ). However, 12-month functional survival was $46.0\%$ in the CR group, which was significantly $\overset{\cdot}{p}=0.014\$ higher than that $(32.0\%)$ of the DC group. In conclusion, the 30-day mortality of CR group was not inferior to that of the DC group while its 12- month functional survival was superior to that of the DC group. This suggests better functional outcome might be obtained for selected large ICH patients with CR than with DC.

# 1. Introduction  

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) accounts for 10 to $20\%$ of all strokes [1,2]. Incidence of ICH in Asian and black populations is even higher $(20-30\%)$ [3]. ICH can be devastating and its mortality rates are high, ranging from 30 to $50\%$ at 30 days [4–6].  

Surgery has the potential to reduce the volume of ICH, especially in large ICH. Clinical and experimental evidence have shown that mass removal might reduce nervous tissue damage by relieving downward herniation and reducing intracranial pressure (ICP) [7–9]. Mass removal also can improve cerebral compliance, cerebral oxygen supply, cerebral blood perfusion, and various abnormal radiologic signs particularly in large volume ICH [10–12].  

Direct hematoma evacuation with open technique remains the most commonly used method in large volume ICH because it affords intraoperative hemostasis and control. Some neurosurgeons prefer decompressive craniectomy (DC) with hematoma removal over simple hematoma removal by craniotomy (CR) for  

ICH patients. Previous retrospective studies or systemic reviews have suggested that DC can improve postoperative mortality and morbidity as well as frequency of good functional outcomes [13– 17]. However, currently there is no consensus on indications for CR versus DC after evacuation of ICH [14,18,19].  

Therefore, the objective of this study was to compare the effect and prognosis between CR and DC in spontaneous supratentorial large volume ICHs following evacuation of ICH.  

# 2. Material and methods  

This is a retrospective evaluation of prospectively collected data. Consecutive patients with spontaneous supratentorial ICH operated with CR or DC following hematoma removal in our hospital between January 2011 and December 2016 were included in this study. Our Institutional Review Board approved this retrospective study.  

From electronic medical records, demographics, data regarding admission Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), hemorrhage location (lobar, basal ganglia, or both), hemorrhage hemisphere (right vs. left), ICH volume (ml), midline shift (mm), presence or absence of IVH, ICH score, time from ictus to surgery, post-operative hematoma volume, midline shift, and Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) were obtained. Preoperative ICH score was calculated according to Hempill et al. [20].  

Inclusion criteria were: (1) spontaneous ICH confirmed by computed tomography (CT); (2) ICH volume ${>}50~\mathrm{ml}$ according to xyz/2 method [21]; (3) surgical evacuation within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of the ictus; and (4) age 30–85 years. Exclusion criteria were: (1) coagulopathy, including INR ${>}1.2$ and anticoagulation therapy; (2) traumatic ICH; (3) intracranial or systemic infection; (4) severe cardiac, hepatic, renal, or pulmonary dysfunction; (5) neoplasm leading to ICH; (6) history of stroke with neurological deficits; (7) intracranial aneurysm or other vascular malformation; (8) endoscopic or stereotactic guided hematoma aspiration.  

The choice of surgical technique employed for evacuating the ICH was at the discretion of the operating/admitting neurosurgeon. In general, decisions were made based on pre-operative clinical and CT characteristics, as well as the degree of intraoperative brain swelling. However, there is no consensus about surgical technique and the operating surgeon made final decision at the time of procedure, in a nonrandomized fashion, whether to perform CR or DC.  

# 2.1. Surgical procedures  

For each case, the goal was to evacuate the hemorrhage. For all cases, a large frontotemporal CR or DC was performed. For DCs, trauma-type flaps were used to maximize decompression of the cerebral hemisphere [22]. They usually extended to the floor of the middle fossa to decompression of the tentorial incisura and brainstem. Decompression of middle fossa was also performed in case of CR to relief tentorial herniation. In some cases, sylvian fissure was split under the microscope to allow less traumatic access to the insula and deep hematoma. However, the hematoma was removed for most cases by making a small corticotomy in the superior temporal gyrus or other noneloquent areas. Following evacuation of the ICH, the dura was closed loosely to minimize the possibility of brain compression. When the preoperative plan was changed from CR to DC due to intraoperative severe brain swelling, cases were not included in this study. In patients who had clot evacuation with using CR as primary procedure, secondary DC was done if rebleeding occurred or there was severe brain swelling not responsive to dehydration or other first-line therapy. These patients were included in the CR group.  

All patients received standard medical treatment according to the guidelines for the treatment of spontaneous ICH in adults of the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association Stroke Council [23]. Additional CT scans were performed at 3 days, 7 days, 1 month, 3 months, and 12 months after the surgery. Postoperative hematoma volume and midline shift were evaluated from 3-day CT scan. Outcome was assessed by 30-day mortality and 12-month Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS). In 12-month functional outcome assessment, GOS score of 3–5 was regarded as functional survival while GOS score of 1–2 was regarded as poor outcome.  

# 2.2. Statistical methods  

All pertinent data of study subjects were calculated and analyzed using SPSS statistical package (version 22; SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were described in frequencies, percentages, means, and SD to examine demographic and clinical characteristics for cases and controls. Statistical significance level was set at $p<0.05$ .  

At univariate level, chi-square test was used to determine 30- day mortality and 12-month GOS in relation to different variables of patients. At multivariate level, multiple binary logistic regression analysis was used to determine variables associated with poor functional outcome. Variables with p-value less than 0.25 at univariate level were included in multivariate modeling. Outcomes were reported as odds ratios (OR) with $95\%$ confidence interval (CI).  

# 3. Results  

During 6-year study period, 286 large volume ICH patients were assessed for the study and 264 patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria were enrolled. 13 patients were excluded due to change of pre-operative plan from CR to DC. Another 9 patients were excluded due to leukemia, liver cirrhosis or end stage renal disease.

Mean ages in cases and controls were $63.15\pm12.57$ and $64.03\pm12.96{\mathrm{y}}$ ears old, respectively. The overall incidence of ICH was slightly higher in males than that in females $(54.5\%~\mathrm{VS}.45.5\%)$ . ICH was more frequent in the right side hemisphere compared to that in the left side hemisphere ( $55.7\%$ vs $44.3\%$ . There was no statistical difference in any preoperative variable between CR and DC groups. There was no significant difference $(p=0.141)$ in postoperative hematoma volume between the two groups. However, postoperative midline shift was more severe $\'p=0.017\$ in the DC group compared to that in the CR group.

In the DC group, 44 $(35.2\%)$ of 125 patients died and 41 $(32.8\%)$ patients had severe disability. A total of 64 $(46.0\%)$ patients in the CR group and 40 $(32.0\%)$ patients in the DC group showed functional survival. There was no GOS 5 due to precondition of large volume ICH.

# 4. Discussion  

Acute ICH causes increase of ICH and compression of the surrounding brain tissues or cerebral vessels, consequently resulting in a series of irreversible pathologic changes [7–9]. ICHs at more than $30\mathrm{cc}$ and secondary edema have significant spaceoccupying effect [24]. The area of brain damage is reported to be much larger than the area occupied by the hematoma itself [25– 27].  

The best treatment for patients with spontaneous ICH remains controversial [23,28–30]. Since the publication of Surgical Trial in Intra-cerebral Hemorrhage (STICH), surgical evacuation of ICH has become less common at many centers [30,31]. However, many studies have demonstrated the good efficacy and prognosis of surgical removal of ICH, especially in large volume hematoma [1,16,17,19,24,32–34]. Pantazis et al. prospectively showed that surgical removal of large subcortical or putaminal hematomas greater than $30~\mathrm{ml}$ yields better functional results than conservative therapy [24]. There are technical advantages to use open surgical approaches for large ICH. The use of open techniques can allow more rapid clot decompression. It might prevent brain injury by herniation and also facilitates meticulous hemostasis at walls of the hematoma cavity.  

DC is widely used to manage malignant middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarctions or traumatic brain injury [28,35–37]. It is occasionally used for aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage or cerebral venous occlusion [15,38–42]. The efficacy of DC in the management of ICH has demonstrated low mortality with good neurologic outcome in many studies [16,17,33]. However, few studies have compared CR and DC in large volume ICH [14,19].  

Other stroke disease, such as large vessel infarct or aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, can induce generalized brain swelling without severe disruption to cerebral architecture. Swollen brain volume is markedly increased than normal brain and it needs large DC to decrease ICP. However, the main problem of ICH is mass effect of hematoma and destruction of circumjacent brain tissue. Damaged brain tissue is removed along with hematoma removal and the volume of saved brain tissue is smaller than original brain volume. Although swelling of remnant and saved brain tissue occurs gradually, the total hemispheric volume after hematoma

In this study population, three patients in the DC group needed secondary operation for postop subdural/epidural hematoma while only one patient in the CR group needed such operation. Constriction of skin, muscle, or fascia after surgical procedure similar to sinking skin flap syndrome can cause additional brain compression on unprotected brain [13,43–45]. The mean midline shift after DC $(6.71\pm5.15\mathrm{{mm}}$ ) was also more prominent $'p=0.017',$ ) than that in the CR group $\mathrm{^{7}5.82\pm5.47~mm}$ ) in this study.  

Our study demonstrated better functional survival at 12-month in the CR group than that in the DC group, although 30-day mortality was similar to each other in the two groups. In this study population, CR was favorable for patients with young age, male sex, right hemisphere involvement, no IVH, lower hematoma volume/ midline shift, and short time from ictus to surgery in particular. However, CR should be applied to selected patients. In our opinion, the most important factors for good outcome of CR are intraoperative finding and the time from ictus to surgery. First of all, hematoma should be removed well and bleeding focus should be controlled properly. Remnant hematoma and poorly controlled bleeding focus can cause postoperative mass effect and additional brain injury, consequently resulting in severe brain edema. Secondary important intraoperative findings are the degree of brain swelling after hematoma removal and hemostasis. If brain swells up after hematoma removal and hemostasis, it means generalized brain injury, remnant, or new hematoma. Lastly, early hematoma removal and early ICP reduction are important factors for favorable outcome of ICH. In the rat experimental model, inflammation and cell death start $^{4\textrm{h}}$ after the ICH [46]. Brain injury and neuronal cell death become more severe over time. It might induce more severe postoperative brain edema although which is not severe at intraoperative field.  

Our study has three primary limitations. The first limitation was that our study was conducted retrospective. Secondly, this study compiled data from a single center. Thirdly, this is not a randomized controlled study and treatment was determined at the time of surgery by the treating surgeon. Despite these limitations, useful clinical conclusions could be drawn from our results. To reach definitive conclusions, further studies are needed to compare CR and DC in a prospective and randomized fashion.  

# 5. Conclusions  

The selection of operation technique between CR and DC should be individualized for each patient considering numerous preoperative clinical factors and intraoperative findings. Results of the current study showed that CR might be safe and effective for treating large volume ICH when operated early in time with proper hematoma removal and hemostasis. Better functional outcome might be obtained for selected patients with CR than with DC. Further studies with prospective randomized trials are needed to confirm the safety and efficacy of CR with hematoma evacuation for treating large ICH.
