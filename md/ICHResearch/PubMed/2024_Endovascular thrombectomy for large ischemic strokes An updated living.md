GRADE Background: Recent studies indicate endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) as a safe, effective treatment for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) with large ischemic regions. Our study updates an ongoing living systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing outcomes of EVT to medical management only. Methods: We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library for RCTs comparing EVT to medical management in AIS patients with large ischemic areas. Using fixed-effect models, we conducted a meta-analysis to compare functional independence, mortality, and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) between EVT and standard medical management. We evaluated bias risk with the Cochrane tool and graded the certainty of evidence using the GRADE approach.   
Results: Of 1363 new citations, we included six RCTs with a total of 1876 patients. We found low-certainty evidence of improved functional independence (risk difference [RD] $29.9\%$ , $95\%$ CI $17.2\%$ to $46.9\%,$ , in­ crease in sICH (RD $2.6\%$ , $95\%$ CI $0.3\%$ to $6.4\%$ , and a non-significant decrease in mortality (RD - $\cdot1.8\%$ , $95\%$ CI - $.3.9\%$ to $0.6\%$ ) for AIS patients with large infarcts who underwent EVT compared to medical management only. Conclusion: Our revised meta-analysis suggests low-certainty evidence that there is improved functional inde­ pendence, a non-significant decrease in mortality, and an increase in sICH among AIS patients with large infarcts who undergo EVT compared to those receiving medical management alone.

# 1. Introduction  

Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) has emerged as the established standard of care for addressing acute ischemic strokes (AIS) resulting from large vessel occlusions (LVO) [1]. According to the AIS manage­ ment guidelines, only patients with an Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) of 6 or higher are considered eligible for EVT if they meet the rest of the eligibility criteria [1,2]. Although a significant portion of LVO stroke involve substantial ischemic regions, estimated at around $20\%$ , these individuals have historically been excluded or un­ derrepresented in EVT trials due to concerns about reperfusion-related bleeding [3–6].  

In light of this, six recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were conducted to evaluate the efficacy of EVT in comparison to standard medical care for AIS patients with large ischemic regions observed on neuroimaging studies [7–12]. To address this timely gap in knowledge, we provide an updated systematic review and meta-analysis addressing the efficacy and safety of EVT in AIS patients with large ischemic regions.  

# 2. Methods  

# 2.1. Standardized reporting and registration  

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) reporting guidelines for this updated sys­ tematic review and meta-analysis (Supplementary File 1) [13]. The methods were done in strict accordance with the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis of Interventions (version 5.1.0) [14]. We also registered our systematic review protocol on PROSPERO (CRD42023398742).  

# 2.2. Data sources and searches  

We encompassed searching through MEDLINE (through Ovid), EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library from inception to February 10, 2023, and updated the search date to October 13, 2023. We also handsearched grey literature and the reference lists of included studies. We did not use any language restrictions. The detailed search strategies are outlined in Supplementary File 2.  

# 2.3. Study selection  

We included all studies satisfying the following criteria:  

# 2.3.1. Population  

Studies on adult patients aged 18 years or older presenting with AIS due to a large vessel occlusion and are found to have a large ischemic area on initial neuroimaging, defined as having an ASPECTS value of ${<}6$ , regardless of imaging modality used, or having an infarct core volume of at least $50\mathrm{mL}$ . In vitro, animal, and post-mortem studies were excluded.  

# 2.3.2. Intervention  

Studies where the experimental arm underwent EVT, regardless of technique (i.e, aspiration, stent retriever). We also included studies that evaluated different types of adjunctive endovascular procedures (e.g., stenting, angioplasty).  

# 2.3.3. Comparator  

Studies where the control arm received medical management only.  

# 2.3.4. Outcomes  

Studies reporting on at least one of the following outcomes of in­ terest: 1) Proportion of patients with functional independence, defined as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0 to 2 at 90 days, 2) Mortality, defined as death after stroke onset due to any cause, at 90 days, and 3) Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), defined as any intracra­ nial hemorrhage associated with neurologic worsening, clinical deteri­ oration, or death, adapted from the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification [15], 4) Proportion of patients with an mRS of 0 to 3 at 90 days, and 5) Procedure-related complications.  

# 2.3.5. Study designs  

Only RCTs were included in this systematic review and metaanalysis. All other study designs, such as cohort studies, editorials, commentaries, literature reviews, systematic reviews and metaanalyses, and news articles were excluded. Preprint studies and con­ ference proceedings were not excluded.  

# 2.4. Study screening  

Reviewers engaged in calibration exercises, utilizing standardized screening forms that had been piloted prior to the screening phase. In pairs, reviewers independently assessed and confirmed each citation. Afterward, we obtained the complete texts of citations that were considered potentially eligible. One reviewer screening each full text, and another verified its eligibility. In case of any disagreements, a third reviewer intervened to resolve them.  

# 2.5. Data extraction  

Reviewers independently collected data from each eligible RCT into a Microsoft Excel 2021 spreadsheet. To ensure accuracy, they crossverified the extracted data using pre-established standardized data abstraction forms. The extracted information encompassed a range of characteristics, including study details (such as country and study design), patient demographics (such as sample size, age, sex, prior medical history, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS] score, involved vessel, tandem occlusion, and stroke etiology), inter­ vention and comparator specifics (e.g., intravenous thrombolytic agent), as well as management outcomes, which encompassed mRS scores, mortality, sICH, and procedural complications.  

# 2.6. Risk of bias assessment  

Two reviewers evaluated the quality of the included trials, adhering strictly to the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2) designed for RCTs [16]. To enhance the reliability of RoB 2, particularly addressing concerns related to low interrater reliability and intricate terminology, we conducted comprehensive training for the authors before they began the assessment process [17]. In cases where disparities arose between the two assessors, they resolved them through discussion, and when necessary, a third assessor was involved to reach a consensus.  

# 2.7. Data synthesis and analysis  

We conducted meta-analysis using the random-effects restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method to calculate risk ratios (RRs) and the associated $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs), for all patient-important outcomes reported by more than one study. To calculate the relative risks (RRs) for dichotomous outcomes, we utilized the crude event rate along with its associated confidence intervals (CIs). For risk differences (RDs) and their corresponding $95\%$ CIs, we applied the RRs to baseline risks obtained from a robust, high-quality multi-center prospective cohort study involving 2420 patients with acute large vessel occlusions [18]. When the data reported median and range values or when standard deviations (SDs) were not available, we estimated means and standard deviations using methodologies outlined in the Cochrane handbook and described by Wan et al. [14,19]. We then synthesized our findings and employed funnel plots to assess potential asymmetry for each outcome. Additionally, we assessed statistical heterogeneity using measures of inconsistency, Cochran’s $\mathsfQ$ test and $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ . The $\chi$ -square statistic was then used to calculate I2 according to the equation: I2=(Q−Qdf)x100%. A χ-square $p$ value ${<}0.1$ was considered as significant heterogeneity. I values $\ge50\%$ were indicative of high heterogeneity. All statistical ana­ lyses were carried out using STATA/MP version 17 for Microsoft Windows.  

# 2.8. Certainty of evidence assessment  

To evaluate the confidence level of evidence for each outcome (classified as high, moderate, low, or very low), we employed the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evalua­ tions (GRADE) approach. To create GRADE evidence profiles, we utilized the GRADEpro app (accessible at www.gradepro.org) [20,21]. In characterizing the strength of comparisons for each outcome, we employed the following standardized terminology: “there is” for highcertainty evidence, “there probably is” for moderate-certainty evi­ dence, and “there possibly is” for low- or very low-certainty evidence. For functional independence, mortality, and sICH, we applied previ­ ously established thresholds for minimally important differences, as derived from a guideline panel strategy developed earlier [22].  

# 2.9. Living review and literature surveillance  

We plan to conduct monthly updates or alerts using our search strategies. If new evidence emerges that is substantial enough to alter the overall assessment, increase the certainty of evidence, and provide additional insights into outcomes, we will meta-analyze these findings and incorporate the results into an updated manuscript.  

# 3. Results  

In our updated literature search from our prior meta-analysis [23], we found 1363 additional records, from which we identified 12 poten­ tially eligible studies. After conducting full-text screening, we ultimately incorporated 3 new RCTs that were relevant to our research question. When considering the 3 RCTs already included in our previous sys­ tematic review, this brings the total number of patients to 1876 (Fig. 1) [23].  

# 3.1. Characteristics of included studies  

The characteristics of the included RCTs reporting on outcomes for  

AIS patients with large ischemic regions who underwent EVT compared to those who received medical management only are summarized in Table 1 [7–12]. The number of included patients ranged from 203 to 455 [7–12]; two studies used alteplase at doses ranging from 0.6 to $0.9\mathrm{mg/}$ kg [9,12], three studies used alteplase at unspecified doses [7,10,11], one study used tenecteplase [10], one study used urokinase [9], and one study did not specify type of thrombolytic agent [8].  

# 3.2. Risk of bias assessment  

While providers or participants were not blinded due to the nature of the intervention, all RCTs used appropriate methods for randomization, had ${<}10\%$ missing outcome data, and assessed outcomes in a blinded fashion. Most RCTs were rated as having a low risk of bias (Supple­ mentary File 3). Additionally, the study outcomes may have been affected by cointerventions that were not accounted for due to the inability of blinding participants or providers.

# 3.3.3. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage  

All 6 studies reported sICH with variability in the definitions used (n $=1876$ patients) [7–12]. Low-certainty evidence from 6 RCTs suggests that EVT, compared with medical management only, possibly results in a small increase in sICH (RR 1.71, $95\%$ CI 1.07 to 2.73; RD $2.6\%$ , $95\%$ CI $0.3\%$ to $6.4\%$ ) (Table 2, Fig. 4). # 3.3.4. Other secondary outcomes  

All studies reported on $\mathrm{mRS0}$ to 3 at 90 days (RR 1.90, $95\%$ CI 1.48 to 2.43) [7–12]. All studies reported on procedure-related complications with a pooled rate of $9.8\%$ (93/947). Further details of these findings can be seen in Supplementary File 4.  

# 4. Discussion  

This meta-analysis serves as an update of our systematic review process aimed to compare outcomes between EVT and medical man­ agement only in AIS patients with large ischemic regions [23]. We previously found that EVT possibly significantly increases functional independence, non-significantly decreases mortality, and nonsignificantly increases the risk of sICH [23]. In this update, our results were nearly consistent with our previously published findings, ac­ counting for imprecision and variability in outcome measurements.  

We included 3 new RCTs comparing EVT to medical management only in patients with large ischemic cores experiencing AIS [7,8,11]. Despite the results from these additional trials, which involved 1032 more participants, not altering the conclusions drawn in our prior metaanalysis for this particular comparison [23], they did provide fresh in­ sights into the impact of EVT on mortality and sICH. Although the findings did not reach statistical significance, the absolute risk differ­ ence suggested a more substantial effect of EVT in reducing mortality. Similarly, the absolute risk difference indicating EVT’s impact on sICH was small but shown to be statistically significant. The imprecision in the data related to EVT’s effect on increasing functional independence was also reduced, allowing for a more confident inference that EVT is beneficial in improving functional outcomes in patients with large ischemic cores.  

There have been several systematic reviews assessing the role of EVT in AIS patients with large ischemic core volumes [24–28]. However, this meta-analysis is the first to provide data from all RCTs on the topic, including the most recent ones, LASTE, TENSION, and TESLA trials [7–12]. Our systematic review not only provides the first insight into data from the additional trials, but certainty of the evidence and risk of bias were assessed using the GRADE approach and the RoB 2 tool, respectively. In addition to quantifying the relative risk, we estimated absolute risk differences to enhance the interpretation of the results.  

While the findings of this meta-analysis suggest that EVT could be a viable consideration for patients with AIS and large ischemic cores, it is crucial to note specific nuances within the literature. First, the diag­ nostic modalities used to identify large cores were different between the RCTs. While the majority of the included trials used baseline ASPECTS as an imaging criterion, the SELECT-2 trial used computed tomography perfusion (CTP) or magnetic resonance imaging perfusion showing a core ${>}50\mathrm{mL}$ in addition to ASPECTS to select patients with large cores [7–12]. The TENSION trial used diffusion-weighted imaging as an additional modality for enrollment, but ${>}80\%$ of patients were enrolled using CT imaging [7]. The LASTE trial also used diffusion-weighted imaging in addition to non-contrast CT [8]. Second, the imaging criteria to identify large cores in AIS patients varied. For instance, ANGEL-ASPECT could not enroll patients with ischemic cores measured ${>}70$ to $100~\mathrm{mL}$ with an ASPECTS value of ${>}5.$ ; RESCUE-Japan LIMIT, SELECT-2, and TENSION excluded AIS patients with ASPECTS ${<}3$ . TESLA recruited participants with ASPECTS values ranging from 2 to 5 [11], and LASTE even recruited patients with ASPECTS values of ${<}2$ , but limited the ASPECTS value to 4 or 5 when recruiting patients at least greater than or equal to 80 years of age [8]. Third, it is important to note that, compared to other systematic reviews on the topic, our study used patient-important outcomes compared to surrogate outcomes (e.g., any intracranial hemorrhage, successful reperfusion on final angiogram); opting for hard endpoints rather than surrogate measures enables a more meaningful and applicable interpretation in patient-centered clinical practice [22,29]. Fourth, there was variability in the definitions for certain outcomes measures, such as sICH. The ANGEL-ASPECT and LASTE trials used the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification [8,9], the RESCUE-Japan LIMIT, TENSION and TESLA trials used an adapted definition from the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification by including those with parenchymal hematoma type 2 only [7,11,12], and the SELECT-2 trial used the Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in StrokeMonitoring Study (SITS-MOST) criteria [10]. Our pooled results did not demonstrate any heterogeneity $(\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%)$ ; our conclusion that EVT, compared to medical management only, possibly increases the risk of sICH, is still valid. The SELECT-2 trial demonstrated the lowest risk of sICH compared to the other trials, but patients who underwent EVT were more likely to have early neurologic worsening on exam, posing the possibility of reperfusion-related edema related to the procedure [10]. There was also variability in the primary outcomes set by each trial. Four RCTs considered ordinal shift in mRS at 90days as their primary outcome [7–10], one RCT considered an mRS score of 0 to 3 at 90 days after stroke onset as the primary outcome [12], and one used utilityweighted mRS at 90 days as the primary efficacy outcome [11]. Although the TESLA trial did not reach statistical significance in eval­ uating its primary outcome based on utility-weighted mRS scores, there was a noticeable trend favoring EVT [11]. The reliance on utilityweighted mRS scores can introduce instability, especially in cases where results are marginally positive or neutral. This is because the weighted scores may not effectively capture patient preferences, potentially influencing the trial outcome [30]. Sixth, although the mortality rate remained notably high within the EVT group, ranging from $7.8\%$ to $38.4\%$ , it is noteworthy that the LASTE trial was the sole study indicating a significant reduction in mortality in the EVT group compared to the group receiving only medical management [8]. The LASTE trial specifically highlighted this mortality difference in patients whose last known well time was less than or equal to $^{6.5\mathrm{~h~}}$ [8]. Addi­ tionally, it is important to mention a recent subgroup analysis revealing ‘the late-window paradox,’ indicating a more substantial benefit of EVT in achieving functional independence for AIS patients with large core strokes presenting beyond 6 h compared to those presenting within $^{6\textrm{h}}$ [31]. Applying these findings to mortality outcomes proves challenging.

While evidence from these RCTs has been instrumental in shaping the consideration of EVT for large core strokes, the optimal timing of the intervention remains undetermined. Furthermore, discussions about the risk of sICH should involve relevant stakeholders, including caregivers and families, when contemplating EVT for such patients. Additionally, a significant consideration is the potential increased costs associated with post-EVT care for these patients. Two cost-effectiveness analyses, both drawing from clinical insights in RESCUE-Japan LIMIT, indicated that EVT was cost-effective for AIS patients with large core infarcts [32,33]. Notably, one study suggested cost-effectiveness for patients with AS­ PECTS values of 4 or 5, rather than a value of 3 [33]. Determining the generalizability of these findings based on clinical inputs from other trials remains challenging to ascertain.  

# 4.1. Limitations  

Our meta-analysis has certain limitations. Firstly, we could not conduct subgroup analyses based on factors like the presence of ipsi­ lateral tandem occlusions or the use of thrombolytic agents. Secondly, incorporating ordinal mRS shifts, which offer more nuanced long-term endpoints compared to dichotomous endpoints, was not feasible in our analysis. Thirdly, inadequate power prevented us from performing a meta-regression analysis. Fourthly, we referenced a conference pro­ ceeding for the LASTE trial and a preprint for the TESLA trial [8,11], but we cross-verified the data with the information presented at these con­ ferences. Lastly, we did not find enough data in the included trials to perform subgroup analysis according to time window of EVT, early versus extended window. This might be crucial to explore if the ‘latewindow paradox’, that was recognized in the pooled analysis of the RESCUE-Japan LIMIT, SELECT-2 and ANGEL-ASPECT trials [31], still persists with the new trials added to the analysis.  

# 5. Conclusions  

Our systematic review and meta-analysis delved into the advantages and safety considerations of EVT as opposed to medical management in patients with AIS-LVO accompanied by large infarct regions. We discovered low-certainty evidence indicating that EVT increases functional independence, non-significantly reduces mortality, and raises the risk of sICH. Further insights from planned sub-studies will help pinpoint specific subgroups that might derive greater benefits from this treatment.
