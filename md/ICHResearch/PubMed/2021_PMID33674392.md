# Embolization of the middle meningeal artery for the treatment of chronic subdural hematoma: considerations for pragmatic trial design  

David Fiorella $\circledcirc$ ,1,2 Joshua A Hirsch $\circledcirc$ ,3 Adam S Arthur ip 4,5  

Chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) is a common neurological disease which is increasing in prevalence with an estimated 60 000 new diagnoses of cSDH per year expected by 2030.1 2 Embolization of the middle meningeal artery (MMA) for the treatment of cSDH has rapidly progressed from an intriguing concept to a relatively routine procedure over the past 2 years. Initial case reports from the first decade of the 2000s were bolstered by larger case series in the years that followed.2–5 These studies led to a larger series from Ban et al, who reported remarkable success rates for this procedure in patients undergoing embolization either as an adjunct to surgical evacuation or as a stand-alone treatment when compared with historical controls.6 Several larger, retrospective, self-adjudicated case series have reported similarly optimistic outcomes.2 MMA embolization would thus appear to offer a minimally invasive, technically straightforward, relatively safe procedure that potentially obviates the need for open surgery and/or decreases the rate of recurrence.  

If proven safe and effective, MMA embolization could fundamentally change the standard of care for the management of cSDH. Prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs) will be essential if this vision is to become a reality. While cSDH as a pathophysiology might seem straightforward, the disease and its management have proven to be extraordinarily complex. Moreover, defining the comparative “effectiveness” of treatment strategies for a disease in which relatively unambiguous radiological findings may be associated with an array of clinical presentations is nuanced. Designing a trial is further complicated when viewed through the lens of the informative prior data, which are largely derived from self-adjudicated retrospective case series, many of which have used inconsistent endpoints to assess safety and effectiveness. This comment will address some of the challenges involved with designing a pragmatic RCT of MMA embolization for the treatment of cSDH.  

# CHALLENGE #1: WHICH SUBDURAL COLLECTIONS SHOULD BE INCLUDED?  

What defines a subdural collection as “chronic”? Prior imaging is often not available for patients presenting with a gradual, progressive neurological deterioration. Many patients present after an acute decompensation, resulting from bleeding into a more chronic appearing fluid collection. A consensus definition seems to be “an extra-axial, crescentic, fluid collection, the majority of which $(>50\%$ volume) is either hypo- or isodense to the underlying brain on CT”.7  

How large does a cSDH have to be before it is “clinically relevant” and thereby appropriate to include in the RCT when there are no established guidelines regarding the minimum size of a symptomatic cSDH to be considered for surgical evacuation? A cSDH thickness of $10\mathrm{mm}$ or greater, associated with local mass effect or midline shift, has been suggested as a minimum size to consider for surgical removal. As such, $10\mathrm{mm}$ (greatest thickness) would seem a reasonable threshold for inclusion in a trial and a reasonable benchmark for radiological success after treatment.6  

# CHALLENGE #2: WHICH CLINICAL APPLICATIONS OF MMA EMBOLIZATION SHOULD BE EVALUATED?  

Patients can be broadly separated into one of two categories described below.  

# SOLE THERAPY  

MMA embolization will be used as a frontline therapy, with surgery reserved for patients where the collection fails to adequately resolve and persistent symptoms or an increase in the size of the collection at follow-up. The “sole therapy” strategy requires that the patient be able to tolerate the cSDH for a long enough period to allow for physiological resolution of the collection. Early clinical experience suggests that cSDH resolution after embolization occurs over several (6–12) weeks to months. The incidence of spontaneous resolution of cSDH with watchful waiting is thought to be relatively uncommon $(<20\%)$ . However, some small medical management trials have presented more optimistic data with spontaneous resolution rates approaching $50\%$ reported with various medical (eg, steroids) interventions.8 On the contrary, most studies of MMA embolization as sole therapy have been more optimistic, with resolution rates ranging from $60\%$ to nearly $100\%$ .6 9–11 From a statistical standpoint, MMA embolization for sole therapy appears to represent “low-hanging fruit" with a large absolute effect size when compared with conservative management.  

# SURGICAL ADJUNCTIVE THERAPY  

Estimates of postoperative recurrence after cSDH evacuation range widely from less than $5\%$ to greater than $30\%$ depending on the patient population(s) included, the definition of recurrence and the surgical technique employed.7 12 Given the generally low quality and heterogeneity of the existing informative data, it is difficult to predict the surgical rate of recurrence in a preselected group of prospectively enrolled trial patients. However, because rates of clinically relevant recurrence after surgery are generally estimated to be lower than $30\%$ , it is likely that the absolute effect size of adjunctive MMA embolization compared with surgery alone would be considerably smaller than the absolute effect size for MMA embolization as sole therapy compared with watchful waiting. At the same time, adjunctive MMA embolization does not disrupt the current standard of care for patients with symptomatic cSDH collections, which is surgical evacuation. So while adjunctive MMA embolization presents a higher statistical bar, if proven effective it would allow the technique to be relevant to a larger population of cSDH patients than would a sole therapy application.  

Additionally, adjunctive MMA embolization can be performed pre- or postoperatively (provided that the surgery leaves the MMA intact). It is currently unknown whether the sequence of embolization impacts its potential effectiveness. Pragmatically, postsurgical adjunctive embolization provides the greatest degree of latitude for the procedure and as patients with larger symptomatic cSDHs who require urgent surgical evacuation can be embolized on a semi-routine basis afterward.  

It would seem that both sole and surgical adjunctive approaches deserve to be studied, potentially requiring unique trials to establish the safety and effectiveness of each approach.  

# CHALLENGE #3: WHAT IS THE DEFINITION OF “EFFECTIVENESS”?  

Ongoing and anticipated trials will have different primary endpoints reflecting the diversity of opinion as to what best demonstrates “effectiveness”. Nonetheless, it is important that each trial collects a common group of radiological and clinical endpoints that will allow comparison of results, as well as later pooling of data for combined analysis.  

# Radiological endpoints  

Radiological success represents the most straightforward and generalizable measure of effectiveness. Two general measures have been used – absolute residual size and percentage resolution compared with baseline. Ban et al’s study used reduction of the cSDH collection to an absolute residual size of less than $10\mathrm{mm}$ at 6 months. This can serve as a benchmark for estimating the sample size for a similarly designed prospective trial using the same radiological endpoint.6 Moreover, $10\mathrm{mm}$ is a pragmatic endpoint for success, as few cSDH collections measuring less than $10\mathrm{mm}$ require surgical evacuation or create significant clinical sequelae. Complete resolution would represent another absolute endpoint; however, this may be too stringent a criterion and might result in clinically insignificant residual collections being considered as treatment failures. Other authors have reported “relative percentage reduction” in cSDH thickness compared with baseline (typically $>50\%$ reduction) as the primary radiological endpoint.2 3 Studies reported in this manner have been largely retrospective, multicenter, self-adjudicated datasets and as such are probably less reliable benchmarks than those established in the Ban study. Moreover, if applied within the context of a study that includes cSDH collections of less than $20\mathrm{mm}$ , this criterion would trigger failure for some residual collections measuring less than $10\mathrm{mm}$ . At the same time, for very large collections $(>20\mathrm{mm})$ it does provide some latitude for success when a treatment effect is observed, but the residual collection still measures greater slightly than $10\mathrm{mm}$ . A hybrid radiological success endpoint such as $>50\%$ reduction OR reduction to less than $10\mathrm{mm}$ in absolute thickness could represent a third consideration which might capture radiological success in more patients .10 11 Regardless of which approach is chosen, trialists should consider analyzing and reporting each of these radiological endpoints to allow for the seamless comparison and pooling of datasets.  

# Clinical endpoints  

As opposed to unambiguous symptoms of emergent large vessel occlusion (ELVO), cSDH can present gradually or acutely with symptoms that range from hemiplegia to chronic headache to subtle cognitive decline. Moreover, the management of cSDH can be confounded by concomitant anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications that can result in both neurological and non-neurological vascular events. For these reasons, finding appropriate clinical endpoints that adequately capture safety and effectiveness is challenging.  

Disabling stroke or neurological death, common major complications resulting from surgery or embolization, represent obvious safety endpoints. Major systemic vascular thrombotic events (eg, pulmonary embolus) related to the discontinuation of anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications is another group of endpoints which are of interest in this population, particularly with respect to the assessment of sole therapy. Specifically, when patients with cSDH are managed with surgical evacuation or conservative observation, medications that disrupt coagulation are usually held or reversed. In contrast, with MMA embolization sole therapy, these medications may be continued. In the Ban series, a significant proportion of the complications observed in the historical control patients were directly attributable to the disruption of anticoagulants.6  

Clinical effectiveness measures are comparatively more elusive. Most obvious is the requirement for unplanned surgical evacuation or repeat surgery for patients managed with initial surgery with or without adjunctive embolization. These would constitute an unambiguous effectiveness failure in its respective cohort. Similarly, measures of healthcare utilization – hospital days, intensive care unit days, and readmissions attributable to cSDH morbidity – represent important indirect assessments of effectiveness.  

Familiar stroke clinical outcomes measures, such as modified Rankin score and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scales, should also be reported. However, given that these metrics are suboptimal for measuring outcomes after ELVO, they are even more challenged as instruments for measuring outcomes in cSDH patients and their often more subtle neurological presentations. For this reason, measurements of quality of life (eg, EQ-5D-5L) and even neurocognitive function may represent more relevant indicators of effectiveness in these patients.  

# CHALLENGE #4: HOW SHOULD MMA EMBOLIZATION BE PERFORMED? Available agents  

Various technical approaches to MMA embolization have been utilized. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) has been used in the majority of reported series to date, and the results have been impressive.5 6 13 14 This approach has the advantage of being inexpensive. In addition, PVA embolization can be performed without general anesthesia as the PVA infusion does not elicit a pain response. At the same time, PVA particle infusions require a larger diameter microcatheter which may create spasm or may be occlusive in the relatively small, tortuous MMA branches of elderly patients. These factors may reduce or arrest anterograde blood flow, preventing particles from penetrating into the distal vasculature. Similarly, the particles are irregularly shaped and tend to form clumps at bifurcation points, again leading to a proximal vascular occlusion and likely not penetrating into the membranes which harbor the fragile neovasculature that represents the underlying pathophysiology of cSDH. The proximal microcatheter position required to allow for anterograde flow during a PVA infusion may also result in non-target embolization.  

More recently, ethylene vinyl alcohol (EVOH) suspensions in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) such as Onyx (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA) and SQUID (Balt USA, Irvine, CA, USA) have been employed for this purpose.15 This technique provides the advantage of using a smaller diameter microcatheter which can be navigated into the distal MMA vasculature. The operator is able to infuse the embolysate under conditions of occlusive flow control and achieve, in most cases, a thorough and permanent embolization of the distal MMA branches. The infusions are fast, the embolysate is easily visualized, and the microcatheter is positioned distal to the more proximal dangerous anastomoses. However, the EVOH-based agents are also considerably more expensive than PVA. These agents are suspended in DMSO, which elicits a pain response during the infusion. For this reason, most operators prefer to perform EVOH-based MMA embolizations under general anesthesia. N-Butyl cyanoacrylate (nBCA) and Precipitating Hydrophobic Injectable Liquid (PHIL) are two other liquid embolysates which may provide many of the advantages of EVOH-based liquid embolics without eliciting the same pain response, thereby facilitating MMA embolization under conscious sedation. Finally, proximal MMA occlusion with embolization coils has been reported, but this technique has not been widely studied and may not meaningfully devascularize the targeted meningeal neovasculature.  

While there are arguments both for and against each of these agents, all are probably deserving of structured investigation and, someday, head-to-head comparison.  

# Procedural technique  

Given how early our experience is with MMA embolization, dogmatic positions regarding the best technical approach are based more on anecdote than science. However, clinical trial design mandates a uniform procedural protocol with clearly defined technical goals.  

In general, it would seem that a thorough distribution of embolysate within both major branches of the MMA is the desired outcome. Catapano et al provide the best available evidence for this, observing that embolization of both main (anterior and posterior) MMA branches with Onyx led to considerably higher rates of complete cSDH resorption than embolization of a single branch alone.16 17 Embolic infusions are variable with respect to the distal penetration achieved and volume of embolysate infused in different patients with differing MMA anatomy. It remains unknown to what degree the embolysate distribution (relatively proximal MMA branch occlusion vs thorough distal casting) or volume of agent infused into the MMA vasculature impacts the degree or rate of subsequent cSDH resolution. The liquid embolic agents (Onyx, SQUID, PHIL and nBCA) are radiopaque and the distribution is easily documented with standard imaging. Image analysis from the ongoing trials of liquid embolic agents may tell us whether the distribution or volume of embolysate effects outcomes.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

MMA embolization for cSDH represents a historic opportunity. Investigators can thoughtfully and collaboratively define the effectiveness and safety of these novel treatments. This process will benefit from a consistent set of metrics to evaluate outcomes within the context of multiple, concurrent, prospective trials. The opportunity to fundamentally advance medicine and improve outcomes for a large group of patients with a common neurological disease rests on neurointerventionalists once again. It is up to us to make the most of it.
