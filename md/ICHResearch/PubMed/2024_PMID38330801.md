# ABSTRACT  

Background: Severe disorders of consciousness (sDoC) are a common sequela of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhages (aSAH), and amantadine has been used to improve cognitive recovery after traumatic brain injury. Objective: This study evaluated the effect of amantadine treatment on consciousness in patients with sDoC sec­ ondary to aSAH.  

Methods: This double-center, randomized, prospective, cohort study included patients $\geq18$ years old with sDoC after aSAH from February 2020 to September 2023. Individual patient data of patients were pooled to determine the effect of amantadine, in comparison to placebo. The primary outcomes at 3 and 6 months after the ictus were evaluated using the modified Rankin scale (mRS) and Glasgow outcome scale (GOS). In addition to all-cause mortality, secondary endpoints were assessed weekly during intervention by scores on Rappaport’s Disability Rating Scale (RDRS) and Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRSR).  

Results: Overall, 37 patients with sDoC and initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) varying between 3 and 11 were recruited and randomized to amantadine (test group, $\mathtt{n}=20.$ ) or placebo (control group, $\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{1}7.$ ). The average age was 59.5 years (28 to 81 year-old), 24 $(65\%)$ were women, and the mean GCS at the beginning of intervention was 7.1. Most patients evolved to vasospasm $(81\%)$ , with ischemia in $73\%$ of them. The intervention was started between 30 to 180 days after the ictus, and administered for 6 weeks, with progressively higher doses. Neither epidemiological characteristics nor considerations regarding the treatment of the aneurysm and its complications differed between both arms. Overall mortality was $10.8\%$ (4 deaths). During the study, four patients had po­ tential adverse drug effects: two presented seizures, one had paralytic ileus, and another evolved with tachy­ cardia; the medication was not suspended, only the dose was not increased. At data opening, 2 were taking amantadine and 2 placebo.  

Conclusion: Despite some good results associated with amantadine in the literature, this study did not find sta­ tistically significant positive effects in cognitive recovery in patients with delayed post-aSAH sDoC. Further large randomized clinical trials in patients’ subgroups are needed to better define its effectiveness and clarify any therapeutic window where it can be advantageous.  



# 1. Introduction  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a significant public health problem, with an overall global incidence estimated at 6.1 per 100000 person-years [1], and a worldwide prevalence of 8.09 million $95\%$ uncertainty interval, 7.02–9.72 million) cases [2]. The improve­ ment of therapeutics over the last decades reduced the mortality from aSAH, however, the number of survivors with cognitive impairment can reach up to $70\%$ in that population [3]. In addition, most survivors of aSAH have severe disorders of consciousness (sDoC), including coma, vegetative state (also known as unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, and minimally conscious state (MCS) [4].  

Considering that neurostimulants such as amantadine improve wakefulness in patients with sDoC after traumatic brain injury, this indication has been extrapolated to treat patients with post-aSAH sDoC [5]. However, there is a paucity of clinical trial data about the impact of amantadine in the treatment of sDoC and non-traumatic cognitive dys­ functions following other causes of brain damage, such as stroke, intracranial hemorrhage or hypoxia [6–9] Thus, randomized clinical trials are justifiable to demonstrate the potential usefulness and efficacy of amantadine in sDoC due to acute non-traumatic brain diseases [10].  

The primary purpose of this study was to describe amantadine administration practices post-aSAH. Secondarily, we sought to identify cognitive and functional responsiveness, the best period for response, safety, and incidence of potential adverse drug effects.

The topog­ raphy and types of the patients’ aneurysms were also collected to characterize the population. All patients were initially and weekly evaluated by physicians utilizing the Rappaport’s Disability Rating Scale (RDRS) and the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRSR) and, after 3 and 6 months after aneurysm rupture, by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and the Glasgow Outcome scales (GOS).  

A case-control study was designed to evaluate the effect of amanta­ dine on cognitive recovery after aSAH. This prospective, randomized controlled study was started in February 2020 and completed enroll­ ment in September 2023. After approval from the institutional Ethics Committee and written informed consent obtained from the patients’ relatives, 37 patients were included in the study. This research is the result of a partnership between the Hospital das Clínicas da Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de Sao Paulo and the Hospital Universitario Cajuru of the Pontifical Catholic University of Parana. It was approved by the national Research Ethics Committee at Plataforma Brasil (register 25930219.3.0000.0068) and published in the Brazilian Registry of Clinical Trials (ReBEC) (register RBR-6789t4), after approval of institutional review board on October.10.2019. The study was written and followed the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Tri­ als) recommendations. [13].  

Potential adverse drug effects were selected based on a published study and the prescribing information for amantadine [11,12]. These included seizures, arrhythmias, spasticity, sleep disruption, agitation, and delirium. If a mild potential adverse drug effect was identified af­ terward, the doses were maintained without subsequential increases. Nevertheless, following the standards of good clinical practice for research, all potential adverse drug effects were reported to the Governmental regulatory agency (Plataforma Brasil).  

All patients recruited were aged beyond 18 years old, and remained with sDoC, with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) between 3 and 11 – as long as criteria for sDoC are met – from 30 to 180 days after aSAH and standard aSAH treatment protocol, such as surgical clipping or endo­ vascular embolization of the ruptured aneurysm. The choice of micro­ surgical or endovascular treatment in both hospitals is multidisciplinary, not affected by conflicts of interest, and is based on evidence according to the characteristics of the aneurysm itself, patient’s health conditions, and the neurosurgeon’s preferences. Exclusion criteria included patients with $\mathrm{mRS}{>}2$ , those receiving only parenteral feeding (because there is no intravenous amantadine in Brazil), uncontrolled seizures, heart fail­ ure, myocardial dysfunction, arrhythmias, and life expectancy below 1 year. Patients were randomized to one of two groups through a website that generates randomized draws. [14] Amantadine was administered in $100~\mathrm{mg}$ tablets once a day (Group A), and placebo tablets in the control group (Group B). The doses were progressively increased each week in both groups, being used $100~\mathrm{mg}$ tablets twice a day in the second week, $150\mathrm{mg}$ tablets twice a day in the third week, and $200\mathrm{mg}$ tablets twice a day for an additional three weeks. The drug treatment was interrupted after the sixth week.  

Patient selection and multivariable regression modeling were used to adjust for differences in intergroup comparison and parameters associ­ ated with consciousness. The normality of data was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous data were analyzed with t-tests or MannWhitney tests, depending on normality. Categorical variables between groups were compared by the chi-square test. The odds ratio was calculated through a computation of the $95\%$ confidence interval uti­ lizing R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; Vienna, Austria, 2021). Statistical significance was considered as $\mathtt{p}<0.05$ .

# 3. Results  

A total of 37 patients with an average age of 59.5 years (28 to 81 year-old), being 24 women $(65\%)$ were evaluated during the 43-month study period. The study design and flow chart are shown in Fig. 1. Amantadine was administered in 20 $(54\%)$ patients, and placebo in 17 $(46\%)$ patients, and the mean GCS at the beginning of intervention was 7.1 in the entire sample.  

Regarding comorbidities, 24/37 patients were hypertensive $(64.9\%)$ , 22/37 were smokers $(59.5\%$ of the total), 12/37 were dyslipidemic $(32.4\%)$ and 11/37 $(29.7\%)$ were diabetic. The average HHS was 2.81, with an average of 2.95 in the amantadine group and 2.64 in the placebo group. The mean Fisher of the entire sample was 3.48, with a mean of 3.29 in the placebo group and 3.65 in the amantadine arm. The average WFNS score was 2.64. The population characteristics, treatment, and complications of aSAH are summarized in Table 1. Most patients in this study had their ruptured cerebral aneurysms treated through endovas­ cular therapy: 25/37 $(67.6\%)$ versus 12/37 ( $32.4\%$ of total) undergoing microsurgery and clipping. The proportion of treatment modality was statistically similar between the groups: 6/20 $(30\%)$ and 6/17 $(35.29\%)$ ) microsurgeries, compared to 14/20 $(70\%)$ and 11/17 $(64.7\%)$ of em­ bolizations in the amantadine and placebo groups, respectively, although the group that received placebo underwent slightly more endovascular treatment. Surgical complications were reported in 4 pa­ tients $\left(10.8\%\right)$ , with all intraoperative aneurysm ruptures resolved: 3 endovascular and 1 craniotomy. Neither epidemiological characteristics nor considerations regarding treatment and its complications differed between both arms.  

Emergency external ventricular drainage was necessary for 12 of the 37 $(32.43\%)$ patients in this series, even before definitive treatment; this occurred in half of the entire amantadine group and only 2 $(11.76\%)$ of the 17 patients in the control group $(\mathtt{p}=0.01)$ . Only 3/37 patients $(8.1\%)$ became shunt-dependent due to chronic hydrocephalus in the evolution, with the use of ventriculoperitoneal shunt, 1 in the Aman­ tadine group and 2 in the placebo group.  

No patient needed to be excluded due to terminal illness or lack of enteral route to administer the medication, and the drugs were initiated a median of 53 days after the aSAH (range 30–123 days). Most patients presented vasospasm $(81\%)$ : 16/20 in the Amantadine group $(80\%)$ and 14/17 $(82.35\%)$ ) in the placebo group. Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) – secondary to vasospasm and/or other various factors – occurred in $73\%$ of the sample (27/37), corresponding to 16/20 on the amantadine arm and 11/17 on the placebo arm. Apparently, in the placebo group, there was proportionally more vasospasm, but fewer patients ended up pro­ gressing to DCI. These data, however, also did not reach a significant pvalue.

Sepsis was a clinical worsening factor in 22/37 patients $(59.45\%)$ : 9/ 20 $(45\%)$ in the amantadine group and 13/17 $(76.47\%)$ in the control group $(\mathtt{p}=0.05)$ . Another complication was CNS infection occurring in 5/37 $(13.51\%)$ of the series, being 4/20 $(20\%)$ of amantadine and 1/17 $(5.88\%)$ of placebo $(\mathbf{p}=0.21)$ . It was also notable that the most “sur­ gical invaded" patients (including both external ventricular drain - EVD and clipping), developed more central nervous system infections. As more patients randomly in the amantadine arm required EVD, more patients in this group had infection. Postoperative infections at such high rates are explained in patients in emergency conditions, but mainly because this was research in 2 university hospitals.  

Although the overall mortality rate was 4/37 $(10.8\%)$ , however, no death was associated with drug administration. In simpler hypothesis testing analyses, the variables associated with death were low HHS $(\mathbf{p}=0.02$ , Wilcoxon); time to begin the treatment $(\mathtt{p}<0.001\$ , T-Test); GCS at the beginning of the intervention $(\mathbf{p}<0.001\$ , Wilcoxon); RDRS at the beginning of the intervention $\mathbf{\tilde{P}}<0.01$ , Wilcoxon); CSRS at the beginning of the intervention $(\mathtt{p}<0.01\$ , Wilcoxon). The patients who were clinically worse at the beginning ended up dying, which is ex­ pected. However, when correcting the data for multiple tests (Bonferroni correction), the HHS is no longer significant.  

Four patients $(10.58\%)$ ) had potential adverse drug effects: the most common was seizures in two patients, one patient had paralytic ileus, and another evolved with tachycardia. In these cases, the medication was not suspended, and the dose was reduced to that well tolerated in the previous week. In the interim analysis, of the 4 possible adverse effects, only 2 were patients who were taking amantadine. Even shown to be a safe drug, the amantadine did not bring clinical superiority or statistically significant differences in any comparisons with the control group (data summarized in Tables 2 and 3), either when comparing neurological recovery at the end of the intervention, or with the beginning.  



# 4. Discussion  

The amantadine was introduced in the 1960 s as an antiviral drug, being then used to treat Parkinson’s disease [15–17]. This drug is an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, and as a dopami­ nergic agonist, its principle would be to act on a proposed “dopami­ nergic coma” occurring after a severe cerebral insult [15,18]. It is postulated that its positive results on cognition come from its dopami­ nergic, antiglutamatergic, anticholinergic, and noradrenergic, as well as anti-inflammatory and neurotrophic actions [19]. The possible role of amantadine in improving sDoC would be: 1) as a dopaminergic agonist, promoting neurocognitive stimulation associated with dopamine to then improvie consciousness; 2) neuroprotective properties, via inhibition of glutamatergic NMDA receptors; 3) reducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors from activated microglia; and 4) increasing the expression of glial lineage-derived neurotrophic factor (GNDF) in astroglia, protecting nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons from neuro­ toxins [19,20].  

Amantadine has been studied in patients with traumatic brain injury, with some promising results [11,15,16,21–26], and although amanta­ dine could reduce the recovery time, no study to date has demonstrated a higher degree of consciousness recovery. However, most studies conclude that amantadine can be a safe and effective treatment option for MCS of any etiology when the relative structural integrity of dopa minergic circuits is preserved [6,10].  

Among reversible causes of coma are DCI, hydrocephalus, status epilepticus, and nonconvulsive seizures [27]. After their treatment, however, few acute interventions to promote cognitive recovery have been studied [27]. One study with cEEG monitoring and machine learning shows that cognitive motor dissociation can occur in up to $15\%$ of patients presenting with acute and severe brain injuries, which in­ cludes aSAH [28]. This has prognostic implications because these pa­ tients are more prone to not recovering consciousness but also have better functional outcomes after 1 year [28,29].  

The potential of amantadine in reducing cognitive dysfunction was extrapolated to patients with sDoC after aSAH [6,10,19,30–32]. The first case report describes a 49-year-old woman with MCS 20 days after a bleeding complication that occurred during embolization of an unrup­ tured aneurysm, treated with amantadine, and demonstrating progres­ sive neurological improvement. Due to symptoms of cardiotoxicity, the drug discontinuation caused neurological deterioration; and the rein­ troduction of amantadine again resulted in neurological improvement. Therefore, a dose-response relationship was observed [6]. Another case report described a 36-year-old woman maintaining MCS nine months after aSAH and malignant hemispheric infarction due to vasospasm, who woke up and recovered noticeably within 16 days of treatment with amantadine [10]. A prospective randomized clinical study of 12 patients with aSAH admitted with GCS below 8, tested the use of amantadine during 30 days against standard treatment; the intervention group had a statistically significant neurocognitive and behavioral improvement compared to the control group [19]. In addition, a systematic review including 20 retrospective studies found that using neurostimulants to promote consciousness recovery after stroke is reasonable [30]. How­ ever, only 11 patients $(18\%)$ had aSAH, and some studies included amantadine and modafinil [29]. The median time from aSAH to initia­ tion of neurostimulants was $\approx39$ days (ranging from 2 to 730 days after ictus), earlier than in this study ${\approx}53$ days), and that difference can have relation with the lack of effect of amantadine [30]. Due to the hetero­ geneity of the studies, no pooled analysis was performed, but the studies showed the safety of using neurostimulants, which could be initiated safely after stroke in the subacute period [5,27,30].

Unlike what was described in other clinical trials applied to sDoC secondary to TBI, this study did not find significant neurological improvement either during the weeks of intervention or after 3 or 6 months. Therefore, we were unable to see practical benefits in the neurological functionality of patients with amantadine. There was a smooth trend of improvement over the months in both RDRS (Fig. 2) and CRSR (Fig. 3), but this would probably already be an improvement that patients would obtain due to their natural history and related to reha­ bilitation therapies. Even after 6 months, patients in both groups maintained very poor mRS and GOS scores, reflecting the sDoC. This has already been stated in the practical guidelines from the American Academy of Neurology 2018, where patients with sDoC of nontraumatic origin have a worse prognosis than those of a traumatic na­ ture [21].  

The aSAH can cause excess glutamate release and NMDA-type glutamate receptor activation [19]. That is associated with cerebral edema and cerebral ischemia (both early and late) leading to brain injury [19]. Considering the anti-inflammatory effects of amantadine, some authors believe that neuroprotection can result from suppression of inflammation and/or elevation of NMDA levels [19]. Therefore, there is a possibility that late use of amantadine, as in the patients in this study, may not improve the cognitive response, but an additional strategy of using amantadine could be neuroprotection, aiming to block the excitatory cascade and reduce the extent of secondary neuronal damage after aSAH [31], as seen in the cited trial [19].

This study has some limitations, especially due to the small sample size, and the study was underpowered due to a lack of neurological re­ covery, which can be related to the delayed starting of the medication. Bias by indication and timing of treatment initiation may have confounded analyses. The assessment of consciousness based on the GCS may also have limited the assessment in intubated and tracheostomized patients. In addition, the patients in this sample did not undergo routine cEEG monitoring to evaluate for breakthrough seizures. In addition, the limited data also prevented guiding combinations and doses of aman­ tadine and other neurostimulants [27].  



# 5. Conclusion  

Despite some good results associated with amantadine in the litera­ ture, this study did not find statistically significant positive effects in the cognitive recovery in patients with delayed post-aSAH sDoC during the treatment weeks, or even after 3 or 6 months. Therefore, further large randomized clinical trials in patients’ subgroups are needed to better define its effectiveness, required doses, and clarify any therapeutic window where it can be advantageous.
