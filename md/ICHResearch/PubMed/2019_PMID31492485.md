Most chronic subdural haematomas (CSDH) are successfully treated neurosurgically. However, operative recurrences occur with a frequency $3-30\%$ , consume resources and potentially prolong length-of stay (LOS). The only adjuvant factor proven to significantly decrease CSDH recurrence rate (RR) is postoperative subdural drainage. Corticosteroids have been used to conservatively manage CSDH. One nonrandomised study also compared dexamethasone (DX) as an adjunct to surgery without post-operative drainage: whilst a null effect was observed, the ‘surgery-alone’ group consisted of only $\mathbf{n}=13$ . We present an interim analysis of the first registered prospective randomised placebo-controlled trial (PRPCT) of adjuvant DX on RR and outcome after CSDH surgery with post-operative drainage. Participants were randomised to either placebo or a reducing DX regime over 2 weeks, with CSDH evacuation and postoperative drainage. Post-operative mortality (POMT) and RR were determined at 30 days and 6 months; modified Rankin Score (mRS) at discharge and 6 months. Post-operative morbidity (POMB) and adverse events (AEs) were determined at 30 days. Interim analysis at approximately $50\%$ estimated sample size was performed $\mathtt{(n=47)}$ . Recurrences were not observed with DX: only with placebo $(0/23~[0\%]~\nu~5/24\$ $\left[20.83\%\right]$ , $\mathrm{P}=0.049$ ). There was no significant between-group differences in POMT, POMB, LOS, mRS or AEs.  

Conclusions: In this first registered PRPCT, interim analysis suggested that adjuvant DX with postoperative drainage is both safe and may significantly decrease recurrences. A $12.5\%$ point betweengroups difference may be reasonable to power a final sample size of approximately $\mathrm{n}=89$ . Future studies could consider adjuvant DX for longer than the arbitrarily-chosen 2 weeks.

# 1. Introduction  

Most chronic subdural haematomas (CSDH) are successfully treated neurosurgically by burr hole aspiration [1]. However, operative recurrences occur with a frequency varying widely from 3 to $30\%$ [2,3]. Recurrences constitute morbidity [4], consume resources and potentially prolong length-of stay (LOS) [4]. Prolonged LOS after CSDH surgery is associated with additional 30-day postoperative morbidity (POMB) [4]. POMB which prolongs LOS after major non-neurosurgery is associated with decreased long–term survival (LTS) [5]. LTS after CSDH is known to be markedly reduced compared to age-matched controls [1].  

The only adjuvant factor so far proven to significantly decrease CSDH recurrence rates (RR) is post-operative subdural drainage for $^{48-72\mathrm{h}}$ [6]. However, corticosteroids have also been prospectively used to conservatively manage CSDH [7,8]. The classic and largest series, reported by Bender ${\bf\Pi}({\bfn}=37\$ ), was marred by heterogeneity in diagnosis (some cases were actually acute subdural haematomas, and all were diagnosed by angiography), and in corticosteroid treatment type (prednisolone or dexamethasone were both randomly used), dose (initial prednisolone varied between 60 and  

$120\mathrm{mg/day)}$ and duration (the ‘average’ treatment was for 21 days) [7]. Notwithstanding, the study demonstrated the relative safety of the corticosteroid regimes, and also suggested that corticosteroids ‘could accelerate’ spontaneous CSDH resorption [7]. A recent systematic review of extant studies has essentially concurred [2].  

In one retrospective analysis of 112 patients managed between 1998 and 1999, Sun et al. effectively compared dexamethasone (DX), as a prospective adjunct to surgery $\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathrm{n}}=69\mathbf{\chi}_{\mathrm{.}}$ , to those undergoing ‘surgery alone’ $\left(\mathtt{n}=13\right).$ [8]. Notably, however, post-operative subdural drainage was not used in either group [8]. Whilst a null effect was observed, the study was not randomised, and the prohibitively small ‘surgery-alone’ group were determined purely by contra-indications to DX. Although prospective conservative management with $(\mathtt{n}=26)$ ) and without $\left(\mathtt{n}=4\right)$ DX was also compared, the ‘without DX’ conservative group were also determined purely by contra-indications to DX and/or surgery [8]. Notwithstanding, Sun et al. again demonstrated the relative safety of DX with surgery; but without post-operative subdural drainage [8].  

One current large multi-centre, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial (PRPCT) [9], registered after our study [10] (but using a similar 2 week reducing DX regime, as originated by Sun et al. [8]), has recently reported methodological issues, without outcome data, in their first 100 patients. Such pilot phase analysis apparently confirmed the relative safety of DX with surgery, although post-operative subdural drainage was not specified. The authors concluded that ‘pilot trials are useful to assess recruitment feasibility and guide conduct of the subsequent substantive trial’ [9]. Importantly, the estimated sample size of the subsequent substantive trial was $\mathrm{n}=750$ . The report summarised potential vicissitudes in achieving this goal [9].  

We set out to perform the first registered PRPCT for adjuvant DX in CSDH surgery with post-operative subdural drainage.  



# 2. Methods  

The study design was a single-centre, double-blind, prospective, randomised, placebo-controlled trial (PRPCT) in patients with CSDH [10]. The trial was conducted in accordance with the SPIRIT statement and procedures following Good Clinical Practice (GCP) principles [11]. Ethical approval was obtained from the local Human Research Ethics Committee, and the study was registered (2013) [10]. The trial was initiated by the investigators and funded by an internal grant received from the principal public hospital. No drug company had any involvement in drug supply, trial conduct or manuscript review. The investigators maintained full autonomy in the design, conduct and reporting of the trial.  



# 2.1. Eligibility and recruitment  

The setting was a tertiary neurosurgery referral centre in a major regional western public hospital. Patients were eligible if they were symptomatic with a CT-proven CSDH deemed by the treating consultant neurosurgeon to require surgical evacuation. Exclusion criteria included children ${<}18$ yrs old [12], patients with a ventriculo-peritoneal shunt [4,6,9,14,19], patients where both the clinical scenario and neuro-imaging were more compatible with primarily an acute subdural haemorrhage, pregnancy, central nervous system tumour or infection, patients with overt contraindications to corticosteroids (e.g. active duodenal ulcers, active psychotic disorders etc.) and patients already taking corticosteroids or immunosuppressants. Any known, or newly diagnosed, diabetes mellitus was to be prospectively monitored by an endocrinologist from admission. The experimental protocol and informed consent were approved by the local hospital research ethics committee, and all subjects gave informed consent. Relative assent was obtained from a next of kin, or family member, wherever patients were considered unable to consent for themselves.  

Burr hole evacuation with saline irrigation was the ‘default’ procedure: variations of a ‘craniotomy’ were only considered wherever CSDH membranes abounded in order to permit fenestration. In all cases, the procedure was combined with a subdural drain for $48{-}72\mathrm{h}$ [4,6]. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered at induction $_{\textrm{Zg}}$ cefazolin), and all drains were antibioticimpregnated (clindamycin and rifampicin [Bactiseal, Codman & Shurtleff, Inc, Raynham, MA 02767, USA, www.depuysynthes.com]) [4]. Patients were encouraged to lie flat until the drain was removed, although they could temporarily ambulate for toileting etc. Drain removal was dictated by drainage output, post-op CT appearances indicating adequate decompression, and overall clinical impression [4]. Unless already taking an anti-convulsant, all patients received levetiracetam $500~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily prophylaxis due to uncertainty over CSDH and seizure induction [4,13]. All patients also received ranitidine $150~\mathrm{mg}$ daily prophylaxis [4] and wore $\mathrm{TED}^{\mathcal{\textregistered}}$ stockings throughout their LOS. In general, patients were re-started on their anti-coagulants 2 weeks after surgery (or sooner with a significant reduction in CSDH and/or elevated medical risk without anti-coagulants) [4]. ‘Recurrences’ were defined as symptoms attributable to a significant recollection on CT deemed by the treating consultant neurosurgeon to require re-operation within 6 months of surgery [4].  



# 2.2. Randomisation and blinding  

The trial pharmacist (un-blinded and independent) generated the randomisation code using a computer-derived permuted block, with varying block size sequence. Patients were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to receive either placebo or a reducing DX regime over 2 weeks. Specifically, patients took ‘DX/placebo’ $4\mathrm{mg}$ as 1 capsule 4 times a day for 3 days, then 1 capsule 3 times a day for 3 days, then 1 capsule twice daily for 3 days, and finally 1 capsule daily thereafter. The reducing schedule spanned a total of 14 days. Manufacturing and preparation of the medication was performed by an external Good Manufacturing Practice accredited facility. The randomisation schedule remained concealed from other researchers. The randomisation process ensured concealed allocation and blinding of the specialist, the participant and the outcome assessor during recruitment, data collection and analysis.  



# 2.3. Trial regimen and procedures  

The study specialists obtained relevant medical and medication history and screened the patient against the eligibility criteria. Non-study specialists obtained consent for participation. The patient was then directed by the trial specialists to the independent trial pharmacist for the supply of ‘DX/placebo’. All patients, or their representative, were fully informed of the possible types of adverse event (AE) associated with DX. Participants received standard neurosurgical care independent of, and parallel to, the trial. Patients declining to participate were offered to participate in the parallel prospective cohort study [4].  



# 2.4. Outcomes and data collection  

Pre-existent co-morbidities were categorized as previously [1,4]. Primary outcome measures included post-operative mortality (POMT) and RR at 6 months, and post-operative morbidity (POMB) by 30 days. Secondary outcome measures included the modified Rankin Score (mRS) at 6 months, and adverse events (AEs) by 30 days. POMT and RR were determined at 30 days [1,4] and at 6 months [1]. mRS was determined at discharge and at 6 months. POMB [4] and AEs were determined at 30 days [9].  

Although RR constitute POMB [4], they were recorded as an outcome measure in this study. The clinical trial protocol required the independent data monitor to review data after approximately $50\%$ of participants were recruited. The predetermined criteria for stopping the trial was a significant difference in POMT, mRS, POMB, RR or incidence of AEs between groups.  



# 2.5. Statistical analysis  

Data were collated using a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism and QuickCalcs. Continuous variables were recorded as mean or median $\pm$ standard deviation. Analysis of variance was performed for normally distributed data. Categorical variables were described as percentages or frequencies and analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. In all cases, $\mathrm{P}<0.05$ was considered statistically significant.  



# 2.5.1. Sample size calculation  

The first prospective randomised controlled study of ‘drain v no drain’ in CSDH found that recurrence rates (RR) reduced from $24\%$ in the ‘no drain’ group to $9\%$ in the ‘drain’ group $\mathbf{\epsilon}(\mathbf{P}=0.03\$ [6]. We therefore assumed a placebo RR in the middle of this range: indeed, Santarius et al. determined a pooled RR of $16.7\%$ [6]. Thus, assuming a proportion of $16.5\%$ RR in the placebo group, and a $4\%$ [3] RR in the DX group, a sample size of $\mathbf{n}=89$ would be predicted at alpha 0.05 and $80\%$ power. Interim analysis would therefore be performed after approximately $\mathtt{n}=45$ had been recruited. Sample size would be re-assessed after this analysis [9].

# 4. Discussion  

In this first registered PRPCT to study adjuvant DX on outcome after CSDH surgery with post-operative subdural drainage for $48-$ $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ [10], interim analysis confirmed that adjuvant DX, administered as a reducing regime over 2 weeks [10] (with ranitidine and antibiotic prophylaxis) [4], is safe and surprisingly free of overt AEs. Such pilot phase analysis can therefore be used to confirm and direct on-going study [9].  

Interim analysis also suggested that a RR reduction of $16.5\%$ to $4\%$ (i.e. 12.5 percentage points) at 6 months, as assumed during study planning and sample size estimation, may indeed be reasonable. In fact, the actual RR reduction observed at interim analysis of $\mathsf{n}=47$ participants (i.e. 21 percentage points, from $21\%$ to $0\%$ ) reached statistical significance $\mathrm{'P}=0.049\$ . If subsequently confirmed with our estimated sample size of $\mathbf{n}=89$ , this suggests that DX could be justified as an adjuvant to CSDH surgery with postoperative subdural drainage for $48{-}72\mathrm{h}$ .  

The estimated sample size is markedly less than that of the UK PRPCT [9]. Whilst mRS at 6 months currently represents the primary outcome measure, RR was also included as a secondary outcome measure [9], and was a pre-eminent primary study aim in an earlier protocol [14]. The authors noted numerous practical issues; most related to the large, multi-centre PRPCT design [9]. Thus, the ‘remote collection of the mRS led to several data errors due to incorrect completion’ (although measures have been subsequently taken to rectify this [9]). A greater concern related to Montreal Cognitive Assessment, where ‘a paucity of data from discharge and follow-up’ resulted in the complete abandonment of this score [9]. Further, the use of post-operative subdural drainage remained unspecified (although the prospective cohort arm noted such use in $85\%$ [15]), whilst anticipated ‘recruitment fatigue’ with prolonged study was already apparent at the pilot phase [9].  

Such vicissitudes associated with large, multi-centre CSDH studies have been previously noted. For example, in our simultaneous consecutive prospective cohort study [4] we recorded significantly greater frequency and diversity of POMB after CSDH surgery than in the analogous, larger, multi-centre UK prospective cohort [15]. Although, like others before [4,17,18], Brennan et al. had suggested similarities between CSDH and fractured neck-offemur (NOF), their results did not actually support this [4,15]. By contrast, our results [4] were comparable with fractured NOF [16]. More control over diverse data collection is available in single centre studies, especially where all data is recorded under a single consultant using the same database [4]. In our PRPCT, POMB which included RR, was similar between DX $(9/23,39\%)$ and placebo $(11/24,46\%)$ . These compare favourably to POMB in our prospective cohort study $(54/114,47\%)$ [4] and to fractured NOF $(45\%)$ [16]. Whilst unclear in the UK PRPCT [9,19], all patients in our PRPCT received anti-convulsant, ranitidine and antibiotic prophylaxis [4,13]. Presumed ‘active-drug’ AEs potentially thwart PRPCT completion. Indeed, un-blinding (with participants excluded from study) was required in 2/49 $(4\%)$ in our PRCPT: both due to presumed DX-related AEs (Fig. 1). One patient developed severe dyspepsia on day 3, despite no risk factors, no precipitant and ranitidine prophylaxis [4]. An on-call junior doctor independently ceased the trial drug, and the patient normalised without recurrence. In one other, delirium occurred on day 4, for which routine investigations revealed no immediate nor subsequent cause [4]. An on-call doctor independently ceased the trial drug, and the patient normalised without recurrence. Notably, ‘un-blinding’ revealed that, in both cases, placebo had been allocated: not DX. Two observations can be made. Firstly, when AEs occur out-of-hours, unexpected decisions may be independently taken. Secondly, ‘drug-placebo’ trials present a unique opportunity to validate  

whether AEs, traditionally ascribed to certain drugs (in this case DX), actually bear scrutiny.  

In our PRPCT, we did not observe the multiple AEs which were singularly recorded in one small prior pilot PRPCT where DX was used to conservatively manage CSDH [20]. Notably, the frequency and severity of AEs reported was also significant even with placebo [20]. Whilst listed as ‘reported’ side effects, the fact that some AEs were recorded with zero frequency suggests that AEs were specifically sought [20]. If so, the range sought was less than that commonly observed [4,20]. The study design required maintenance of maximal DX doses (i.e. $4\mathrm{mg}$ tds) for 3 weeks, only tapering in the 4th week [20]. By contrast, most other studies [7,9,10] have incorporated a reducing regime from the outset. Notwithstanding, the singularly high frequency of AEs observed could not be uniformly thus explained. The absence of hyperglycaemic complications in our study, which have ranged from $8\%$ [9] to $40\%$ [20] in other studies, is possibly due to the fact that, in our PRPCT, any known, or newly diagnosed, diabetes mellitus was prospectively monitored by an endocrinologist from admission.  

The principal criticism of our interim report is that it necessarily comprises small ‘within-group’ RR. Although statistical significance was demonstrated with RR, the independent trial monitor has naturally recommended study continuation to maximize statistical power, as originally planned. Whilst having noted singular advantages associated with single-centre data collection above, it is nevertheless stressed that multi-centre studies generally possess the advantage of increased representativeness regarding discrete, well defined, data (e.g. POMT). Notably, our study confirmed similarly significant recruitment limitations to the UK PRPCT: thus, only $42\%$ participated from those eligible (Fig. 1). Unfortunately, this appears to represent a dilemma inherent to most PRPCTs. Finally, unlike the UK PRPCT, our study did not include conservative management.  

Importantly, even if our interim data is subsequently refuted, and a null result eventually returned, neither our study nor the UK PRPCT [9] could conclude that adjuvant DX has no beneficial effect on CSDH surgery with post-operative subdural drainage. Corticosteroid use with conservative management has ranged from 3 to 4 weeks [7,8,20]. Because corticosteroid AEs classically relate to continued use beyond one month, our use of a 2 week reducing regime [10] represented a compromise. Since no study had hitherto unequivocally assessed adjuvant DX with surgery and postoperative subdural drainage, we included antibiotic prophylaxis [4] and antibiotic-impregnated drains [4] (neither stipulated in other studies [9,14,15,19]) to minimize infection risk. Since our interim analysis confirms the relative safety of adjuvant DX given such precautions, future studies could therefore consider adjuvant DX for progressively longer post-operative periods than the arbitrarily-chosen 2 weeks [8–10].  



# 5. Conclusion  

In this first registered PRPCT of adjuvant DX with CSDH surgery and post-operative subdural drainage, interim analysis suggests that adjuvant DX is both safe and may significantly decrease recurrences. Further, a $12.5\%$ point between-groups difference may be reasonable to power a final sample size of approximately $\mathbf{n}=89$ . Future studies could consider adjuvant DX for longer than the arbitrarily-chosen 2 weeks.
