BACKGROUND. Prediction of outcomes in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is challenging using current clinical predictors.  

OBJECTIVE. The purpose of our study was to evaluate the utility of machine learning (ML) models incorporating presentation clinical and CT perfusion imaging (CTP) data in predicting delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) and poor functional outcome in patients with aSAH.  

METHODS. This study entailed retrospective analysis of data from 242 patients (mean age, $60.9\pm11.8$ [SD] years; 165 women, 77 men) with aSAH who, as part of a prospective trial, underwent CTP followed by standardized evaluation for DCI during initial hospitalization and poor 3-month functional outcome (i.e., modified Rankin scale score $\geq4.$ ). Patients were randomly divided into training $(n=194)$ and test $(n=48)$ sets. Five ML models (k-nearest neighbor [KNN], logistic regression [LR], support vector machine [SVM], random forest [RF], and category boosting [CatBoost]) were developed for predicting outcomes using presentation clinical and CTP data. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator method was used for feature selection. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed in the training set. Traditional clinical models were developed using stepwise LR analysis of clinical, but not CTP, data.  

RESULTS. Qualitative CTP analysis was identified as the most impactful feature for both outcomes. In the test set, the traditional clinical model, KNN, LR, SVM, RF, and CatBoost showed AUC for predicting DCl of 0.771, 0.812, 0.824, 0.908, 0.930, and 0.949, respectively, and AUC for predicting poor 3-month functional outcome of 0.863, 0.858, 0.879, 0.908, 0.926, and 0.958. CatBoost was selected as the optimal model. In the test set, AUC was higher for CatBoost than for the traditional clinical model for predicting DCI $(p=.004)$ and poor 3-month functional outcome $(p=.04)$ . In the test set, sensitivity and specificity for predicting DCI were $92.3\%$ and $60.0\%$ for the traditional clinical model versus $92.3\%$ and $85.7\%$ for CatBoost, and sensitivity and specificity for predicting poor 3-month functional outcome were $100.0\%$ and $65.8\%$ for the traditional clinical model versus $90.0\%$ and $94.7\%$ for CatBoost. A web-based prediction tool based on CatBoost was created.  

CONCLUSION. ML models incorporating presentation clinical and CTP data outperformed traditional clinical models in predicting DCI and poor 3-month functional outcome.  

CLINICAL IMPACT. ML models may help guide early management of patients with aSAH.  

Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) affects approximately $30\%$ of patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) and is the primary factor contributing to poor functional outcome in these patients [1]. DCI is defined as neurologic deterioration that occurs between 4 and 21 days (and most commonly between 4 and 14 days) after the initial bleeding episode and is recognized to be a multifactorial process that evolves over time. In patients with aSAH, the functional outcome depends mainly on the severity of the initial bleeding episode and on the development of complications within the subsequent 2 weeks [2]. The early identification of DCI and personalized prediction of patients who are at risk of poor functional outcome can guide decisions to escalate the intensity of monitoring and to potentially implement early advanced interventions.  



# Yinetal.  

Previous studies have explored potential predictors of DCI and poor functional outcome in patients with aSAH based on data available at the time of presentation, including the volume of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) and clinical severity indicators such as the modified Fisher scale (mFS) [3], Hunt-Hess scale (HH) [4], and World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFN) scale [5]. Such measures have yielded varying results, and prediction of these outcomes remains a challenging task. ML leverages artificial intelligence technologies to reveal patterns in data that may be undetected by traditional statistical methods [6]. Although studies have attempted to use ML models incorporating clinical data at presentation to predict DCI [7–10], reliable ML prediction models are lacking.  

CT perfusion imaging (CTP) provides various functional parameters relating to tissue perfusion and circulatory status. Numerous studies have used CTP to identify DCI during the 4- to 14-day period after aSAH, which is the most common time frame for DCI occurrence [11]. One study explored the use of CTP parameters obtained within 72 hours after admission to predict DCI and poor functional outcome [12]. A study by Dong et al. [13] described the development of a one-stop whole-brain CTP protocol that was performed within 24 hours after presentation with aSAH and showed the protocol’s utility in predicting DCI. However, that study did not evaluate the protocol’s ability to predict poor functional outcomes.  

The aim of this study was to evaluate the utility of ML models based on the combination of clinical and CTP data at presentation in predicting DCI and poor functional outcome in patients with aSAH.  



# Methods Patients  

This study entailed retrospective analysis of data collected as part of a prospective trial exploring the role of CTP in patients with suspected aSAH. This study was approved by the institutional review board, and all patients or their families provided written informed consent for trial participation. The trial also involved standardized evaluation of clinical and CTP data at presentation as well as of clinical endpoints including DCI during the initial hospitalization and poor 3-month functional outcome after discharge, as described later in the Methods. # Clinical Management of Subarachnoid Hemorrhage  

All patients were hospitalized from the ED for management of SAH. During hospitalization, all included patients received nimodipine via continuous IV micropump at a rate of $10\mathrm{mL/h}$ at a concentration of $0.2\mathrm{mg/mL}$ for a duration of 10 days. The infusion rate was reduced to $5\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{h}$ if the systolic blood pressure dropped below $110\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ . Nimodipine administration was extended for up to 14 days if DCI was suspected or diagnosed. No patient received intraarterial treatment of vasospasm.  



# Clinical Outcomes  

As part of the prospective trial, an attending radiologist (Y.Z., 10 years of posttraining experience) and an attending neurosurgeon (J.Y., 9 years of posttraining experience) who were blinded to the results of CTP performed at presentation determined in consensus the presence of DCI in all trial participants, and the outcome of this determination was recorded in the EMR. DCI status was determined in a manner consistent with established expert consensus criteria [15]. This determination was based on the results of daily clinical observation (including assessment for new neurologic symptoms or a change in Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] score), follow-up CT or MRI every 2–4 days, and possible follow-up CTP. Specifically, DCI was defined as a new infarct on follow-up CT or MRI or as new unexplained significant hypoperfusion on follow-up CTP prompted by the combination of a new neurologic symptom and a decrease in GCS score. Figure 3A provides a flowchart for the determination of DCI.  

The previously noted neurosurgeon (J.Y.) performed an assessment of 3-month functional outcome using the modified Rankin scale (mRS), a commonly applied measure for describing a patient’s degree of dependence or disability [16], as part of the prospective trial. If the patient returned for routine 3-month follow-up after hospital discharge, then the mRS score was determined during a face-to-face appointment. If the patient did not return, then the patient was called by telephone to determine the mRS score. A good functional outcome was defined as an mRS score of 3 or less, and a poor functional outcome was defined as an mRS score of 4 or greater. Figure 3B shows the process for the determination of mRS score and classification of functional outcome.  



# CT Perfusion Imaging Protocol  

CT examinations using the one-stop whole-brain CTP protocol were performed using a dual-source CT scanner (Somatom Definition Flash, Siemens Healthcare). A noncontrast CT scan was first obtained with a tube voltage of $120\mathsf{kVp}$ and a tube current– time product of $390~\mathsf{mAs}$ . Each patient received a total of $55~\mathsf{mL}$ of nonionic contrast agent (ioversol, $350\mathrm{mg}1/\mathrm{mL})$ , injected into the antecubital vein at a flow rate of $5\mathrm{m}\mathsf{L}/\mathsf{s},$ followed by a $40\mathrm{-mL}$ saline flush injected at the same rate, using a dual-power injector.  

After contrast medium injection, CTP acquisition was performed in a 4D spiral mode covering a $150\mathrm{-mm}$ range from the base of the skull to the top of the head. Dynamic CTP images were obtained over 25 phases and a total acquisition time of 103.5 seconds. The CTP parameters included the following: tube voltage, $80~\mathsf{kV};$ tube current–time product, $100\mathsf{mAs};$ and section collimation, $32\times1.2\mathrm{mm}$ with a ${_z}$ -flying focal spot. The images were reconstructed with a section thickness of $3\mathrm{mm}$ and an increment of $3~\mathsf{mm}$ by using convolution kernel H30f for perfusion analysis. The phase in which the cerebral arteries showed the highest attenuation was selected for CTA reconstruction (slice thickness, $1.5\mathrm{mm}$ ; slice increment, $1.5\mathrm{mm}$ ; convolutional kernel, H30f).  



# Clinical Data at Emergency Department Presentation  

As part of the prospective trial, clinical data recorded within 24 hours after ED presentation included sex, age, location of aneurysm based on CTA (classified as anterior circulation or posterior circulation), therapy method (classified as conservative, coiling, or clipping), history of hypertension, HH (1–5 scale), WFN scale (1–5 scale), mFS (1–4 scale), subarachnoid hemorrhage early brain edema score (SEBES) (0–4 scale), and GCS.  



# CT Perfusion Imaging Data at Presentation  

As part of the prospective trial, the previously noted attending neurosurgeon (J.Y.) and attending radiologist (Y.Z.) performed a qualitative analysis and quantitative analysis of the CTP data in consensus while blinded to clinical and other imaging data.  

The reconstructed perfusion images were uploaded to the workstation (syngo.via, version VB10A, Siemens Healthineers). After step-by-step optional motion correction, 4D noise reduction, segmentation, and identification of vessels (CT Neuro Perfusion, version 6.25, Siemens Healthineers), gray-scale and color-coded perfusion parameter maps were obtained for the following seven cerebral blood measures: cerebral blood flow (CBF), cerebral blood volume (CBV), mean transit time (MTT), time to delay (TTD), time to start (TTS), transit time to the center of the impulse response function (TMax), and flow extraction product (FEP). Once the seven maps were generated, qualitative CTP analysis was performed, as previously described [13]. In this process, the color-coded maps were collectively assigned to one of three patterns: 0, nearly normal perfusion; 1, localized hypoperfusion; and 2, diffuse hypoperfusion.  

According to established methods [13] for quantitative CTP analysis, 16 ROIs were manually delineated along the cortical flow territories of the anterior cerebral artery, middle cerebral artery, posterior cerebral artery, basal ganglia, and cerebellum on five slices. Sixteen symmetric ROIs were automatically generated in the contralateral hemisphere. The mean value was automatically extracted from each ROI for each of the seven parametric perfusion maps. In each patient, the mean values of the 32 ROIs were computed for each map and denoted as mCBF, mCBV, mMTT, mTTD, mTTS, mTMax, and mFEP.  

For the purposes of the present investigation, a second radiologist (C.Z., 8 years of posttraining experience) repeated the qualitative and quantitative CTP analyses in a subset of 60 randomly selected patients from the training set (as described later in the Methods) solely to assess interobserver agreement. This radiologist’s results were not otherwise used in the remainder of the analysis.  



# Statistical Analysis Including Machine Learning Methodology  

Interreader agreement for qualitative CTP analysis was assessed using a weighted kappa coefficient (treating nearly normal perfusion, localized hypoperfusion, and diffuse hypoperfusion in an ordinal fashion). The weighted kappa coefficient was assessed as follows: less than 0.200, poor agreement; 0.200– 0.399, fair agreement; 0.400–0.599, moderate agreement; 0.600– 0.799, substantial agreement; and 0.800 or greater, almost perfect agreement. Agreement for the seven quantitative CTP metrics, which correspond to the seven CTP maps, was assessed by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC of greater than $0.750$ was considered to indicate high reproducibility.  

Patients were randomly divided into a training set $(n=194;$ $80\%$ of patients) and test set $(n=48)$ ; $20\%$ of patients). Clinical and CTP variables were compared between the two groups and within each group between patients without and those with DCI and 3-month poor functional outcome. Categoric variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, and continuous variables were compared using the t test or Mann-Whitney test on the basis of the data distribution determined by the Shapiro-Wilk test.  

ML models were developed for prediction of DCI and of poor 3-month functional outcome using clinical and CTP data at presentation. For the purpose of these models, the clinical and CTP data were standardized using z scores. Given the imbalance in the frequency of the dependent variable, the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) [17] was used to augment the minority class within the training set. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) [18] was then used for feature selection among the clinical and CTP variables at presentation. The LASSO method identifies those factors among multiple covariates with the greatest impact on the dependent variable. Features with nonzero weight-scaled coefficients were selected for inclusion in the models. Five models having trade-offs between performance and complexity were developed for the endpoints of DCI and poor 3-month functional outcome: k-nearest neighbor (KNN), logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and category boosting (CatBoost). In the training set, a 10-fold cross-validation approach, repeated 10 times, was used to train and evaluate the ML models and to tune the model measures. A similar process was followed to construct an additional model for the prediction of poor 3-month functional outcome that included clinical and CTP data at presentation as well as the development of DCI during the initial hospitalization.  

Traditional clinical models for prediction of DCI and poor 3-month functional outcome (without DCI vs with DCI) were constructed in the training set using stepwise LR analysis. These models incorporated the clinical data but did not incorporate the qualitative or quantitative CTP data.  

The models’ performance for predicting the outcomes was assessed in both the training and test sets. This assessment of model performance included determination of AUC. The diagnostic performances of the five ML models were compared for each outcome, and the diagnostic performance for predicting poor 3-month functional outcome in patients without DCI and in those with DCI were compared for each model on the basis of assessment for overlap of $95\%$ CIs.  

An optimal threshold, based on models’ predicted probability, was determined by the Youden index; separate thresholds were determined for each outcome in both the training set and the test set. Accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were determined at the selected thresholds for each model. In the training set, these performance metrics represented mean values from the 10-fold cross-validation process.  

An optimal model for predicting DCI and poor 3-month functional outcome was selected on the basis of a review of the AUC values. A web-based calculator for deriving probabilities of DCI and poor 3-month functional outcome was constructed for the selected optimal model. AUC for each outcome was compared between the traditional clinical model and the selected optimal model using the Delong test.  

Figure 4 summarizes the workflow used for implementation of the ML analysis; $p$ values were considered statistically significant when less than .05. The rms package in R (version 4.2.1) was used for univariable analyses. The scikit-learn, imblearn, and catboost libraries in Python (version 3.6) were used for ML model development.

# Interreader Agreement  

Table 2 summarizes the results of the interreader agreement analysis in the subset of 60 randomly selected patients. # Feature Selection  

Tables 3 and 4 show the comparisons of all clinical and CTP features between patients with and without DCI as well as between patients without and with poor 3-month functional outcome, respectively, for both the training and test sets.  

The features with the greatest impact on prediction of DCI based on the LASSO method included the following in order of greatest to least importance: qualitative CTP analysis, mFEP, aneurysm location, history of hypertension, therapy method, mFS score, mMTT, mCBF, and GCS score. In the test set, AUC was significantly higher for CatBoost than for the traditional clinical model for predicting DCI $(p=.004)$ and predicting poor 3-month functional outcome $(p=.04)$ but was not significantly higher for predicting poor 3-month functional outcome when including DCI $(p=.34)$ . In the test set, for predicting DCI, the traditional model had sensitivity of $92.3\%,$ specificity of $60.0\%$ , and accuracy of $68.8\%,$ and CatBoost had accuracy of $87.5\%$ , sensitivity of $92.3\%$ , and specificity of $85.7\%$ . In the test set, for predicting poor 3-month functional outcome, the traditional clinical model had sensitivity of $100.0\%$ , specificity of $65.8\%,$ and accuracy of $72.9\%$ , and CatBoost had accuracy of $93.8\%$ , sensitivity of $90.0\%,$ and specificity of $94.7\%$ . In the test set, for predicting poor 3-month functional outcome when including DCI during the initial hospitalization, the traditional clinical model had sensitivity of $80.0\%$ , specificity of $94.7\%$ , and accuracy of $93.8\%$ and CatBoost had accuracy of $91.7\%$ , sensitivity of $90.0\%,$ and specificity of $92.1\%$ . Table 5 also provides the thresholds for the various models for the various outcomes.  

A web-based prediction tool—that is, a calculator for deriving probabilities of DCI and of poor 3-month functional outcome— based on the CatBoost model was created.

This study explored the utility of ML models for predicting development of DCI and poor 3-month functional outcome on the basis of clinical and CTP data at presentation in patients presenting with aSAH. The CatBoost model was selected as the optimal model and outperformed traditional clinical models for both endpoints. The findings indicate a potential role for ML incorporating CTP findings in guiding early prognostic assessments and facilitating early treatment and intervention.

A study from 2021 showed that clinical variables that are routinely collected in the early stages of hospitalization can predict DCI [21]. In an additional study, the MTT from CTP, as determined within the initial 24 hours after admission, showed strong association with 6-month functional outcome [22]. In the current study, qualitative CTP analysis and quantitative CTP parameters derived from CTP performed at presentation were strong influential factors in predicting both clinical endpoints. The earlier and present studies collectively support the importance of early collection of clinical and perfusion data in predicting DCI and functional outcome.  

Among all clinical and CTP variables, qualitative CTP analysis was the most important variable in the ML models (aside from age in the model for predicting poor 3-month functional outcome). Among quantitative CTP variables, mFEP was the most important for predicting both DCI and poor 3-month functional outcome. This parameter reflects the permeability of the blood-brain barrier; blood-brain barrier disruption is a prominent pathologic feature of both ischemia and hemorrhagic stroke and is a critical aspect of early brain injury and DCI [23–25]. A study by Zhang et al. [14] also supported mFEP as a superior CTP parameter for early prediction of DCI. Whereas mFEP was the second-most important predictor of DCI, it was the fourth-most important predictor of poor 3-month functional outcome; this difference may reflect the reversible nature of blood-brain barrier disruption [14].  

DCI is an important cause of disability and death, often leading to deterioration of a patient’s condition or quality of life. A poor functional outcome is closely related to DCI and usually occurs in patients who previously experienced DCI [26]. We thus explored the impact of inclusion of DCI during the initial hospitalization on the models’ performance in predicting poor 3-month functional outcome and found no significant improvement in comparison with prediction based solely on clinical and CTP data at the time of presentation. Implementation of the model using only information available at the time of presentation—without inclusion of DCI during hospitalization—improves the model’s practicality and reduces difficulties associated with additional data collection.  

We used the LASSO regression method to select features owing to the method’s ability to address limitations inherent in the least-squares estimation method and to overcome the problem of local optimal estimation in stepwise regression analysis. Additionally, LASSO effectively resolves the issue of multiple collinearities among features and performs well on complex mixed features with both categoric and continuous variables [18]; these characteristics result in the LASSO method being well suited for the current study’s data.  

Traditional ML models have limitations. For example, the KNN model is highly dependent on the training data and may lack robustness in the presence of incorrect training data, whereby even a small number of incorrect data points close to the value being classified can result in inaccurate prediction. LR analysis, essentially a linear classifier, is limited in its capacity to capture complex interrelationships among factors that contribute to model outcomes. In contrast, the RF model requires large amounts of data to perform well and may not generate good classification results in the presence of fewer or low-dimensional datasets. The performance of SVMs is significantly influenced by the choice of kernel function, which is usually based on experience. CatBoost is a member of the family of gradient-boosted decision tree ML ensemble techniques, which are powerful tools for classification and regression tasks in ML [27]. Since its debut in late 2018, CatBoost has been successfully applied in a wide range of ML studies [28, 29]. In this study, the CatBoost model showed significantly better performance for predicting aSAH outcomes than ML models in earlier studies [30, 31]. There are several possible explanations for this difference [28, 29, 32]. First, as we previously noted, CatBoost uses gradient boosting. Gradient boosting iteratively adds decision trees to predict the residual errors of the previous trees and is highly effective in handling complicated nonlinear relationships among variables. Second, CatBoost has been designed specifically to identify subtle patterns in datasets comprising categoric variables (which are prevalent in medical datasets). Third, this study included additional variables, such as CTP parameters, that were not included in prior studies [7–10].  

This study had limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a small sample size. In particular, the test set included only 48 patients. Second, external validation was lacking. Third, qualitative CTP analysis was assessed subjectively. Fourth, the use of mean values from ROIs across the brain for quantitative CTP parameters may have resulted in an underestimation of hemodynamic impairments. Fifth, different thresholds were applied for the various endpoints in the training and test sets. Sixth, although CatBoost outperformed the traditional clinical model for predicting poor 3-month functional outcome, this comparison was not significant when including DCI. Finally, although CatBoost was selected as the optimal ML model, comparisons of performance between CatBoost and the other models were not statistically significant.  



# Conclusion  

ML methods can use clinical and CTP data from the time of presentation with aSAH to predict DCI and poor 3-month functional outcome. Inclusion in models of development of DCI during the initial hospitalization did not improve predictive performance for poor 3-month functional outcome. The CatBoost model was selected as the optimal ML model, which outperformed traditional clinical models for predicting both outcomes. The findings indicate a role for ML based on clinical and CTP parameters at presentation in the early management of patients with aSAH.

# Editorial Comment: Prediction of Cerebral Ischemia After Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage by CT Perfusion Imaging on Admission  

This study showed the clinical utility of CT perfusion imaging (CTP) data obtained on admission, in addition to the World Federation of Neurological Surgeons scale and modified Fisher scale scores conventionally used by neurosurgeons, to predict the prognosis of patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) due to aneurysmal rupture. This report is significant for radiologists as it reveals a potentially important contribution to patient management from minimally invasive CTP.  

In patients with SAH, the development of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCl) is known to be closely related to the patient's prognosis and to be associated with prolonged hospital stay, poor functional outcome, and death. CTP detects abnormal bloodbrain barrier permeability, a precursor of DCI, that develops immediately after SAH onset. In addition, this study was based on CTP and CTA examinations obtained within 24 hours after admission. Of benefit to the patients in the study, the CTA data used for the diagnosis of a ruptured aneurysm and the CTP data were all obtained as part of a single imaging examination.  

An additional notable element of the study is the authors’ assessment of whole-brain CTP parameters. Although numerous whole-brain metrics contributed to predicting DCI, mean flow extraction product had particular diagnostic utility. The postprocessing required to calculate these metrics used commercial software and is presumed to be simple. Accordingly, an advantage of the use of whole-brain metrics is its practicality.  

As I previously mentioned, this study’s main purpose was to evaluate the possibility of predicting the risk of DCI on the basis of whole-brain CTP data. Hopefully, it will prove possible to also predict the localization and degree of DCI occurrences.
