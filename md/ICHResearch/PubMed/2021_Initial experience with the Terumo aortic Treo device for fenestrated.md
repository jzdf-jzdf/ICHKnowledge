# Initial experience with the Terumo aortic Treo device for fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair  

Jake F. Hemingway, MD, Benjamin W. Starnes, MD, Brenda R. Kline, ARNP, and Niten Singh, MD, Seattle, Wash  

# ABSTRACT  

Objective: The Terumo aortic (TA) Treo device (Terumo, Somerset, NJ) is an endograft with unique features that lends itself to fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair (FEVAR), including a low device profile, a wide amplitude stent design, and an increased interstent distance. We have described our initial experience with the Treo device for FEVAR to treat short neck and juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysms.  

Methods: As part of an ongoing physician-sponsored investigational device exemption clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT01538056), subjects were prospectively enrolled and underwent elective FEVAR using a variety of devices. Demographic and procedural details were collected. The data from subjects treated specifically with the Treo device from November 3, 2016 to May 2, 2019 were collected and analyzed.  

Results: Of a cohort of 161 patients who had undergone elective FEVAR, 46 had been treated with the TA Treo device. Most patients were men $(70\%)$ ), with a mean age of 75 years and high rates of hypertension $(74\%)$ , hyperlipidemia $(83\%)$ , coronary artery disease $(33\%)$ , and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease $(33\%)$ . The mean aneurysm size was $66\mathsf{mm}$ , the mean preoperative infrarenal neck length was $5~\mathsf{mm}$ , and the mean final seal zone length was $45~\mathsf{mm}$ . The average hospital and intensive care unit lengths of stay were 2.4 and 1.5 days, respectively. A total of 129 fenestrations were created for 44 superior mesenteric and 85 renal arteries (2.8 fenestrations per patient). Technical success, defined as successful implantation of the device with all target vessels preserved, was $98\%$ (45 of 46), with only one renal artery not successfully preserved. The mean follow-up period was 598 days. During the study period, 18 endoleaks were detected (17 type II and 1 type III), with one patient with a type III endoleak requiring reintervention. Three subjects had died within 30 days, one of intracranial hemorrhage, one of respiratory failure, and one of ischemic colitis. The graft modification times for the TA Treo were significantly shorter (43 minutes) than those for other commercially available devices (Cook Zenith, 55 minutes; Medtronic Endurant, 54 minutes; $P<$ .0001).  

Conclusions: Our institution has reported exclusive worldwide experience using the TA Treo device for FEVAR. This device provides for a highly efficient and technically successful procedure for most patients. The procedural and fluoroscopy times were low even in the setting of high complexity. The technical success rates and simplification of the FEVAR procedure have made this approach a preferred technique for most patients at our institution. (J Vasc Surg 2021;74:823-31.)  

Keywords: Aneurysm; Endovascular aneurysm repair; Fenestrated; FEVAR; Physician modified  

Although fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair (FEVAR) has been established as a successful treatment paradigm for patients with complex, juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysms (JAAAs), its use has remained limited by the lack of available devices.1-9 Although a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)eapproved custom-made device is available for use in the United States, at present, it requires significant manufacturing time and cannot accommodate all patient anatomies, limiting its use.  

Physician-modified endografts (PMEGs) have been proposed as an alternative to commercially available devices, in which grafts are customized to the individual patient’s anatomy.2,3,9 In addition to the theoretical benefits of any fenestrated repair, which include maximizing the length of the healthy aortic neck, PMEGs allow for the possibility of an off-the-shelf option that could be applied to patients requiring repair in a timely fashion. Despite the excellent short- and medium-term results for patients with JAAAs undergoing FEVAR using PMEGs, their widespread adoption has been limited by multiple factors, including ongoing concerns regarding the complexity in planning and successful execution of these repairs.2,3,9,10 Additionally, the lack of quality control, inherent to physician-modified devices, and the unknown long-term durability of these repairs, has limited their widespread use.  

Our previous study demonstrated the usefulness of automated planning software in simplifying this planning process to make PMEGs more accessible. However, limitations in this technique still exist owing to the stent graft design features, including narrowly spaced stents that occasionally require placement of a fenestration overlying a stent strut.11 The Terumo aortic (TA) Treo device (Terumo, Somerset, NJ) has a low-profile design with wide amplitude stents and increased interstent distances that lends itself to FEVAR (Fig 1).  

The primary aim of the present study was to specifically evaluate the ease of modification, effectiveness, and safety of the TA Treo device, given the theoretical benefits inherent to its specific stent graft design. We hypothesized that the wider interstent distances would allow for shorter modification times without sacrificing the high technical success and safety outcomes previously demonstrated with PMEGs.  

# METHODS  

As part of an ongoing physician-sponsored investigational device exemption (IDE) clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.- gov identifier, NCT01538056), the included subjects had undergone elective physician-modified FEVAR using a variety of devices, including the TA Treo device. This clinical trial was conducted in accordance with the FDA policies and regulations. The institutional review board of the University of Washington approved the present study, and all patients provided written informed consent. The study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria, protocol, and endpoints have been previously described.2,3  

Patients with JAAAs who had met the eligibility criteria (Supplementary Table, online only) but were not candidates for open repair were offered FEVAR with either a PMEG or a Cook Zenith fenestrated device (Cook Medical, Bloomington, Ind). Patients who were not candidates for a Zenith fenestrated device and had met the inclusion criteria were only offered FEVAR with a PMEG. After a thorough discussion of the risks, benefits, and alternatives to FEVAR and if a PMEG were chosen, the patients were enrolled in the IDE clinical trial. All endografts used in the present study had been approved for use, with each modification occurring under sterile conditions and performed by the treating physician. In the present report, we have described the results for those patients treated with the TA Treo device. All patients in the present study were treated using a bifurcated stent graft, and all procedures were performed in a hybrid operating room.  

# ARTICLE HIGHLIGHTS  

Type of Research: A single-center retrospective review of prospectively collected data from a physician-sponsored investigational device exemption clinical trial   
d Key Findings: Fenestrated endovascular repair of 46 patients with juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysms using the Terumo aortic Treo device (Terumo, Somerset, NJ) resulted in a $98\%$ technical success rate with low 30-day mortality $(7\%)$ and shorter graft modification times (43 minutes) compared with other available devices.   
d Take Home Message: The Terumo aortic Treo device can safely be used for fenestrated endovascular repair of juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysms with high technical success rates and short graft modification times.  

The creation of an individualized fenestrated template using automated software and the process of endograft modification and deployment have also been previously described.11 During preparation of the patient in the operating room, the proximal three stents of the TA Treo device were exposed under sterile conditions on the back table. Using the diameters of the intended fenestrations, distances from the proximal fabric edge, and arc length measurements of the renal arteries laterally from the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) fenestration, the fenestration locations were marked on the graft fabric using a sterile pen. Ophthalmic cautery was then used to cut fenestrations in the graft fabric, which were marked and reinforced using an Amplatz goose neck snare (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minn)da highly radiopaque loop of gold-plated tungsten coil surrounding a nitinol core wire. This was secured in place using a running 4-0 polypropylene suture in a $360^{\circ}$ fashion. The modified endograft was then re-constrained using Rummel tourniquets and a spiral wrap of umbilical tape, which was sequentially unwrapped as the delivery system sheath was advanced over the endograft. After endograft deployment, the renal arteries were routinely stented using 6- or 7-mm balloon expandable iCAST polytetrafluoroethylene-covered stents (Atrium, Hudson, NH) that were flared to 8 or 9 mm proximally; the SMA was only selectively stented. A completion cone beam computed tomography scan was performed for all patients with normal renal function to assess for graft narrowing and target vessel kinking.  

The demographic, procedural, and periprocedural (30- day) data were collected. Hypertension was defined as a history of systolic blood pressure ${>}140\mathrm{mm}\ H9$ , diastolic blood pressure ${\tt>}90$ mm Hg, or pharmacologic treatment of essential hypertension. Hyperlipidemia was defined as a history of a total serum cholesterol $>200\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , serum triglycerides $>150\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , or pharmacologic treatment of hyperlipidemia. Coronary artery disease was defined as a history of coronary artery bypass or percutaneous coronary intervention. Congestive heart failure was defined as a history of demonstrated systolic or diastolic dysfunction on echocardiography or pharmacologic treatment of heart failure. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was defined as a history of obstructive physiology found by pulmonary function testing (forced expiratory volume in 1 second/forced vital capacity ratio ${<}0.7\$ ) or pharmacologic treatment of COPD. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a history of a hemoglobin A1c ${>}6.5\%$ or pharmacologic treatment of diabetes. Chronic renal insufficiency was defined as a history of an estimated glomerular filtration rate $<60\mathrm{mL/min/}$ $1.73~\mathsf{m}^{2}$ that was stable for multiple measurements.

The primary endpoint of the present study was technical success, which was defined as successful implantation of the device with all target vessels preserved. The secondary endpoints included the graft modification time, procedure time, major adverse events (MAE), hospital and intensive care unit lengths of stay, and 30-day mortality. MAE included death, myocardial infarction, stroke, renal failure, respiratory failure, paralysis, bowel ischemia, and procedural estimated blood loss ${>}11$ . All adverse events were adjudicated by a clinical events committee.2,3,9,11  

The data are reported as frequencies, the mean $\pm$ standard deviation, proportions, and ranges, as appropriate. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare the graft modification times between the devices. A $\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Pi}}_{P}$ value ${<}.05$ was considered statistically significant.  

# RESULTS  

Of 161 consecutive patients enrolled in the ongoing physician-sponsored IDE trial, 46 had undergone elective FEVAR with the TA Treo device from November 3, 2016 to  

May 2, 2019. The patient demographics and baseline anatomic details are presented in Table I. The mean aneurysm size was 66 mm, with a mean preoperative neck length of $5\mathsf{mm}$ .  

The procedural details are listed in Table II. A total of 129 total fenestrations (2.8 fenestrations per patient) were created, 44 for SMAs and 85 for renal arteries. No scallops were used. Of the 46 patients who had undergone attempted FEVAR with the TA Treo device, all had had successful device implantation, with $99\%$ of the branch vessels successfully preserved. Technical success, defined as successful implantation of the device with all target vessels preserved, was $98\%$ , with only one renal artery not successfully preserved. Failure to preserve the renal artery had resulted from significant ostial stenosis that prevented successful cannulation of the renal artery, an outcome anticipated preoperatively owing to the extent of the patient’s renal artery atherosclerosis $(>90\%)$ . The mean final seal zone length was 45 mm.  

The mean follow-up was 598 days. During follow-up, 18 endoleaks were detected (17 type II, 1 type III). Only one reintervention was required to treat a type III endoleak discovered on the 1-month postoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) examination. This type III endoleak had occurred as a result of a technical error, in which, during contralateral gate selection after main body deployment, the ipsilateral limb had been inadvertently selected, with both iliac limbs deployed from the ipsilateral limb of the main body. This was not recognized during the procedure because of parallax but was found on the 1-month routine postoperative CTA examination and was successfully treated with Amplatzer plug (St Jude Medical, St Paul, Minn) occlusion of the main body contralateral gate and balloon-expandable stent placement within the existing iliac limbs for reinforcement. During the follow-up period, no aneurysmal sac expansion was seen in any of the patients with a type II endoleak; thus, no interventions were required. No ruptures or conversions to open repair occurred. The mortality and hospital and intensive care unit lengths of stay are listed in Table III, and the 30-day MAE are listed in Table IV. Within 30 days, 7 patients $(15\%)$ had experienced a MAE, including 3 patients who died.  

The first patient who died was a 66-year-old woman. She had a history of a ischemic stroke 9 months previously. She was found dead at home at 2 weeks postoperatively after she had experienced an intracranial hemorrhage. She had been cleared by neurology for systemic heparinization at 6 months after her stroke. However, her repair had been delayed an additional 3 months in an attempt to avoid intracranial hemorrhage in accordance with the neurology recommendations. The second patient who died was an 84-year-old woman with Gold stage II COPD and a history of partial lobectomy for left lung cancer. Her postoperative course had been complicated by pneumonia and COPD exacerbation. She ultimately died of respiratory failure. The final patient had died of ischemic colitis, which had developed on postoperative day 2 and was, ultimately, the result of an access complication that had caused hypotension (owing to retroperitoneal bleeding from a high access) in a patient with a poor baseline cardiac status. She had then likely developed a watershedtype ischemic injury to her small bowel and colon, which was not survivable. During her workup, the SMA fenestration was shown to be widely patent by both duplex ultrasound and CTA examinations.

# DISCUSSION  

FEVAR of JAAAs using PMEGs has repeatedly demonstrated durable results, with high technical success rates and good safety outcomes.2,3,9,11 Nonetheless, concerns regarding the complexity and time required for planning and the efficient execution of these procedures have continued to limit widespread use of this technique.12 Although the use of an automated planning software to simplify operative planning and fenestration creation is promising, the stent graft design has continued to impose challenges in adopting PMEGs.11 Of the 46 TA Treo devices modified in the present study, 19 were modified using a template.  

The TA Treo device is characterized by several unique design features that navigate these challenges. With the wider amplitude stents and larger interstent distances, the TA Treo device naturally lends itself to easier modification, with a lower risk of struts having to cross fenestrations (Fig 1). These theoretical benefits resulted in shorter modification times in the present study compared with the modification times required for the Zenith and Medtronic stent grafts (Table II). These shorter modification times did not, however, compromise the technical success rates or safety, with PMEGs using the TA Treo device having similar success, endoleak, and MAE rates compared with those of other stent graft types.2

Combined with automated planning software, the PMEG process has continued to evolve and simplify, allowing for greater application to a wider variety of patients. Despite the advances seen with this technique, the technical success rates, rates of endoleak development, and MAE rates have remained comparable to those from previous studies examining endovascular outcomes after both standard and short-neck infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) repair.2-9,11 Our preliminary results are promising and have demonstrated the safety, effectiveness, and efficiency of PMEGs with the TA Treo device.  

Given that the present study was performed under the umbrella of an IDE and describes the off-label use of a commercially available device, wide dissemination of this technique is not yet achievable. The TA Treo device has not been specifically tested for use as a fenestrated device, and ongoing concerns regarding the lack of quality control of PMEGs were not addressed in the present study. Additionally, the PMEG technique is associated with a previously described learning curve.12 Learning this technique requires expert training by an individual with the interest and clinical volume required to become proficient. Finally, although our preliminary results are promising, longer term data and larger experience confirming these results are required before advocating the use of the TA Treo device for PMEGs outside the context of an IDE trial, especially given recent studies that have questioned the long-term durability of PMEGs.13

Additional concerns with the use of PMEGs for fenestrated repair of complex AAAs include the possibility of proximal aortic degeneration above the stent graft seal zone with development of proximal aneurysmal disease, which has been previously demonstrated in perivisceral and thoracoabdominal aneurysms, especially when using two to three fenestrations vs four.14,15 Although these factors might favor the use of branched endografts or four-vessel FEVAR, these challenges were not applicable to the present study. Because it was an FDAeapproved IDE clinical trial, strict inclusion criteria (n $\mathsf{ack}>2\mathsf{mm}$ ) limited enrollment in the present study to only those patients with confirmed juxtarenal AAAs. The present study did not include patients with pararenal or type 4 thoracoabdominal aneurysms, which can be treated in a second, completely separate, physician-sponsored IDE clinical trial at our institution, which has the ability to use both custom-modified (including fenestrations) and branched devices.  

The high rate of postoperative type II endoleak $(37\%)$ was surprising. Also, although the exact cause for this remains unclear, the rate itself was not concerning because no aneurysm sac enlargement occurred during the study period. Additionally, our previously reported PMEG experience demonstrated very low rates of intervention required for the treatment of type II endoleaks that had occurred after PMEG, suggesting that few of these will require treatment in the future.2 Nevertheless, these findings also reinforce the importance of ongoing surveillance after all FEVAR procedures.  

One significant advantage of the TA Treo device is the ability to collapse the graft in situ when two points of fixation are created by inserting a 20F DrySeal sheath (W. L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, Ariz) into the contralateral gate. Simply twisting the delivery system handle will allow the graft to torque down to a small diameter, allowing repositioning of the endograft and creation of a gap between the graft and aortic wall, facilitating easy target vessel cannulation (Fig 2). No diameter reducing ties will, therefore, be required to safely implant the device. Two additional features are distinctdboth supra- and infrarenal active fixation help to prevent migration of the main body, and the interlocking limbs prevent component separation.

The described technique, in which two active points of fixation allow for the graft to be collapsed in situ, was used successfully in 15 patients in the present series $(33\%)$ , all without evidence of any complications. None of these 15 patients had any evidence of embolic complications, including no case of bowel ischemia, limb malperfusion, renal infarction, or creatinine increase to ${\ge}20\%$ greater than baseline. This technique facilitates efficient cannulation of difficult target vessels by providing offset of the graft away from the target vessel ostia (Fig 3). Although in our small series, no embolic complications occurred, caution is still required when using this technique in any patient with aortic wall irregularity or thrombus-laden aortic necks, given the concern for distal embolization.  

As the national population continues to age, with the anticipation of the approaching “Silver Tsunami,” the number of patients requiring treatment of short-neck, complex JAAAs will continue to increase. Thus, we require more easily accessible treatment options, with the PMEG technique offering one such solution. Ongoing simplification of this process will continue to increase the availability of treatment options for these patients.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

Our institution has exclusive worldwide experience using the TA Treo device for FEVAR. This device provides for a highly efficient and technically successful procedure for most patients, with low procedural and fluoroscopy times, even in the setting of high complexity. The technical success rates and simplification of the FEVAR procedure have made this approach the preferred technique for most patients treated at our institution, with results that warrant further investigation of the medium- and long-term durability of the TA Treo device when used for FEVAR.
