# Serum arachidonic acid levels is a predictor of poor functional outcome in acute intracerebral hemorrhage  

Junichiro Takahashi \*, Kenichiro Sakai, Takeo Sato, Hiroki Takatsu, Teppei Komatsu, Hidetaka Mitsumura, Hidetomo Murakami, Yasuyuki Iguchi  

Department of Neurology, The Jikei University School of Medicine, Japan  

# ARTICLE  INFO  

# ABSTRACT  

Keywords:   
Intracerebral hemorrhage   
Lipid metabolism   
Outcome  

Background and aims: Correlations between serum levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) such as eico­ sapentaenoic acid (EPA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), arachidonic acid (AA) and dihomogammalinolenic acid (DHLA) and outcomes following cardiovascular disease have been reported. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between serum levels of PUFAs (including AA) and functional outcomes among intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) patients.  

Methods: From November 2012 to July 2020, ICH patients within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ from onset were enrolled. Patients were divided into a good functional outcome group (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score at 3 months, 0–3) and a poor functional outcome group (mRS score at 3 months, 4–6). We compared baseline variables between groups.  

Results: Participants comprised 133 patients (mean age, 60 years), with 106 patients $(80\%)$ in the good functional outcome group and 27 patients $(20\%)$ in the poor functional outcome group. Higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score and larger hematoma on admission were more frequent in the poor functional outcome group (median NIHSS score 6 vs. 14, $\mathbf{p}<0.001$ ; median hematoma volume, $7.5~\mathrm{ml}$ vs. $13.5~\mathrm{ml}$ , $\mathbf{p}=$ 0.07). In terms of serum PUFA levels, only median serum AA level was significantly lower in the poor functional outcome group $(212\upmu\mathrm{g/ml}$ vs. $179\upmu{}g/{}\mathrm{ml}$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.002,$ . Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed lower serum AA level was independently associated with poor functional outcome (odds ratio 0.986, $95\%$ confidence interval 0.976–0.996, $\mathbf{p}=0.009.$ ).  

Conclusion: Lower serum AA level was associated with poor functional outcome in ICH patients. AA may represent an important biomarker of severity among ICH patients.  

# 1. Introduction  

Acute intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is estimated to affect over 1 million people worldwide each year and the incidence in Japan is twice that in Western countries, with most cases representing potentially devastating neurological emergencies with major consequences for long-term functional independence [1]. ICH constitutes $10{-}15\%$ of all strokes and carries high risks of morbidity and mortality. Despite ad­ vances in the treatment of cerebral infarction and subarachnoid artery hemorrhage, therapies with proven benefit in improving outcomes after ICH remain lacking. Preventing the onset and controlling the progres­ sion of ICH are thus crucial [2]. In some experimental stroke models, brain lipid metabolism, especially that of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), has been gaining attention as a possible area of therapeutic importance in terms of neuroprotective effects in the brain.  

PUFAs such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), arachidonic acid (AA) and dihomogammalinolenic acid (DHLA) are poorly synthesized in the human body. Large-scale epidemiological studies have suggested that intake of foods enriched with EPA, DHA, and fish oils may reduce the incidences of cardiovascular diseases and ischemic stroke [3–5]. In experimental models, anti-oxidative products mainly derived from AA may have beneficial effects, increasing levels of the protein peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor- $\upgamma$ (PPARγ), a regulator of antioxidative proteins such as catalase, and reducing expression of the gene for the proinflammatory protein nuclear factor $\mathrm{\kappaB}$ (NFκB) [6–8]. PUFAs such as AA may thus play an important role in reducing neuron damage and improving functional recovery.  

There are some predictive biomarkers of major coronary events such as Troponin I or H-FABP [9,10].  

Serum levels of PUFAs also could provide useful markers to determine the incidence of not only major coronary events, but also peripheral artery disease, and early neurological deterioration after acute ischemic stroke [11]. In contrast, no clinical studies have explored the associations of functional outcome from ICH with PUFA levels. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between serum PUFA levels (including AA levels) and clinical outcome in ICH patients.  

# 2. Methods  

# 2.1. Selection of subjects  

The present study was based on an analysis of data acquired from a retrospective registry of acute hypertensive ICH patients within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after onset between November 2012 and July 2020. Acute ICH was diagnosed from computed tomography of the brain. To evaluate the functional outcome, we used modified Rankin scale [mRS], defined as follows: grade0: no symptoms at all, grade 1: no significant disability despite symptoms: able to carry out all usual duties and activities, grade 2: slight disability: unable to carry out all previous activities but able to look after own affairs without assistance, grade 3: moderate disability: requiring some help, but able to walk without assistance, grade 4: moderately severe disability: unable to walk without assistance, and unable to attend to own bodily needs without assistance, grade 5; severe disability: bedridden, incontinent, and requiring constant nursing care and attention [12].  

Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) pre-stroke disability or medical illness (mRS score 2–5); 2) surgical intervention such as external ven­ tricular drainage, endoscopic, and direct hematoma evacuation; 3) secondary ICH caused by tumor, arteriovenous malformation and fis­ tula; 4) missing clinical outcome at 3 months after discharge; or 5) ever taking EPA or DHA agents  

All patients were divided into a good functional outcome group (mRS score at 3 months, 0–3) and poor functional outcome group (mRS score at 3 months, 4–6). The following clinical data were collected from all subjects: atherosclerotic risk factors (including hypertension [HT], dia­ betes mellitus [DM] and dyslipidemia [DLP]), radiological findings, and laboratory findings including serum PUFA levels at baseline. Outcome data, including neurological status at 3 months, were evaluated at outpatient follow-up visit after hospital discharge or by letters for pa­ tients who could not visit our hospital at 3 months. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The inde­ pendent ethics committee at our hospital approved this study (approval no. 8811).  

# 2.2. Risk factors  

Atherosclerotic vascular risk factors were defined as follows: 1) HT (history of using antihypertensive agents, systolic blood pressure $>140$ $\mathrm{mmHg}$ or diastolic blood pressure $>90\mathrm{mmHg}$ at 14 days after stroke onset); 2) DM (use of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin, or glycosy­ lated hemoglobin $[\mathrm{Hb}]\mathrm{A}1\mathrm{c}\geq6.5\%)$ ; 3) DLP (use of antihyperlipidemic agents, serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [ $\mathrm{LDL-Cl}\geq140\mathrm{mg/dl},$ high-density lipoprotein cholesterol $\mathrm{[HDL-C]}~<~40~\mathrm{mg/dl}_{;}$ or tri­ glycerides $\mathrm{[TG]}\geq150\mathrm{mg/dl})$ ; 4) current cigarette smoking $(>5$ ciga­ rettes a day for ${>}6$ months); 5) coronary artery disease (history of angina pectoris or myocardial infarction); or 6) peripheral artery disease (his­ tory of peripheral artery disease or ankle-brachial index $<1.0$ )  

# 2.3. Laboratory findings  

Serum levels of PUFAs including EPA, DHA, AA, and DHLA were assayed by gas chromatography at an external laboratory (SRL Inc., Tokyo, Japan). We also analyzed serum levels of LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, glucose, HbA1c, platelet count, and coagulation-related factors including fibrinogen, activated partial thromboplastin time [APTT], and prothrombin time-international normalized ratio [PT-INR]. Blood examinations were conducted within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of admission.  

# 2.4. Radiological findings  

All patients underwent CT of the brain at $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after onset, and hematoma volumes at each time were calculated according to a standardized technique [13]. Hematoma expansion was defined as an increase of $>33\%$ or $>\mathrm{~6~ml~}$ on follow-up CT compared to the description of the CT on admission. Brain edema including peri­ hematomal edema was defined as a low-density area beyond the boundary of the apparent hematoma on CT [14].  

# 2.5. Statistical analysis  

Numerical values are reported as medians and interquartile ranges. Baseline characteristics, vascular risk factors, brain CT findings, and laboratory data were compared between good and poor functional outcome groups. Data were statistically analyzed using the chi-square test for categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney $U$ test and Kruskal-Wallis test for nonparametric analysis. Two-sided $p$ -values less than 0.05 were considered significant. As a guide for building a multi­ variate model, we applied the backward selection methods, which starts with a full model that contains all the predictors in the model. The least significant factors: that is, the ones having the largest P value $(>10\%)$ are eliminated, and the model is then refitted. Each step removes the least significant variables from the model until the remaining variables have their P value smaller than the specified 0.10. The method gives each variables an opportunity to become associated with the outcome, and built a parsimonious multivariate model.  

To elucidate the contribution of serum AA level to clinical charac­ teristics of ICH patients, patients were also classified according to the tertile of serum AA level, and clinical outcomes were compared with these factors. All data were analyzed using SPSS for Windows version 15.0 statistical software (SPSS, Chicago, IL).  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Study population and clinical characteristics  

A total of 133 patients (median age, 60 years; range 53–68 years; 99 men) with primary ICH met our inclusion criteria and were included in the present study. Of these, 106 patients $(80\%)$ were categorized to the good functional outcome group and 27 patients $(20\%)$ to the poor functional outcome group. Table 1 shows baseline characteristics and radiological and laboratory findings. Age was significantly higher for patients in the poor functional outcome group, but the frequency of male patients did not differ between groups. Among atherosclerotic risk fac­ tors, frequencies of HT and current cigarette smoking were lower for patients in the poor functional outcome group.  

NIHSS scores and frequency of disturbance of consciousness on admission (defined as Glasgow Coma Scale $[{\mathrm{GCS}}]\leq8$ according to a description in the literature were higher for patients in the poor func­ tional outcome group. For CT findings, maximum hematoma volume and frequency of hematoma expansion and cerebral edema showing midline shift were higher for patients in the poor functional outcome group. In terms of laboratory data, serum glucose level was higher, while albumin level was lower for patients in the poor functional outcome group. Among serum PUFA levels, only serum AA levels were signifi­ cantly lower for patients in the poor functional outcome group. No significant differences in other PUFAs levels (EPA, DHA, and DHLA) were found between groups (Fig. 1).  

# 3.2. Factors correlating independently with outcome for ICH  

In multivariate logistic regression analysis, we began with full model which includes all predictors in the model and eliminate variables one at a time. As the candidate variables, we selected well-established pre­ dictor of poor functional outcome in acute ICH; Age on admission, the severity of disturbance of consciousness, size of hematoma, whether the origin of hemorrhage was infratentorial or not [14].

# 3.3. Comparison of clinical characteristics according to the tertile of serum AA level  

Patients were classified into three tertiles of AA: tertile $\mathrm{I},\leq181.5\upmu{g}/$ ml; tertile II, $181.6{-}233.3~{\upmu\mathrm{g/ml}};$ and tertile II $\mathrm{I},>233.3\upmu{g/ml}$ . Table 3 shows a comparison of clinical characteristics, radiological and labora­ tory data according to the AA tertile. Patients in AA tertile III displayed the lowest frequency of poor functional outcome (tertile I, $33\%$ vs. tertile II, $20\%$ vs. tertile III, $7\%$ ; $p=0.008,$ and cerebral edema with midline shift $(7\%$ vs. $14\%$ vs. $0\%$ ; $p=0.039.$ ). No significant decreases were identified in hemorrhage volume on admission, maximum hema­ toma volume within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , or frequency of hematoma expansion.  

# 4. Discussion  

The present study revealed three major findings. First, serum AA level was the only PUFA level significantly lower among the poor functional outcome than among the good functional outcome. In multivariate logistic regression analysis, lower serum AA level and higher age were independently associated with poor functional outcome. Second, patients with highest-tertile AA levels showed lower frequencies of cerebral edema with midline shift and poor functional outcome. Finally, no significant differences in functional outcome were seen for levels of other PUFAs (DHLA, EPA and DHA) in ICH patients.  

Lower serum AA levels were independently associated with poor functional outcome in ICH. Some studies have demonstrated associa­ tions between serum PUFA levels and incidence of major coronary events, peripheral artery disease and early neurological deterioration after acute ischemic stroke [11]. However, the relationship between serum PUFA levels and functional outcomes has remained unclear in ICH patients. To the best of our knowledge, this study offers the first demonstration that lower serum AA levels may be independently asso­ ciated with poor functional outcome in ICH. The reason why lower serum AA level related to poor functional outcome in ICH was thought to be as follows. Some experimental studies have suggested that AA plays protective roles for neurons via anti-oxidant effects. In rodent models, 15(S)-hydroxy eicosatetraenoic acid, as one of the principal 12/15-lip­ oxygenase (12/15-LOX) products derived from AA, increased levels of PPARγ protein and PPARγ-regulated genes such as catalase expression, and reduced expression of the gene for $\mathrm{NF}\kappa\mathrm{B}$ , thus contributing to the neuroprotective effects of AA. As a result, high serum AA levels on admission may offer a predictor of poor functional outcome in ICH pa­ tients [8].  

Patients with high-tertile AA levels showed the lowest frequency of poor functional outcome and cerebral edema with midline shift. Higher serum AA levels may relate to favorable functional outcomes via reduction of cerebral edema. In ICH, rupture of cerebral blood vessels and subsequent red blood cell lysis results in release of heme in hem­ orrhagic stroke-affected tissues. This heme is metabolized by heme oxygenase (HO) in the brain into free iron. Free iron leads directly to brain edema following free radical formation and lipid peroxidation [15–17].  

Lipid peroxidation may be a key element in the formation of brain edema. The anti-oxidant effects of AA may reduce lipid peroxidation by free iron, thus improving outcomes. Regarding PUFAs, AA has antioxidative effects and protects neurons against oxidative stress. Other PUFAs have not been shown to offer antioxidative effects [17]. The neuroprotective effects of AA could contribute to differences in functional outcomes among patients with similar lesions and hematoma volumes, and therefore serum AA levels, but not levels of other PUFAs, should be evaluated as a predictor of poor functional outcome in ICH

This study has several limitations. First, the data in the present study were retrospectively derived from a single center, and the number of subjects was quite small. Second, PUFA formulations were not consid­ ered after ICH onset. Because serum PUFA levels were examined within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ of admission, these could have been affected by ICH itself, diet or infusion therapy after admission. Finally, dietary habits, daily compli­ ance with medications, and monitoring of serum PUFA levels were not followed after discharge. Collectively, some biases regarding patient selection and treatment effects might have been present in this study, representing an issue regarding the generalizability of the results to the wider population of patients with ICH. As the associations between PUFA levels and stroke, particularly ICH, remain unclear, further studies are warranted.

# 5. Conclusion  

Lower serum AA levels on admission were independently associated with poor functional outcome in acute hypertensive ICH subjects. AA may be a predictive biomarker of neuroprotection in the clinical course of ICH.
