# Conivaptan for the Reduction of Cerebral Edema in Intracerebral Hemorrhage: A Safety and Tolerability Study  

Jesse J. Corry1,4 · Ganesh Asaithambi1,4 · Arif M. Shaik1,4 · Jeffrey P. Lassig2,4 · Emily H. Marino3,5 · Bridget M. ${\sfHo}^{3,5}$ Amy L. Castle1,4 · Nilanjana Banerji3,5 · Megan E. Tipps3,5  

Published online: 6 April 2020   
$\circledcirc$ Springer Nature Switzerland AG 2020  



# Abstract  

Background  Perihematomal edema (PHE) growth in intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a biomarker for worse outcomes. Although the management of PHE is potentially beneficial for ICH patients, there is currently no proven clinical therapy that both reduces PHE and improves outcomes in this population.  

Objective  To examine the safety and tolerability of conivaptan, a non-peptide vasopressin (AVP) receptor antagonist, for the management of PHE in ICH patients.  

Methods  We performed a single-center, open-label, phase I study in seven patients with ICH at risk for developing PHE. Conivaptan $(20\mathrm{mg)}$ was administered every $^{12\mathrm{h}}$ for 2 days, along with the standard ICH management. Electrolyte levels, renal and cardiac function, and vital signs were monitored throughout treatment. Neurological status, ICH, and PHE volumes were assessed at study baseline, $^{24\textrm{h}}$ , 72 h, and 7 days from the first conivaptan administration, as well as at the 3-month follow-up.  

Results  Conivaptan was well tolerated in our patients. We observed the expected increase in sodium levels following conivaptan administration $(p=0.01)\$ ), with no change in cardiac or renal function. All patients survived to follow-up, and adverse event rates were comparable with those of the neurocritical care unit overall.  

Conclusions  These data indicate that conivaptan can be safely administered to ICH patients and support further clinical investigation into the efficacy of this drug for ICH treatment.

# 1  Introduction  

The use of aggressive blood pressure reduction, hemostatic therapies, and surgery have all failed to demonstrate a consistent, positive effect on patient outcomes for intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) [1–4]. Although preventative measures may reduce the incidence of ICH, the mortality rate once ICH has occurred remains unchanged [5]. To date, only admission to a neuro-intensive critical care unit and the use of computer topography (CT) have been shown to improve outcomes in ICH [6, 7], highlighting the need for novel interventional targets. While much of the current research focuses on clot expansion, another potential focus is the development of perihematomal cerebral edema (PHE). PHE development correlates with age, disease severity, ICH volume, irregular shape, and glucose levels [8]. Faster edema growth results in greater midline shift, with resulting herniation and mortality, and PHE expansion in the first $^{24\mathrm{h}}$ correlates inversely with clinical outcome [9]. In deep hemorrhages, PHE expansion at 3 days correlates with poor outcome [10]. Although the management of PHE might be beneficial among ICH patients, there is currently no proven clinical therapy that both reduces PHE and improves outcomes in this population.  

We present a pilot study using conivaptan, a non-peptide vasopressin (AVP) receptor antagonist, in patients with ICH at risk for developing PHE. Conivaptan has been used within the neurocritical care population [11], and data in traumatic brain injury (TBI) patients demonstrate that conivaptan is a safe and well-tolerated approach for increasing sodium levels and reducing intracranial pressure [12]. These data suggest that conivaptan could be safely applied to ICH patients as a method for reducing PHE.  



# 2  Methods  



# 2.1 Study Design  

This was a single-center, open-label, phase I study to assess feasibility, drug safety, and tolerability among ICH patients. Feasibility was assessed by recruitment rate, completion of data acquisition, drug delivery logistics, and ability to manage cerebral edema concomitantly with the study drug. The primary measures of safety and tolerability were hemodynamic stability, sodium stability, all-cause deaths, ICH and PHE expansion, and occurrence of adverse events (AEs).  

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the national research committee (Quorum, QR#32036/1) and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.  



# 2.2 Study Population  

The study included patients between the ages of 18 and 80 years with a primary diagnosis of ICH that was $\geq20$ cc in volume. Patients were enrolled within $48\mathrm{~h~}$ of initial symptoms. Patients were excluded if they: demonstrated a current need for renal replacement therapy; had a glomerular filtration rate $\mathrm{(GFR)}<30\mathrm{mL/min}$ at admission; were participating in another study for intracranial or intraventricular hemorrhages; had an ICH related to infection, thrombolysis, subarachnoid hemorrhage, or tumor; had HIV or active fungal infection; continued the use of digoxin or amlodipine; demonstrated active hepatic failure; had serum sodium $\geq145\mathrm{mmol/L}$ ; were pregnant or lactating; were unable to receive conivaptan due to contraindications; were not expected to survive beyond $48\mathrm{~h~}$ of admission; or had a diagnosis of brain death. A list of all excluded patients and reasons for exclusion can be found in the Online Supplemental Material (Table S1).  



# 2.3 Conivaptan Administration  

Patients were given $20\mathrm{mg}$ of conivaptan intravenously (IV) every $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ for 2 days, for a total of $80~\mathrm{mg}$ , in addition to the standardized ICH management. The system-wide cerebral edema protocol and the full protocol for conivaptan dosing can be found in the Online Supplementary Material. Conivaptan delivered IV has a peak effect in $^{2\textrm{h}}$ , with increased urine output for up to $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ after administration, and an elimination half-life of $^{5\mathrm{~h~}}$ . Data were divided into two windows—on-drug and off-drug—starting from the first conivaptan administration. Data collected prior to the first conivaptan dose were used as the baseline. On-drug was defined as the time between the administration of the first dose until $20\mathrm{~h~}$ after the last dose. A full 24-h post-drug washout period was identified after the final conivaptan administration, and the remaining data was classified as off-drug.  



# 2.4  Data Collection  

Neurological assessments, including the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), the Glasgow Coma Score (GCS), the APACHEII (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II), and ICH scores were all taken at baseline. Relevant assessments were also performed at 24 h, $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ , and 7 days after the first conivaptan administration, as well as at discharge and the 3-month follow-up (see Supplemental Materials). Clinical measures, including sodium levels, measures of renal function, heart rate and arterial pressure, were taken at baseline, following every conivaptan administration, and throughout the first 7 days of treatment.  



# 2.5 Imaging  

Hemorrhage and edema volumes were assessed via CT scans at study baseline, $24\mathtt{h},72\mathtt{h}$ , and 7 days from the first conivaptan administration. A final scan was obtained at the 3-month follow-up. The volumes for hemorrhage and edema were calculated using the $\mathbf{AxBxC}/2$ assessment [13, 14], and are reported as ICH and $\mathrm{ICH+PHE}$ . CT scan measurements of hemorrhage and edema volumes were performed by a single board-certified neuroradiologist (JPL) on a dedicated PACS workstation.  



# 2.6  Data Analysis  

Data were analyzed using Prism 8 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Repeated measures across the study periods were compared using the Friedman test. For all tests, $p<0.05$ was considered significant.  



# 3  Results  

A total of 83 patients were screened and seven were enrolled in the study. The demographic information for excluded patients and reasons for exclusion can be found in the Online Supplemental Material (Table S1). The demographic information, medical history, and disease severity for the seven patients included in the study are shown in Table 1.  



# 3.1 Safety and Tolerability  

None of the patients enrolled in the study required an external ventricular drain, bolt/intracranial pressure monitor, or craniotomy. Further, none of the patients developed a precipitous drop in blood pressure, an unexpected elevation of sodium, pontine demyelination, or an acute kidney injury. Review of AEs found that two of the study patients developed deep venous thrombosis (DVT), which is comparable to the rate of DVT for unenrolled patients during this same period. One patient developed ventilator-acquired pneumonia (VAP), and another developed hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP). However, both occurred in patients with prolonged intubations and developed many days after completion of the study drug.  

As expected, sodium levels were significantly elevated relative to baseline during the on-drug period (Friedman test: $\chi^{2}(3)=7.14$ , $p{=}0.01\$ ), and remained elevated for the following off-drug period (Fig. 1a and Online Supplemental Material, Fig. 1). None of the patients showed signs of renal dysfunction, pontine myelinolysis, or excessive jumps or drops in sodium. All other electrolytes remained within normal limits or easily supplemented with standard replacement protocols, and none of the patients required mannitol administration. Estimated GFR (eGFR) and daily urine output were stable across test periods (Fig. 1b, c). Conivaptan demonstrated a safe cardiovascular profile, with no significant changes in mean arterial pressure (MAP) or heart rate (Fig. 1d and Online Supplemental Material, Fig. 2) and no arrhythmias.

# 4  Discussion  

PHE occurs as a function of water influx/efflux through the astrocyte compartment. A primary target for modifying water content is aquaporin 4 (AQP4), the main class of water channel in the brain [15]. Animal models have demonstrated AQP4 expression increases within $^{6\mathrm{~h~}}$ from injury, and this increase parallels water content in hemorrhagic models [16]. Previous work suggests that inhibition of water permeability via reductions in AQP4 may have therapeutic potential for cerebral edema in the clinical setting [17].  

Conivaptan, a dual antagonist of AVP V1 and V2 receptors, increases sodium levels by enhancing effective water clearance and is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a treatment for hyponatremia. Interestingly, the antagonism of AVP V1 receptors in models of traumatic brain injury (TBI) and stroke is associated with parallel reductions of AQP4 expression and cerebral edema [17, 18]. This suggests that conivaptan may represent a novel approach for reducing water content in the brain, and potentially PHE, following ICH.  

A number of prospective studies have confirmed the utility of conivaptan as a treatment for patients with hyponatremia across a number of clinical settings [19–23]. Previous work regarding the use of conivaptan specifically in the neurocritical-care population includes two case reports [12, 24], a prospective trial of six patients without ICH [20], and a series of retrospective data analysis studies [11, 25, 26]. These studies, while encouraging for the use of conivaptan in neocritical care patients, reported on a mixed patient population that included ICH as well as ischemic stroke, status epilepticus, traumatic brain injury, brain tumors, and others. Our study represents the first prospective investigation of conivaptan specifically for use in the ICH patient population with a focus on the potential use in PHE management.  

This pilot study has a number of limitations. Although the ABC/2 method of PHE and ICH volume assessment offers a reasonable approximation of volume with high inter-rater agreement [13, 14], it overestimates somewhat, particularly with irregular lesions [27–29]. For the purposes of the current study, which was to exclusively assess safety, a volume approximation was sufficient to track patient progress. Future studies assessing dose effects in ICH and PHE volumes will require a more quantifiable volumetric approach. Our study also focuses on a small number of patients. In order to demonstrate that the use of conivaptan was safe in the ICH population, we modeled our work on a previous pilot study for novel drugs in neurocritical care patients [30], which used a similar sample size. While this sample size is sufficient to demonstrate safety and feasibility in our target population, future work assessing the potential benefits of conivaptan on PHE volumes in ICH patients will require a larger sample size and a placebo arm for comparison.  



# 5  Conclusions  

Our data demonstrate that conivaptan is well tolerated in ICH patients. Given the current lack of a clinical therapy that can both reduce PHE and improve outcomes in these patients, the safety and efficacy profile described here warrant further clinical investigation. Future studies incorporating a placebo comparison group to assess conivaptan effects on PHE volumes, hypertonic saline use, and overall cost of care are required.
