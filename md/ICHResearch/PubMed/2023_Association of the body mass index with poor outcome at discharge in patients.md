# Association of the body mass index with poor outcome at discharge in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage: a registry study

# Abstract  

Although several studies have reported on the impact of the body mass index (BMI) on functional outcome of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH), the relationship remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the risk factors of poor outcome of aSAH, with particular attention to BMI. A total of 860 patients with aSAH were enrolled in our registry at Shimane Prefectural Central Hospital between 2000 and 2017, of whom 393 were included in the analysis. Basic patient characteristics, including BMI, and data related to aSAH were recorded. We conducted a univariable analysis, followed by a multivariable analysis to identify the risk factors of poor outcome, defined as a modified Rankin Scale score ${>}2$ at discharge. We also compared our study with previous studies that reported a relationship between BMI and aSAH. Multivariable analysis revealed that age (odds ratio [OR], 1.09; $95\%$ confidence interval [CI], 1.07–1.12), underweight (OR, 2.36; $95\%$ CI, 1.13–4.90), overweight (OR, 2.22; $95\%$ CI, 1.06–4.64), World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade (III vs I: OR, 3.10; $95\%$ CI, 1.03–9.35; IV vs I: OR, 8.02; $95\%$ CI, 3.54–18.19; V vs I: OR, 13.37; $95\%$ CI, 5.33–33.54), and symptomatic vasospasm (OR, 3.40; $95\%$ CI, 1.73–6.70) were risk factors for poor outcome at discharge. This study showed the association of underweight $(\mathbf{BMI}<18.5\mathrm{kg/m}^{2})$ and overweight $\mathrm{(\geq25kg/m^{2}}\$ with poor outcome; therefore, both high and low BMI are associated with a poor outcome at discharge for patients with aSAH. Clinical trial registry: University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN000035160; date of registration: December 6, 2018).

# Introduction  

The obesity paradox is a finding of some studies that patients with overweight or obesity have favorable outcomes in several acute illnesses, such as ischemic stroke, ischemic heart disease, and heart failure [1–5]. Moreover, being overweight or obese was associated with good outcomes in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage or ischemic stroke [2, 6, 7].  

There are some reports on the relationship of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) with BMI as well, but no conclusion has been obtained to date. Meanwhile, according to previous reports, a higher BMI was significantly associated with cerebral infarction within 3 months after aSAH [8, 9]. Another study reported that although overweight (BMI $25{-}29.9\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{2}.$ ) has an inverse risk of poor outcomes, obesity $\mathrm{(BMI>}30\mathrm{kg/m}^{2}\mathrm{)}$ has a risk of unfavorable outcomes [8]. However, many studies did not show a significant relationship between BMI and poor outcomes in aSAH [10–15]. Additionally, Rinaldo et al. [16] reported that BMI affects the functional outcome after aSAH either positively or negatively depending on the treatment modality. As mentioned earlier, the association of BMI with functional outcome after aSAH remains inconclusive [8]; therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association of BMI with poor outcomes in patients with aSAH and summarize existing studies that reported on the association of BMI with aSAH.  

# Materials and methods  

# Ethics statement  

All study data were anonymized, and the requirement for informed consent was addressed using by the opt-out method. This study was registered in clinical trial registry of the University Hospital Medical Information Network in Japan (ID: UMIN000035160; no. R000040074; RL: https://upload.umin.ac.jp/cgi-open-bin/ctr/ctr_view.cgi? recptno ${\mathfrak{i}}=$ R000040074; December 6, 2018).  

# Patient selection and data collection  

In total, the medical data of 860 patients with a primary diagnosis of aSAH between January 2000 and December 2017 were obtained from our observational database and reviewed retrospectively. aSAH was diagnosed based on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging findings. The inclusion criteria were adult patients (18 years and older) with SAH from a saccular aneurysm and preevent modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score $\leq2$ and who underwent aneurysm treatment (surgical clipping or endovascular coiling) within 4 days after onset. Patients younger than 18 years, those with pre-event mRS score $\geq3$ , SAH due to any other cause or structural abnormality (unknown of origin, dissecting aneurysm, or infectious aneurysm), those who did not receive aneurysm treatment (including cerebrospinal fluid diversion), those who were treated later than 5 days, those who were treated with both surgical clipping and endovascular coiling, and those with any missing data were excluded. The flowchart of the patients with aSAH enrolled in this retrospective analysis is presented in Fig. 1.  

The patients were treated with surgical clipping or endovascular coiling according to the judgment of specialized neurosurgeons and endovascular specialists. The following data were collected from patients’ medical records: age, sex, BMI at admission, medical history (presence of hypertension and stroke), aneurysm characteristics (size and location), World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grade, Fisher CT group, symptomatic vasospasm, treatment (surgical clipping or endovascular coiling), and mRS score at discharge. Patients were divided into three groups according to BMI: underweight $(<18.5~\mathrm{kg/m}^{2})$ , normal ( $18.5\mathrm{-}<25~\mathrm{kg/m^{2}},$ , and overweight $(\geq25\mathrm{~kg}/\mathrm{m}^{2})$ , based on the World Health Organization BMI classification [17]. Additionally, blood samples were obtained to measure serum albumin levels based on existing literature [18]. Symptomatic vasospasm was defined according to the 2009 consensus, which includes the following: presence of neurological worsening, including focal deficit, decline in consciousness level, and motor paresis; no other identifiable cause (such as intracranial disorders or systemic complications) of neurological worsening; and confirmation of vasospasm by medical examination, including evidence of vasospasm [19]. We defined vasospasm $\mathrm{as}>30\%$ constriction of vessels according to digital subtraction or CT angiography and symptoms unlikely to be the result of other causes. Poor outcomes were defined as mRS score $>2$ at discharge after onset of aSAH.  

# Statistical analysis  

Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher exact test or chi-square test. Continuous variables were compared using $t$ -test or Mann–Whitney $U$ test. Multivariable logistic regression analyses of poor outcomes (mRS score ${>}2$ ) at discharge were conducted for all patients. The odds ratio (OR) and $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) were calculated for all BMI groups. For multivariable logistic regression analyses, independent variables were selected according to those present in existing literature [20], after variable selection by univariable analysis. $P$ -values $<0.05$ were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 15 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).  

# Results  

# Patient characteristics  

Table 1 shows the baseline patient characteristics. In total, 393 patients (male, 124; female, 269) were analyzed in this study. The median age was 66 years (interquartile range: 55, 76). The proportions of patients in each BMI category at admission were as follows: underweight, $16.0\%$ ; normal, $63.6\%$ ; and overweight, $20.4\%$ . Surgical clipping was performed in $80.2\%$ of patients. Poor outcomes at discharge were observed in $38.4\%$ of patients.  

# Risk factors of poor outcomes  

Based on the results of the univariable analysis, the following factors were extracted as variables for the multivariable analysis: age, BMI at admission, hypertension, WFNS grade, Fisher CT group, treatment modality, and symptomatic vasospasm. Age (OR, 1.09; $95\%$ CI, 1.07–1.12), underweight (OR, 2.36; $95\%$ CI, 1.13–4.90), overweight (OR, 2.22; $95\%$ CI, 1.06–4.64), WFNS grade III vs I (OR, 3.10; $95\%$ CI, 1.03–9.35), WFNS grade IV vs I (OR, 8.02; $95\%$ CI, 3.54–18.19, WFNS grade V vs I (OR, 13.37; $95\%$ CI, 5.33–33.54), and symptomatic vasospasm (OR, 3.40; $95\%$ CI, 1.73–6.70) were extracted as risk factors of poor outcomes at discharge for all patients (Table 2).

# Discussion  

In this retrospective study, we analyzed the risk factors of poor outcomes at discharge after aSAH and specifically investigated the impact of BMI at admission on poor outcome. Results showed that underweight or overweight based on BMI were associated with poor outcomes at discharge in patients with aSAH.  

We conducted a literature review of references mentioning a relationship between BMI and aSAH in English language published from January 2000 to April 2022 in PubMed. The date of the final search was April 30, 2022. First, we searched the following words in PubMed: [subarachnoid hemorrhage, BMI, poor outcome]. Then, we checked the references cited in the articles. Our literature review showed nine references that mentioned a relationship between BMI and functional outcome after aSAH (Supplementary Table 1) [8–16]. Of these nine studies, three reported that a higher BMI was a risk for poor outcome [8, 9, 16]. Furthermore, two of the three studies reported that the cutoff values of BMI were 32.3 [16] and $30\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ [8]. In our study, we also found that overweight $(\geq25~\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{2})$ is a risk factor for poor outcome. In order to reveal the cutoff value, we performed a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The cutoff was $26.05\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ in the overweight group (area under the curve [AUC], 0.50) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Meanwhile, two studies reported a positive effect of BMI on outcome [8, 16]. The patients with aSAH with $\mathrm{BMI}\geq32.5\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ and who treated by coiling had a lower risk of poor outcome than their counterparts. Another study revealed that BMI of 25 to $\scriptstyle\gamma<30\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ , defined as overweight, was significantly associated with a lower odds of poor outcome after aSAH [8]. Based on the aforementioned findings, obesity can have a harmful effect in patients with aSAH, but mild overweight may have a protective effect on outcome. This may be explained by the protective mechanisms of increased metabolic reserve of fat and muscle mass that can improve tolerance to major catabolic and inflammatory effects [21]. Moreover, the obesity paradox might be applied to the poor functional outcome of patients with aSAH to some extent as well as for those with heart failure and ischemic stroke [1, 2, 5].

We also found that underweight was a risk factor for poor outcome. An association between a lower BMI and worse mRS score was reported by Tawk et al. [15]; the reason for the association was unclear, but frailty could be one of the reasons. Underweight $(\mathbf{BMI}<18.5~\mathrm{kg/m}^{2})$ was reported as one of the factors with significantly higher risk of frailty [22]. Furthermore, another report described the relationship between preoperative frailty and poor outcome after neurosurgery [23]. Age was also reported to be a factor, with older age being linearly proportional with frailty [24], and the present study included a large number of elderly patients (median age, 66 years). For these reasons, underweight reflects some of frailty and it may have contributed to poor outcome. As with the overweight group, we conducted a ROC analysis to obtain the cutoff value. The result was $16.42\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ (AUC, 0.58) (Supplementary Fig. 2).  

While a significant relationship between BMI and poor outcome of aSAH has been mentioned in several studies, more reports have denied any impact of BMI on the outcome of aSAH. Nine studies investigated the association between BMI and poor outcome of SAH, of which six concluded that BMI was not associated with a poor outcome in patients with aSAH [10–15]. Rautalin et al. [21] performed a systematic review of the obesity paradox in SAH, with focus on BMI and mortality; however, the authors did not reach a definitive conclusion. In the present study, we placed importance on the functional outcome, and it was the definitive difference between our study and Rautalin et al.’s study.  

Although the current study showed an association between BMI and poor outcome of aSAH, it has several limitations. First, this report was a retrospective singlecenter registry-based study; therefore, some data are missing because the database was not designed specifically for this study. As the result, only $46\%$ of the patients within this registry were eligible for analysis; however, a complete dataset without missing data was analyzed in this study. Second, we only examined the outcome at discharge; therefore, we did not determine the impact of BMI on medium- to longterm outcomes. Lastly, our data were obtained from one of the regions with the highest population of elderly people in Japan. Future studies are needed to prospectively accumulate BMI data and examine the medium- to long-term impact of BMI on outcomes.  

In conclusion, in elderly patients with aSAH, underweight and overweight are risk factors of poor outcome at discharge. Based on previous reports, the impact of BMI on outcome in patients with aSAH is still controversial. Further study is needed to determine the impact of BMI on medium- to long-term outcomes.
