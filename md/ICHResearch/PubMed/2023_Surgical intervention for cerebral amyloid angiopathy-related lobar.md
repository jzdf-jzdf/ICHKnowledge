# Surgical intervention for cerebral amyloid angiopathy–related lobar intracerebral hemorrhage: a systematic review  

Ole F. de Bruin, BSc,1 Sabine Voigt, BSc,1,2 Jan W. Schoones, MA,3 Wouter A. Moojen, MD, PhD,4–6   
Ellis S. van Etten, MD, PhD,1 and Marieke J. H. Wermer, MD, PhD1,7  

Departments of 1Neurology, 2Radiology, and 4Neurosurgery, Leiden University Medical Center, Leiden; 3Directorate of Research Policy, Leiden University Medical Center, Leiden; 5Department of Neurosurgery, Haaglanden Medical Center, The Hague; 6Department of Neurosurgery, Haga Teaching Hospital, The Hague; and 7Department of Neurology, University Medical Center Groningen, The Netherlands  

OBJECTIVE  The risks and benefits of surgery for cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA)–related lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) are unclear. The aim of this study was to systematically review the literature on this topic.  

METHODS  The authors conducted a systematic review according to the 2020 PRISMA statement. PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Emcare, and Academic Search Premier were searched (on December 27, 2022) for relevant articles. Study inclusion criteria were: 1) randomized controlled trial (RCT), cohort study, crosssectional design, or case series with more than 5 patients; 2) possible, probable, or definite CAA according to the Boston criteria (version 1.0 or 1.5) or autopsy; 3) surgical intervention for acute ICH; and 4) data on peri- and/or postoperative outcomes. Primary outcomes were the presence of intraoperative hemorrhage (IOH), postoperative hemorrhage (POH), and early ICH recurrence. Secondary outcomes were 3-month mortality, late ICH recurrence, functional outcome at discharge, and factors associated with poor outcome. Pooled estimates were calculated, and the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tool was used to assess risk of bias.  

RESULTS  Four cohort studies and 15 case series $(\mathsf{n}=738$ patients, mean age 70 years, $56\%$ women) were included. IOH occurred in 2 $(0.6\%)$ of 352 patients. Pooled estimates for POH were $13.0\%$ (30/225) for less than 48 hours and $6.2\%$ (3/437) for 48 hours to 14 days. Overall recurrent ICH (mean follow-up 19 months, $\mathsf{n}=5$ studies) occurred in $11\%$ of patients. Outcome was predominantly poor with a pooled 3-month mortality rate of $19\%$ and good outcome of $23\%$ . Factors associated with poor outcome were advanced age, poor condition on admission, preexisting dementia, and concomitant intraventricular, subarachnoid, or subdural hemorrhage. All studies contained possible sources of bias and reporting was heterogeneous.  

CONCLUSIONS  Surgery in CAA-related ICH is safe with no substantial IOH, POH, and early recurrent hemorrhage risk. Outcome appears to be poor, however, especially in older patients, although good quality of evidence is lacking. Patients with CAA should not be excluded from ongoing surgery RCTs in ICH to enable future subgroup analysis of this specific patient population.

KEYWORDS  cerebral amyloid angiopathy; craniotomy; intraoperative complications; lobar intracerebral hemorrhage; postoperative outcomes; vascular disorders  

erebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a smallvessel disorder characterized by deposition of the protein amyloid $\upbeta$ in cerebral cortical and lepto  
meningeal arteries, arterioles, and capillaries. CAA is a   
common cause of cognitive decline and (recurrent) lobar  

intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH).1–4 Every year, approximately 2 million people worldwide suffer from ICH.5 It is estimated that CAA accounts for $19\%{-24\%}$ of all ICH and for $50\%-57\%$ of all lobar ICH.6 CAA-related ICH generally has a severe disease course. It also has one of the highest recurrence rates of all stroke subtypes, with an overall recurrence risk upwards of $7.4\%$ per year, versus $1.1\%$ in non–CAA-related ICH.7,8 Therefore, rapid treatment of the ICH and prevention of new ICH appears essential, but until now, evidence on the best management for CAA-related ICH is sparse.8  

The value of surgery for ICH is a subject of debate. Several years ago, the Surgical Trial in Lobar Intracerebral Haemorrhage (STICH)–I, which investigated the safety and efficacy of early surgery versus conservative treatment in spontaneous supratentorial ICH, showed no overall benefit of surgery.9 However, a post hoc analysis showed potential benefit in patients with superficial lobar hemorrhages less than $100~\mathrm{ml}$ without intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) and surgery within 48 hours of symptom onset. Nevertheless, the subsequent STICH-II, investigating this patient subgroup, failed to validate these favorable results, showing only a slight decrease in mortality and no increase in favorable outcome at 6 months follow-up.10 In contrast to these findings, a recent meta-analysis on surgery for ICH in general showed favorable outcomes for all surgical modalities as compared with standard medical treatment, with particularly promising results for minimally invasive surgery.11 Mortality was also lower for all surgical modalities. However, the 4 included high-quality studies did not report significant superiority of a specific surgical intervention. Unfortunately, it is unclear how many patients with CAA were part of the meta-analysis. CAA can only be diagnosed with certainty after autopsy and MRI is necessary to confirm possible/probable CAA. Because MRI is usually performed in the acute phase of ICH, (minimally invasive) surgery in supratentorial spontaneous lobar ICH—especially early after onset of symptoms—may be beneficial, but no trials have been performed in CAA-related ICH.  

Surgical evacuation of CAA-related ICH remains controversial. This is partly because patients are predominantly elderly (mean age 73 years), fragile, and suffer from cognitive decline.6 Moreover, some case studies show concerns regarding intraoperative hemostasis and recurrent postoperative bleeding.12,13 In contrast, there are also other case series that report favorable peri- and postoperative outcomes.14–16 We performed a systematic review to investigate all current available evidence on the effect of surgery in patients with CAA-related ICH on intraoperative (IOH) and postoperative hemorrhage (POH) and early ICH recurrence. In addition, we assessed clinical outcome and long-term (hemorrhagic) complications, as well as risk factors for poor outcome, to enable clinicians to make more evidence-based decisions in patients with CAA-related ICH.  

# Methods  

# Search Strategy  

We used the PRISMA 2020 statement for reference in writing this systematic review.17 The protocol of this review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (systematic review registration no.: CRD42022275348 [PROSPERO]; https:// www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/). We searched PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library,  

Emcare, and Academic Search Premier (on December 27, 2022). No restrictions in publication date were applied. The searches were limited to papers written in English, Dutch, German, or French. The search syntax was built in consultation with an expert medical information specialist with extensive systematic review experience (J.W.S.). The query consisted of the combination of the following three concepts: CAA, ICH, surgical procedures. All relevant keyword variations for these concepts were used, including database vocabularies and free-text word variations. The search strategy was optimized for all consulted databases. The detailed search strategy can be found in the Supplementary Tables and Appendix.  

# Eligibility Criteria  

We included studies investigating surgical treatment in patients with lobar ICH related to CAA (diagnosed either in life or postmortem using recognized diagnostic methods and criteria (i.e., Boston criteria/pathological staining). Inclusion criteria were: 1) randomized controlled trial (RCT), cohort study, cross-sectional design, or case series with more than 5 patients; 2) possible, probable, or definite CAA according to the (modified) Boston criteria (version 1.0 or 1.5) or autopsy; 3) surgical intervention for acute ICH; and 4) data on peri- and/or postoperative outcomes. Traumatic causes of ICH were excluded. We excluded patients with ICH who had an indication for surgical intervention but a suspicion of a non–CAA-related ICH cause (e.g., unidentified mass lesions, suspicion of intracranial tumors, etc.). Studies were included when peri-procedural events, postoperative functional outcomes, and/or events during follow-up were reported. For all studies, the patient population was screened for eligibility of individual patients.  

# Study Selection  

Two independent reviewers (O.F.D.B. and S.V.) first screened the titles and abstracts of publications according to the predetermined eligibility criteria, followed by a second screening of the full text. Screening was based on: 1) demographic characteristics; 2) definite, probable, or possible diagnosis of CAA according to the Boston criteria and/or pathological staining; and 3) surgical evacuation of CAA-related hematoma, regardless of surgical technique.  

# Data Extraction  

The following data properties were extracted from included studies: study characteristics (study center, data source, era of accrual, time of follow-up, setting); patient characteristics (sex, age, presurgical Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] score); clinical symptoms (hemiparesis, headache); history of previous ICH; history of dementia; history of hypertension (HT), dyslipidemia, and/or diabetes mellitus (DM); use of antihypertensive (AHT) medication; use of anticoagulant (AC) or antiplatelet medication at the time of surgery; $APOE$ genotype; surgical modality (craniotomy, stereotactic surgery, etc.); and presurgical neuroimaging data (location, volume, midline shift, presence of IVH, subarachnoid hemorrhage [SAH], or subdural hemorrhage [SDH]).

# Outcome  

The primary outcomes of the review were the presence of IOH, POH, and early ICH recurrence. IOH was defined as bleeding occurring during the surgical procedure. POH was defined as any new additional ICH confirmed by postoperative CT or MRI within 48 hours after neurosurgery.18 Early recurrent ICH was defined as an ICH 48 hours to 14 days following surgery.  

Secondary outcomes were postoperative outcome measures such as 3-month mortality, late ICH recurrence, postoperative functional outcome at discharge (Glasgow Outcome Scale [GOS] or modified Rankin Scale [mRS] scores, or good outcome), and factors possibly influencing poor postoperative outcomes, as described by the included articles. Good outcome at discharge was defined as a GOS score $>3$ and/or mRS score $\leq3$ . Good outcome was also assumed when reported as such, without explicit reporting of GOS or mRS scores. Late recurrent ICH was defined as occurring more than 14 days following surgery. Data were extracted from included studies by one researcher (O.F.D.B.). Accuracy of data was assessed by a second researcher (S.V.).  

# Risk of Bias Assessment  

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two researchers (O.F.D.B. and S.V.) using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for case series and cohort studies.18,19 All included studies were scored as low, moderate, or high risk of bias. Case studies were scored based on the following items: 1) clear inclusion criteria,  

2) reliable and standard measurement of the condition, 3) valid identification of condition in all participants, 4) consecutive inclusion, 5) complete inclusion, 6) clear reporting of baseline demographics, 7) clear reporting of clinical information, 8) clear reporting of outcomes, 9) reporting of presenting clinic’s demographic information, and 10) appropriate statistical analysis. Included cohort studies were scored based on the following items: 1) groups selected from the same population, 2) similar measurement of exposures in both groups, 3) valid and reliable measurement of exposure, 4) identification of confounders, 5) adjustment for confounders, 6) population free of outcome at baseline, 7) valid and reliable outcome measurement, 8) follow-up time, 9) complete follow-up, 10) strategy for incomplete follow-up, and 11) appropriate statistics. Any disagreement was resolved in consultation with a third researcher (M.J.H.W.)  

# Data Synthesis and Analysis  

Outcomes were analyzed using descriptive statistics (IBM SPSS Statistics version 26, IBM Corp.). For POH, early and late recurrence, 3-month mortality, and good outcome, random-effects meta-analysis was performed using the metafore package in R (version 4.2.2; The R Project for Statistical Computing, https://www.r-project. org/). Forest plots and pooled estimates were reported. Heterogeneity between outcomes for each of the pooled estimates was reported in terms of the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ statistic.  

# Results  

# Study Characteristics  

In total, 986 articles were identified. After removal of duplicates, 472 articles were available for eligibility assessment. After title and abstract screening, 433 articles were excluded. The full texts of the 39 remaining articles were screened for fulfillment of the inclusion criteria. Finally, 19 articles with 738 surgically treated patients with CAA-related ICH (mean age 70, $\dot{5}6\%$ women) were included (Fig. 1).13–16,20–34 Of the 19 studies, 15 were case series and 4 were cohort studies (Table 1). The study characteristics, baseline characteristics, and clinical and imaging data, as well outcomes, are shown in Tables 1–4 and Supplementary Table 4. In the risk-of-bias analysis, 9 studies were appraised as high risk, 9 as moderate risk, and 1 as low risk (Fig. 2). Consequently, the overall risk of bias was evaluated as being high (Fig. 2, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).35  

# IOH, POH, and Early ICH Recurrence  

Fourteen ( $74\%$ , $\mathrm{n}=352$ patients) of 19 studies reported the occurrence of IOH (Table 4). In total, 2 patients $(0.6\%)$ suffered from IOH (range $0\%{-5\%})$ , while difficulty with maintaining intraoperative hemostasis during surgery was experienced with 19 studies reporting POH. The pooled estimate mean for POH was $13.\mathrm{\bar{0}\%}$ $95\%$ CI $8.6\%-\mathrm{\hat{l}}9.2\%$ , range $0\%{-}33\%$ ; Fig. 3A). The heterogeneity for this pooled estimate (I2) was $17.6\%$ .  

Recurrence of ICH was reported by 15 $(79\%)$ of the 19 studies $(\mathtt{n}=705\$ patients). The overall recurrence rate among these studies was $11\%$ (77/705). The rate of early recurrent ICH was $0.7\%$ (3/437 patients) with a pooled estimate of $6.2\%$ $95\%$ CI $2.7\%{-}1\dot{3}.8\%$ , $\mathrm{I}^{2}=14.8\%$ ; Fig. 3B).

# Discussion  

Our systemic review suggests that IOH and problems with hemostasis during the procedure are rare in patients with CAA who undergo surgery for lobar ICH. In addition, the rate of early POH less than 48 hours after surgery was low. However, despite few hemorrhagic complications in the acute phase, good outcome was uncommon, with not even one-quarter of the patients reaching functional independency. The most often reported factors associated with poor outcome were advanced age $(\geq75$ years), poor clinical condition on admission, dementia, IVH, SAH, SDH, and CAA severity.  

Early case reports suggested that patients with CAA should be precluded from surgical ICH evacuation because of difficulty maintaining hemostasis and subsequent IOH.37–39 One paper suggested that decreased vessel contractility in CAA might interfere in hemostasis, while 2 others suggested that weakened vessel walls in CAA could accelerate ruptures of adjacent vessels.37–39 Our results do not support these hypotheses. In our review, only 2 of 352 patients were found to have difficulties in hemostasis leading to IOH. In 2 cases, intraoperative bleeding occurred contralaterally from the initial hematoma.32 This patient was pathologically graded as grade 4 advanced CAA.36 An additional case report not included in our review also reported a contralateral hemorrhage during surgery in a patient with CAA.40 These contralateral IOHs suggest that they are due to the inherent high risk of hemorrhage in CAA and not especially precipitated by surgical intervention.  

The overall occurrence of POH in CAA-related ICH in our review was similar to the rate reported for ICH generally. A recent study showed that the overall incidence of postoperative bleeding in patients with ICH is approximately ${\hat{1}}3\%$ , which is similar to the incidence rate in our study.41 POH is often attributed to surgical technique.42 However, postoperative bleeding in patients with CAA, especially at different sites than the initial hemorrhage, could also be related to the pathophysiology of CAA instead of the procedure (similarly to the IOHs).15,16 Our results suggest that surgery does not lead to a particularly high rate of POH in patients with CAA-related ICH.  

The rate of early recurrent ICH in patients with CAA was relatively low. In contrast, a relatively high incidence of late ICH recurrence was found. In our review, preoperative IVH, advanced age, and poor presurgical condition were associated with poor outcome. IVH was the most frequently reported risk factor.49 Risk factors for poor outcome observed in surgically treated patients with CAA-related ICH—such as IVH, advanced age, and poor clinical condition—have previously been used in prediction models assessing 30-day mortality in spontaneous ICH.50 Another clinical factor that might be considered in decision-making in CAA-related ICH is preexisting dementia.30,44 The APOE-ε2 genetic phenotype could also play a role in vascular fragility and might predispose patients to intraoperative bleeding and recurrent hemorrhage, leading to poor outcome, but this factor is often unknown in the acute phase of ICH.27,51 Further stratification of risk of poor outcome is necessary to improve patient selection.  

# Limitations of the Study  

Our study has several limitations. Although our systematic review is one of the first major studies systematically investigating postoperative outcomes in patients surgically treated for CAA-related ICH, the quality of evidence was poor. First, risk of bias was substantial across the studies, which mostly consisted of small, retrospective, single-center case series without control groups. Second, the occurrence of selection bias is likely in the included studies. Fragile patients may be underrepresented across studies because surgery is infrequently performed in those patients. Conversely, patients with rapid decline of consciousness and mass effect on CT may be overrepresented, leading to an overestimation of the severity of complications. Third, the included patients underwent surgery in predominantly Western developed countries, limiting the external generalizability to different clinical circumstances. Lastly, this review is limited by the heterogeneity of the included articles. Various demographic and clinical variables were inconsistently reported. Furthermore, a variety of point estimates for outcome were used and were reported at various follow-up time points. Moreover, diverse surgical modalities were used such as open craniotomy, stereotactic surgery, microscopic surgery, and minimally invasive surgery. These disparities between studies affect patient comparability. The heterogeneity is evident in the calculated pooled estimates.  

Despite these limitations, we believe that, based on the results of our study, patients with CAA should not be automatically precluded from surgery, although the decision should be carefully considered based on age, IVH, and presurgical neurological condition, as poor outcomes are prevalent in this patient group. Patients in a moderate to good presurgical condition could potentially be included in trials to enable pooled subanalyses to study the safety and efficacy of surgery in CAA. Recent developments in CAA diagnosis on CT with the Edinburgh criteria could aid acute clinical decision-making, trial inclusion, and pooled subgroup analyses without the necessity for MRI.52 Although the quality of evidence is limited and there was a high risk of bias in most of our included studies, our results can aid neurosurgeons in making evidence-based decisions when confronted with patients with CAA-related ICH, as this review presents the most current evidence available.  

# Conclusions  

Surgery in CAA-related ICH appears to be relatively safe with no substantial IOH, POH, and early recurrent hemorrhage risk compared with other causes of ICH. The mortality rate after surgery for CAA-related ICH also is relatively low, yet surgery frequently leads to poor neurological functional outcome, especially in elderly patients with a poor neurological condition on admission and IVH. Unfortunately, high-quality studies are lacking. We recommend including patients with CAA-related ICH who are in a moderate to good pre-admission condition in ongoing and future randomized trials on surgery for ICH to elucidate whether surgical intervention is safe and efficacious in patients with CAA.
