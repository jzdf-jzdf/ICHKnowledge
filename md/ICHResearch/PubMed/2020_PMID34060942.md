Background:  Intracranial hemorrhage is a known complication after endovascular treatment in patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion, but the association between radiologic hemorrhage severity and outcome is controversial.  

Purpose:  To investigate the prevalence and impact on outcome of intracranial hemorrhage and hemorrhage severity after endovascular stroke treatment.  

Materials and Methods:  The Efficacy and Safety of Nerinetide for the Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke (ESCAPE-NA1) trial enrolled participants with acute large vessel occlusion stroke who underwent endovascular treatment from March 1, 2017, to August 12, 2019. Evidence of any intracranial hemorrhage, hemorrhage multiplicity, and radiologic severity, according to the Heidelberg classification (hemorrhagic infarction type 1 [HI1], hemorrhagic infarction type 2 [HI2], parenchymal hematoma type 1 [PH1], and parenchymal hematoma type 2 [PH2]) was assessed at CT or MRI 24 hours after endovascular treatment. Good functional outcome, defined as a modified Rankin score of 0–2 at 90 days, was compared between participants with intracranial hemorrhage and those without intracranial hemorrhage at follow-up imaging and between hemorrhage subtypes. Poisson regression was performed to obtain adjusted effect size estimates for the presence of any intracranial hemorrhage and hemorrhage subtypes at good functional outcome.  

Results:  Of 1097 evaluated participants (mean age, 69 years $\pm14$ [standard deviation]; $551~\mathrm{men}$ ), any degree of intracranial hemorrhage was observed in 372 $(34\%)$ . Good outcomes were less often achieved among participants with hemorrhage than among those without hemorrhage at follow-up imaging (164 of 372 participants $[44\%]$ vs $500$ of 720 $[69\%]$ , respectively; $P<.01$ ). After adjusting for baseline variables and infarct volume, intracranial hemorrhage was not associated with decreased chances of good outcome (adjusted risk ratio [RR] $=0.91$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.82, 1.02], $P=.10\AA,$ , but there was a graded relationship of radiologic hemorrhage severity and outcomes, whereby PH1 $'\mathrm{RR}=0.77$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.61, 0.97], $P=.03.$ ) and PH2 ( $\mathrm{RR}=0.41$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.21, 0.81], $P=.01$ ) were associated with decreased chances of good outcome.  

Conclusion:  Any degree of intracranial hemorrhage after endovascular treatment was seen in one-third of participants. A graded association existed between radiologic hemorrhage severity and outcome. Hemorrhagic infarction was not associated with outcome, whereas parenchymal hematoma was strongly associated with poor outcome, independent of infarct volume.

Also, some types of intracranial hemorrhage might affect long-term clinical outcomes even if they do not lead to immediate changes in neurologic status. # Materials and Methods  

The ethics board at each site approved the study. Data are from the ESCAPE-NA1 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01778335), a multicentric, double-blind, randomized controlled trial that investigated the safety and efficacy of intravenous nerinetide in participants with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion who were undergoing endovascular treatment. A list of ESCAPE-NA1 investigators is given in Appendix E1 (online). Signed informed consent was obtained from the participants or a legally authorized representative. Table E1 (online) provides an overview of all studies that have been published from the ESCAPE-NA1 trial. No data on the association between intracranial hemorrhage and outcome in the trial have been previously reported (7). The full study protocol can be accessed at https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/ PIIS0140-6736(20)30258-0/fulltext.  



# Study Sample  

Trial participants were enrolled from March 1, 2017, to August 12, 2019, and were randomized to a single $2.6~\mathrm{mg/kg}$ dose of intravenous nerinetide or placebo in addition to best medical treatment, including intravenous alteplase if indicated. Imaging eligibility criteria for the trial were as follows: (a) large vessel occlusion (eg, intracranial internal carotid artery, occlusion of the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery, or functional occlusion of the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery [occlusion of both M2 branches]) at baseline CT angiography, $(b)$ moderate-to-good collateral vessels (defined as filling $\geq50\%$ of the middle cerebral artery territory at multiphase CT angiography), and $(c)$ an Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score of 5 or higher at baseline unenhanced CT. Clinical eligibility criteria were as follows: (a) age 18 years or older, (b) baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score higher than 5, (c) functional independence before the ischemic stroke (Barthel Index score ${>}90$ ), and (d) presentation within 12 hours from the time last known to be well. Unenhanced CT and multiphase CT angiography were performed at baseline, and follow-up unenhanced CT or MRI of the head, including gradient-echo or susceptibilityweighted sequences, was performed 24 hours after endovascular treatment. Because ESCAPE-NA1 was an international multicentric trial, there was some degree of variation with regard to scanning parameters, as each individual hospital used its own local imaging protocols. The details of an exemplary detailed imaging protocol as it was used in the corresponding author’s institution have been published previously (8). Patients were excluded from this analysis if 24-hour follow-up images were not available or not interpretable or if image postprocessing was not possible because of corrupted source imaging files.  



# Image Analysis  

All imaging data were reviewed in consensus readings by an interventional neuroradiologist (M.G., with 24 years of experience) and a general radiologist (J.M.O., with 4 years of experience). The readers were blinded to treatment allocation, participants’ clinical baseline characteristics, and other imaging studies (ie, during assessment of baseline imaging, readers were blinded to follow-up imaging and vice versa). Intervals between the reading sessions were at least 4 weeks. Presence of any intracranial hemorrhage (eg, intraparenchymal or extra-axial) at 24-hour follow-up imaging was evaluated qualitatively through visual inspection. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was defined, according to the Heidelberg classification (2), as any intracranial hemorrhage associated with clinical evidence of neurologic worsening, with the hemorrhage judged to be the principal cause of neurologic decline. Whether a hemorrhage was symptomatic and causally related to clinical neurologic deterioration was assessed by the neurology and neuroradiology attending physicians treating the patient (ie, the diagnosis of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was based on clinical judgement). Parenchymal hemorrhage was classified according to the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification (2). HI1 was defined as small petechiae along the margins of the infarct, HI2 was defined as confluent petechiae within the infarcted area but no space-occupying effect, PH1 was defined as hemorrhage in $30\%$ or less of the infarcted area with some slight space-occupying effect, and PH2 was defined as hemorrhage in more than $30\%$ of the infarcted area with a substantial space-occupying effect. Substantial space-occupying effect was defined as evidence of at least one of the following findings: (a) midline shift, (b) distortion of adjacent anatomy with obliteration or distortion of adjacent cerebrospinal fluid spaces (ie, subarachnoid spaces or ventricles), or $(c)$ in extreme cases, any type of brain parenchymal herniation. Figure E1 (online) shows examples for each hemorrhage subtype. Remote hemorrhages (ie, hemorrhage not within or adjacent to the infarct territory) were uncommon and grouped according to the four subtypes. Parenchymal hemorrhage was classified as a single hemorrhage (ie, one hemorrhagic area) versus multiple hemorrhages (ie, two or more hemorrhagic areas with interposed nonhemorrhagic tissue) and any extra-axial hemorrhage (ie, subarachnoid, subdural, or epidural). Hemorrhage volume was determined at axial unenhanced CT or gradient-echo or susceptibility-weighted MRI, and total infarct volume in milliliters was determined at 24-hour axial unenhanced CT or MRI follow-up imaging through manual segmentation using the open-source ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0, http://www.itksnap.org). This required conversion of the axial source images into a Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative format. Hemorrhage within or adjacent to the infarct but not remote hemorrhages were included in the infarct segmentation volume. Subarachnoid and intraparenchymal hemorrhages were segmented as a single volume because the total volume of subarachnoid blood is small in most cases and delineation of sulcal subarachnoid from cortical parenchymal hemorrhage is often not possible. Subdural and epidural hemorrhages were uncommon and excluded from hemorrhage volume segmentation.  



# Outcomes of Interest  

The primary outcome in this study was good functional outcome, defined as a modified Rankin score of 0–2 at 90 days. Secondary outcomes were excellent outcome, defined as a modified Rankin score of 0–1 at 90 days, and mortality at 90 days.  



# Statistical Analysis  

Participant baseline characteristics, clinical outcomes, prevalence of any intracranial hemorrhage and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, hemorrhage subtypes, and volumes were described as counts and percentages for categoric variables and means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Baseline characteristics and proportions of primary and secondary outcomes were compared between participants with and participants without any visible intracranial hemorrhage at follow-up imaging.  

The relationship between hemorrhage volume and outcome was modeled using logistic regression for clinical outcomes, adjusted for age, and graphed as predicted probabilities of outcome versus infarct volume. The association between the hemorrhage grouping or subtype and outcome was modeled using Poisson regression, with robust variance estimation using the Huber-White sandwich estimator method and adjustment for variables used in the randomized minimization algorithm (ie, participant age, sex, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, occlusion location, intravenous alteplase, nerinetide treatment, and 24-hour infarct volume). All risk ratios (RRs) reported in the results are adjusted for these variables unless stated otherwise. We also tested for study drug treatment according to radiologic hemorrhage subtype interaction by including a multiplicative interaction term in the above model.  

Sensitivity analysis was performed by reporting baseline characteristics, clinical outcomes, and final infarct volume in participants with versus those without any intracranial hemorrhage at follow-up imaging after stratifying participants according to follow-up imaging modality (unenhanced CT vs MRI). No imputation was performed for missing data because missing data were minimal.  

All statistical tests were two sided, and $P<.05$ was considered a statistically significant difference. All analyses were performed with Stata software (version 16, StataCorp).  



# Results  



# Participant Baseline Characteristics  

Of 1105 randomized participants, 1097 had available and interpretable follow-up scans on which visual hemorrhage assessment could be performed (mean age, 69 years $\pm14$ [standard deviation]; $551\mathrm{men}$ ). Of the 1097 participants, any intracranial hemorrhage was visible in 372 $(34\%)$ , 351 of whom $(94\%)$ had parenchymal hemorrhages. Hemorrhage segmentation could not be performed in 43 of 372 participants because image postprocessing was technically not feasible, leaving 329 participants for hemorrhage volume segmentation (Fig 1). Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics and treatment variables. Participants with evidence of any intracranial hemorrhage at follow-up imaging had higher baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores (median, 17 [interquartile range {IQR}, 14–21] vs 17 [IQR, 12–21], respectively; $P=.02\$ ) and lower baseline Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Scores (median, 8 [IQR, 6–8] vs 8 [IQR, 7–9]; $P<.01$ ), worse collateral vessels (23 of 370 participants $[6\%]$ vs 23 of 715 participants $[3\%]$ with poor collateral vessels, $P=.04,$ ), and longer onset to randomization times (median, 246 minutes [IQR, 146–378 ­minutes] vs 163 minutes [IQR, 113–263 minutes]; $P<.01$ ). In participants with evidence of any intracranial hemorrhage, stroke onset was less often witnessed (189 of 371 participants $[51\%]$ vs 437 of 723 participants $[60\%]$ , $P<.01\$ ), their blood glucose level was higher (median, $7.0\mathrm{mg/dL}$ [IQR, $6.1{-}8.9\mathrm{mmol/L}]$ vs 6.6 $\mathrm{mmol/L}$ [IQR, $5.8{-}7.6\mathrm{mmol/L}]$ , $P<.01\$ ), and they were more likely to have diabetes (93 of 372 participants $[25\%]$ vs 123 of 725 participants $[17\%]$ , $P=.01$ ). Participants with evidence of any intracranial hemorrhage were also less often treated with intravenous alteplase (202 of 372 participants $[54\%]$ vs $452$ of 725 participants $[62\%]$ , $P=.01\$ ) and more often underwent endovascular treatment with general anesthesia (81 of 371 participants $[22\%]$ vs 109 of 720 participants $[15\%]$ , $P=.02,$ ). The proportion of participants with any intracranial hemorrhage ( $P$ $=.80)$ and hemorrhage subtypes $\left(P=.13\right)$ did not differ between participants treated with nerinetide and those treated without nerinetide. We also found no evidence of study drug treatment according to hemorrhage subtype interaction.

# Discussion  

Intracranial hemorrhage is a potential complication after endovascular treatment in acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion. We observed intracranial hemorrhage at 24-hour follow-up CT or MRI in 372 of 1097 participants $(34\%)$ . The less severe hemorrhage types, hemorrhagic infarction type 1 (risk ratio [RR] $=1.06$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.92, 1.23], $P=.39.$ ) and hemorrhagic infarction type 2 $\mathrm{{RR}}=0.94$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.79, 1.12], $P=.49_{\cdot}$ ), were not significantly associated with decreased chances of a favorable outcome, but the more severe types, parenchymal hematoma type 1 $\mathrm{{'RR=0.77}}$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.61, 0.97], $P=.03)$ and parenchymal hematoma type 2 $\mathrm{'RR}=0.41$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.21, 0.81], $P=.01\$ ), were associated with markedly reduced chances of good outcome, independent of infarct volume. Symptomatic hemorrhage, which is an important safety outcome in randomized endovascular treatment and intravenous thrombolysis trials, was observed in $3.7\%$ of participants, which is slightly lower compared with previously published data from the Highly Effective Reperfusion Evaluated in Multiple Endovascular Stroke Trials meta-analysis (9).  

Importantly, there appears to be a difference between hemorrhage that is immediately “symptomatic” and hemorrhage that is “prognostically relevant.” Proportions of good outcome were lower in participants with evidence of any intracranial hemorrhage ( $44\%$ vs $69\%$ , $P<.01\$ ), and mortality was almost twice as high ( $19\%$ vs $10\%$ , $P<.01\$ ). Confirming previous studies (4,5,10), median baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores were higher, Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Scores were lower, collateral vessels were worse, and onset to randomization times were longer in participants with intracranial hemorrhage, suggesting that hemorrhage tends to occur in participants with poor prognostic baseline variables.

Previous work has also reported that not only parenchymal hemorrhage but also hemorrhagic infarction might lead to worse short-term neurologic outcomes (5,11). In our study, we observed a graded association with outcome according to radiologic hemorrhage subtypes. Proportions of good outcome were nominally higher in participants with no evidence of hemorrhage compared with those with HI1, and they decreased further with increasing European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study score (HI1 $>\mathrm{HI}2>\mathrm{PH}1>\mathrm{PH}2,$ . However, contrary to our expectations, only PH1 $(\mathrm{RR}=0.77\$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.61, 0.97], $P=.03,$ ) and PH2 $\mathrm{'RR}=0.41$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.21, 0.81], $P=.01$ ), but not HI1 $\mathrm{{(RR=1.06}}$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.92, 1.23], $P=0.39^{'}$ ) and HI2 (RR $=0.94$ $[95\%$ CI: 0.79, 1.12], $P=.49\$ ), were independently associated with outcome after adjusting for participant baseline characteristics and infarct volume, suggesting that the nominally worse outcomes in patients with hemorrhagic infarction may be related to infarct volume or reperfusion status or both (12).  

One would intuitively expect proportions of good outcome to decrease with extraparenchymal hemorrhage. However, in our study, small subarachnoid or intraventricular hemorrhage had a similar magnitude of association with outcomes as HI2 hemorrhage, suggesting that these types of hemorrhage are not as severe as commonly assumed. After stratifying participants according to follow-up imaging modality (MRI vs unenhanced CT), clinical outcomes were generally better in participants with follow-up MRI, possibly because of a selection effect—MRI is hard to perform in participants who are agitated and intubated. Finally, the volume of hemorrhage was strongly associated with outcome. Large-volume hemorrhages are invariably fatal, and there appears to be a threshold effect such that hemorrhages larger than approximately $75~\mathrm{mL}$ have a $50\%$ mortality at 90 days and are associated with a near zero probability of a favorable outcome.  

Our study had limitations. First, different follow-up imaging modalities, namely unenhanced CT and MRI, were used to assess hemorrhagic changes. Sensitivity and interreader agreement of MRI are much higher than that with unenhanced CT (13), which could have resulted in imprecision of our effect size estimates. Blooming at gradient-echo or susceptibility-weighted MRI might have led to overestimation of hemorrhage volumes (14). We performed hemorrhage measurements for these sequences nevertheless because T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences were not available in most participants. Second, the Heidelberg classification used was originally developed for unenhanced CT but successfully applied to MRI (15). Third, unenhanced CT imaging is not able to accurately help distinguish parenchymal contrast material staining from intracerebral hemorrhage (16). However, the only way for contrast material staining to occur is when the blood-brain barrier breaks down, which will always result in simultaneous hemorrhage. Thus, contrast material extravasation is strongly associated with hemorrhage, and it is commonly assumed that any area of contrast staining contains some degree of parenchymal hemorrhage (17–19). Differentiating between contrast material extravasation and hemorrhage is possible with dual-energy CT (20), but for reasons of practicability, dual-energy CT was not part of the ESCAPE-NA1 trial protocol. Fourth, it is possible that low-degree hemorrhagic changes (eg, HI1) might have led to subtle deterioration in outcomes, and the modified Rankin score, used to define primary and secondary outcomes, might not be granular enough to capture such changes.

In conclusion, any degree of intracranial hemorrhage at follow-up imaging after endovascular treatment of acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion was seen in one-third of participants. There was a graded relationship of radiologic hemorrhage severity and outcome. After adjusting for infarct volume, only the more severe subtypes, parenchymal hematoma types 1 and 2, were associated with worse outcomes, suggesting that the relatively poor outcomes in participants with the less severe radiologic subtypes, hemorrhagic infarction subtypes 1 and 2, were related to large infarct volume.
