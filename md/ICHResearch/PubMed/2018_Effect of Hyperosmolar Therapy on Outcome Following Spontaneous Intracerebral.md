# Effect of Hyperosmolar Therapy on Outcome Following Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage: Ethnic/Racial Variations of Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ERICH) Study  

Manan Shah, MD,\* Lee Birnbaum, MD, MS,† Jennifer Rasmussen, MD,‡ Padmini Sekar, MS, $\S$ Charles J. Moomaw, $\mathrm{PhD},\S$ Jennifer Osborne, RN, BSN,§ Anastasia Vashkevich, BA, $\parallel$ and Daniel Woo, MD, MS§  

Purpose: We aimed to identify the effect of hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol and hypertonic saline) on outcomes after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in the Ethnic/Racial Variations of Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ERICH) study. Methods: Comparison of ICH cases treated with hyperosmolar therapy versus untreated cases was performed using a propensity score based on age, initial Glasgow Coma Scale, location of ICH (lobar, deep, brainstem, and cerebellar), logtransformed initial ICH volume, presence of intraventricular hemorrhage, and surgical interventions. ERICH subjects with a pre-ICH modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 3 or lower were included. Treated cases were matched 1:1 to untreated cases by the closest propensity score (difference ≤.15), gender, and race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, or Hispanic). The McNemar and the Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare 3-month mRS outcomes between the 2 groups. Good outcome was defined as a 3-month mRS score of 3 or lower. Results: As of December 31, 2013, the ERICH study enrolled 2279 cases, of which 304 hyperosmolar-treated cases were matched to 304 untreated cases. Treated cases had worse outcome at 3 months compared with untreated cases (McNemar, $P=.0326;$ ), and the mean 3-month mRS score was lower in the untreated group (Wilcoxon, $P=.0174^{\circ}{}_{,}$ ). Post hoc analysis revealed more brain edema, herniation, and death at discharge for treated cases. Conclusions: Hyperosmolar therapy was not associated with better 3-month mRS outcomes for ICH cases in the ERICH study. This finding likely resulted from greater hyperosmolar therapy use in patients with edema and herniation rather than those agents leading to worse outcomes. Further studies should be performed to determine if hyperosmolar agents are effective in preventing poor outcomes. # Introduction  

Spontaneous, nontraumatic intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality throughout the world. Best clinical management of ICH remains unclear due to unproven therapies, such as craniotomy, craniectomy, and hyperosmolar agents. Prior ICH trials of clinical outcomes have shown mixed results.1-3 Therefore, current treatment strategies are not standardized, and they focus on variables associated with poor outcome, such as intracranial hypertension and cerebral edema.4  

The use of hyperosmolar therapy in ICH is based on its action of intracranial pressure (ICP) reduction. Physiological actions of hyperosmolar agents have been known since the early 1900s.5,6 Mannitol and hypertonic saline (HTS) have shown the most promise in clinical application and potential efficacy. Both mannitol $(5\%$ - $15\%$ ) and HTS $(2\%-23.4\%)$ ) have been used in varying concentrations and methods. Hyperosmolar therapy is thought to reduce tissue volume by inducing fluid transfer down osmotic gradients of capillaries and cell membranes.7 A few studies have demonstrated the efficacy of hyperosmolar therapy in lowering ICP in traumatic brain injury8-13 but have failed to show improved clinical outcomes.14 Furthermore, data are sparse regarding its efficacy and ICP-lowering action in spontaneous ICH and postischemic edema. It may be possible that the use of such agents, although temporarily relieving increased ICP, does not ultimately affect outcome. Therefore, we sought to demonstrate if there was benefit compared with no treatment after matching factors associated with the use of hyperosmolar therapy, such as the severity of ICH.  

To test this hypothesis, we examined the effect of hyperosmolar therapy on patient outcomes enrolled in the Ethnic/Racial Variations of Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ERICH) study. Given that specific features likely predict which patients would be placed on hyperosmolar agents (such as large volume and mass effect or herniation), we identified these risk factors for hyperosmolar agents and then matched these factors for the propensity of use to cases that did not receive hyperosmolar agents. We utilized a propensity score model to ensure that the baseline characteristics of the treated and the untreated groups were not significantly different.  

# Methods  

# Study Protocol  

A detailed methodology of the ERICH study has been published previously.15 In brief, the ERICH study is a large, multicenter ICH study that aims to identify genetic variation, differences in the distribution of risk factors and imaging characteristics, and outcomes in non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, and Hispanic patients. Cases were identified using a prospective hot-pursuit method of subject enrollment to limit survival bias. The institutional review boards of each enrolling site approved the study. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects or their legal representatives. For the purpose of the present investigation, we chose a case-case comparison analysis with propensity score matching.  

# Case Definition  

All spontaneous ICH phenotypes in the ERICH database met the following eligibility criteria: age 18 years or older; residency for at least 6 months within a 75- or 100-mi radius of the recruiting center, depending on the population of the region; white, black, or Hispanic race and ethnicity by self-report; and ability of the patient or the legal representative to provide informed consent.15 Research personnel reported the use of hyperosmolar agents on chart abstraction forms by reviewing hospital records. For the present analysis, “treated” cases received hyperosmolar agents including mannitol and HTS during hospitalization irrespective of concentration, frequency, or time to initiation.  

# Screened Variables  

Demographics, including age, gender, and ethnicity, were captured in a baseline interview with the patient or suitable proxy. Details of hospitalization for each ICH subject, including severity variables that could independently affect outcome, were obtained through chart abstraction and recorded on case report forms by research personnel. Among the data included were ICH score variables, serial computed tomography (CT) results, surgical interventions, and complications such as brain edema or herniation. Preadmission functional status was assessed and recorded on the chart abstraction form as a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score. We excluded subjects with a premorbid mRS score of 4 or higher, multiple ICH locations, missing data regarding initial CT results, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), surgical procedures, or 3-month mRS score. Because GCS data were collected only for cases that presented to an emergency department (ED), in-hospital cases, direct admits, and cases with unavailable ED data could not be included in the analysis.  

# ICH Score Variables  

The ICH score incorporates age, GCS on ED presentation, ICH location (supratentorial versus infratentorial), ICH volume, and presence of intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH). Although the original purpose of the ICH score was to predict 30-day mortality, the individual variables were validated as independent predictors of longterm outcome in ICH populations.16,17 CT scans for all study subjects were reviewed by a central imaging center in a standardized fashion. ICH location was categorized as deep, lobar, brainstem, or cerebellar. Deep ICH was defined as originating from the thalamus, basal ganglia, internal capsule, or deep periventricular white matter. Lobar ICH was defined as originating at the cortex or at the cortical-subcortical junction. ICH volume was measured with planimetric analysis using Alice software (Parexel Corporation, Waltham, MA), which has an excellent interrater reliability. The presence of IVH was reported separately by the central imaging center and was not included in ICH volume calculations.  

# Surgical Interventions  

Major neurosurgical procedures included craniotomy or craniectomy for clot evacuation, stereotactic clot aspiration, thrombolytic injection into ventricles, and other neurosurgeries deemed major by the clinical team. Other interventions, although not major neurosurgical procedures, included external ventricular drain (EVD) without thrombolytic injection and ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement.  

# Outcomes  

There were 3 categories of discharge disposition: (1) discharge to the home of the patient, relative, or friend, or to assisted living; (2) discharge to a medical facility, such as a rehab or a skilled nursing facility, or to another acute-care hospital; and (3) inpatient death or discharge to hospice. Subjects who availed of comfort care were included in the analysis. Trained research personnel interviewed subjects or proxies in person or via telephone at 3 months after ICH and assessed the subject’s functional status with mRS scores. We defined bad outcome as an mRS score of 4 or higher, which has been shown to be effective in shift analysis in large acute-stroke trials.19  

# Statistical Analysis  

Initial comparison of baseline characteristics between treated and untreated cases revealed significant differences in age, ICH location, log-transformed ICH volume, presence of IVH, presenting GCS, craniotomy for clot evacuation, stereotactic clot evacuation, EVD placement, and thrombolytic injection into ventricles. These variables were included in an initial stepwise analysis to create a propensity score model20 to best emulate randomization in this observational study. After stepwise reduction, the effects of ICH location and the presence of IVH were not significant but were forced into the final model because they were elements of the ICH score. The effects of stereotactic clot evacuation and thrombolytic injection into ventricles were not significant and were excluded from the final model.  

Based on the final model, treated cases were matched to untreated cases by closest propensity score (difference in scores $\leq.15\$ ), gender, and racial or ethnic group for comparisons in outcome analysis. By design, both groups were identical in terms of gender and race or ethnicity distribution. The significant variables in the initial baseline comparison were retested between the matched groups and verified to be nonsignificant after propensity matching.  

Mean values for age, ICH volume, and IVH volume were compared between the 2 groups using the Student t test. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for median GCS values. Pearson chi-square test was used for craniotomy, EVD placement, presence of IVH, and ICH location. The McNemar test for matched pairs was used to compare the outcomes between treated and untreated cases. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to test for differences in the 3-month mRS scores between the 2 groups. Pearson chi-square test was used in the post hoc analysis to compare discharge dispositions, brain edema, and herniation between the 2 groups in unmatched analysis.  

# Results  

# Description of Cohort  

Between September 22, 2010, and December 31, 2013, the ERICH study enrolled 2279 cases, of which 410 were treated with hyperosmolar therapy. Following exclusion criteria, 330 treated cases remained, and 1446 untreated cases were available as potential untreated cases for propensity score modeling (Fig 1). The untreated cases were more likely to be excluded for loss to follow-up at 3 months $(P<.01)$ . The treated and untreated cases had significantly different baseline severity variables (Table 1).

# Outcome  

Among the 304 matched pairs, 106 had different outcomes: in 64 pairs, the treated case had a bad outcome and the untreated case had a good outcome, whereas in 42 pairs, the opposite was noted (Table 2). A higher proportion of treated cases had poor 3-month mRS scores than the untreated cases $73.0\%$ versus $65.8\%$ ). Additionally, the mRS difference for each matched pair was calculated (mRS difference $=$ untreated mRS − treated mRS). The mean value for mRS differences was $-.3322$ $(P=.0174)$ , indicating a worse outcome in the treated cases.  

# Post Hoc Analysis  

All available data for discharge dispositions, brain edema, and herniation were compared in the post hoc analysis between treated and untreated cases (Table 3). For discharge disposition, treated cases were less likely to be discharged home or to a rehab or nursing facility and were more likely to have died. Additionally, treated cases had significantly more brain edema and herniation.

# Discussion  

ICH has a relatively high incidence (15-35 per 100,000 persons)21-23 and is associated with poor outcomes $(30\%-40\%$ 30-day mortality rate).21,23-26 Management of ICH poses a significant challenge due to the lack of proven therapies. With the exception of the recently published CLEAR III study,27 neither medical nor surgical interventions have been shown to significantly improve outcomes, including survival and quality of life.23 Smaller studies of ICH have looked at hyperosmolar therapy and demonstrated no outcome improvement.28-30  

A large-scale systematic effort to validate the use of hyperosmolar therapy in primary ICH has not been previously performed.  

The evaluation of hyperosmolar therapy in clinical practice is complicated by its frequent use in more severely ill patients. To minimize this bias, we used propensity score modeling to best match markers of illness severity, including the ICH score and surgical interventions. Our results suggest that the use of hyperosmolar therapy does not improve clinical outcomes compared with expected results based on severity characteristics. Our neutral results, however, are likely a result of limitations in our propensity score model, specifically the lack of ICP values and the degree of cerebral edema. Furthermore, treated patients in our study design may trend toward worse outcome due to reverse causality. In other words, patients who are imminently herniating or demonstrating substantial edema or mass effect are more likely to be treated with hyperosmolar therapy. Indeed, treated patients in our study had significantly more herniation and cerebral edema compared with untreated patients.

An additional limitation of the results is the paucity of details related to the hyperosmolar therapy regimens. We did not have specifics for strength of solution, duration of therapy, time to treatment initiation, or treatment setting (intensive care unit [ICU] versus nonICU). These factors are heterogeneous and likely influence clinical outcomes. For example, timing of therapy may influence its efficacy, as the predominant type of edema varies during different phases of ICH evolution. Significant regional and institutional differences exist in the clinical administration of hyperosmolar therapy and are influenced by physician experience with agent and dosing. Finally, although the data collected for hyperosmolar therapy included the use of mannitol, HTS or both, we did not have sufficient power to evaluate these agents separately.  

In addition to the previously mentioned study limitations, the pathophysiology of ICH may be less tolerant of hyperosmolar side effects and contribute to our negative results. Cerebral edema in ICH is predominantly vasogenic,29 which is associated with disruption of the blood-brain barrier. Hyperosmolar substances may be less effective in this type of edema because blood-brain barrier disruption allows the passage of hyperosmolar agents into brain tissue, which neutralizes the osmotic gradient and has been associated with rebound intracranial hypertension. Additionally, there are counteracting, poorly understood brain adaptive mechanisms in place. After the initial effect of hyperosmolar therapy, brain cells tend to normalize by processes that increase the cellular content of osmotic molecules. This process counteracts the desirable hypertonic osmotic effect, leads to normalization of the cell volume, and may result in subsequent recurrence or worsening of tissue swelling.31,32  

Besides the molecular explanations, patients receiving hyperosmolar therapy may have worse outcomes due to unintended clinical consequences and side effects. Utilization of hyperosmolar therapy requires more intensive monitoring, which may prolong patients’ ICU stay, increase susceptibility to other medical complications, and delay rehabilitation evaluation and treatment. Potential side effects of hyperosmolar therapy include hemolysis, hypotension, pulmonary edema, and renal insufficiency with electrolyte disturbances. Mannitol has been shown to have a strong osmotic diuretic effect, which may result in hyponatremia followed by hypernatremia.33 Repeated infusions of HTS may increase sodium and chloride concentrations to critical values that result in hyperchloremic acidosis. Close monitoring of sodium is also important in these patients as severe hypernatremia, with a serum sodium level higher than 160, is found to be an independent factor of mortality in the literature.34  

The strengths of the present study lie in its national, multicenter database with a large, triethnic population. The size and breadth of this database provide sufficient power to use propensity score modeling with 1:1 matching to mimic the characteristics of a randomized controlled trial. Our study is unique in its design to adjust for numerous variables associated with severity and outcomes of ICH. Although some predictive variables were not available for inclusion in the propensity score model, our conclusion is important for advancement to a clinical trial. We demonstrate that despite our best matching for the propensity of hyperosmolar agent utilization, this treatment did not render better outcomes than expected by available severity criteria. Nevertheless, some matched pairs did have better outcomes and many matched pairs had no benefit, but no worsening of outcomes. Thus, this report may help guide criteria or expectations for the use of hyperosmolar therapy toward cases where benefit may exist or away from those cases where preliminary evidence suggests potential futility.  

# Conclusions  

Hyperosmolar therapy was not associated with improved clinical outcome in our ERICH case-case comparison study. Although propensity score modeling was used for a quasi-randomized analysis, post hoc analysis revealed an imbalance with treated cases having more cerebral edema. Further study of hyperosmolar therapy for ICH is needed and should consider matching on quantitative measurements of cerebral edema and ICP.
