# ABSTRACT  

Background and Purpose: Post thrombolytic intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is associated with higher rate of death or disability in acute ischemic stroke patients. We investigated the relationship between post thrombolytic ICH volume and change in volume and death or disability at 90 days in acute ischemic stroke patients.  

Methods: We analyzed 110 patents recruited in the Safety Evaluation of 3K3A-APC in Ischemic Stroke (RHAP­ SODY) trial who received intravenous tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) followed by mechanical thrombectomy (if indicated) and 3K3A-APC or placebo. ICH volume was measured at Day 2 and Day 7 using susceptibility weighted sequence (SWI) on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). We also calculated the post thrombolytic ICH volume change between Day 2 and Day 7. Outcomes were determined by using utility weighted modified Rankin scale (UW-mRs) at 90-days, Outcomes were determined by using utility weighted modified Rankin scale (UWmRS) at 90 days. To minimize interpretation bias, outcome assessors were blinded to the treatment allocation and clinical data.We adjusted for age, gender, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score $\left(<10,10-\right.$ 19 and ${\geq}20.$ , location of hemorrhage (single basal ganglia hemorrhage, single lobar, single cerebellum, and multiple sites) in multivariate regression analysis.   
Results: A total of 88 $(80\%)$ of 110 patients had post thrombolytic ICH (mean volume $28.3\mathrm{ml}\pm\mathrm{SD}62\mathrm{ml})$ . The strata of ICH volume were not associated with UW-mRs at 90 days: ${<}20\mathrm{cc}$ (regression coefficient (RC)-0.05, $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p}}=$ 0.58), 20-39 cc (RC-0.22, $\scriptstyle{\mathtt{p}=0.17})$ , or $\geq40$ cc (RC-0.34, $\mathbf{p}=0.083$ ) compared with no ICH after adjusting for potential confounders. Change in ICH mean volume $26.78\mathrm{ml}\pm59.68$ , 52 had increase in volume) between Day 2 and day 7 was not associated with UW-mRS at 90 days (RC -67.71, $\mathtt{p}{=}0.06\$ ).   
Conclusions: We did not observe any independent effect of post thrombolytic ICH volume on death or disability in acute ischemic stroke patients. Although further studies must be done, our data suggest that strategies to prevent ICH expansion such as antifibrinolytic medications and reduction in ICH volume such as surgical evacuation may not reduce death or disability in acute ischemic stroke patients with post thrombolytic ICH.  

# Introduction  

Intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) occurs in some patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) following intravenous thrombolysis and/or me­ chanical thrombectomy and may be associated with increase in rates of death or disability. However, ICH can vary in severity from petechial hemorrhage to large space occupying hematomas with variable effect on death or disability.1,2 Previous studies have used qualitative definition to characterize the radiology severity of ICH. According to the definition employed (based on the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS), European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study (ECASS-II, III), Alteplase Thrombolysis for Acute Noninterventional Therapy in  

Ischemic Stroke (ATLANTIS), and Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke-Monitoring Study (SITS-MOST),3,4 the risk of sICH ranges from 2 to $8\%$ , whereas the prevalence of asymptomatic hemorrhagic trans­ formation (HT) of ischemic stroke ranges between 18 and $40\%$ .4 Quantitation of ICH volume in patients with acute ischemic stroke following intravenous thrombolysis and/or mechanical thrombectomy has been proposed but relevance to rates of death or disability are un­ clear.4 A recent study conducted in South Korea, post thrombolytic ICH volume of ${>}25~\mathrm{mL}$ was associated with worse 90-day morbidity (mRS ${\geq}3)$ and mortality.5 Previous studies on spontaneous ICH have shown that volume of ICH is a strong predictor of 90-day morbidity $(\mathrm{mRS}\leq2)$ and mortality.6 A clear understanding of the effect of ICH on outcome remains a knowledge gap.  

Here we explored the effect of post thrombolytic intracerebral hemorrhage volume on 90-day outcomes in acute ischemic stroke pa­ tients using data from Safety Evaluation of 3K3A-APC in Ischemic Stroke (RHAPSODY) trial.  

# Methods  

Briefly, the RHAPSODY trial was a randomized, controlled, pro­ spective multicenter clinical trial conducted to assess the safety, phar­ macokinetics, and preliminary efficacy of 3K3A-activated protein C (APC) in combination with tissue Plasminogen Activator (t-PA), me­ chanical thrombectomy, or both, in patients with moderate-to-severe acute ischemic stroke. The primary objective was to evaluate the safety of ascending intravenous (IV) doses of 3K3A-APC and secondary objective was to determine whether 3K3A-APC treatment reduced the frequency or severity of ICH related to recanalization therapy.7 Eligible participants (18-90 years) were required to have a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score ${\ge}5$ with symptom onset within 90 min (later extended to $120~\mathrm{min})$ ). Patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy who did not meet specific criteria (e.g., onset time to arterial puncture time $<6$ h and baseline computed tomography scan excluding large core infarctions) were not included.  

# Measurement of ICH volume  

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) was performed at day 2, 7, 30 and 90 post initial stroke. The susceptibility weighted sequence (SWI) on MRI were used to estimate ICH volume. The T2 FLAIR sequence was used to recognize infarct region, then the SWI sequence was viewed. Any abnormal findings were encircled, and a threshold was applied within that object to mark any ICH. The number of pixels less than the threshold was produced by the SPIN software and changed into areas (Spintech Inc., Bingham Farms MI, USA). At the ${30}^{\mathrm{th}}$ day MRI, if the total hema­ toma volume was $>0.06\mathrm{ml}$ $\mathrm{5~mm}$ diameter, accepted definition of microbleeds [CMB]), then the scan was considered as “positive for hemorrhage” (including hematoma, petechiae and CMB). This value was chosen as it was equivalent to one microbleed and it was assumed that the detection threshold should not miss more than one CMB. To avoid any artificiality due to semi-automated image analysis (like cal­ cium, vein), a sensitivity analysis was conducted for the presence of bleeding using different volume thresholds for the positive identification of bleeding, equivalent to 0, 0.06, 0.5, and $1.8\mathrm{ml}$ , respectively.  

# Outcome assessed  

Utility weighted modified Rankin scale (UW-mRS) was used to assess the 90-day death or disability of post thrombolytic ICH and was the outcome of interest. UW-mRS is a 7-grade scale that measures disability or dependence in everyday life. UW-mRS was calculated by multiplying number of patients with $\mathrm{mRS0}$ to 1.0, number of patients with $\mathrm{mRS1}$ to 0.91, number of patients with mRS 2 to 0.76, number of patients with mRS 3 to 0.65, number of patients with mRS 4 to 0.33, and number of patients with mRS 5 and 6 to 0 respectively. The UW-mRS has been suggested as the main patient-oriented outcome measure for acute stroke interventions trials. A study demonstrated that the UW-mRS was just as effective as ordinal analysis of the mRS in determining treatment effects.9 This has led to the UW-mRS being utilized as the main endpoint in the Diffusion-Weighted Imaging or Computerized Tomography Perfusion Assessment With Clinical Mismatch in the Triage of Wake Up and Late Presenting Strokes Undergoing Neurointervention With Trevo (DAWN) trial.  

# Statistical analysis  

We determined the effect of volume of ICH $(>0\mathrm{cc}-<20\mathrm{cc}$ , $\geq20\mathrm{cc}-$ $_{<40\mathrm{cc}}$ , and $\geq40\mathrm{cc}$ on 90-day UW-mRS after adjusting for age, sex, NIHSS divided as a score of $^{<10,10-19}$ and ${\geq}20$ , location of hemorrhage (single basal ganglia hemorrhage, single lobar, single cerebellum, and multiple sites). We also calculated the post thrombolytic ICH volume change between Day 2 and Day 7 and determined the effect of change in volume of ICH $\scriptstyle0\csc-20\csc$ , $\geq21\mathrm{cc}-40\mathrm{cc}$ , and $\geq41\mathrm{cc}$ ) on 90-day UWmRS after adjusting for the above-mentioned confounders. In our anal­ ysis, we included all the patient from RHAPSODY data set who were found to have any volume of ICH as compared to RHAPSODY trial in which minimum volume of $0.06\mathrm{ml}$ was used to be considered as positive for ICH. For the multivariate linear regression model, t-test was used for the hypothesis testing. All analysis was performed using R studio with p value of 0.05 deemed to be of statistical significance.  

# Results  

We analyzed data from a total of 110 patients divided into 2 groups, the placebo group $(\mathtt{n}=44)$ and the "3K3A-APC" group $(\mathtt{n}=66)$ . The overall mean age was 64 years in both groups, with standard deviation (SD) of 12 and 15.2 respectively. A total of 17 of 110 had ICH on Day 2 with the mean volume of $20.48\mathrm{ml}$ (SD 31.28). When considered for any amount of ICH detected on MRI, a total of 61 out of 98 patients with ICH had increasing in ICH volume between Day 2 and Day 7 with the mean volume of $46.46\mathrm{ml}$ (SD 67.28). The mean UW-mRS score at 90 days was 9.91 (SD 0.27). The relationship between ICH volume and UW-mRS at 90 days is presented in Fig. 1. The relationship between change in ICH volume and UW-mRS at 90 days is presented in Fig. 2.  

In the multivariate analysis, the volume of ICH, did not show sig­ nificant associations with UW-mRS scores: ICH volumes of $>0\mathrm{cc}$ and $<$ 20cc (regression coefficient (RC)0.05, $95\%$ CI: -0.21 to 0.12, $\mathbf{p}=0.573.$ ) and $\geq20\mathrm{cc}$ and $<40\mathrm{cc}$ (RC -0.22, $95\%$ CI: -0.53 to 0.09, $\mathbf{p}=0.170)$ . For larger ICH volumes $\geq40\mathrm{cc}$ , there was a trend towards lower UW-mRS (RC -0.34, $95\%$ CI: -0.73 to 0.05, $\mathbf{p}=0.083.$ ). When the volume of ICH was added as a continuous variable (RC -.002, CI: -0.006 to 0.002,0.298) the volume of ICH, did not show significant associations with UW-mRS scores (RC -0.05, $95\%$ CI: -0.21 to 0.12, $\mathbf{p}=0.58\$ ) as shown in Table 1. It’s important to note that these analyses assume a linear relationship between ICH volume and outcomes, which may not fully capture the complexity of this association. Nonlinear models might reveal some relationships that are not apparent with linear regression.  

In the second multivariate analysis, the change in volume of ICH, did not show significant associations with UW-mRS scores: ICH volumes of $>0\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}$ and $<20\mathrm{cc}$ (RC 16.22, $95\%$ CI: -18.85 to 51.31, $\mathbf{p}=0.360)$ and $\geq$ 20cc and $<40\mathrm{cc}$ (RC 3.49, $95\%$ CI: -57.42 to 64.40, $\mathbf{p}=0.909.$ ). For larger change in ICH volumes $\geq40\mathrm{cc}$ , there was a trend towards lower UW-mRS (RC 135.91, $95\%$ CI: 61.35 to 210.46, $\mathbf{p}=<0.001.$ ). When volume of ICH was added as a continuous variable the change in volume of ICH, did not show significant associations with UW-mRS scores as shown in Table 2.  

With respect to the location of ICH, the results showed the co­ efficients for specific locations effect on 90-day outcomes, included: single hemorrhages in the basal ganglia (RC 0.14, $95\%$ CI: -0.33 to 0.25, $\mathtt{p}=0.146)$ , the cerebellum (RC 0.10, $95\%$ CI: -0.68 to 0.46, $\mathbf{p}=0.697)$ , and lobar regions (RC-0.10, $95\%$ CI: -0.55 to 0.03, $\mathbf{p}=0.325$ ). None reached statistical significance. Moreover, the presence of multiple hemorrhages $\mathrm{(RC0.03}$ , $95\%$ CI: $-0.48$ to 0.16, $\ensuremath{\mathbf{p}}=0.722)$ also did not significantly impact patient outcomes.

# Discussion  

Similar to previous studies,5,6 ICH volume alone may not be a pre­ dictor of 90-day death or disability as measured by UW-mRS, while extremely large volumes may have some modest impact. It is also possible that the relationship between ICH volume and outcomes is nonlinear, and our linear regression models may not fully capture this complexity. Further analyses using nonlinear models could provide additional insights into these relationships. Additionally, since p-values was close to the threshold of statistical significance (e.g., $\mathbf{p}=0.083$ for ICH volumes $\ge40\mathrm{cc}$ , a lack of any association between ICH volume and UW-mRS cannot be assumed until further confirmation is obtained from studies with larger sample sizes. Previous studies have reported that PH2  

ICH has been associated with poor functional outcomes. In our anal­ ysis, we included all the patients who were found to have ICH on MRI scans between day 2 and day 7. We further categorized into strata of ICH volumes (0-20 cc, 21-40 cc, and $\geq40$ cc did not have a significant impact on UW-mRs at 90 days: compared with no ICH after adjusting for po­ tential confounders (age, sex, single vs multiple hemorrhage location). Furthermore, change in ICH volume (mean volume $27.43\mathrm{ml}\pm59.01$ , 61 out of 98 had increase in volume) between Day 2 and day 7 was not associated with UW-mRS at 90 days. One of the reasons for lack of relationship between ICH volume and UW-mRS at 90 days may be that outcomes is predominantly determined by infarct volume11,12 unlike in spontaneous ICH. Size of infarct core has a nonlinear impact on the link between infarct volume and clinical outcome. Patient with moderate-to-large infarcts had a linear correlation between infarct vol­ ume and lower odds of a favorable outcome.  

In RHAPSODY trial, SWI sequence on MRI brain was used for ICH volume calculation as compared to previous studies that looked at ICH but used non-contrast computed tomography (CT) head scan.13,14 A recent retrospective study18 have showed CT head after contrast have been found to have high interrater and intrarater consistency when measuring hematoma size and volume after an acute ICH. Although MRI has good sensitivity for detecting ICH, its ability to quantify the size of the hematoma may not be accurate enough to compare with the results of the initial non-contrast CT scan, and sometime SWI sequence can overestimate the ICH volume18. In RHAPSODY trial,7 to overcome interrater variability a single rater analyzed all the scans at a central facility who was blinded to outcomes and treatment status.

# Limitations  

Our study has several limitations. It relies on secondary analysis of the RHAPSODY trial, which focused on a highly selective group of AIS patients, potentially limiting generalizability. We did not characterize ICH in relation to the ischemic core, whether the ICH was contained within the ischemic core, expanded beyond the ischemic core or was located in a remote territory and thus the analysis assumes that there is no difference in outcomes based on further characterization. Addition­ ally, the RHAPSODY trial did not account for factors like pre-stroke functional status, time to treatment, and specifics of thrombolytic therapy, which might introduce unmeasured bias. The trial’s specific inclusion criteria (age, NIHSS score, and timing of intervention) may not fully represent the broader AIS population, impacting the generalized applicability of our results.  

The small sample size $\scriptstyle(\mathrm{N}=110.$ ) limits statistical power and may affect the detection of subtle differences. Patients with large initial ICH who died before day 7 MRI were excluded, potentially underestimating ICH progression. The exclusion of these patients potentially biases the volume change analysis, as it may underestimate the severity and impact of ICH progression in those with more severe deficits. Additionally, SWI on MRI and CT scans detect different components of ICH. SWI is sensi­ tive to deoxygenated blood and microbleeds, whereas CT scans detect hyperdense areas indicative of acute hemorrhage. SWI may also detect edema to some extent, but it is less specific compared to CT scans. This differentiation is crucial as it impacts the interpretation of ICH volume and its clinical implications. This limitation should be considered when interpreting our findings, and future studies should aim to include such cases to provide a more comprehensive assessment of ICH volume changes. The patients presenting with large core ischemic strokes were excluded from the RHAPSODY trial, which limits the applicability of our study’s findings to all post-thrombolytic ICH patients. This exclusion criterion means that our results may not be generalizable to those with more severe ischemic strokes and larger infarct cores, who might exhibit different outcomes and responses to treatment. Therefore, further studies that include patients with large core ischemic strokes are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of postthrombolytic ICH volume on outcomes. ICH volume was measured from susceptibility-sensitive sequences on MRI. However, MRI, specif­ ically SWI, may differ from ICH volumes obtained from other methods such as non-contrast CT scans. While MRI has good sensitivity for detecting ICH, its ability to quantify the size of the hematoma may not be as precise as CT scans. Additionally, no data was available regarding the infarct core volume, which prevented us from analyzing the rela­ tionship between ICH and infarct volumes. Therefore, we are unable to differentiate the effect of infarct volume and ICH volume on patient outcomes. Future studies should include both ICH and infarct volumes data to allow identification of effect of ICH volume on outcome inde­ pendent of infarct volume. Additionally, the outcome assessment using the UW-mRS could be subject to interpretation bias. Although outcome assessors were blinded to the treatment allocation and clinical data, the potential for interpretation bias remains. This limitation should be considered when interpreting our findings, and future studies should ensure robust blinding procedures to minimize such biases.  

# Conclusion  

We did not observe any independent effect of post thrombolytic ICH volume on death or disability in acute ischemic stroke patients. Our analysis did not specifically investigate the efficacy of strategies to prevent ICH expansion or reduce ICH volume, such as antifibrinolytic medications or surgical evacuation. While our data do not suggest a significant impact of ICH volume on outcomes, we cannot definitively conclude the effectiveness or lack thereof of these interventions. Future studies should explore these strategies in detail to provide more comprehensive evidence on their potential benefits in reducing death or disability in acute ischemic stroke patients with post thrombolytic ICH.  

Our analysis study has some limitations as it is a secondary analysis of a single study, the RHAPSODY trial, which is representative of a highly selective group of AIS patients and may limit the generalizability of our findings. We did not characterize ICH in relation to the ischemic core, whether the ICH was contained within the ischemic core, expanded beyond the ischemic core or was located in a remote territory and thus the analysis assumes that there is no difference in outcomes based on further characterization. Since the study restricts recruitment based on potentially important factors, such as pre-stroke functional status, time to treatment, and specifics of thrombolytic therapy. The restricted recruitment might introduce an unmeasured selection bias in the analysis. While the study adjusts for several potential confounders, other important factors, such as pre-stroke functional status, time to treatment, and specifics of thrombolytic therapy, were not considered. These unadjusted factors might influence the outcomes and should be taken into account in future studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between ICH volume and patient outcomes. The participants in the RHAPSODY trial may not fully represent the broader population of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients. This trial included patients based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, such as age range, baseline NIHSS score, and timing of inter­ vention, which may not adequately reflect the diversity of the general AIS patient population. Consequently, our findings may have limited applicability to general clinical practice. Further studies involving a more heterogeneous patient population are necessary to confirm the generalizability of our results.
