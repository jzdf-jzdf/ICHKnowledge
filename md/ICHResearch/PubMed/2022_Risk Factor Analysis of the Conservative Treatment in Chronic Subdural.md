# INTRODUCTION  

Chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) has had an increasing incidence among elderly individuals [1]. There is no definite cause of the occurrence, development, and deterioration of CSDH. Current pathological theories, such as bridging vein injury, abnormal vascular repair, abnormal inflammatory response, and hyperfibrinolysis, cannot completely explain the whole pathophysiological process of this disease [2, 3].  

Surgical evacuation of the hematoma through a twist drill or a burr hole opening remains the mainstay of treatment for symptomatic patients with CSDH [4]. For asymptomatic patients, who are not surgical candidates, several drug therapies have been considered, including the use of glucocorticoids, mannitol, statins, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and tranexamic acid [5]. However, the aforementioned methods lack well-designed and well-conducted evidencebased medical evidence. On the basis of statin’s pertinent mechanism of action as an anti-inflammatory medication, as well as the mobilization of endothelial progenitor cells for vascular repair in preclinical experiments, we hypothesized that the modulation of local inflammatory and neovascular reactions at selected points could promote rapid hematoma absorption and improve the clinical outcomes of patients with CSDH [6–9]. Through the ATOCH (ATOrvastatin on Chronic subdural Hematoma) study, which was a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial, we found that atorvastatin at $20\mathrm{mg}$ daily was safe and effective as a conservative treatment of CSDH [10]. However, the risk factors for patients with worsening progression to surgical treatment during a wait-and-observation treatment regimen and with conservative treatment with atorvastatin have not yet been tested in previous randomized trials. In addition, in clinical practice, we are often puzzled as to what is the threshold at which the initial hematoma volume is more likely to fail with conservative treatment.  

A previous ATOCH study showed that atorvastatin may be a safe and efficacious nonsurgical alternative for CSDH. However, a subgroup analysis on patients taking atorvastatin treatment versus a placebo group can still provide important information for clinical practice. The current retrospective analysis of the ATOCH trial aimed to evaluate whether the baseline parameters can affect the outcome of therapeutic efficacy, which can be helpful for optimal clinical decision-making regarding CSDH treatment.  

# METHODS  

# Study Design and Participants  

The study design and the primary results of the ATOCH have been reported previously [10, 11]. Briefly, ATOCH was a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial conducted in 25 centers in China from February 2014 to November 2015. For this trial, we approached 254 patients with CSDH, and 200 $(78.7\%)$ were enrolled and registered at http:// www.ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02024373. The study protocol adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial protocol and supporting documentation were approved by the ethics committee of the Tianjin Medical University General Hospital institutional review board and the ethics committees of all participating hospitals. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient or from a legally acceptable surrogate.  

Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for the ATOCH study are described. The current study enrolled 196 eligible ATOCH participants, excluding those missing data at baseline. Eligible participants were men and women aged 18–90 years, with a diagnosis of unilateral or bilateral supratentorial CSDH by computed tomography (CT), with no previous CSDH surgery, with no statin treatment in the previous 6 months, without the risk of a cerebral hernia, without the need for immediate surgery, and with a Markwalder’s Grading Scale–Glasgow Coma Scale (MGS-GCS) grade of less than 3. Patients who had a high risk of cerebral hernia, required emergency surgery, were allergic to statins, received a diagnosis of cancer, had historical and current bleeding or thrombosis with or without treatment (or were taking prophylactic antiplatelet medications), or had developed multiple organ failure were excluded.  

# Intervention and Follow-Up  

Eligible participants were randomly assigned, in a 1:1 ratio, to one of two treatment groups: a daily oral dose of one tablet containing $20\mathrm{mg}$ atorvastatin (Pfizer) for 8 weeks or a daily oral dose of one tablet of placebo. All study investigators and participants were blinded to the randomization procedure and the treatment assignments. Participants were scheduled for follow-ups during the next 16 weeks. During the trial, if the patient’s neurological dysfunction deteriorated (worsened headache, progressive limb paralysis, or changes in levels of consciousness) or if imaging showed an enlargement of the hematoma and/or a midline shift of more than $1\mathrm{cm}$ , the patient would have surgical treatment. Refer to the ATOCH study for a detailed trial process.  

# Outcome Assessment  

The all-cause switching to surgery, the one prespecified endpoint of the ATOCH trial, was an important outcome in this analysis. The allcause switching to surgery included all baseline parameters: age, sex, BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, MGS-GCS score, ADLBI score, platelet count, total cholesterol, triglycerides, coagulation function, the hematoma volume, maximum layer thickness of the hematoma, midline shift, compression of the brain basal cistern, and the hematoma morphology.  

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to identify the optimal cutoff of the hematoma volume by testing the relationship between the surgical rate and nonsurgical rate. Other measures include a stratified analysis of the two groups and a description of the characteristics of the natural outcome of the hematoma in the placebo group.  

# Statistical Analysis  

The strata-specific treatment effects of surgical and nonsurgical patients at baseline were also estimated and compared under the same regression models. Baseline characteristics are presented as the means (continuous variables) or percentages (categorical variables) as appropriate and were stratified by the hematoma volume. The logistic regression model was used to determine whether the baseline parameters had prognostic significance with respect to the outcome. The mean volume of the hematomas in each patient in the surgical and nonsurgical groups was plotted on a ROC curve. The predictive accuracy of the tests was assessed by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). According to these studies, we determined the cutoff values based on clinical information. Therefore, a cutoff was considered the optimal volume for which the patient was most likely to succeed with conservative treatment. Statistical analysis was performed by means of the SPSS 20.0 software package (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism 9. A $p$ value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.  

# RESULTS  

A total of 254 eligible participants with CSDH were screened, and 196 patients with complete baseline data were enrolled and analyzed in this study (98 in the atorvastatin group and 98 in the placebo control group). Two patients from the atorvastatin group were excluded after randomization but before treatment began because they were switched to surgery for worsened neurological conditions, and one patient died of myocardial infarction 1 month after enrollment. Two patients from the placebo group were also excluded: one because of a misdiagnosis and the other because of refusal of treatment. One patient died of a pulmonary embolism 5 months after enrollment. Ninety percent (177/196) of the patients had a history of a recent traumatic event that could be remembered. In the atorvastatin group, 21 patients had bilateral hematoma; in the control group, 37 patients had bilateral hematoma. Eleven patients on atorvastatin $(11.2\%)$ and 23 on the placebo $(23.5\%)$ underwent surgery during the trial for an enlarging hematoma and/ or deteriorating clinical condition (HR 0.47, $95\%$ CI 0.24–0.92, $p=0.03$ ).  

In the atorvastatin monotherapy group, the mean hematoma volume, hematoma thickness, and midline shift were $63.24\pm30.52\mathrm{mL},$ $15.16\pm5.09\mathrm{mm}$ , and $2.93\pm3.55\mathrm{{mm}}$ in the successful conservative treatment subgroup vs.  

$93.69\pm29.88\mathrm{ml}_{}$ , $19.09\pm4.70\mathrm{mm},$ , and $7.45\pm4.41\mathrm{mm}$ in the switched surgery treatment subgroup. Meanwhile, in the placebo group, the hematoma volume, hematoma thickness, and midline shift were $63.83\pm33.92\mathrm{mL}$ , $15.31\pm5.43\mathrm{mm}$ , and $2.68\pm3.45\mathrm{mm}$ in the successful conservative treatment subgroup vs. $93.84\pm41.34\mathrm{ml}$ , $18.61\pm6.99\mathrm{mm}$ , and $4.26\pm4.21\mathrm{mm}$ in the surgery subgroup (Table 1). After 8 weeks of atorvastatin treatment or observation, the residual mean volume of the hematoma in the atorvastatin group was $36.20\mathrm{ml}$ and it was $52.70\mathrm{ml}$ in the control group. Patients with smaller hematomas and better MGS-GCS scores than previously had a complete absorption of the hematoma during the final 16-week followup period, even if the hematoma did not completely disappear after 8 weeks. The mean time interval between switching to surgical treatment in the atorvastatin group was 13 days. Among them, three patients were transferred to surgery within 3 days after enrollment, and eight patients were transferred to surgery within 2 weeks.  

The univariate analysis showed that the factors significantly associated with the surgical treatment were lower total cholesterol $(p=0.03)$ , higher hematoma volume $(p=0.006)$ , more hematoma thickness $(p=0.023)$ , more midline shift $(p=0.002)$ , and more basal cistern compression $(p=0.001)$ in the atorvastatin monotherapy group; however, only higher hematoma volume $(p=0.002)$ and hematoma thickness $(p=0.024)$ were significantly related in the placebo group (Table 2). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified lower total cholesterol (OR 0.247; $95\%$ CI 0.076–0.798; $p=0.020)$ ), higher hematoma volume (OR 1.028; $95\%\mathrm{CI}1.006{-}1.050$ ; $p=0.012,$ , and more midline shift (OR 1.352; $95\%$ CI 1.001–1.827; $p=0.049_{,}$ ) as independent risk factors for the outcome of atorvastatin treatment for CSDH, and only larger hematoma volume (OR 1.019; $95\%$ CI 1.002–1.037; $p=0.029)$ was an independent risk factor for whether the hematoma could be spontaneously absorbed in the placebo group (Table 3). When 196 patients were categorized into two groups based on whether they were switched to surgical treatment, multivariate logistic regression analysis identified that only ‘‘watch and wait’’ conservative observation treatment (OR 0.251; $95\%$ CI 0.073–0.862; $p=0.028)$ , lower total cholesterol (OR 0.457; $95\%$ CI 0.247–0.843; $p=0.012)$ , and higher hematoma volume (OR 1.028; $95\%$ CI 1.006–1.050; $p=0.012,$ ) as independent risk factors for the outcome of conservative treatment for CSDH (Tables 1–3 in the Supplementary Material). When the initial hematoma volume was less than $30\mathrm{ml}$ , the hematoma could be absorbed regardless of the drugs used, and the patients would be able to avoid surgical treatment (Table 4).  

AUCs were calculated for the factor’s efficacy on the conservative treatment outcome (surgical or nonsurgical treatment). The cutoff value of the baseline hematoma volume was $68.5\mathrm{ml}$ (sensitivity $73.5\%$ , specificity $74\%$ ), and the corresponding AUC value was 0.728 $({\ttse}=0.047\$ ; $95\%$ CI 0.637–0.819; $p=0.000)$ , which predicted that patients with CSDH who switched to surgical treatment were more likely to have a baseline hematoma volume greater than $68.5\mathrm{ml}$ (Fig. 1).  

# DISCUSSION  

According to current data, twist-drill craniotomy or bur-hole craniotomy has long been the first choice for treating symptomatic CSDH. Even though the outcome of surgical treatment is considered good, the incidence of intractable CSDH is rising as a result of the combination of an aging population and the increasing use of anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications [12, 13]. Therefore, conservative treatment may be highly valuable in some situations. The ATOCH trial (well-conducted evidence-based medical evidence in randomized clinical trials) can now provide patients with a potential nonsurgical option to reduce the risk of deterioration, instead of the traditional method of observation alone [10]. However, the criteria for conservative treatment and the factors influencing the outcome of conservative treatment have been controversial until now.  

According to previous research, the formation of a CSDH has been hypothesized to be triggered by minor trauma to bridging veins, which facilitate the chronic accumulation of blood. Then, impaired angiogenesis in the neomembrane and excessive localized inflammation play key roles in the pathophysiology of CSDH, leading to the development of immature ‘‘leaky’’ vessels and a subsequent expanding hematoma [2, 3, 14]. Recent studies have shown that dysplastic dural lymphatics may also be involved in the physiological mechanism of the disease [15]. By reducing inflammation-induced vascular leakage and promoting angiogenesis, atorvastatin prevents the formation and accelerates the absorption of hematoma to improve the neurological function of patients with CSDH [16–22]. The efficacy of atorvastatin monotherapy is not ideal in stopping the vicious cycle of repeated leakage at an early stage but improved a difficult situation, especially in patients with a large hematoma. The effect of atorvastatin on CSDH was slow, and many patients had no choice but to have surgery because the clinical symptoms were obviously aggravated before the hematoma began to decrease. We are trying to improve the clinical outcome of CSDH by using statins in combination with dexamethasone to strengthen the modulation of local inflammation and the lymphatic/angiogenesis state, which would improve the absorption of the hematoma [23].

A recent ATOCH trial, as a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled phase 2 clinical trial involving 25 neurosurgical centers in China, showed that atorvastatin could reduce the hematoma volume and can reduce the need for surgery in patients with CSDH and appeared to be especially effective for older patients (age 65 years or older) with relatively larger hematomas (volume greater than $30\mathrm{ml}$ ) [10]. In our multivariate analysis, a larger hematoma volume was the most important independent risk factor for conservative treatment failure in conservative patients (Table 3 and Supplementary Material Table 3). With the descriptive stratified analysis, when the initial hematoma volume was less than $30\mathrm{ml}$ , the hematoma could be absorbed regardless of the drug used, and patients could eventually avoid surgical treatment (Table 4). With regards to an increasing hematoma volume, there was a difference as to whether to convert to surgical treatment when the hematoma volume was $40\mathrm{ml}$ or above in the control group, while there was a significant difference only when the hematoma volume was $60\mathrm{ml}$ in the atorvastatin group, which indicates that atorvastatin treatment is more likely to benefit patients who are having conservative treatment. Therefore, for clinicians, what is the threshold of the hematoma volume where conservative therapy is more likely to fail? The ROC analysis showed that when the patient’s hematoma was greater than $68.5\mathrm{ml}$ , the risk of surgical treatment increased significantly. In addition, lower total cholesterol, larger hematoma volume, and more midline shift can provide a reference for clinicians to comprehensively determine the best choice of CSDH treatment.

After 2 months of treatment with atorvastatin, lower total cholesterol was another independent risk factor for surgical treatment. Statins, as the drug of choice for hypercholesterolemia, may reduce the overall incidence of stroke [24], but conflicting results were also found regarding the impact of the prior use of statins on the prognosis of intracranial hemorrhage [25, 26]. The relationship between cholesterol level and hemorrhagic stroke is still inconclusive. Previous epidemiologic studies have shown a similar phenomenon: hypercholesterolemia was associated with a lower risk of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) [27]. A systematic review reported an inverse relationship between total cholesterol levels and the risk of hemorrhagic stroke [28]. Recent research has found that a baseline total cholesterol (TC) level lower than $160\mathrm{mg/dl}$ was common in patients with acute ICH and was associated with greater neurological severity on presentation and poor 3-month outcomes compared to patients with a TC greater than $200\mathrm{mg/dl}$ [29]. Research on aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage found that a lower baseline total cholesterol was associated with increased mortality and a lower high-density lipoprotein was associated with increased delayed cerebral ischemia [30]. Lower cholesterol levels must be accounted for in the measurements of poor health status and may have a direct or indirect pathophysiological effect on the vascular structure and function. Further research must be done to better elucidate the effect of lipids on CSDH.  

# Limitations  

Several potential concerns or limitations are worth mentioning. Our study was limited by a particular sample range and was retrospective in nature. This study focused on a selected group of patients with CSDH who were asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic according to the criteria for admission. As a result, the potential target group of patients with CSDH fitting the criteria of this study who might benefit from statins was small. This trial used a fixed dosage of atorvastatin $(20\mathrm{mg/day)}$ ; the optimal dosage for patients remains to be established.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

The current post hoc analysis of the ATOCH trial indicates that atorvastatin monotherapy is effective in the treatment of CSDH (mild to moderate). Low total cholesterol, high hematoma volume, and midline displacement are independent risk factors for the progression of atorvastatin in the treatment of CSDH. Only a large hematoma volume was an independent risk factor for the spontaneous absorption of hematoma in the placebo group. The ‘‘wait-andwatch’’ regimen is sufficient for most small hematomas. A hematoma volume of $68.5\mathrm{ml}$ was established as a cutoff to determine whether patients could successfully be treated through conservative treatment.
