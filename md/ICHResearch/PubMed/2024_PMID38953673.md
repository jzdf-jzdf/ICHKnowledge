# Association of Mismatch Profiles and Clinical Outcome from Endovascular Therapy in Large Infarct: A Post-Hoc Analysis of the ANGEL-ASPECT Trial  

Xiaochuan Huo, MD,1 Thanh N Nguyen, MD,2 Dapeng Sun, MD, PhD $\circledcirc,3,4$ Raynald, MD,3 Yuesong Pan, PhD ,3,4 Gaoting Ma, MD,5 Xu Tong, MD,3 Mengxing Wang, PhD ,4   
Ning Ma, MD $\circledcirc,3,4$ Feng Gao, MD,3,4 Dapeng Mo, MD $\circledcirc,3,4$ Mohamad Abdalkader, MD,2 Hesham E. Masoud, MD,6 Raul G. Nogueira, MD,7 and Zhongrong Miao, MD,3,4 for the ANGEL-ASPECT study group  

Objectives: We investigated whether patients with large infarct and the presence or absence of perfusion mismatch are associated with endovascular treatment benefit.  

Methods: This is a post-hoc analysis of the Endovascular Therapy in Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusion with a Large Infarct (ANGEL-ASPECT) randomized trial, which enrolled patients within 24 hours of onset with ASPECTS 3 to 5 or ASPECTS 0 to 2 with an infarct core 70 to $100~\mathrm{{m}}$ . Mismatch ratio was defined as time-to-maximum $\left(\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{max}}\right)>6\mathsf{s}$ cerebral volume/ischemic core volume, and mismatch volume was defined as $\tau_{\operatorname*{max}}>6$ s volume minus ischemic core volume. We divided patients into mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\geq10~\mathrm{m}$ , and mismatch ratio $\geq1.8$ and mismatch volume $\ge15~\mathsf{m}$ groups. The primary outcome was the 90-day modified Rankin Scale score ordinal distribution. Safety outcomes were symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage and 90-day mortality.  

Results: There were 425 patients included. In both the mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\geq10~\mathsf{m}$ (mismatch $^+$ , $n=395$ ; mismatch, $n=31$ ) and mismatch ratio $\geq1.8$ and mismatch volume $\ge15\mathrm{~m}$ groups (mismatch $^+$ , $n=346,$ ; mismatch, $n=80.$ ), better 90-day modified Rankin Scale outcomes were found in the endovascular treatment group compared with the MM group (4 [2–5] vs 4 [3–5], common odds ratio [cOR], 1.9, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 1.3–2.7, $p=0.001$ ; 4 [2–5] vs 4 [3–5], cOR, 1.9, $95\%$ CI 1.3–2.8, $p=0.001$ , respectively), but not in patients without mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\geq10~\mathsf{m}$ (5 [3–6] vs 5 [4–6], cOR, 1.2, $95\%$ CI 0.3–4.1, $p=0.83)$ , and mismatch ratio $\geq1.8$ and mismatch volume $\ge15~\mathsf{m}$ (4 [3–6] vs 5 [3–6], cOR, 1.2, $95\%$ CI 0.6–2.7, $p=0.60\AA$ . However, no interaction effect was found in both subgroups $(p$ interaction ${>}0.10;$ ).  

Conclusion: Endovascular treatment was more efficacious than MM in patients with mismatch profiles, but no treatment effect or interaction was noted in the no mismatch profile patients. However, the small sample size of patients with no mismatch may have underpowered our analysis. A pooled analysis of large core trials stratified by mismatch is warranted.

A “penumbral” imaging mismatch profile defined as a mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.2$ and mismatch volume ${\geq}10~\mathrm{ml}.$ , or mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ , was used to select LVO patients without large infarct who could benefit from EVT in previous randomized controlled trials.7–9 In patients with large infarct, it would be intuitive that the presence of perfusion mismatch could serve as a helpful imaging biomarker to select patients for reperfusion therapy where cerebral tissue could be salvaged. However, the SELECT2 trial4 demonstrated that the presence of imaging mismatch was not a treatment effect modifier between patients who were treated with EVT compared with MM; although, few patients without mismatch profile were enrolled.10 As the imaging criteria were different between the Endovascular Therapy in Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusion with a Large Infarct (ANGEL-ASPECT) and SELECT2 trials, it remains unknown whether a mismatch profile can modify the effect of EVT in the ANGELASPECT trial.  

Hence, we performed a post-hoc analysis of the ANGEL-ASPECT trial to compare the outcomes of EVT and MM in patients with or without mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10\mathrm{ml}$ , as well as in patients with or without mismatch ratio $\geq1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ .  



# Methods  



# Patient Population  

The present study was a post-hoc analysis of the ANGELASPECT randomized trial. The ANGEL-ASPECT trial was a multicenter prospective, randomized, open-label, blinded end-point clinical trial, conducted in China at 46 comprehensive stroke centers between 2 October, 2020, and 18 May, 2022, including 456 patients with large infarct due to anterior circulation LVO who were randomly assigned to the EVT or MM groups at a 1:1 ratio. The eligibility criteria for participants in this study aligned with our trial criteria.3 The imaging criteria for inclusion were as follows: (1) Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) values between 3 and 5, determined from noncontrast computed tomography scan taken within 24 hours after the onset of stroke (defined as the time when the patient was last known to be well). There were no restrictions on ischemic core volume for this group; (2) ASPECTS ranging from 0 to 2, based on noncontrast computed tomography within 24 hours after the onset of stroke, accompanied by an ischemic core volume between $70~\mathrm{ml}$ and $100~\mathrm{ml}$ ; and (3) ASPECTS ${>}5$ , determined from noncontrast computed tomography acquired between 6 and 24 hours after stroke onset, along with an ischemic core volume ranging from $70~\mathrm{ml}$ to $100~\mathrm{ml}$ . Details of the inclusion/exclusion criteria in the ANGEL-ASPECT trial were previously reported.11 The - ANGEL-ASPECT trial was approved by the ethics committee at Beijing Tiantan Hospital (IRB approval number: KY2020-072-02) and all participating centers. Participants or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent before commencing the study.  

The inclusion criterion of this analysis was patients enrolled in the ANGEL-ASPECT trial, and the exclusion criteria were: (1) withdrawal of consent; and (2) mismatch ratio or mismatch volume was missing. The patients of the present study were divided according to the presence or absence of mismatch based on the EXTEND $\mathrm{IA}^{7}$ and SWIFT PRIME9 trial definitions: (1) patients with a mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10~\mathrm{ml}$ versus no mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10~\mathrm{ml}$ groups7; or (2) mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ versus no mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ groups.9 Informed consent was obtained from the patient or the patient’s legally authorized representative before enrollment into the trial. The ethics committees of each participating center approved this study.  



# Clinical and Radiological Data  

The baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, vascular risk factors, pre-stroke mRS, time intervals from symptom onset to admission, blood pressure at admission, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, qualifying imaging characteristics, randomization group, puncture time, and recanalization were provided by the site investigators and prospectively collected. The 90-day mRS was assessed by telephone interviews with recording for quality control.  

An independent imaging core laboratory blinded to all clinical information assessed the baseline ASPECTS, occlusion site, reperfusion level, target recanalization at 36 hours according to the modified arterial occlusive lesion $\mathrm{(mAOL)}^{12}$ on follow-up computed tomography $\mathrm{(CT)/mag-}$ netic resonance (MR) angiography, and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). The ischemic core was estimated by MR imaging (MRI; apparent diffusion coefficient of ${<}620\upmu\mathrm{m}^{2}/s)$ or CT perfusion (CTP; relative cerebral blood flow ${<}30\%$ of normal brain blood flow). Critically hypoperfused tissue (ischemic core and penumbra volume) was estimated with time-to-maximum $\left(\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{max}}\right)$ volumes over 6 seconds. Lesion volumes were processed and calculated using the RAPID software (version 5.0.4; iSchemaView, Menlo Park, CA, USA). The mismatch ratio was defined as the critically hypoperfused volume divided by the core volume. The mismatch volume was defined as the critically hypoperfused volume minus the core volume. Patients with a mismatch ratio are those who had a “mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\geq10~\mathrm{ml}^{\flat}$ or “mismatch ratio $\geq1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ ,” and patients without a mismatch ratio are those who did not have a “mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\geq10~\mathrm{ml}^{\flat}$ or “mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ .”  



# Study Outcomes  

The primary outcome was the ordinal mRS score at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included functional independence (mRS 0 to 2), independent ambulation $\mathrm{(mRS~0~}$ to 3) at 90 days, early neurological improvement defined as NIHSS score of 0 or 1 or improvement by $\geq10$ points at 36 hours, change in infarct volume defined as from baseline imaging (CT perfusion or diffusionweighted imaging) to non-contrast CT at 7 days or at discharge (whichever is earlier) or to MRI at 36 hours, and target artery recanalization at 36 hours that was defined as mAOL grade 2–3.12 Safety outcomes were symptomatic ICH within 48 hours following randomization, defined by the Heidelberg bleeding classification,13 any ICH within 48 hours, death within 90 days from the onset of stroke, and use of decompressive craniectomy during hospitalization.  



# Statistical Analysis  

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were described using medians with interquartile ranges. The Mann– Whitney $U$ test was utilized for continuous variables, and the $\mathtt{c}^{2}$ -test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as applicable, were used to compare the baseline characteristics between EVT and MM in each subgroup. Baseline characteristics with a $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ value $<0.05$ were selected as the confounders. To adjust for confounders, we performed an ordinal logistic regression model to calculate the common odds ratio (OR) with the $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) for the ordinal shift in the distribution of the mRS score toward a better outcome for the primary outcome. A Cox proportional-hazards model was utilized to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) with $95\%$ CI for death within  

90 days, a general linear model to calculate mean differences with $95\%$ CI for change from baseline ischemic core volume, and a generalized linear model to calculate relative risk (RR) with $95\%$ CI for other study outcomes. Additionally, we investigated whether there was an interaction effect on all study outcomes between the assigned treatment and mismatch profiles.  

Statistical analyses were carried out using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), with statistical significance defined as a two-tailed $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ value of $<0.05$ .  



# Results  

There were 456 patients enrolled in the ANGEL-ASPECT randomized trial, of whom 1 patient withdrew consent, and 29 had missing mismatch measurements, leading to 426 patients for this analysis. There were 395 patients with mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10~\mathrm{ml}$ , and 346 patients with mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ (Figure 1).  

Patients with mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10~\mathrm{ml}$ had lower median NIHSS (16 [13–19] vs 19 [14–21], $p=0.03\$ ), lower rate of hypoperfusion intensity ratio (HIR) $<0.4$ $15.0\%$ vs $51.9\%$ , $\phi<0.001;$ , smaller ischemic core volume (59 [28–82] ml vs 74 [22–119] ml, $\begin{array}{r}{\ p=0.05.}\end{array}$ ), longer time from onset to door (357 [209–651] vs 225 [120–505] minutes, $\ensuremath{p}=0.04)$ , and longer time from onset to randomization (463 [307–768] vs 321 [212–760] minutes, $\begin{array}{r}{\ p=0.05,}\end{array}$ ) than those without mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10~\mathrm{ml}$ . Moreover, patients with mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ had lower median NIHSS (16 [13–19] vs 18 [14–21], $p=0.01$ ), and smaller ischemic core volume (52.5 [22–77] ml vs 82 [67–126] ml, $\begin{array}{r}{\phi<0.001.}\end{array}$ ) than patients without mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ (Table 1 and Supplemental Table S1). There was no association between the time from onset to randomization and the proportion of patients with the presence or absence of mismatch profile (All $\boldsymbol{\mathscr{p}}$ for trend ${>}0.1$ ; Supplemental Figure S1).  

Among the 395 patients with mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10~\mathrm{ml}$ , EVT was significantly associated with a shift to better outcomes than MM (4 [2–5] vs 4 [3–5]; common OR 1.9, $95\%$ CI 1.3–2.7; $p=0.001\$ ). In the 31 patients without mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10~\mathrm{ml}$ , EVT was not significantly associated with a shift to better outcomes than MM (5 [3–6] vs 5 [4–6]; common OR 1.2, $95\%$ CI 0.3–4.1; $p=0.83$ (Figure 2). No interaction effect was found on the primary outcome $\cdot\boldsymbol{p}=0.63$ ; Table 2).

Among the 346 patients with mismatch ratio $\geq1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ , EVT was significantly associated with a shift to better outcomes than MM (4 [2–5] vs 4 [3–5]; common OR 1.9, $95\%$ CI 1.3–2.8; $p=0.001,$ ). However, in the 80 patients without mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ , EVT was not significantly associated with a shift to better outcomes than MM (4 [3–6] vs 5 [3–6]; common OR 1.2, $95\%$ CI 0.6–2.7; $\mathrm{\Delta}p=0.60\mathrm{\AA}$ ) (Figure 2). No interaction effect was found on the primary outcome $\cdot_{\mathscr{P}}=0.43$ ; Table 3).  

In patients with mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10~\mathrm{ml}$ , EVT was associated with higher rates of functional independence $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2;31.3\%$ vs $11.8\%$ , RR 2.2, $95\%$ CI 1.5–3.2, $\phi<0.001,$ ), independent ambulation $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{(mRS~}}0{\mathrm{-}}3{\mathrm{:~}}47.1\%$ vs $34.2\%$ , RR 1.4, $95\%$ CI 1.1–1.8, $p=0.004,$ ), target artery recanalization at 36 hours $(86.5\%$ vs $37.8\%$ , RR 1.5, $95\%$ CI 1.3–1.7, $\begin{array}{r}{\phi<0.001,}\end{array}$ ), and any ICH within 48 hours $(50.5\%$ vs $18.2\%$ , RR, 2.6, $95\%$ CI: 1.9–3.7, $\begin{array}{r}{\phi<0.001.}\end{array}$ ) than MM (Table 2).  

In patients without mismatch ratio $\geq1.2$ and mismatch volume $\ge10\mathrm{ml}$ , there was no significant difference in the EVT versus MM groups with regard to functional independence $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2{:}6.7\%$ vs $6.3\%$ , RR 9.5, $95\%$ CI 0.0–7776.1, $p=0.51$ ) and target artery recanalization at 36 hours $(75.0\%$ vs $36.4\%$ , RR 1.6, $95\%$ CI 0.4–6.3, $\begin{array}{r}{p=0.49;}\end{array}$ ). A lower rate of death within 90 days in the EVT compared with the MM group was observed $(33.3\%$ vs $37.5\%$ , RR 0.2, $95\%$ CI: 0.0–0.9, $\ensuremath{p}=0.04$ ; Table 2).  

In patients with mismatch ratio $\geq1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ , EVT was associated with higher rates of functional independence $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}2;33.7\%$ vs $11.7\%$ , RR 3.0, $95\%$ CI 1.9–4.7, $\phi<0.001,$ ), independent ambulation $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{(mRS~}}0{\mathrm{-}}3{\mathrm{:~}}48.4\%$ vs $35.2\%$ , RR 1.4, $95\%$ CI 1.1–1.8, $p=0.01$ ), target artery recanalization at  

36 hours $(87.5\%$ vs $38.5\%$ , RR 1.9, $95\%$ CI 1.6–2.2, $\phi<0.001\$ , and any ICH within 48 hours $(51.1\%$ vs $20.4\%$ , RR 2.4, $95\%$ CI 1.7–3.4, $p<0.001$ ) than MM (Table 3).  

In patients without mismatch ratio ${\geq}1.8$ and mismatch volume $\geq15~\mathrm{ml}$ , EVT was associated with a higher rate of target artery recanalization at 36 hours $(76.7\%$ vs $34.4\%$ , RR 1.8, $95\%$ CI 1.1–2.9, $p=0.02\$ ). However, there was not a significant difference in independent ambulation $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}3{:}35.9\%$ vs $26.8\%$ , RR 1.1, $95\%$ $\mathrm{~C~I~}0.6{-}2.1\$ , $p=0.68,$ and death within 90 days $(30.8\%$ vs $29.3\%$ , RR 0.8, $95\%$ CI 0.4–1.9, $p=0.60,$ between the EVT and MM groups, respectively (Table 3).  

No interaction effect on all secondary outcomes or safety outcomes was found in each subgroup.  



# Discussion  

This post-hoc analysis of the ANGEL-ASPECT trial found that in patients with large ischemic core, EVT was associated with better outcomes than MM in patients with either of the two assessed mismatch profiles, but not in patients without mismatch profiles. Additionally, we found no interaction effect on any of the study outcomes between mismatch profile and EVT compared with MM for large ischemic core due to LVO (Figure 2).  

In concordance with a previous study, the present investigation showed that the proportion of patients attaining good or ambulatory functional outcomes, defined as a 90-day mRS score of 0–2 or 0–3, was higher among those presenting with a perfusion mismatch compared with those without mismatch.14 Furthermore, our analysis detected a discernible shift toward improved functional outcomes following EVT among patients afflicted with large ischemic core, compared with those treated with BMM. Moreover, infarct size might be an oversimplistic concept, as it merely represents a volumetric quantitative measurement without accounting for the qualitative aspects of the infarct, meaning endovascular reperfusion may still rescue elements of the neurovascular unit within the infarcted area, which would translate into better functional outcomes. Notwithstanding, our prior work showed better outcomes with EVT versus medical management in patients with ASPECTS of 3 to 5 and smaller compared with larger ischemic core volumes $\left(<70~\mathrm{ml}$ vs ${>}70~\mathrm{ml}$ ), and that the benefit of EVT was reflected with larger ischemic core size with a more permissive mRS range $(0{-}3\mathrm{vs}0{-}2)$ .15

Data from the HERMES collaboration suggests that the reduction in final infarct volumes related to EVT only explains approximately $12\%$ of the relationship between EVT and functional outcomes.16 This notion is further supported by the fact that the mean infarct volume at the 24-hour $(\pm6)$ CT or MRI follow-up in the TENSION Trial was not different between patients in the EVT and control groups (205.8 vs $227.7\mathrm{~ml}$ , $p=0.35$ ).1 Similarly, the SELECT2 trial also did not find a difference in infarct growth with EVT versus MM,10 nor did we find a difference in infarct growth in a prior ANGEL-ASPECT analysis among patients with ASPECTS 3 to 5.15 However, in patients with unsuccessful recanalization $(\mathrm{mTICI}0{-}2\mathrm{a})$ in ANGEL-ASPECT, we observed significantly greater infarct growth in the EVT compared with MM groups despite no difference in very poor outcomes between the two groups.17

What one can visually see by perfusion imaging as mismatch may not reliably translate to the clinical benefit of endovascular reperfusion in patients with a large core. This is exemplified by the SELECT2 trial, which demonstrated a significant treatment benefit of EVT regardless of the presence of imaging mismatch.4 In patients with a small to moderate ischemic core, the HERMES penumbral meta-analysis did not show an association of CTP mismatch volume with clinical outcomes (utility weighted mRS or functional independence) when adjusted for core volume.18 Further investigation is of interest, as the combination of imaging characteristics, such as the degree of hypodensity, the eloquence of the ischemic core area, absence of mismatch, very large ischemic core volumes $>100~\mathrm{ml}$ or ASPECTS 0 to 2, and other prognostic factors, may facilitate a nuanced and individualized approach to decision-making for patients with large ischemic core volumes.19,20 This assertion is substantiated by research conducted by the HERMES and other large core studies, which reported that favorable functional outcomes among thrombectomy patients were associated with various factors, such as patient age, ischemic core volume, and the time interval between imaging and reperfusion.21

An additional potential explanation for the lack of interaction could be attributed to the potential overestimation of infarct volume through the use of CTP. Although CTP offers information on core volume and penumbra in the acute phase of ischemic stroke, it is acknowledged that segmentations of cerebral blood flow maps may harbor false positive regions, particularly in regions characterized by hypodense white matter.22 Notably, a substantial number of patients who showed quantitative overestimation of the ischemic core by CTP displayed these false positive regions within the white matter adjacent to the lesion. Interestingly, a previous study did not reveal any indication that spatial and volumetric accuracy suffered in patients with shorter intervals between imaging and reperfusion.23 The temporal gap between CTP and the reestablishment of blood flow (a period during which infarct expansion can occur) represents another critical factor that may contribute to the relatively limited accuracy for the estimation of infarct volumes.  

RAPID, an imaging software tool, has demonstrated its ability to estimate follow-up infarct volume.24,25 It has been used in previous studies, such as SWIFT PRIME,9 EXTEND-IA,7 DAWN,26 and DEFUSE 3.8 However, it is important to acknowledge limitations inherent to the coregistration of disparate imaging modalities, as these limitations can influence the spatial congruence between the baseline CTP-derived ischemic core and the subsequent infarct volume. Sensitivity analyses conducted in previous research have underscored a greater degree of inaccuracy in patients who developed hemorrhagic transformation and associated edema, which in turn affected the spatial agreement between these parameters.23 Additionally, this study revealed that the estimated ischemic core volume on the baseline CTP generally appeared smaller than the infarct volume observed on the 24-hour follow-up MRI scan.23 Furthermore, it is important to consider the potential for infarct growth in regions that persistently show hypoperfusion, as achieving a mTICI score of 2b, indicative of flow restoration to over $50\%$ of the affected territory, may not preclude this phenomenon. The development of vasogenic edema, while less conspicuous at the 24-hour mark compared with the 3 to 5-day interval, may lead to an inflation of the measured ischemic core. Consequently, distinguishing the effects of interval infarct growth and edema from potential core underestimation by CTP remains a challenge that merits further exploration. Moreover, it is imperative to refine the accurate classification of tissue viability in white matter as a focal point for future efforts aimed at enhancing the precision of CTP-derived ischemic and penumbra core segmentation.  

We acknowledge limitations in the present study. First, it is essential to consider the potential for infarct growth. As reported in the literature, the ischemic core continues to undergo dynamic changes in the days following the stroke.27 This phenomenon may result in an overestimation of the true infarct volume. To provide an understanding of the infarct volume’s evolution, future investigation at different time points is warranted to ascertain which infarct volume best represents the final outcome. Second, there was an imbalance in the distribution of patients with and without perfusion mismatch in our study. Our findings indicated a high proportion of patients with mismatch in both treatment groups, specifically $92.7\%$ and $81.2\%$ when utilizing the 1.2 and 1.8 mismatch ratio thresholds, respectively. This proportion may be reflective of the fact that clinicians were selecting patients for enrollment with the presence of mismatch in the ANGEL-ASPECT trial. We do not know about the mismatch ratios of patients with large cores who were not selected for ANGEL-ASPECT. This imbalance has the potential to introduce bias that may have affected the interpretation of the results. Additionally, the current subgroup analysis showed limited statistical power to fully assess the benefit of EVT for patients without mismatch, potentially resulting in a type II error owing to the small sample size in the group of patients without mismatch. Therefore, more data are needed to analyze the benefit of EVT for large core patients without mismatch. Last, the present study population was comprised of individuals from the Chinese population, raising concerns regarding the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Nevertheless, the randomized, multicenter design of our study enhances the quality and reliability of our results, potentially making them more representative of the broader Asian population.

# Conclusions  

EVT was more efficacious than MM in patients with mismatch profiles, but no treatment effect or interaction was noted in the no mismatch profile patients. However, the small sample size of patients without mismatch may have led to a lack of statistical power to detect an effect. A pooled analysis of large core trials stratified by the presence or absence of mismatch profile is warranted.
