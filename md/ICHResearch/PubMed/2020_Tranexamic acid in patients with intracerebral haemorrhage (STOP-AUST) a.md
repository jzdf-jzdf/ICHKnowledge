# Tranexamic acid in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage (STOP-AUST): a multicentre, randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial  

Atte Meretoja, Nawaf Yassi, Teddy Y Wu, Leonid Churilov, Gerli Sibolt, Jiann-Shing Jeng, Timothy Kleinig, Neil J Spratt, Vincent Thijs, Tissa Wijeratne, Der-Yang Cho, Darshan Shah, Geoffrey C Cloud, Thanh Phan, Christopher Bladin, Andrew Moey, Richard I Aviv, Christen D Barras, Gagan Sharma, Chung Y Hsu, Henry Ma, Bruce C V Campbell, Peter Mitchell, Bernard Yan, Mark W Parsons, Marjaana Tiainen, Sami Curtze, Daniel Strbian, Sung-Chun Tang, Jackson Harvey, Christopher Levi, Geoffrey A Donnan\*, Stephen M Davis\*  

# Summary  

Background Despite intracerebral haemorrhage causing $5\%$ of deaths worldwide, few evidence-based therapeutic strategies other than stroke unit care exist. Tranexamic acid decreases haemorrhage in conditions such as acute trauma and menorrhoea. We aimed to assess whether tranexamic acid reduces intracerebral haemorrhage growth in patients with acute intracerebral haemorrhage.  

Methods We did a prospective, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, investigator-led, phase 2 trial at 13 stroke centres in Australia, Finland, and Taiwan. Patients were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had an acute intracerebral haemorrhage fulfilling clinical criteria (eg, Glasgow Coma Scale score of ${>}7_{\ensuremath{\mathrm{~\AA~}}}$ , intracerebral haemorrhage volume ${<}70~\mathrm{mL}$ , no identified or suspected secondary cause of intracerebral haemorrhage, no thrombotic events within the previous 12 months, no planned surgery in the next $24\mathrm{~h_{i}~}$ and no use of anticoagulation), had contrast extravasation on CT angiography (the so-called spot sign), and were treatable within $\mathbf{4}{\cdot}5\mathbf{h}$ of symptom onset and within $^{1\mathbf{h}}$ of CT angiography. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either ${\textbf{1g}}$ of intravenous tranexamic acid over ${\bf10}{\bfmin}$ followed by ${\bf18}$ over ${\mathfrak{sh}}$ or matching placebo, started within $\mathbf{4}{\cdot}5\mathbf{h}$ of symptom onset. Randomisation was done using a centralised web-based procedure with randomly permuted blocks of varying size. All patients, investigators, and staff involved in patient management were masked to treatment. The primary outcome was intracerebral haemorrhage growth $(>33\%$ relative or ${>}6\mathrm{mL}$ absolute) at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ . The primary and safety analyses were done in the intention-to-treat population. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01702636).  

Findings Between March 1, 2013, and Aug 13, 2019, we enrolled and randomly assigned 100 participants to the tranexamic acid group $(\mathbf{n}{=}50)$ or the placebo group $\scriptstyle(\mathbf{n}=50)$ . Median age was 71 years (IQR 57–79) and median intracerebral haemorrhage volume was $14{\cdot}6~\mathrm{mL}$ (7·9–32·7) at baseline. The primary outcome was not different between the two groups: 26 $(52\%)$ patients in the placebo group and 22 $(44\%)$ in the tranexamic acid group had intracerebral haemorrhage growth (odds ratio [OR] 0·72 $95\%$ CI 0·32–1·59], $\mathbf{p}{=}0\cdot41)$ . There was no evidence of a difference in the proportions of patients who died or had thromboembolic complications between the groups: eight $(16\%)$ in the placebo group vs 13 $(26\%)$ in the tranexamic acid group died and two $(4\%)$ vs one $(2\%)$ had thromboembolic complications. None of the deaths was considered related to study medication.  

Interpretation Our study does not provide evidence that tranexamic acid prevents intracerebral haemorrhage growth, although the treatment was safe with no increase in thromboembolic complications. Larger trials of tranexamic acid, with simpler recruitment methods and an earlier treatment window, are justified.

# Introduction  

Globally, 18 million people live with the sequelae of intracerebral haemorrhage and 3 million die because of the condition each year, comprising $5\%$ of all human deaths.1,2 Although specialised multidisciplinary treatment in stroke units benefits all patients who have a stroke, there are no strongly evidence-based acute therapies specific to intracerebral haemorrhage.3,4  

The main determinants of clinical outcome in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage are age, neurological deficit, and haemorrhage cause, location, and volume.5,6 Of these, volume is the most important factor. As with ischaemic stroke, intracerebral haemorrhage is frequently a dynamic process, with growth occurring in most patients over the first few hours from symptom onset, presenting a target window for intervention.7–9 Some haemostatic agents have been tested for attenuating intracerebral haemorrhage growth.10 Recombinant activated coagulation factor VII (rFVIIa) was tested in two trials in 1240 patients with intracerebral haemorrhage within $^{4\textrm{h}}$ of symptom onset,

# Evidence before this study

Tranexamic acid has been studied in other intracranial conditions, in patients with subarachnoid haemorrhage, with a Cochrane review from 2013, and more recently in patients with traumatic brain injury, with several meta-analyses. # Added value of this study  

# Research in context  

The STOP-AUST trial is, to our knowledge, the first trial of tranexamic acid in patients with known ongoing intracerebral haemorrhage at time of recruitment. Tranexamic acid given within $4{\cdot}5{\mathrm{~h~}}$ of symptom onset did not reduce intracerebral haemorrhage growth or improve functional outcome, but was safe. The results will help inform design of future trials.  

# Implications of all the available evidence  

Current evidence does not justify routine use of tranexamic acid in patients with acute intracerebral haemorrhage. Despite thousands of patients with intracerebral haemorrhage presenting at the STOP-AUST trial sites annually, it took several years to recruit the trial target of 100 participants, suggesting that a simpler protocol would be more likely to translate into practice. The combination of convincing safety evidence, high mortality, absence of evidence-based therapies, and often reduced patient capacity might justify the use of deferred consent in future trials. Future research efforts should concentrate on ultra-acute streamlined delivery of tranexamic acid.

# Methods  

STOP-AUST was a prospective, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, investigator-led, phase 2 trial done at 13 hospitals in Australia (ten hospitals), Finland (one hos­ pital), and Taiwan (two hospitals; appendix p 2).  

# Study design and participants  

Patients were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had a non-traumatic intracerebral haemorrhage with a spot sign, and were treatable within 4·5 h of symptom onset and within 1 h of CT angiography. Imaging for participant selection mandated a CT angiography before randomisation to demonstrate a spot sign. The spot sign was defined as contrast extravasation within the haemorrhage, assessed according to three criteria, all of which must be present: (1) serpiginous or spot-like appearance within the margin of a parenchymal haematoma without connection to an outside vessel; (2) the density (in Hounsfield units) should be greater than that of the background haematoma (site investigators are not required to document the density); and (3) no hyperdensity at the corresponding location on non-contrast CT (to exclude calcium mimics). The spot signs were centrally adjudicated after database lock by two readers. Exclusion criteria were Glascow Coma Scale score of less than 8; contraindications for antifibrinolytic therapy; very large intracerebral haemorrhage $(>70~\mathrm{mL})$ ; brainstem haemorrhage; intracerebral haemorrhage known or sus­ pected by study investigator to be secondary to trauma, aneurysm, vascular malformation, haemorrhagic transformation ofischaemic stroke, cerebral venous thrombosis, thrombolytic therapy, tumour, or infection; contrast already administered in $24\mathrm{~h~}$ before initial CT or contraindication to contrast agents; thromboembolic events in the past 12 months; planned surgery for the intracerebral haem­ orrhage within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ ; hereditary or acquired haemorrhagic diathesis or coagulation factor deficiency; use of anti­ coagulation agents; pregnancy; concurrent use of haemo­ static agents; participation in another investigational study in the past 30 days; known terminal illness; or any condition in which the study therapy is contraindicated or that could affect participation in the study, as judged by the investigator. Use of antiplatelet agents was not an exclusion criterion.  

The trial was performed in accordance with the principles of good clinical practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained for each site and country by review boards as per local regulations before patient recruitment. The protocol has been published18 and protocol changes are described in the appendix (p 3). Investigators sought written informed consent from each participant if they were able to provide it. If the participant was able to consent but could not write, oral informed consent in the presence of a witness was allowed. When participants could not consent, a relative or representative gave proxy consent. The ethics approval at some sites allowed for deferred consent when a patient was unable and representatives were not available. When consent was deferred or given by a proxy, we informed the participant about the trial as soon as possible and sought their consent.  

# Randomisation and masking  

Patients were randomly assigned to receive either placebo or tranexamic acid (1:1) using a centralised web-based procedure with randomly permuted blocks of varying size. Patients and all those involved in patient manage­ ment or clinical or imaging assessment of adverse events or outcomes were masked to treatment allocation. The investigational product was distributed to participating centres in externally indistinguishable sealed treatment kits containing either tranexamic acid or placebo $(0\cdot9\%$ NaCl) in non-identical standard off-the-shelf ampoules. After randomisation, the sealed treatment kit corres­ ponding to the randomisation kit number was handed to an external unmasked person not involved in patient management or evaluation (a pharmacist, nurse, or other professional on the basis of local practices) who inserted the two ampoules into two bags of normal saline, and gave the appropriately labelled masked investigational product to the study personnel for administration to the patient.  

# Procedures  

All participants received either intravenous tranexamic acid ${1\mathrm{g}}$ in $100\mathrm{mL}$ $0\cdot9\%$ NaCl over $10\mathrm{min}$ followed by ${1\mathrm{g}}$ in $500~\mathrm{mL}$ $0\cdot9\%$ NaCl infusion over $^{8\textrm{h}}$ or placebo with the same administration schedule, started within 4·5 h of symptom onset. A second CT scan was done at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ $(\pm3)$ after start of study drug administration to assess haem­ orrhage growth. Vital signs were recorded during and after infusion, and blood pressure was managed according to standard care. The protocol mandated electrocardi­ ography at baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , but troponin was only assessed if electrocardiogram or clinical symptoms were suggestive of myocardial ischaemia. Neurological deficit was assessed at 21–27 h and functional outcome was assessed at 90 days $(\pm7)$ after start of study drug admin­ istration. All investigators were trained and certified to assess modified Rankin Scale (mRS), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and spot sign.  

Patients were followed up for 90 days $(\pm7)$ after start of study drug administration for adverse events.  

# Outcomes  

The primary outcome measure was presence of intracere­ bral haemorrhage growth by 24 h $(\pm3)$ after start of study drug administration as defined by at least a $33\%$ or $6\mathrm{mL}$ increase from baseline, adjusted for baseline intracerebral haemorrhage volume.

# Statistical analysis  

The sample size of 100 was based on $80\%$ power to detect a significant absolute difference of $30\%$ in the proportion of patients with haemorrhage enlarge­ment at $24\mathrm{h}$ ( $31\%$ in treatment vs $61\%$ in control arm) at a two-sided statistical significance threshold of $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}05$ . The control group rate was based on the PREDICT study.17 Justified by absence of previous efficacy data, our protocol dictated an adaptive sample size re-estimation with a potential increase up to 150 after the first 75 participants.18 The interim analysis did not recommend a sample increase.  

The statistical analysis followed an analysis plan fixed before database lock. Missing primary outcome data were imputed assuming worst possible outcome. The primary outcome was compared between treatment and control groups adjusted for baseline volume using binary logistic regression. The baseline volume was assessed on the most recent scan before randomisation, whether that was the non-contrast CT or CT angiogram. Both baseline and $24\mathrm{~h~}$ intracerebral haemorrhage volumes were obtained in Osirix using planimetric techniques as described previously.19 Intraventricular haemorrhage volumes were also analysed with the same planimetric technique but were not included in the intracerebral haemorrhage volumes.

For the functional outcomes, the analysis plan mandated testing for mRS dichotomised at 0–3 and 0–4, where getting back to a prestroke score counted as the better outcome, using binary logistic regression adjusting for age and baseline intracerebral haemorrhage volume. Addi­ tionally, mRS categorical shift (common odds ratio [OR] on logistic regression) was to be estimated on the full range of the mRS if proportional odds assumptions were met (both Brant test and approximate likelihood-ratio test of proportionality of odds not significant) or otherwise by using the assumption-free Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney gen­ eralised ORs.20  

For analysis of absolute intracerebral haemorrhage and intraventricular haemorrhage growth by $24\mathrm{~h~}$ a median regression model with bootstrapped standard error estima­ tion and 500 repetitions was estimated, with treatment group as an independent variable and absolute intra­ cerebral haemorrhage or intraventricular haemorrhage growth as dependent variables, adjusting for baseline volumes correspondingly. The safety outcomes of major thromboembolic events and death by 90 days were analysed using binary logistic regression, with age and base­ line intracerebral haemorrhage volume adjustment for the mortality outcome only.  

Predefined subgroup analyses are provided for the primary outcome measure, the results of which were regarded as hypothesis-generating only. Exploratory posthoc analyses were done with adjustment for both time from onset to treatment and baseline volume; interaction with time from onset to treatment as a continuous variable; and further subgroups dichotomised at $2\textrm{h}$ and 4 h from onset to treatment. Death at 7 days was also assessed.  

The primary efficacy and safety analyses were done on an intention-to-treat basis. Stata, version 15, was used for analyses.  

The study was run by an executive committee co-chaired by SMD and GAD and a steering committee including all site primary investigators. An independent data safety monitoring board reviewed the unmasked data after every 25 participants had completed follow-up and performed a formal safety interim analysis after 60 patients had completed follow-up. The trial is regis­ tered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01702636) and the EU Clinical Trials Register (EudraCT 2013-001262-42).

# Results  

We recruited 100 participants between March 1, 2013, and Aug 13, 2019. Comprehensive screening data for all consecutive patients with intracerebral haemorrhage was not available at all sites because study staff availability for screening varied. Based on screening logs complemented by local prospective stroke registries, screening and exclusion data were obtained from seven of the 13 sites (four in Australia, one in Finland, and two in Thailand). These sites admitted 3325 patients with intracerebral haemorrhage over the study period, of whom 88 $(2\cdot6\%)$ were recruited into the trial. Recruitment by site varied from $1\cdot5\%$ (ten of 653) to $5\cdot0\%$ (40 of 793) of all patients admitted with intracerebral haemorrhage. Late presenta­ tion and absence of spot sign were the main reasons for not being recruited, ruling out about $40\%$ of patients each. Anticoagulation therapy excluded about $5\%$ of patients at the Australian sites and $10\%$ in Finland, but was uncom­ mon in Taiwan. Early surgery was a common exclusion reason in Taiwan $(>10\%$ of those screened), but quite uncommon in Finland and Australia.  

50 participants were randomly assigned to each group and all received the allocated treatment (figure 1). One participant in the tranexamic acid group did not have a follow-up scan because of early palliation and was assigned the intracerebral haemorrhage growth outcome as per the statistical analysis plan. All patients had complete 3-month follow-up and study masking was not broken in any individual.  

At baseline, median age of the participants was 71 years (IQR 57–79), median intracerebral haemorrhage volume was $14{\cdot}6~\mathrm{mL}$ (7·9–32·7), and median NIHSS score was 13 (8–18). Median time from onset to baseline scan was 105 min (77–155), from baseline scan to treatment was 41 min (29–56), and onset to treatment was $150\mathrm{min}$ (118–203). Participant characteristics were reasonably balanced between the groups (table 1).  

The primary efficacy outcome was not different between the two groups, 26 $(52\%)$ of 50 patients in the placebo group and 22 $(44\%)$ of 50 of the tranexamic acid group had growth of intracerebral haemorrhage from baseline to $24\mathrm{h}$ (OR 0·72 $[95\%$ CI 0·32–1·59], $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}41)$ . An exploratory post-hoc analysis showed no change in the primary outcome if adjusted for time from onset to therapy in addition to baseline volume $(\mathrm{OR}0.72[0\cdot32\mathrm{-}1\cdot62],\mathrm{p}\mathrm{=}0\cdot43)$ .  

The distribution of functional outcome at 3 months did not fulfil the proportional odds assumptions, and the assumption-free analysis showed no difference between the treatment groups (generalised OR 1·01 $[95\%$ CI 0·63–1·61], $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}97$ ; appendix p 10).

# Discussion  

Our study of tranexamic acid versus placebo within $4{\cdot}5\mathrm{h}$ of symptom onset in patients with acute haemorrhage did not show evidence of a significant reduction in intra­ cerebral haemorrhage growth. There were no treatmentrelated safety concerns. Prespecified subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature and might serve to inform future trial hypothesis design.  

We chose the 4·5-h window for this study because haematoma growth in intracerebral haemorrhage is known to occur early and therefore earlier treatment is likely to be more beneficial.8 Although patients with ischae­ mic stroke and intracerebral haemorrhage cannot be distinguished from each other with clinical findings alone, emergency medical services are traditionally organised to bring in patients with acute stroke with haste for diagnosis and treatment within $4{\cdot}5\mathrm{h}$ of symptom onset.21  

In patients with stroke, tranexamic acid was initially extensively tested in clinical trials for subarachnoid haemorrhage. A Cochrane meta-analysis of 1904 patients concluded that there was no benefit because the treatment effect on re-bleeds (OR 0·65 $[95\%$ CI 0·44–0·97]) was offset by an excess of cerebral ischaemic events (OR 1·41 [1·04–1·91]).22 The trials were mostly done in the 1970s and 1980s with doses much larger than in our trial— typically $6\mathrm{~g~}$ daily for 3–6 weeks.

Interest in tranexamic acid was revived in 2010 after positive results of the placebo-controlled CRASH-2 trial in trauma patients $(\mathrm{n}{=}20211^{\cdot}$ ) using an identical dose to the present study.23,24 Patient outcomes were improved, especially when treatment was initiated early within $^{3\mathrm{~h~}}$ of trauma. Notably, thromboembolic complications were not increased. Published in 2020, the HALT-IT trial in 12 009 patients with gastrointestinal bleeding with a larger dose of $4\mathrm{~g~}$ over $24\textrm{h}$ did not show clinical benefit and showed an increase in venous thromboembolic events.25  

Tranexamic acid was first tested in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage in a small Malaysian trial $\left(\mathrm{n}{=}30\right)^{26}$ and the two TICH trials.13,27 Following the TICH pilot trial $(\mathrm{n}{=}24)$ , 2325 patients with intracerebral haemorrhage in the TICH-2 trial received tranexamic acid or placebo in a dose identical to in our study.13,27 Patients were not selected on the basis of ongoing haemorrhage and the time window was longer, up to $^{8\mathrm{~h~}}$ from onset, although half of the patients were treated within $^{4\mathrm{h}}$ . Functional outcome at 90 days in patients with available growth data did not differ between the groups (adjusted OR 0·88 $[95\%$ CI 0·76–1·03], $\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}11)$ , despite there being haemorrhage growth in a smaller proportion of patients in the tranexamic acid group (265 $[25\%]$ of 1054 in the tranexamic acid group vs 304 $[29\%]$ of 1058 in the placebo group, OR 0·80 $\left[0\cdot66-0\cdot98\right]$ , $\mathrm{p}{=}0\cdot030)$ . 56 patients (24 in tranexamic acid group and 32 in the placebo group) of the 249 patients who had a CT angiography were spot sign positive, but the treatment effect of tranexamic acid on this subgroup has not been published.  

Two previous phase 2 trials using the spot sign as a selection criterion have been reported, namely the STOP-IT and SPOTLIGHT trials, published in 2019.28 These trials recruited 69 patients with intracerebral haemorrhage over 6 years to receive rFVIIa or placebo, with a median time from CT scan to treatment of 79 min (IQR 61–99) in the rFVIIa group, and did not show significant reductions in proportions of patients with intracerebral haemorrhage growth (13 $[41\%]$ of 32 in the rFVIIa group vs 16 $[43\%]$ of 37 in the placebo group, $\mathrm{p}{=}0\cdot83)$ or severe disability (nine $[30\%]$ of 30 vs 13 $[38\%]$ of 34, $\mathtt{p}{=}0\cdot60)$ .  

The sample size for our trial was set at a time when no data on possible effect of tranexamic acid on intracerebral haemorrhage growth was available. Because larger doses of rVIIa had halved absolute intracerebral haemorrhage growth compared with placebo in two trials,11,12 our trial was ambitiously powered for a $30\%$ absolute risk dif­ ference in patients with growth. We tried to mitigate the uncertainty by including an adaptive sample size design, but with the observed effect size it would have needed hundreds of patients to show any possible efficacy. Because the trial took 6 years to recruit 100 patients, a much larger trial would have been challenging to run with this design and available funding. We chose a dichotomous growth outcome for the trial over a continuous growth outcome to achieve clear clinical meaning, to simul­ taneously include both absolute and relative considerations of growth, and to enable imputation of growth by prespecified rules as we have done for the one palliated patient. The continuous secondary outcome was also non-significant, so a different choice would not have made a difference in this trial.  

The choice of running an imaging biomarker phase 2 trial before a larger clinical primary outcome phase 3 trial appears wise in retrospect. Recruitment on the basis of the spot sign was difficult because many sites did not routinely perform CT angiography for all of their patients with intracerebral haemorrhage and identification of the spot sign was not always easy. We observed a nonsignificant $8\%$ absolute difference in growth between the treatment groups, compared with the $4\%$ absolute difference in the non-selected TICH-2 trial.13 It is therefore plausible that the selection using the spot sign leads to a more responsive target population. However, with an inexpensive and safe therapy such as tranexamic acid, the benefits of strict patient selection and narrow target population might be lost, especially because many patients have haemorrhage growth without the spot sign. In this setting, it might be wiser in future trials to concentrate on an early time window instead of the spot sign. Because of long recovery periods in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage, we think that the preferred trial functional outcome would be 6-month mRS.  

Although the interaction term for age was not significant $(\mathrm{p}{=}0{\cdot}083)$ , the point estimate was towards more benefit with tranexamic acid in younger patients. In the much larger TICH-2 trial, there was no interaction by age $(\mathrm{p}{=}0\cdot39)$ . We would be wary of interpreting our signal as having clinical relevance or as a cause for age exclusions in future trials.  

In acute trauma, tranexamic acid has a strong time to treatment interaction, with earlier treatment being better, preferably within $^{1\textrm{h}}$ of trauma, with treatment effect possibly lost beyond $^{3\textrm{h}}$ .25 Similarly, time is known to be of the essence in reperfusion therapies of acute ischaemic stroke, in which most benefits are seen with early treatment and treatment effect is lost beyond $4{\cdot}5\mathrm{h}$ or $6\mathrm{~h~}$ in most patients—only selected patients can still be identifiable at later time windows using advanced imag­ ing.29 Further trials of tranexamic acid in patients with intracerebral haemorrhage in the early time win­ dow are already ongoing (EudraCT 2012-005594-30 and ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03385928).  

Our study has limitations. First, the small sample size makes a type II error possible. The growth rate of $52\%$ in the placebo group was less than the $61\%$ expected based on previous literature.17 Still, the effect size point estimate of our trial, OR 0·72 $95\%$ CI 0·32–1·59), was similar to that of the TICH-2 trial, OR 0·80 $\left(0\cdot66{-}0\cdot98\right)$ . Several tranexamic acid trials are underway and an individual patient meta-analysis will be done to address study power issues.30 Second, the median delay from baseline scan to treatment of 41 min was suboptimal to test the study hypothesis, because much of the potential haemorrhage growth might have already occurred before treatment commenced. In the setting of no evident safety concerns, deferred consent should be considered for speeding up start of therapy in future tranexamic acid trials. Third, recruitment using advanced imaging in a very tight time schedule is difficult and complicates a trial for many sites less familiar and comfortable with the technique. Although 13 sites successfully recruited participants into the trial, two-thirds came from two sites, and there were seven other sites that were unable to recruit a single patient. This limits the generalisability of our findings and suggests that simpler trials using a very early time window might be more applicable in clinical practice.  

In summary, our study does not provide evidence that tranexamic acid prevents intracerebral haemorrhage growth, although the treatment was safe with no increase in thromboembolic complications. Because haemorrhage growth is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in acute intracerebral haemorrhage, new, safe, and effective treatments to stem ongoing haemorrhage are urgently required. Larger trials of tranexamic acid, with simpler recruitment methods and an earlier treatment window, are justified.
