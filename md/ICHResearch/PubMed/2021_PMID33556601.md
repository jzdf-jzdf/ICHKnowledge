# INTRODUCTION  

neurysms located on the middle cerebral artery (MCA) are considered by many surgeons to represent a distinct subgroup of intracranial aneurysms for which clipping may still be the best management option. Most MCA aneurysms are accessible, proximal control can readily be secured, and clip application can typically proceed without requiring the dissection of perforating arteries. In comparison, certain anatomic features of MCA aneurysms such as a wide neck, often including a branch artery origin, frequently render endovascular management more difficult.1,2 New endovascular devices were and continue to be introduced to address these anatomic difficulties, including stents,3 flow diverters,4 and intrasaccular flow disruptors (ISFDs). Thus, although most aneurysms are increasingly treated with endovascular methods, many patients with MCA aneurysms are still managed surgically, but convincing evidence of which management paradigm is best is lacking.  

ISAT (International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial) has shown that patients with good-grade, small, anterior circulation ruptured aneurysms (RAs) have better 1-year clinical outcomes after being coiled,6 a finding supported by BRAT (Barrow Ruptured Aneurysm Trial).7 However, there are many reasons to suspect that those results do not apply to aneurysms located at the MCA bifurcation. Only $\mathrm{I}4\%$ of aneurysms in ISAT were on the MCA, likely because lesions in this location were preferentially treated with surgery. Even after selection, the MCA subgroup results were similar for coiling and clipping (relative risk, 1.01 [0.71e 1.45]).8 In the all-inclusive BRAT trial, $2\mathtt{I}/3\mathtt{I}$ $(68\%)$ of MCA aneurysms assigned to coiling were crossed over to clipping.9  

For unruptured aneurysms, comparative case series suggest that clipping of MCA aneurysms may lead to better long-term anatomic outcomes10 but all available data are subject to the shortcomings of observational studies. The use of novel endovascular devices such as flow diverters and ISFDs is increasingly reported for unruptured MCA aneurysms but they have been associated with substantial perioperative morbidity or suboptimal long-term angiographic results.4,5  

Although patients and clinicians are drawn toward novel endovascular options, surgical clipping is “fighting an uphill battle with fewer and fewer troops.”11 More than expert opinion and case series may be required to justify open neurosurgical aneurysm repair in the future. There are ongoing comparative trials addressing the best treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) and  

RAs in all locations.12,13 These all-inclusive trials do not take into consideration the specific nature of MCA aneurysms and their treatment. Our goal is to present the MCA aneurysm results from CURES (Collaborative UnRuptured Endovascular vs. Surgery) and ISAT-2, not because they provide final answers to the clinical dilemma, but because they may serve to inform the conduct of future trials designed to offer optimal care to patients with MCA aneurysms.  



# METHODS  

This report was prepared in the spirit of the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) recommendations,14 but this pooled subgroup comparison is not a report of a stand-alone trial. The study protocols for both the CURES and the ISAT- $^{.2}$ trials (registered at Clinical trial.gov NCT01139892, NCT01668563) have been published, and interim results for each trial reported in 2017 and 2019, respectively.12,13,15,16 CURES and ISAT-2 are investigatorled, pragmatic randomized care trials conducted in North American and European centers. Data capture and management are through secure servers in compliance with Good Clinical Practice requirements. Protocols are approved by local institutional review boards of each participating institution and all patients (or representatives) provided written informed consent. Case report forms are simple, and required data collection made sparing to facilitate completion by normal care personnel. Screening logs are not required per protocol.  



# Patients  

All patients with MCA aneurysms included in CURES from inception in September 2010 to July 2020 and all patients with MCA aneurysms included in ISAT-2 from inception in November 2012 to July 2020 are reported. CURES includes independent (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score ${<}3{\mathrm{)}}$ , adult patients with an intradural unruptured aneurysm measuring $3^{-25~\mathrm{mm}}$ , with life expectancy ${>}\mathbf{\tau}0$ years. ISAT-2 includes any adult patient with an intradural aneurysm that has ruptured within the last 30 days, for whom the results of the initial $\mathrm{ISAT}^{6}$ are judged to not necessarily apply. Patients with grade 5 subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) are excluded. The patients with MCA aneurysms in CURES and ISAT-2 were enrolled at 3 Canadian, 1 American, and 3 European participating centers (Montréal, Edmonton, Ottawa, New York, Lille, Liège, and Barcelona).  



# Interventions  

Patients are treated with surgical clipping or endovascular techniques as per local practices, with technical details left to individual operators. The use of stents, flow diverters, and other novel endovascular devices or adjunctive methods is recorded.  



# Outcome Measures and Blinding  

The primary composite outcome measure of CURES is treatment failure, defined as 1) failure of intended modality to treat the aneurysm, 2) intracranial hemorrhage during follow-up, or 3) a residual aneurysm at 1 year. A crossover from one treatment modality to the other with no attempt to treat the aneurysm was not considered to reach the primary outcome of treatment failure. CURES also records clinical outcomes (mRS score) at discharge and 1 year, by treating physicians not blinded to treatment assignment (as part of routine care).  

The primary outcome of ISAT- $^{2}$ is death or dependency (mRS score ${>}2$ ) at 1 year. A secondary outcome, imaging results (using catheter, computed tomography [CT], or magnetic resonance angiography [MRA]) at 1 year was assessed according to a predefined scale : complete occlusion, residual neck, or residual aneurysm. Blinding to treatment assignment of patients, physicians, and outcome assessors was not possible.  



# Randomization  

For both studies, parallel-group randomization is 1:1, concealed, and generated by a secure platform (MedSciNet) available $24/7$ . Details of minimization algorithms are available.15,16 In May 2016, the protocols were modified at 1 center (Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal, Montréal) to enable prerandomization.18  



# Statistical Analyses  

Interim subgroup results from ongoing randomized trials are not usually reported. We restricted the use of statistical tests to a Fisher’s exact test of the primary outcome of patients with MCA aneurysms recruited in CURES. Comparisons of baseline characteristics were by visual inspection only. All reported proportions are provided with $95\%$ confidence intervals, using the Wilson score method to allow for small numbers.  



# Role of the Funding Source and Data Integrity  

CURES was funded by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (MOP 119554); ISAT-2 has not received any funding. Both studies are sponsored by Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal, Montréal. Neither funding agency nor sponsor had any part in study design, data collection, analysis, or reporting. The full study protocols have been published.15,16

# Unruptured MCA Aneurysms  

There were 100 patients with unruptured MCA aneurysms, or $30\%$ of the 330 patients enrolled in CURES to date. Patient and aneurysm characteristics for the surgical and endovascular groups are presented in Table 1, and the patient flowchart in Figure 1. Data presented in Tables 2 and 3 are intent-to-treat unless otherwise specified; results of the as-treated analysis are available in Supplementary Table 1 (online).  

Ten patients with UIA have been enrolled but have not yet been treated (9 surgical and 1 endovascular).  

There were $5/90$ $\left(6\%\right)$ crossovers. Three patients with MCA UIA crossed over from endovascular to surgical management (all patient choice), and 2 crossed over from surgical to endovascular management (also patient choice). One surgically managed patient with an UIA $\left(\mathtt{I}/42;~2^{0}/6\right)$ required an extracranial-intracranial bypass. Endovascular UIA treatment consisted of simple coiling $\pm$ balloon assistance in $44/48\:\:(92\%)$ of patients, with adjunctive devices infrequently used (stents, ${\mathfrak{n}}={\mathfrak{7}}$ ; flow diverter, $n=\tt{I}$ ; or ISFD, $\mathfrak{n}=\mathbf{I}$ ).  

Length of hospitalization after clipping was longer than after endovascular treatment (median of 5.0 days after surgery and 1.0 day for endovascular, whereas new neurologic deficits after treatments were similar $\left(6/42\:\left(\mathrm{I}4^{\%}\right)5\%\right)5\%$ confidence interval [CI], $0.07^{-0.28)})$ ) for surgery and $8/48$ $[\mathrm{I}7^{0}/0$ ; $95\%$ CI, $0.09^{-0.30)}$ for endovascular, including 1 intraprocedural death) (Table 3).  

The number of surgically managed patients with unruptured MCA aneurysms who experienced the primary end point of treatment failure at 1 year is $3/42$ $(7\%$ ; $95\%$ CI, $0.02\substack{-0.19})$ compared with $13/48\:(27\%;95\%$ CI, $0.17^{-}0.4\mathrm{I}\big)$ for endovascular $\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{P}=0.025$ ; Fisher’s exact test) (Table 2). Safety results (discharge mRS score) are available for all treated patients, and 1-year mRS score results are available for 80/90 $(89\%)$ . At 1 year, surgical management of unruptured MCA aneurysms led to $_{0/42}$ $\left(0\%\right)$ ; $95\%$ CI, $_{0^{-}0.08)}$ and endovascular management to $\mathrm{I}/48\:\left(2.\mathrm{I}^{0}/0;\right.$ $95\%$ CI, $_{0-0.\mathrm{II}})$ dead or dependent (mRS score ${>}2$ ) patients (Table 3).  

One-year imaging results were available in $80/90~(89\%)$ treated patients with UIA (Table 3). For surgically managed patients, most of the 1-year imaging determinations $\left(34/40;85\%\right)$ were on the basis of CT angiography. Endovascularly managed patients had most of the 1-year imaging determinations made on the basis of MRA $(26/$ 40; $65\%$ ). Complete MCA aneurysm occlusion was found in $30/40$ patients allocated clipping $(75\%$ ; $95\%$ CI, $0.60\substack{-0.86})$ and $\mathrm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{I}4}/40$ patients with UIA allocated coiling $(35\%$ ; $95\%$ CI, $0.22\substack{-0.50})$ . # DISCUSSION  

Patients with MCA aneurysms randomly allocated to surgical clipping had similar clinical outcomes (number of patients with mRS score ${>}2$ ) but better treatment efficacy outcomes at 1 year than did patients allocated endovascular treatment. These findings require confirmation in a future trial dedicated to patients with MCA aneurysms.  

Our study differs from previous randomized evidence regarding ruptured MCA aneurysms. First, ISAT included disproportionally few selected patients with MCA aneurysms $\scriptstyle(30\mathrm{I}/2\mathrm{I}43$ or $14\%$ , compared with $38\%$ in ISAT-2) recruited between 1994 and 2004, a time when only simple coiling was available. Although ISAT showed better clinical outcomes for coiled aneurysms at 1 year, this overall trial result was not confirmed for MCA aneurysms. The clinical primary end point of mRS score ${>}2$ was reached in $39/\mathrm{I}39$ clipped patients $(28.\mathrm{{I}}^{0}/\mathrm{{0}}$ ; $95\%$ CI, $0.2\mathrm{I}^{-0.36)}$ and $46/\uptau62$ coiled patients $\left(28.4\%\right)$ ; $95\%$ CI, $_{0.22-0.36)}$ .8 However, subgroup findings that differ from overall trial results should always be interpreted with caution.20  

The suspicion that only selected patients with MCA aneurysms were judged eligible for endovascular treatment at the time of ISAT is supported by the prerandomized BRAT study: of 61 patients with ruptured MCA aneurysms included between 2003 and 2007, 30 were assigned clipping and 31 coiling. Of the 31 $(68\%)$ endovascular patients, 21 were crossed over to the surgical arm. Thus, it was reported that “direct comparison of the treatments was impossible.”9 The Finnish randomized controlled trial (RCT) on clipping versus coiling21,22 reported only 19 ruptured MCA aneurysms because 59 patients with MCA aneurysms were excluded. One RCT reported in 201223 included only 35 patients with ruptured MCA aneurysms and it did not provide angiographic results per aneurysm location. By contrast, there were few RA crossovers $(7\%)$ in the present study. If most MCA aneurysms can be treated endovascularly, clinical results of contemporary technical achievements remain to be properly compared with surgical clipping. In particular, although overall clinical results were similar at 1 year in ISAT-2, rebleedings and retreatments after endovascular treatment are worrisome. This randomized comparison of angiographic results for ruptured MCA aneurysms is the largest to date because angiographic follow-ups were not required in ISAT.  

ISAT-2 was designed to be, and can still be considered, a continuation of the original ISAT trial, with a superiority hypothesis in favor of endovascular treatment.16 The results presented here suggest that this hypothesis may not be appropriate for ruptured MCA aneurysms. Showing a result for a subgroup (the MCAs) that differs from the overall results at the end of ISAT-2 risks being scientifically problematic just as in the original ISAT. For this reason, a new trial dedicated to ruptured MCA aneurysms is needed.

With respect to unruptured MCA aneurysms, other than from CURES,12 there is no randomized evidence. An abundance of observational data exists from multiple case series.24-26 The most recent meta-analyses of case series of unruptured MCA aneurysms have shown superiority of clipping in terms of safety in one report, and superiority of coiling in another, but numbers remain small and uncertain.24,26 These meta-analyses reported complete occlusion rates of $95\%$ $(95\%$ CI, $0.90\substack{-0.98)}$ with clipping compared with $60\%$ $(95\%$ CI, $0.45^{-0.74)}$ with coiling.26 The high rates of complete occlusions in reported case series compared with the randomized evidence presented here are likely related to publication bias, although a recent nonrandomized comparison of clip-first and coil-first paradigms for MCA aneurysms reported complete occlusion rates of $86\%$ (69/80) for clipped aneurysms versus $27\%$ $\left(20/74\right)$ for coiled aneurysms at $6\mathrm{-}\mathbf{\tau}_{12}$ months follow-up.

Meta-analyses of the angiographic results of coiling of MCA aneurysms focus on “complete or near-complete occlusion” or “adequate occlusion” rates.28,29 The choice to assimilate complete occlusions with aneurysm necks comes from RA studies, because both results have been shown to prevent rebleeding. Complete occlusions may be more pertinent to the management of UIAs, because incomplete occlusions and long-term recurrences still affect many endovascular options while long-term protection from rupture remains the goal of therapy.  

All available evidence on unruptured MCA aneurysms is observational and all is affected by systemic biases. The present study is the only randomized comparison of endovascular and surgical treatment of unruptured MCA aneurysms and results are compatible with a hypothesis in favor of clipping, because treatment failures were more common after endovascular treatment.  

The current study brings the issue of the role of trials in clinical care to the fore. At the very least, to justify making treatment decisions in practice, we need to ensure that the alleged increased efficacy of any more invasive, potentially more risky or novel option has been proven in a rigorous RCT, a task that remains to be accomplished for surgical clipping as well as for most recent endovascular innovations.30-33 Some novel endovascular options may claim that they can lead to complete aneurysm occlusions more often than historical endovascular controls, but they have never been properly compared with the results of surgical clipping.5  

Patients with MCA aneurysms were frequently recruited in both CURES and ISAT-2, a sign of the ongoing clinical uncertainty regarding the proper management of these patients. CURES and ISAT-2 are care trials designed to guide practice in the presence of uncertainty.34 However, these trials have not been popular with clinicians and neurovascular centers and recruitment has been slow, perhaps as a consequence of their pragmatic design and all-inclusive nature. Patients with MCA aneurysms may need a specific trial to find out what is the best management of their condition. For clinicians, a benchmark level of surgical results obtained in patients with MCA aneurysms whose treatments have been randomly allocated is needed to establish the merits of clipping and serve as a reference for which other trials could aim.  

The problem is how to determine what constitutes optimal care, while simultaneously trying to provide optimal care. The solution is an RCT designed in the best interests of participating patients.35 Clinicians can then offer surgical clipping as optimal care in the presence of uncertainty through 1:1 randomized allocation of surgery versus any endovascular option. The additional merit of such a trial would be that it could help maintain the open neurosurgical skills necessary for safe neurosurgical management in a field now threatened by obsolescence, at the same time offering an opportunity to participate in the progress of endovascular therapy. The broad outlines of a potential new trial dedicated to MCA aneurysms is presented in Supplementary Table 3.  

Many surgeons believe clipping to be superior in terms of long-term angiographic results and it would be tempting to design the new study accordingly. However, there are multiple problems with using a surrogate angiographic outcome measure.36 One systematic difference between the 2 treatments concerns the use of follow-up imaging studies (CT angiography for clipped and MRA for coiled MCA aneurysms). The diagnostic accuracy of each method in detecting aneurysm residua differs.37,38 Requiring 1-year catheter angiography for all patients is unlikely to be clinically acceptable and is contrary to the pragmatic care trial philosophy.34 Moreover, it is uncertain whether “same-sized” postcoiling and postclipping residua, even on catheter angiography, have the same clinical significance. Our studies suggest more frequent complete occlusions with clipping. The crucial empirical question, for clipping as for any endovascular innovation, is how much more morbidity (if any) is justified to achieve better angiographic results, and whether angiographic results are predictive of better long-term outcomes.  

Limitations of this study include that it is an unpowered subgroup analysis of 2 ongoing trials; some patients have yet to be treated, and not all treated patients have 1-year follow-up results. The number of patients is small, and the imaging evaluations were not performed by a core laboratory. The choice to compare “complete aneurysm occlusion” rates is debatable. Thus the findings reported here qualify as exploratory. They should be interpreted with caution and be used only as hypotheses for future trials.39 Only a few $(\mathrm{ro}/48,~2\mathrm{r}^{0}/\mathrm{o})$ of the endovascularly managed cases in this work used adjunctive devices such as stents, flow diverters, or ISFDs. The hypothesis that more liberal use of novel endovascular adjuncts could decrease the incidence of treatment failure or improve angiographic results should be tested in randomized trials.  

We believe that our results should nevertheless encourage surgeons to participate in a new trial dedicated to aneurysms in this location. From the endovascular perspective, a variety of innovative minimally invasive solutions are increasingly tried for MCA aneurysms. These innovations should also be tested in a randomized trial.40 Patients recruited in such trials should be offered at least a chance to be treated surgically. We suggest that the benchmark to be used to compare endovascular innovations for MCA aneurysms should be surgical clipping.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

Randomized subgroup data from 2 trials show that better efficacy may be obtained with surgical management of patients with MCA aneurysm. This finding should be confirmed by randomized trials dedicated to MCA aneurysms.
