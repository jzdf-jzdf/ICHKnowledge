# Risk of intracranial hemorrhage with direct oral anticoagulants: a systematic review and meta‑analysis of randomized controlled trials  

Tingting $\mathsf{W}\mathbf{u}^{1,2}$ · Chenyang $\mathsf{L}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}^{1,2}$ · Lishui Wu3 · Wenjun Chen1,2 · Meina $\mathsf{L}\mathsf{v}^{1,2}$ · Shaojun Jiang1,2 · Jinhua Zhang1,2

# Abstract  

Objective  We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the risk of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) between direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and other antithrombotic drugs in detail across all diseases.  

Methods  PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched for relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Heterogeneity was examined using the $I^{2}$ statistic. Risk ratio (RR) and $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) were calculated using random-effects meta-analysis.  

Results  Fifty-five RCTs were included in this meta-analysis. Compared with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), dabigatran reduced the risk of ICH by $60\%$ (RR 0.40; $95\%$ CI 0.28–0.57), apixaban by $57\%$ (RR 0.43; $95\%$ CI 0.31–0.58), edoxaban by $56\%$ (RR 0.44; $95\%$ CI 0.29–0.67) and rivaroxaban by $41\%$ (RR 0.59; $95\%\mathrm{CI}0.44–0.80\$ ). Compared with low-molecularweight heparins (LMWHs), apixaban, edoxaban and rivaroxaban had a similar risk of ICH. Compared with aspirin, dabigatran and apixaban had a similar risk of ICH, while rivaroxaban posed an increased risk of ICH (RR 2.12; $95\%$ CI 1.31–3.44). For secondary prevention stroke, DOACs reduced the risk of ICH by $46\%$ compared with warfarin (RR 0.54; $95\%$ CI [0.42–0.70]) and had a similar risk of ICH compared with aspirin.  

Conclusion  All DOACs had a lower risk of ICH than VKAs. In terms of the risk of ICH, DOACs were overall as safe as LMWHs, and apixaban and dabigatran were as safe as aspirin, but rivaroxaban was not. For secondary prevention stroke, the risk of ICH with DOACs was overall lower than warfarin and similar to aspirin, but it should be noted that compared with aspirin, rivaroxaban may increase the risk of ICH. This is the first pair-wise meta-analysis that compares the risk of ICH between DOACs and other antithrombotic drugs in detail across all diseases, which may have certain significance for patients with high risk of ICH to choose antithrombotic drugs in clinical practice.  

Keywords  Direct oral anticoagulants $\cdot$ Antithrombotic drugs $^{\ast}$ Intracranial hemorrhage $\cdot$ Meta-analysis  

# Introduction  

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have a wide therapeutic window, predictable pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, no food interactions, few drug interactions and do not require routine coagulation detection [1–4]. The use of antithrombotic drugs is often associated with the risk of bleeding, and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a possible serious complication, leading to severe disability and high mortality [5–7]. For patients with high risk of ICH, clinicians are often faced with the problem of how to choose antithrombotic drugs. Therefore, the comparison of the risk of ICH between DOACs and other antithrombotic drugs has become a concern. Previous meta-analyses did not have a pair-wise comparison in detail between DOACs and other antithrombotic drugs [8, 9]. Furthermore, since the publication of previous meta-analyses, new randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been published and new diseases have been studied. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively compare the risk of ICH between DOACs and other antithrombotic drugs in detail across all diseases.  

# Methods  

This study was performed according to the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [10].  

# Data sources and database searches  

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and The Cochrane Library for relevant articles published in English prior to August 19, 2020. The search terms used were “dabigatran OR Pradaxa OR rivaroxaban OR Xarelto OR apixaban OR Eliquis OR edoxaban OR Savaysa OR non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants OR non-vitamin K antagonists OR NOACs OR NOAC OR direct oral anticoagulants OR DOACs OR DOAC OR novel oral anticoagulants OR new oral anticoagulants OR factor Xa inhibitors OR factor IIa inhibitors OR direct thrombin inhibitors” and “randomized controlled trials”. Three researchers independently conducted the literature search and screen.  

# Study selection  

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a randomized clinical trial study design; (2) comparison between DOACs (dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban or edoxaban) and control, including low-molecular-weight heparins (LMWHs), vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), or antiplatelet agents; (3) data of ICH reported in two groups; and (4) for trials comparing various doses of DOACs with control, only the regular dose arms were included in our study.  

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) DOACs in combination with other antithrombotic drugs; (2) relevant ICH data not obtained.  

# Data extraction and quality assessment  

Data were extracted independently by three researchers and included clinical conditions, number of patients, age, sex, follow-up duration, and the number of patients with ICH in each group. All included studies were assessed by three independent researchers using the Cochrane Collaboration risk of bias tool [11]. Any disagreements were resolved by means of a joint discussion.  

# Statistical analysis  

The Cochrane Collaboration’s Review Manager (RevMan 5.3) was used for this meta-analysis. Risk ratio (RR) and $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) were calculated using random-effects model. Publication bias was evaluated by inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s tests. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using the method of eliminating studies one by one. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified with the $I^{2}$ statistic and $P<0.1$ was considered statistically significant. We compared the risk of ICH between DOACs and control in detail, including individual DOACs versus VKAs, individual DOACs versus LMWHs and individual DOACs versus aspirin.  

Because neurologists and stroke physicians are particularly interested in secondary stroke prevention, we compared the risk of ICH between DOACs and control for it, and performed a subgroup analysis based on the difference of control.  

# Results  

# Study selection and study characteristics  

We initially identified 22,987 studies, 6124 of which were duplicate publications and were excluded. After screening and filtering, 55 studies were included (Fig. 1) [12–66], which covered a total of 184,839 patients.  

The characteristics of the 55 included RCTs are shown in Supplementary Table 1. For DOACs, 19 of the studies included rivaroxaban, 13 included dabigatran, 13 included apixaban, and 10 included edoxaban. For control, 29 of the studies included VKAs, 20 included LMWHs, and 6 included aspirin. The clinical conditions involved atrial fibrillation (AF); venous thromboembolism (VTE) treatment; VTE prophylaxis (including hip/knee replacement, medically ill patients, and lumbar spine surgery); a history of stroke; VTE with cancer; stable cardiovascular disease; cerebral venous thrombosis; acute non-neoplastic portal vein thrombosis; after thrombolysis of acute iliofemoral venous thrombosis; and hemodialysis with AF. The Cochrane risk of bias assessment result is shown in Supplementary Table 2.  

# Meta‑analysis  

Pooling the 55 included RCTs using the random-effects model showed that the overall risk of ICH with DOACs was lower than control (RR 0.62; $95\%$ CI 0.47–0.81; $P<0.001$ ; $I^{2}{=}47\%$ ; Fig. 2). Sensitivity analysis was performed by eliminating studies one by one and there was no significant change in the outcome.  

# Risk of intracranial hemorrhage (individual DOACs vs. VKAs)  

We compared the risk of ICH between individual DOACs and VKAs (Fig. 3). The results of the random-effects model show that, compared with VKAs, dabigatran was associated with a $60\%$ lower risk of ICH (RR 0.40; $95\%$ $\operatorname{CI}0.28–0.57$ ; $P{<}0.001$ ; $I^{2}{=}0;$ , apixaban was associated with a $57\%$ lower risk of ICH (RR 0.43, $95\%$ CI [0.31, 0.58], $P<0.001;I^{2}=0)$ , edoxaban was associated with a $56\%$ lower risk of ICH (RR 0.44, $95\%$ CI [0.29, 0.67], $P{<}0.001$ ; $I^{2}{=}11\%$ , and rivaroxaban was associated with a $41\%$ lower risk of ICH (RR 0.59, $95\%$ CI [0.44, 0.80], $P<0.001;I^{2}{=}0)$ . Sensitivity analysis was performed by eliminating studies one by one for apixaban, and found that apixaban compared with VKAs was associated with a similar risk of ICH (RR 0.45, $95\%$ CI [0.14, 1.47], $P{=}0.19;I^{2}{=}0)$ by removing the Granger trial [39]. Similarly, sensitivity analysis was performed for edoxaban by removing the Giugliano trial [36] and found that edoxaban had a similar risk of ICH compared with VKAs (RR 0.54, $95\%$ CI [0.07, 4.23], $P=0.56;I^{2}=47\%)$ . No significant changes in the outcomes were found in the sensitivity analyses for dabigatran and rivaroxaban.

# Risk of intracranial hemorrhage (individual DOACs vs. LMWHs)  

We compared the risk of ICH between individual DOACs and LMWHs (Fig. 4). The results of the random-effects model show that, compared with LMWHs, apixaban (RR 0.25, $95\%$ CI [0.05, 1.21], $P=0.08$ ; $I^{2}=0.$ ), edoxaban (RR 0.50, $95\%$ CI [0.09, 2.73], $P{=}0.42$ ) and rivaroxaban (RR 1.74, $95\%$ CI [0.28, 10.68], $P=0.55$ ; $I^{2}=0$ ) were associated with a similar risk of ICH. Although four studies were included to compare dabigatran with LMWHs, statistical analysis could not be performed because the incidence of ICH in all these studies was 0. Sensitivity analysis was performed by eliminating studies one by one and there were no significant changes in the outcomes.

# Risk of intracranial hemorrhage (individual DOACs vs. Aspirin)  

We compared the risk of ICH between individual DOACs and aspirin (Fig.  5). The results of the random-effects model show that, compared with aspirin, both apixaban (RR 0.84, $95\%$ CI [0.38, 1.87], $P=0.67$ ) and dabigatran (RR 0.97, $95\%$ CI [0.60, 1.58], $P=0.92$ ; $I^{2}{=}0\%$ ) were associated with a similar risk of ICH, while rivaroxaban was associated with a higher risk of ICH (RR 2.12, $95\%$ CI [1.31, 3.44], $P=0.002$ ; $I^{2}{=}8\%$ ). The corresponding result was not available due to the lack of studies comparing edoxaban with aspirin. Sensitivity analysis was performed by eliminating studies one by one and there were no significant changes in the outcomes.

# Discussion  

In this meta-analysis, 55 RCTs were included, with a total of 184,839 patients. To our knowledge, this is the first pairwise meta-analysis that compares the risk of ICH between  

DOACs and other antithrombotic drugs in detail across all diseases.  

ICH, which is the most terrible complication of antithrombotic therapy, results in declining health [71, 72] and high mortality and disability rate [5–7], hence a great concern of clinicians. The risk factors for ICH that have been reported include increasing age, concomitant antiplatelet drugs use, history of stroke or transient ischemic stroke, history of bleeding, reduced serum albumin, reduced platelet count below, race (Asia, Latin Americaor black), hypertension, etc. [73–77]. Hypertension is a major risk factor for ICH [78]. Good control of blood pressure is important for preventing most ICH. The results of this meta-analysis may assist clinican–patient who have an increased risk of ICH informed decision-making and potentially provide an evidence base for better individualized patient care.  

![](images/d674516b5cd03f9cdfc238ecc93abb86c3be23283126d7249fc5202beb85d98e.jpg)  
Fig. 5   Risk ratio of intracranial hemorrhage (individual DOACs vs. aspirin)  

Wolfe et al. [9] found that dabigatran reduced the risk of ICH by $70\%$ , edoxaban by $63\%$ , apixaban by $54\%$ , and rivaroxaban by $31\%$ compared with VKAs through the network meta-analysis (17 RCTs, including patients with AF, VTE and VTE prophylaxis). Another meta-analysis [79] found that dabigatran reduced the risk of ICH by $58\%$ , edoxaban by $56\%$ , apixaban by $55\%$ , and rivaroxaban by $40\%$ compared with VKAs (19 RCTs, including patients with AF and VTE). Touma et al. [80] only discussed the risk of ICH with apixaban and found that it was $58\%$ lower than VKAs. However, our meta-analysis found that compared with VKAs, the risk of ICH with dabigatran was reduced by $64\%$ , apixaban was reduced by $57\%$ , edoxaban was reduced by $56\%$ , and rivaroxaban was reduced by $41\%$ by pair-wise comparison. Compared with previous meta-analysis, our meta-analysis included 55 RCTs and new diseases, providing the lastest, more direct and extensive evidence.  

Our study found that apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban were associated with a similar risk of ICH as LMWHs. The previous meta-analysis [81] showed that the overall ICH risk with DOACs was similar to that of LMWHs, but did not compare the ICH risk of individual DOACs with LMWHs. Another network meta-analysis [9] showed that the results of apixaban, edoxaban and rivaroxaban compared with LMWHs were consistent with ours. Combined with the results of our study and previous meta-analysis, it is further confirmed that DOACs is as safe as LMWHs in terms of the risk of ICH.  

A previous meta-analysis [82] compared the risk of ICH between DOACs and aspirin. The study included all doses of rivaroxaban and did a subgroup analysis based on the dose. It showed that a once-daily dose of $15/20~\mathrm{mg}$ rivaroxaban was associated with an increased risk of ICH compared with aspirin, while smaller daily doses of rivaroxaban were not. In our opinion, for indications, the statistical results of unconventional doses may be of little significance for clinical guidance and may cause bias of results. Therefore, only the studies including regular doses were included in our study, and found that the risk of ICH with rivaroxaban was about twice that of aspirin. Moreover, the meta-analysis did not directly compare the risk of ICH between dabigatran and aspirin due to lack of relevant studies, and our study found that the risk of ICH with dabigatran was similar to that of aspirin.  

For patients with a history of stroke, are at high risk for thromboembolism [70, 83], and antithrombotic drugs are needed for stroke secondary prevention. However, the use of antithrombotic drugs is accompanied by the risk of bleeding, of which ICH is one of the most feared complications. Previous meta-analyses [84, 85] evaluated the safety and efficacy of DOACs versus VKAs in patients with AF and previous stroke or transient ischemic attack, and the results showed that the risk of ICH with DOACs was significantly lower than that of VKAs. Compared with them, our meta-analysis included a new study [34], and the results were consistent with them. However, no meta-analysis has been performed to compare the risk of ICH between DOACs and aspirin in secondary stroke prevention. For aspirin, sensitivity analysis by removing the Hart trial [41] found that $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ went from $71\%$ to 0. Although it did not lead to a change in the overall results of DOACs versus aspirin, the study showed that rivaroxaban had a higher risk of ICH than aspirin, which to some extent implies that we may need to pay more attention to the risk of ICH with rivaroxaban.

The mechanism for lower risk of ICH with DOACs compared to VKAs is not fully understood. One possible reason is that DOACs target only one clotting factor, while VKAs inhibit several clotting factors [86]. The risk of ICH with rivaroxaban was significantly higher than that of aspirin, while dabigatran and apixaban are similar to aspirin. In addition, all DOACs had a lower risk of ICH than VKAs, among which rivaroxaban decreased the least. The risk of ICH with rivaroxaban among DOACs seems to be relatively higher, which may be related to drug interactions. Dabigatran is a substrate of p-glycoprotein (P-gp) [87], but rivaroxaban, apixaban and edoxaban are all substrates of CYP450 and P-gp [87]. In the meanwhile, rivaroxaban is also a substrate of breast cancer-resistance protein (BRCP) [87]. The drug interaction of rivaroxaban may be more complicated, which affects its pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. DOACs can be absorbed rapidly and has a short half-life [87]. Heidbuchel and Vrijens [88] found that under the same total daily dose, the peak-to-trough variability is larger for once-daily dosing than twice-daily dosing, which could be related to increased risks of bleeding or thrombotic events. Both dabigatran and apixaban are used twice daily, while rivaroxaban is mainly used once daily. Therefore, in practice, once-daily dosing for rivaroxaban may require more vigilance for single missed or extra doses and thus closer management of patient adherence. Taking into account rivaroxaban’s once-daily dosing regimen and drug interactions, rivaroxaban may have a higher ICH risk than aspirin, whereas dabigatran and apixaban show a similar risk. The mechanism for risk of ICH with DOACs remains to be further studied.  

The interaction mechanism over P-gp or CYP3A4 may affect the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of DOACs. In general, DOACs use is not recommended in combination with strong inhibitors of CYP3A4 or P-gp (such as Itraconazole, ketoconazole, etc.) [89]. Grapefruit is also a potent inhibitor of CYP3A4 and P-gp [90], so it should be avoided with DOACs. Conversely, strong inducers of P-gp and/or CYP3A4 (such as rifampicin, carbamazepine, etc.) will markedly reduce DOACs plasma levels, such combinations should be avoided or used with great caution and surveillance [89]. Dabigatran has the greatest renal clearance and the lowest bioavailability of the DOACs, so it is not suitable for patients with impaired renal function, and it is subject to huge changes in blood levels with changes in absorption, or with drug interactions. The principle of large interactions of drugs with low bioavailability is illustrated by statins [90, 91]. So strong P-gp inhibitors or inducers should be avoided with dabigatran.  

Our study has the following advantages: first, it included a large number of RCTs and a large sample size; second, our study included all different kinds of diseases, which was from an overall view of the ICH risk between DOACs and other antithrombotic drugs. So it may have certain significance for the patients who had multiple diseases at the same time in clinical practice; third, the previous pair-wise meta-analyses only compared the overall DOACs with each control (DOACs vs. VKAs, DOACs vs. LMWHs, DOACs vs. Aspirin) [8, 9], compared each DOACs with the overall control (rivaroxaban vs. controls, dabigatran vs. controls, apixaban vs. controls, edoxaban vs. controls) [8]. To our knowledge, our study is the first pair-wise meta-analysis that compares the risk of ICH between DOACs and other antithrombotic drugs in detail across all diseases. Finally, the results of our study comparing DOACs with aspirin were different from the previous meta-analysis [82]. On the one hand, we only included regular doses, which may be more meaningful for clinical practice. On the other hand, we newly found that dabigatran was associated with a similar risk of ICH compared with aspirin.  

This study has certain limitations. To begin with, the Granger trial [39] was removed for sensitivity analysis when apixaban was compared with VKAs, the result changed. The Giugliano trial [36] was removed for sensitivity analysis when edoxaban was compared with VKAs, the result also changed. The one reason may be that these two trials had a large sample size and carried more weight. Another reason may be that the follow-up time of these two trials was longer than other studies, so it may also have an impact on the incidence of ICH. But these two trials were low-risk and highquality studies, so we did not remove them. The results need more high-quality studies for further verification. In addition, because the incidence of ICH in the four studies that comparing dabigatran with LMWHs was 0, the statistical analysis was not available. Finally, due to the lack of studies comparing edoxaban with aspirin, we did not compare the risk of ICH between edoxaban and aspirin, and further research is needed.  

# Conclusion  

All DOACs were associated with a lower risk of ICH compared with VKAs. In terms of the risk of ICH, DOACs were overall as safe as LMWHs, and apixaban and dabigatran were as safe as aspirin, but rivaroxaban was not. For secondary prevention stroke, the risk of ICH with DOACs was overall lower than warfarin and similar to aspirin, but it should be noted that compared with aspirin, rivaroxaban may increase the risk of ICH. Our study may have certain significance for patients with high risk of ICH to choose antithrombotic drugs in clinical practice.
