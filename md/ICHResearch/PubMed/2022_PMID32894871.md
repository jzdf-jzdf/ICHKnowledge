# Umbilical Cord Milking in Extremely Preterm Infants: A Randomized Controlled Trial Comparing Cord Milking with Immediate Cord Clamping

# Abstract  

Objective This study aimed to assess potential benefits of umbilical cord milking (UCM) when compared with immediate cord clamping (ICC) in extremely preterm infants.  

Study Design This is a single-center, randomized controlled trial of infants $24^{0/7}$ to $27^{6/7}$ weeks’ gestation who received UCM versus ICC. In the experimental group, $18\mathrm{cm}$ of the umbilical cord was milked three times. The primary aim was to assess the initial hemoglobin and to assess the number of blood transfusions received in the first 28 days after birth. Secondary outcomes were also assessed, including intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH). A priori, neurodevelopmental follow-up was planned at 15 to 18 months corrected gestational age (CGA).  

Results Baseline characteristics for 56 enrolled infants were similar in both groups with a mean gestational age of $26.1\pm1.2$ weeks and a mean birth weight of $815\pm2049$ There were no differences in the mean initial hemoglobin in the UCM group when compared with the ICC group, $13.7\pm2.0$ and $13.8\pm2.6{\:}\mathrm{g/dL}$ , respectively $(p=0.95)$ , with no differences in median number of blood transfusions after birth between the ICC group and the UCM group, 2 (interquartile range [IQR]: 1–4) versus 2.5 (IQR: 1–5) $(p=0.40)$ . There was also no difference in the rate of severe IVH. At 15 to 18 months CGA, there were no differences in death or disability in the ICC group compared with the UCM group $(26{\ttvs.}22\%;p=1.0)\$ and no differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes.

# Key Points  

• Umbilical cord milking may be an alternative to delayed cord clamping, but its safety and efficacy are not established in extremely premature infants.   
• There are minimal available published data on the longer term neurodevelopmental outcomes in extremely premature infants who receive umbilical cord milking compared with immediate clamping.   
• We did not find a significant difference in the primary outcomes of initial hemoglobin and blood transfusions between the groups, nor did we find a difference in severe IVH with umbilical cord milking.

Placental transfusion strategies such as delaying the clamping of the umbilical cord after birth are recommended by both the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology (ACOG).1,2 This follows a growing body of evidence demonstrating benefits to both preterm and term infants.3 Preterm infants, in particular, seem to benefit from placental transfusion by stabilizing hemodynamic parameters of transition, including increased superior vena cava (SVC) blood flow, improved right ventricular output, increased blood pressures, and less need for pressor medication support in 24 hours following delivery.4,5 These hemodynamic factors are associated with decreased intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), and other complications of prematurity.  

The current ACOG guideline (endorsed by the AAP) recommends a 30- to 60-second delay in cord clamping in vigorous term and preterm infants.1 For infants not vigorous, there is no formal recommendation. Initiating neonatal resuscitation on an intact umbilical cord may be an option to support nonvigorous infants, but its utility remains under investigation.6 Umbilical cord milking (UCM) or stripping has been proposed as an alternative placental transfusion method to delayed cord clamping (DCC). A meta-analysis of several small studies suggests a reduced oxygen requirement at 36 weeks corrected gestational age (CGA) in 277 infants ${<}33$ weeks, along with a decrease in IVH with no difference in death, but there was no subanalysis of extremely premature infants.8  

Despite early promise of UCM, its safety and efficacy are not well established, particularly for preterm infants at the highest risk of complications of prematurity. Because of insufficient evidence, the practice is not currently recommended for routine use outside of randomized controlled trials (RCTs).9 A recent study in sheep found that cord milking did not result in the expected placental transfusion to the lamb, and found significant hemodynamic disruption.10 Additionally, there are minimal longer term neurodevelopmental follow-up data on infants receiving a milking intervention in the published literature. Because of these factors, further study of UCM in premature newborns is needed.  

We compared UCM with immediate cord clamping (ICC) in a randomized trial. We hypothesized that infants receiving UCM would have higher early hemoglobin levels and a reduction in blood transfusions when compared with infants receiving ICC. A priori, we planned an analysis of neurodevelopmental outcomes up to 18 months CGA.  

Women were identified antenatally on hospital admission and eligible mothers were approached for written consent. A consenting team was available on a 24-hour basis. Eligible ages were $24^{0/7}$ to $27^{6/7}$ week’s gestation. Only singleton pregnancies were included. Multiple gestations, infants with known congenital anomalies, hydrops fetalis, hemolytic diseases, or infants not receiving active resuscitation were excluded.  

# Randomization  

Block randomization was done by computer algorithm, and a sequentially numbered, opaque envelope was opened just before each delivery which indicated study arm (UCM or ICC). The arm was then communicated to the obstetric team right at the time of delivery. The neonatal teamwas not blinded to the intervention but did not record the study arm in the electronic medical record and the arm was not communicated to the providers outside of the delivery room. Most of the infants were transferred to SSM Health Cardinal Glennon Children’s Hospital, a nearby quaternary free-standing children’s hospital with a level IV neonatal intensive care unit.  

# Study Intervention  

To minimize variation, the study intervention (UCM) was done only by a limited number of obstetric providers, third and fourth years obstetric resident physicians, who all received one-on-one training. UCM was performed by holding the infant at the introitus for vaginal deliveries or laying the infant on the maternal pelvis just caudal to the incision site for cesarean deliveries. Cord milking was performed by milking $18\mathrm{cm}$ of cord beginning at the placental end continuing toward the infant three times. This cord length was chosen as it is the length of the 18-cm ring forceps instrument on the sterile field, ensuring a consistent reference for providers. The cord was milked over 2 seconds, then released completely to allow the cord to refill over 1 to 2 seconds’ duration between each milking motion (total milking duration ${\sim}10$ seconds). The cord was then clamped and cut, and standard neonatal stabilization was conducted per Neonatal Resuscitation Program guidelines. Those randomized to the control arm (ICC) had cord clamping immediately after delivery, and stabilization per routine. At the time of the enrollment period, DCC was not practiced in this delivery center.  

Blood was obtained within 2 hours after birth from an umbilical catheter for a complete blood count per the usual routine. Data were abstracted from the electronic medical record.  

# Materials and Methods  

This is a single-center, randomized controlled study conducted at a tertiary perinatal center (SSM Health St. Mary’s Hospital – St. Louis) comparing UCM with ICC, which was the hospital’s standard practice at the time of the enrollment period. This study was approved for human subjects research by the Saint Louis University Institutional Review Board and was also registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT 1666847). Infants were enrolled from October 2012 to May 2015, and were followed up through 18 months CGA through 2017 for neurodevelopmental assessments.  

# Study Outcomes  

The primary outcomes were initial hemoglobin and hematocrit from an umbilical catheter obtained within 2 hours of birth, and the absolute number of blood transfusions the infant received within the first 28 days after birth. Guidelines for transfusion were in place per previously published guidelines.7 Infants were begun on parental nutrition immediately after birth with early provision of human milk (maternal or donor), typically within 24 to 48 hours after delivery. Enteral iron sulfate at $4\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{kg}$ was given when full enteral feeds were achieved. For secondary outcomes, the total volume of blood transfused in mL/kg was recorded. The maximum total bilirubin in the first 7 days after birth, and the number of days of phototherapy in the first 14 days were also recorded. Mean blood pressures were recorded frequently for the first 24 hours by blood pressure cuff. Any intervention for hypotension was recorded for the first 72 hours, including use of a fluid bolus, use of vasopressors/inotropes, or use of hydrocortisone for blood pressure control.  

Common complications of extreme prematurity were examined. The diagnosis of patent ductus arteriosus was determined by Doppler echocardiography. IVH was assessed by a head ultrasound obtained at 7 to 10 days and graded according to the Papile system by board-certified pediatric radiologists who were blinded to the study arm intervention.11 Follow-up ultrasounds were also obtained at 36 weeks CGA or prior to discharge. Severe retinopathy of prematurity was defined as stage 3 or greater as determined by a boardcertified pediatric ophthalmologist.  

After discharge, all infants were scheduled for follow-up visits at 15 to 18 months CGA. A Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (BSID), third edition assessment was administered by trained psychologists. Additional data about markers of neurodevelopment were collected including unilateral or bilateral hearing loss, vision loss, or cerebral palsy. If a BSID was not able to be done due to severe disability, we assumed scores of 69 (less than 2 standard deviations lower than that of the mean). Neurodevelopmental disability was defined as one or more of the following: BSID cognitive ${<}70$ , bilateral vision loss, hearing loss requiring amplification, or moderate/severe cerebral palsy (Gross Motor Function Classification System $\geq2$ ).  

# Statistical Analysis  

Expecting a combined outcome of a $15\%$ increase in hemoglobin and a $75\%$ reduction in blood transfusions, a power analysis suggested a sample size of 64 infants. This analysis was based on prior work by Hosono et al.  

Differences in demographic, delivery room characteristics, and neonatal outcomes between infants receiving ICC and UCM were assessed using chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Independent Student’s t-test and the nonparametric Mann–Whitney’s $U$ test were used for continuous variables depending on distribution normality. A p-value of $_{<0.05}$ was used for statistical significance. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 for Windows.  

# Results  

Study eligibility and recruitment are shown in ►Fig. 1 (consort diagram). There were 203 pregnancies assessed for eligibility, of which 121 did not meet study criteria. Fiftysix infants, 27 in the ICC group and 29 in the UCM group, were enrolled. Three randomized subjects were excluded from the analysis (one was not viable and died in the delivery room, one was transferred to another hospital making data not available, and one did not receive the allocated intervention due to a miscommunication with the obstetrics provider in the adjoining operating room). Enrollment was stopped prior to reaching our target due to a hospital practice change in favor of DCC. From the original study groups, 54 infants were assessed for long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes. Nine infants died before their 18-month evaluation (three in the ICC group and six in the UCM group). We had a total of 29 infants who completed a full neurodevelopmental evaluation at 15 to 18 months CGA (17 in ICC group and 12 in UCM group). An additional 16 infants (7 in the ICC group and 9 in the UCM group) did not have a full neurodevelopmental assessment at 15 to 18 months CGA, but we were able to determine death or disability based on primary care or other clinical visits with data from the electronic medical record.  

The maternal demographic and clinical characteristics did not differ between the two groups as shown in ►Table 1 with $>95\%$ of mothers receiving antenatal steroids. Preterm labor was the most common reason for delivery for both the ICC and UCM groups at 41 versus $45\%$ $\overset{\cdot}{p}=0.42\$ . Additionally, there was no statistical difference in the infants’ clinical characteristics with a mean gestational age (GA) of 26.1 in both groups, and birth weights of $818\pm181$ and $813\pm225\mathrm{g}$ in the ICC versus UCM groups, respectively $(p=0.93)\$ .  

The study’s primary and secondary outcomes are shown in ►Table 2. For the primary outcome, there was no difference in the initial hemoglobin between the two groups ${\phantom{-}}13.7\pm2.0\:\mathrm{g/dL}$ ICC group vs. $13.8\pm2.6\:\mathrm{g/dL}$ UCM group, $p=0.95\$ . The median number of blood transfusions given in the first 28 days was also not different between the two groups, 2 (interquartile range [IQR] 1–4) vs. 2.5 (IQR: 1–5), $p=0.40$ . Moreover, the highest bilirubin level reached in the first week of life and the number of phototherapy days in the first 2 weeks of life were not different. There were no differences in mean blood pressures or urine output in the first 24 hours when measured at 6-hour intervals. The use of any blood pressure intervention within 72 hours after birth including fluid boluses, hydrocortisone, or any vasopressor/ inotrope did not differ between the groups, $67\%$ in the ICC group and $62\%$ in the UCM group $(p=0.78)$ .  

►Table 3 demonstrates inpatient outcomes at 36 weeks CGA. Mortality was not different between the two groups ( $7\%$ ICC vs. $14\%$ UCM, $p=0.67$ ). There was no difference between the two groups in the incidence of severe IVH ( $11\%$ in ICC group vs. $7\%$ in UCM, $p{=}0.74\$ ). Although there was a higher incidence of chronic lung disease (CLD) in the UCM group compared with the ICC group, the difference was not statistically significant $(p=0.06)$ . Other complications of prematurity did not differ between the two groups. When initial hemoglobin, severe IVH, or death by 36 weeks was analyzed by mode of delivery, there were no differences between ICC and UCM for vaginal or cesarean delivery (►Supplementary Table S1, available in the online version).  

Longer term neurodevelopmental outcomes were also assessed at a follow-up visit at 15 to 18 months CGA (►Table 4); nine infants died by 18 months CGA (three in the ICC group and six in the UCM group, $p=0.5$ ). BSID scores were available on 29 subjects (17 in ICC group and 12 in UCM group) for analysis. # Discussion  

In this RCTof extremely preterm infants less than 28 weeks GA, we found no difference in UCM versus ICC in the short-term primary outcomes of early hemoglobin levels or number of blood transfusions. We also found no differences in common complications of prematurity through 36 weeks CGA, including death or IVH of any grade. Finally, we found no difference in neurodevelopmental outcomes at 15 to 18 months CGA, although this study was not powered for this outcome.

Few other studies have specifically examined this high-risk, premature cohort with ICC compared with UCM. Hosono et al reported a reduction in blood transfusions from 4 to 1.7 $(p=0.02)$ , and an increase in hemoglobin from 14 to $16\mathrm{g/dL}$ $(p<0.01)$ ) in 20 infants randomized to receive UCM compared with ICC.7 This finding was supported by March et al, showing $14\%$ fewer blood transfusions in 56 infants, but this result was not statistically significant.12 Both of these studies included only infants ${<}29$ weeks. Other RCTs have also shown improvements in hemoglobin, urine output, IVH, but these studies werelimited by the inclusion of neonates whowere ${>}28$ weeks and are thus at less risk of adverse events.13–15 In addition to these outcomes, Katheria et al recorded increased hemodynamic factors of increased SVC blood flow in 30 infants with UCM.16 In these studies that used ICC as the comparison group, no evidence of harm was reported.  

Our study in contrast to other studies directly comparing ICC with UCM did not find a difference in any of our primary and secondary outcomes, including initial hemoglobin, blood transfusion, severe IVH, or death between our two study groups. The reason for this is likely multifactorial. One reason might be the milking technique. Other reported techniques include using $20\mathrm{cm}$ of cord length at speeds of 5 to $10\mathrm{cm}/\mathsf{s}$ .5,7,14,17 We used an 18-cm instrument as a gauge while limiting providers doing the procedure to ensure consistency of the milking intervention. It is possible that the length of cord milked in our study was insufficient, or that the cord did not adequately refill between milking attempts. It is also plausible that the milking action pushes blood toward the infant, but when the cord is released during the “refilling” period between milking motions, blood might backflow toward the placenta. This could result in limited net transfer of blood to the infant’s circulation. Others have described a technique of milking the cord only once, and evacuating the blood in the cord entirely from the placenta to the infant before cutting.18 This may correspond to an estimated $15.5\mathrm{mL}/\mathrm{kg}$ when milking a mean cord length of $30\mathrm{cm}$ for infants ${<}28$ weeks’ gestation.  

Our findings are consistent with a recent study on preterm lambs which demonstrated fluctuation of mean arterial pressures and cerebral blood flows without positive net transfer of blood to the preterm lamb, failing to provide placental transfusion.10 This is also consistent with a 2018 study of 73 infants examining ICC compared with UCM. It showed no difference in SVC flow at 4 to 6 hours after birth, although the initial hemoglobin was higher in the UCM arm.19 Our study similarly did not find a difference in hemodynamic parameters such as need for blood pressure support, blood pressure, and urine output in the first 24 hours after birth. None of the published cord milking studies has evaluated ventilation prior to cord clamping. This is important, as lamb models suggest that breathing with an intact umbilical cord (due to expansion of the lungs and subsequent decreased pulmonary pressures) may increase the efficacy of placental transfusion.21 UCM without the benefit of lung expansion may restrict the blood volumes infused by milking the cord. Because of these factors, milking may not provide sufficient placental transfusion or may cause fluctuations in hemodynamics which may not be beneficial to the preterm infant. While the generalizability of our findings is limited by a small sample size within single center, our trial is consistent with these data showing a potential lack of efficacy of UCM in this population.  

There is a larger body of evidence in this preterm population comparing DCC with UCM. A meta-analysis by Rabe et al found no differences in IVH, CLD, or death in preterm infants, but included infants up to $36^{6/7}$ weeks’ gestation. The largest study to date, which randomized 236 infants to UCM, found no differences in early hemoglobin, NEC, CLD, or death when compared with DCC. However, this study found a significant increase in grades 3 to 4 IVH in infants ${<}28$ weeks who received UCM compared with DCC (22 vs. $6\%$ ; $p=0.003,$ , which raises concerns about the safety of the UCM practice.22  

There are limited published data on neurodevelopmental outcomes in infants receiving UCM. Very few studies have focused on the neurodevelopmental outcomes of cord milking, and none has focused solely on infants ${<}28$ weeks. In our current study, we found no difference in neurodevelopmental outcomes at 15 to 18 months CGA. Rabe et al found no difference in composite BSID outcomes at 2 years for infants ${<}33$ weeks CGA who received UCM compared with DCC.23 Katheria et al in 2018 also found no difference in neurodevelopmental impairment at 22 to 26 months CGA comparing, but did find an improvement in cognitive and motor function. A key difference is that this study compared UCM with DCC and included infants up to 32 weeks’ gestation.24 To our knowledge, our study is the only clinical trial presenting data on neurodevelopmental outcomes for infants ${<}28$ weeks’ GA who received cord milking compared with ICC. The interpretation of our neurodevelopmental findings is limited by the small sample size and fairly high rate of infants not receiving a BSID examination.  

The strengths of this study include the fact that this is a RCT of the highest risk population of extremely preterm infants less than 28 weeks’ gestation. Consenting and enrollment were available around the clock. Randomization was done at the time of delivery, reducing bias. This is one of few studies to examine long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes of placental transfusion, especially UCM directly compared with ICC. Limitations of this study include a relatively small size. Although it was powered for our primary outcomes, a large effect size estimate (based on early studies of UCM) was chosen. Enrollment was stopped before reaching our targeted sample size of 64, due to a unit practice change to DCC. However, enrolling to the desired target would have been unlikely to change our primary outcome results. The study was not powered to assess a difference in neurodevelopmental outcomes, especially in light of an expected loss in follow-up, but published data for this population are very limited. A further limitation is that although some follow-up data were obtained in the clinic notes, this does not constitute a thorough neurodevelopmental assessment like the BSID. This could affect neurodevelopmental outcome results.  

# Conclusion  

UCM in infants ${<}28$ weeks’ gestation did not show an increase in early hemoglobin or decrease the number of blood transfusions. The lack of placental transfusion may be due to the cord milking technique or due to infant physiology. Our study also did not identify any safety concerns. Further investigation is required before changing practice to UCM from other placental transfusion strategies.
