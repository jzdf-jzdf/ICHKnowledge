Introduction: Tranexamic acid (TXA) administered within $^{2\textrm{h}}$ of injury reduces mortality in traumatic brain injury (TBI) with intracranial hemorrhage. TXA also reduces the seizure threshold in a dose-dependent manner. We examined whether a 2-g bolus of prehospital TXA administered in moderate or severe TBI is associated with seizure activity within 72 h of injury. Methods: Patients from the prehospital TXA for TBI trial with Glasgow Coma Scale $<13$ , blunt head injury, and time-of-seizure data were included in this analysis. The original trial randomized patients with suspected TBI to placebo, 1-g TXA bolus þ 1-g 8-h TXA infusion, or 2-g TXA bolus within $2\mathrm{~h~}$ of injury. In this secondary analysis, multivariable logistic regression was performed to examine the association of treatment group with seizure incidence. The model controlled for age, Glasgow Coma Scale, Injury Severity Score, intracranial hemorrhage, Abbreviated Injury Scale-head, and home antiseizure medication use.  

Results: Of the 786 patients who met the inclusion criteria, 19 had seizures within $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ (five in placebo, two in 1- $^{\cdotg}$ bolus/1- $^{\cdotg}$ infusion, and 12 in $_{2-\mathtt{g}}$ bolus). The 2-g TXA bolus was not associated with increased seizures compared to placebo (odds ratio 0.41, $95\%$ confidence interval 0.12-1.18, $\begin{array}{rlr}{{\bfP}}&{{}=}&{0.12\$ ). Home antiseizure medication use was associated with increased seizures (odds ratio 15.95, $95\%$ confidence interval 3.79-60.57, $P<0.001$ ).  

Conclusions: A prehospital 2-g TXA bolus in moderate or severe TBI was not associated with increased seizure activity during the first $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ after injury; however, limited power, limited use of continuous electroencephalography, and unavailable seizure prophylaxis data highlight the need for further study.

# Introduction  

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is the leading cause of death and disability due to trauma;1 however, few treatments for TBI have been found to be effective in reducing mortality.2,3 Tranexamic acid (TXA) is an antifibrinolytic medication that inhibits the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin.4 TXA has been shown in a recent trial to decrease mortality in patients with TBI with intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), though the exact mechanism remains unknown.3,5 While TXA is thought to primarily inhibit fibrinolysis and reduce secondary brain injury, alternative mechanisms including immunomodulation have been proposed.6 Though the mechanism is not fully understood, there have been concerns in multiple disciplines about the safety of administering TXA based on TXA’s ability to decrease the seizure threshold.7,8 Compounding this problem is the fact that the development of seizures is a common complication associated with TBI itself.9  

TXA is thought to disrupt inhibitory neurotransmission, potentially by acting as a competitive antagonist at the $\boldsymbol{\Upsilon}$ - Aminobutyric acid type A $\big(\mathtt{GABA}_{\mathrm{A,A,}}$ ) receptor, thus leading to a disinhibited and hyperexcitable state which has the potential to lower the seizure threshold.7 Additionally, patients with TBI are already at increased risk for developing posttraumatic seizures. Because the Prehospital TXA for TBI trial represents the first use of a 2- $^{\cdotg}$ TXA bolus in patients with TBI, we sought to examine the association between two different dosing regimens of TXA with the development of seizures in patients with moderate or severe TBI. We hypothesized that patients with moderate or severe TBI who received a $_{2-\mathrm{g}}$ TXA bolus within the $2\textrm{h}$ of injury would have a higher prevalence of seizure activity in the first $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ after injury.  

# Methods  

This is a retrospective secondary analysis of data collected during the prehospital TXA for TBI trial, which was a multiinstitutional, double-blinded, randomized phase II clinical trial performed across 20 centers and 39 emergency medical systems in the United States and Canada from May 2015 to November 2017. Patients with suspected TBI, a Glasgow Coma Scale $\left(\mathrm{GCS}\right)<13$ prior to administration of any sedatives and at least one reactive pupil, older than $15~\mathrm{{y}}$ , estimated time from injury to hospital arrival of $<2\mathrm{~h~}$ , and systolic blood pressure $>90$ were randomized to placebo, prehospital $_{1-\mathrm{g}}$ TXA bolus þ $1{\cdot}\mathsf{g}8{\cdot}\mathrm{h}$ in-hospital TXA infusion (1-g bolus/1-g infusion) or prehospital 2-g TXA bolus $+8\mathrm{-h}$ in-hospital placebo infusion (2-g bolus). TXA is not currently approved by the Food and Drug Administration for treatment of TBI, and its use in this trial was investigational. All treatments were initiated within $2\textrm{h}$ of injury [ClinicalTrials.gov(NCT01990768)].  

The trial was conducted under US regulations for Exception from Informed Consent Requirements for Emergency Research (21 CFR $\S50.24)$ and the Canadian Tri-Council Policy  

Statement 2 (Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans). All individual site ethics review boards and the Human Research Protection Office of the US Department of Defense reviewed and approved the local community consultation process, public disclosure plans, and conduct of the study. Because the trial was conducted under an Exception from Informed Consent Requirements for Emergency Research, informed consent was not required for participants to be enrolled or remain in this investigation. Patients who were able to provide consent, or their legally authorized representative (LAR), were consented as soon as feasible. Patients who were not able to provide their own consent and for whom an LAR or family member was not identifiable after multiple attempts remained in the trial under the Exception from Informed Consent Requirements for Emergency Research. All efforts to contact LAR or family members were documented and provided to each study site institutional review board for continued review.3  

For this secondary analysis, we included patients with a GCS $<13$ and available time-of-seizure data. Patients with penetrating head injuries were excluded (Fig.). The primary outcome was seizure within $72\mathrm{h}$ , which was defined as observed seizurelike activity combined with administration of antiseizure medication in the prehospital setting, or electroencephalogram (EEG) confirmation in-hospital within $72\mathrm{~h~}^{3}$ This secondary analysis was determined to be exempt by the institutional review board at the University of Chicago (IRB23-0169).  

Differences in demographic factors between treatment groups were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test for ordinal variables, t-test for continuous variables, and a $\mathbb{\chi}2$ test for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression models were performed to identify variables associated with seizure, controlling for treatment group, age, Injury Severity Score (ISS), Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) head, ICH, and home antiseizure medication use. For variables with missing values, imputation was performed using the median. Multivariable logistic regression models controlling for the same variables except for antiseizure medication were also created to examine associations between seizures and secondary outcomes of 28-d mortality, 6-mo Disability Rating Scale (DRS), and 6-mo Glasgow Outcome Scaledextended (GOSE). GOSE was dichotomized into unfavorable (one-four) and favorable (five-eight) and DRS was dichotomized into unfavorable (eight-30) and favorable (0-seven). In all analyses, a P value $\le0.05$ was used as the cutoff for significance. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, The R Foundation) with the packages tidyverse (version 2.0.0) and dplyr (version 1.1.2). The analyses were performed on July 28, 2023.  

# Results  

Of 966 patients in the parent trial’s analysis population, timeof-seizure data were unavailable for 150 patients and 30 of those with time-of-seizure data available had penetrating head injuries and were excluded. A total of 786 patients met the inclusion criteria of blunt mechanisms of injury, available time-of-seizure data, and $GCS<13$ . There were 10 missing ISS $(1.27\%)$ and seven missing AIS-head $(0.89\%)$ values which were imputed using their respective medians. No patient who experienced a seizure had missing values of AIS-head or ISS.

On univariable analysis, the median age of the $2{\cdot}\tt{g}$ bolus group $(42~\mathrm{{y}}$ , interquartile range 27-56) was higher than the placebo group $(35{\mathrm{y}}$ , interquartile range 24-51) $(\ensuremath{\mathsf{P}}=0.03)$ . There were 19 patients with a clinical seizure in the first $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ : five in placebo, two in 1-g bolus/1- $^{\cdotg}$ infusion, and 12 in the 2-g bolus group (Table 1). The number of seizures was higher in the $2{\cdot}\tt{g}$ bolus group compared to the 1-g bolus/1- $^{\cdotg}$ infusion group $(P_{\mathrm{~\scriptsize~=~}}0.05)$ . AIS-Head, GCS, ISS, ICH, and home antiseizure medications were similar among groups.  

In the multivariable logistic regression, 2-g TXA bolus was not associated with increased clinical seizure activity compared to placebo (OR 0.41, $95\%\operatorname{CI}0.12\ –1.18,P=0.12$ ). Home antiseizure medication use was associated with increased clinical seizure activity (OR 15.95, $95\%$ CI 3.79-60.57, $P<0.001$ ) (Table 2). Of the 28 patients taking antiseizure medications at home, four had seizures (placebo: 2/9, 1-g bolus/1-g infusion: 0/ 10, and 2- $^{\cdotg}$ bolus: 2/9). Seizure activity was not associated with any of the measured patient outcomes (28-d mortality $P=0.33$ ; GOSE $P=0.57$ ; DRS $\bar{P}=0.60\$ ) (Table 3e5).  

# Discussion  

This retrospective analysis of the prehospital TXA for TBI trial demonstrated that administration of a $_{2-\mathrm{g}}$ bolus of TXA administered to patients with moderate or severe TBI within 2 h of injury may not be associated with an increase in clinical seizure activity during the first $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ of hospital admission. Furthermore, in those patients that did develop seizures, there was no association with worse clinical outcomes.  

In patients with TBI, seizures have been associated with negative outcomes including hippocampal atrophy, coma, and encephalopathy.10,11 More than half of the seizures detected in patients with TBI may be nonconvulsive and only detectable by EEG.12 Studies of patients with TBI receiving continuous EEG have estimated that $16\%-22\%$ of patients experience seizures.10,12 However, among this cohort from the prehospital TXA for TBI trial, only $2.4\%$ experienced seizures. This is similar to the overall prevalence reported in the Clinical Randomization of an Antifibrinolytic in Significant Haemorrhage (CRASH) three trial $(2.8\%)$ , which also examined the use of TXA in patients with TBI.3,5 The consistency of seizure prevalence in these trials but relatively low percentage relative to other literature is likely due to the limited use of continuous EEG in the two trials. Understanding the etiology of these seizures and the potential influence TXA has in this patient population is critical for balancing the potential risk for adverse effects with the potential for reduced mortality.  

Animal models have elucidated possible mechanisms of TXA-associated seizures. TXA is an analog of $\mathtt{GABA_{A}}$ and is thought to displace GABAA at its receptor. This competitive antagonism of $\mathrm{GABA_{A}}$ and interactions with glycine receptors combined with persistent presence of TXA in cerebrospinal fluid are thought to reduce the seizure threshold in a dose-dependent manner.Theseeffects,however,haveonlybeenobservedat TXA concentrations higher than those detected in the human central nervous system, suggesting there are yet undiscovered targets of TXAinthe nervoussystem.7Furthermechanisticexaminationof TXA in patients with TBI (in which there is frequently violation of the blood-brain barrier) will be essential to better understand the association between TXA and seizure activity.  

The lack of association between TXA treatment and increased clinical seizure activity in our study aligns with previous reports related to the association between TXA and seizures. The CRASH-3 trial randomized 12,737 adult participants from 29 countries with moderate or severe TBI to receive either a 1-g TXA bolus (initiated within 3 h of injury) followed by a 1-g TXA 8-h infusion or placebo and found no difference in the incidence of seizures between treatment groups within $28{\textup{d}}$ .13 However, in contrast to the prehospital TXA for TBI trial, CRASH-3 did not include a $_{2-\mathrm{g}}$ TXA bolus arm. This is important as we observed a higher number of seizures in the 2-g bolus group compared to the 1-g bolus/1-g infusion group, which was the dosage used in CRASH-3 (12 seizures in $2{\cdot}\tt{g}$ bolus group, two seizures in 1-g bolus/1- $^{\cdotg}$ infusion). In this study seizures were not associated with worse clinical outcomes; however, the lack of association between seizures and clinical outcomes observed in this study is in contrast to several other studies that have included larger samples with EEG monitoring.9,11 Thus, the clinical significance remains unclear and warrants further study. Finally, we observed an association between home antiseizure medication use and the development of seizures in patients with moderate or severe TBI who received TXA, which has not been previously examined. The same proportion of patients taking home antiseizure medication in the placebo group and 2-g TXA bolus group experienced seizures, suggesting that in this subgroup there may not be a dose-dependent association of TXA with seizures.

This study has several strengths and limitations. First, this study included only seizures that occurred within the first $72\mathrm{h}$ after hospital admission. In contrast to previous analyses that include seizure occurrence within 28 d of injury, seizures that occurred within h to d of TXA administration in this analysis are more likely to be related to TXA administration. Additionally, this analysis included the use of home antiseizure medication, which addresses the potential confounding related to previous seizure history.  

The most important weakness of this study is its limited power, as the population size is not sufficient to detect small differences or compare this relatively rare event. To quantify the study size that would be required to examine this rare event, a power analysis was conducted to determine the number of subjects required per group to observe differences in seizure rate between patients in the placebo and 2- $_{8}$ TXA bolus groups. The calculation used the observed incidence of seizures in the placebo group in the CRASH-3 trial $(2.3\%)$ to avoid basing the power calculation entirely on data from the prehospital TXA for TBI trial. However, because CRASH-3 did not include a 2-g TXA bolus group, the observed incidence of seizures in the 2-g bolus group of the prehospital TXA for TBI trial $(4.1\%)$ was also used in the calculation as the other population proportion. This calculation suggests the need for 750 subjects per group. Basing the calculation on the observed incidences from only the prehospital TXA for TBI trial in the placebo group $(2.1\%)$ and the $2{\cdot}\tt{g}$ bolus group $(4.1\%)$ suggests that 589 subjects would be needed in each group. This further supports the limitation that the study is underpowered given that this analysis included only 249-294 subjects per group.

Another weakness is potential underreporting of seizure activity, which can be difficult to detect without continuous EEG monitoring. This limitation is supported by the discrepancy between the number of seizures detected in the trial population compared with studies using continuous EEG monitoring in TBI. Additionally, data were not available regarding which seizures were identified by clinical observation with antiseizure medication administration versus EEG confirmation. Another limitation is that selection bias may be introduced because time-of-seizure data were not available for all patients in the trial. Although there is potentially selection bias introduced by excluding patients with seizure who had unknown time of seizure, seizure beyond $72\mathrm{h}$ are unlikely to be related to TXA administration and thus we included only patients with a known time of seizure. Finally, due to limitations in data collection formatting, data related to in-hospital seizure prophylaxis and treatment were not included in this analysis, which should be addressed in future studies to account for variability in seizure risk and presentation.

# Conclusion  

Based on these limited data, a $_{2-\mathrm{g}}$ tranexamic acid bolus within $2\textrm{h}$ of injury in patients with moderate or severe traumatic brain injury may not be associated with increased seizures in the first $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ after injury. However, the limitations of the study and clinically apparent difference in the absolute number of seizures detected between groups highlight a need for further study using sensitive methods of seizure detection in larger populations to answer this question more fully.
