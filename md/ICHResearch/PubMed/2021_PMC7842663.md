# Why Are Women Less Represented in Intracerebral Hemorrhage Trials?  

Tatiana Greige, MD1, Casey Norton, BS1, Lydia D. Foster, MS2, Sharon D. Yeatts, PhD2, Andre Thornhill, MA2, Jessica Griffin, BS2, Jeffrey Wang, MD1, Courtney M Hrdlicka, MD1, Magdy Selim, MD, PhD1 on behalf of the iDEF Investigators+  

1Division of Stroke and Cerebrovascular Diseases, Department of Neurology, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, MA;  

2Data Coordination Unit, Department of Public Health Sciences, Medical University of South Carolina, Charleston, SC  

# Abstract  

Background and Purpose: Fewer women than men tend to be enrolled in clinical trials of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). It is unclear whether this reflects lower prevalence of ICH in women, selection bias or poor recruitment efforts. We undertook this study to examine differences between men and women in the reasons for exclusion from the intracerebral hemorrhage DEFeroxamine (iDEF) trial.  

Methods: The screen failure log included 29 different reasons for exclusion. Chi-square statistics were used to evaluate the differences in reasons for exclusion between men and women.  

Results: $38.2\%$ of participants in iDEF were women. 3982 women $(45.7\%)$ and 4736 men $(54.3\%)$ were screen failures $\mathrm{\bf\tilde{p}}{<}0.0001\$ ). Similar proportions of women $(1.28\%)$ and men $(1.73\%)$ were excluded due to inability to obtain consent $(\mathrm{p}{=}0.1)$ ). Patients or families declined participation in $1.26\%$ of women versus $1.31\%$ of men $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p}}=0.9,$ ). More women than men failed screening because of age $\mathord{\left\langle>}80\right.$ $22.40\%$ versus $12.61\%$ ; adjusted $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p=0.0007}}$ ) and pre-existing do-not-resuscitate/do-notintubate (DNR/DNI) $3.69\%$ versus $2.83\%$ ; adjusted $\mathtt{p=}0.067.$ ).  

Conclusions: Lower rates of women enrollment in the iDEF trial may be attributed to older age. Inability to obtain consent or declining participation was similar between women and men, arguing against selection bias. Our findings should be confirmed in other ICH trials in order to determine best strategies to improve women’s representation in future trials.

# Introduction  

The National Institutes of Health and the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke mandate adequate representation of women and minorities in clinical trials . Despite a higher burden of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in women , their enrollment in clinical trials of ICH remains low .  

Previous studies have suggested that the pathophysiology and outcome of ICH may vary based on sex. Female sex may be associated with worse functional recovery and higher mortality, independently of $\mathrm{age}^{2,5}$ . Given these sex-related differences in stroke outcome, it is crucial that trial enrollment parallels prevalence of the disease and that women and minorities are adequately represented in order to improve generalizability of trial results. Epidemiological studies report various women/men ICH incidence ratios, with several demonstrating no sex differences in overall ICH incidence3. It remains unclear whether the under-representation of women in ICH trials reflects a lower prevalence of ICH in women, selection bias, or poor recruitment2. We sought to investigate this by examining the reasons for exclusion of women, compared to men, from the iDEF trial.  

# Materials and Methods  

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The iDEF trial was a prospective, multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, phase 2 trial, that evaluated the use of deferoxamine mesylate versus placebo (saline) infusions in patients with ICH at 40 hospitals in Canada and the USA. The details of the trial, eligibility criteria, and final results have been previously published .  

Sites were required to maintain a screening log for all ICH patients found ineligible to participate in the study and were asked to report the patient’s age and sex, as well as the primary reason for exclusion from the study from a pre-defined list of 29 reasons (Table 1). If the primary reason for exclusion was age ${>}80$ years, sites were then required to further document other reasons for exclusion, if any. In this case, only age $>80$ was considered as the primary reason for exclusion for the purpose of this analysis (see Supplemental Table 1). Each site was required to enter the screening log data into the web-based trial-management system (WebDCU™) on a monthly basis throughout the enrollment period (11/23/2014– 11/10/2017).  

Ethics approval was obtained at each participating site. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or legally authorized representative according to local regulations.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Chi square $(\chi^{2})$ test was used to evaluate whether women and men differed with regard to reason for exclusion. To adjust for multiple testing, the false discovery rate was controlled at $5\%$ using the Benjamini and Hochberg linear step-up method; both the raw and p values are provided.  

# Results  

The trial enrolled 293 subjects; $38.2\%$ of whom were women. Out of the 9069 patients screened for enrollment, 8776 patients were identified as screen failures. 58 patients were excluded from the analysis due to missing data regarding sex. A total of 8718 patients were included in the analysis; 3982 $(45.7\%)$ were women (Figure 1).  

The enrollment rates were $2.7\%$ in women versus $3.7\%$ in men $(\mathrm{p}{=}0.01\$ ). Table 1 lists the reasons for screen failure stratified by sex. Women were more likely to be excluded than men because of age ${>}80$ years ( $22.4\%$ versus $12.61\%$ ; adjusted $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p=0.0007}}$ ). The mean age for women was $68.26{\pm}25.71$ years vs. $63.57{\scriptstyle\pm15.49}$ for men. On the other hand, men were more likely to be excluded because of mild ICH severity $\mathrm{(NIHSS~}<6\$ ), coagulopathy, inability to comply with the study protocol, serum creatinine $>2\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{dL}$ , drug/alcohol dependence, and evidence of aspiration at presentation.  

Inability to obtain consent was similar between men and women ( $1.73\%$ versus $1.28\%$ ; adjusted $\mathrm{p}{=}0.186,$ ). There were no differences between men and women in patient or family refusal to participate in the clinical trial ( $1.31\%$ versus $1.26\%$ ; adjusted $\mathrm{p}{=}0.830;$ ).  

# Discussion  

Our study demonstrates some sex-related differences in the reasons for exclusion from the iDEF trial. Women were more likely to be excluded because of older age. The reported median age of women at onset of ICH is higher than men5,7. In one study, women were on average six years older than men with a median age of $81^{7}$ . In other studies, the interquartile range for age was 65 to 83 years in women versus 59 to 80 years in men . Since women are presenting later in life with ICH, they are more likely to be excluded due to the age cutoff limit seen in most clinical trials. In iDEF, the age cutoff limit was 80 years old. Although older age at the time of stroke plays an important role in the poor functional outcome and mortality in women8, previous studies evaluating sex-related differences in ICH have reported conflicting results regarding the correlations between age and outcome after $\mathrm{ICH}^{5,8-11}$ ,.  

A numerically higher rate of women was excluded because of a pre-existing DNR/DNI orders or because of an indication that withdrawal of life-sustaining measures will be implemented within 72h of hospitalization. We hypothesize that the higher rate of DNR/DNI orders may be attributed to the older age of women in our study. Previous studies examining the use of DNR/DNI orders in an inpatient setting have also shown that women are more likely to receive DNR/DNI orders than men after admission to the hospital for various medical illnesses, including $\mathrm{{ICH}}^{5,12}$ . One study found that women with ICH were more  

likely to receive early DNR/DNI orders, independent of age and clinical severity12. Studies have suggested that women are more likely to express their wishes in advance regarding end-of-life decisions than men and ask for less aggressive, heroic resuscitative measures than their male counterparts12–13.  

Approximately, $45\%$ of screened patients were women. This is in line with previously reported national incidence rate of admission with ICH to hospitals in the United States . Women were not more likely to refuse to participate in the trial than men, providing some evidence that the under-representation of women in iDEF was not related to a poor recruitment effort. Our findings are similar to a previous study that showed that sex had no impact on the decision to participate in acute stroke clinical trials15. While the similarity in the inability to obtain consent between men and women might suggest lack of a selection bias, we urge caution interpreting this result.  

We found that men were more likely to be excluded because of mild ICH severity (NIHSS ${<}6,$ ) at presentation. A number of studies have evaluated sex difference in ICH severity and several reported that women have increased stroke severity compared to men as at admission7,16. Much, although not all, of the difference was explained by pre-morbid comorbidities and risk factors. In addition, gonadal steroids have been shown to have neuroprotective effects17, and it has been postulated that loss of gonadal hormones during menopause in women may be a factor9. The reasons for the observed sex differences in our screen failure cohort are difficult to ascertain because variables such as pre-morbid conditions, volume and location of ICH, were not collected as part of the screen failure questionnaire.  

Our study has limitations. We did not collect data regarding race and ethnicity of patients excluded from the trial. Historically, ethnic and racial minorities are underrepresented in neurological clinical trials as well. In addition, since incidence rates of ICH vary by race and are greater in Asians, Blacks and Hispanics than in Whites , ensuring adequate representation of women and racial minorities in ICH trials is essential to improve the generalizability of trial results. Our study was also limited to ICH population in North America. Further studies in ICH populations in other geographical locations are warranted. Lastly, the screen failure log from participating sites was not monitored against the discharge diagnoses list from each site. Therefore, the possibility of under-reporting and selection bias cannot be excluded. Indeed, some of our reported observations, such as rates of patients excluded because of coagulopathy, pre-morbid poor functional status, pre-existing DNR, and refusal to consent, were lower than what we anticipated . While these lower-than-anticipated rates could potentially be attributed to under-reporting, restricting sites to report one primary reason for exclusion, or geographical/temporal variations, our rates seem to be comparable to data from the Minimally Invasive Surgery with Thrombolysis in ICH Evacuation (MISTIE-III) trial18, which was temporally concordant with iDEF. This suggests that our observed rates may be a reflection of decreased prevalence of anticoagulation-related ICH with increasing utilization of direct oral anticoagulants in place of warfarin, better organization of trials’ conduct and consenting process, or improved patients/families attitudes towards participation in clinical research.  

Our findings unveil potential opportunities to improve women’s participation in ICH trials. Many trials in ICH limit enrollment to patients $\mathbf{\underline{{\otimes}}0}$ years of age, largely because of accumulating co-morbidities and increased likelihood of withdrawal-of-care after ICH. Our results indicate that this may inadvertently limits participation of otherwise eligible women in ICH trials. Substituting an upper age limit as an entry criterion with a pre-morbid functional threshold, such as modified Rankin Scale score and/or a co-morbidity index could potentially lead to increased participation of women in ICH trials. This is in line with the recent recommendations of the Hemorrhagic Stroke Academia Industry (HEADS) roundtable19. It is also apparent that future trials should ensure that their screen failure logs include data on race and ethnicity of excluded subjects to fully capture the representation of diverse populations.  

# Summary  

In conclusion, lower rates of women enrollment may be attributed to older age. The inability to obtain consent or declining participation was similar between men and women, which suggests the lack of a selection bias. Our findings should be confirmed in other ICH trials and in different geographical, ethnic, and racial cohorts in order to determine the best strategies to improve women’s representation in future trials.
