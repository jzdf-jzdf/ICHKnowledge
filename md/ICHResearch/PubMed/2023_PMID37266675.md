# Does dual antiplatelet therapy increase the risk of haematoma enlargement in the acute stage? A retrospective study of the use of stent‑assisted coiling versus coiling alone or balloon‑assisted coiling for the treatment of ruptured intracranial aneurysms combined with intracranial haematoma

# Abstract  

This study aims to identify the efficacy and safety of stent-assisted coiling (SAC) treatment of ruptured intracranial aneurysms (RIAs) combined with intracranial haematoma (ICH) compared to coiling alone or balloon-assisted coiling (non-SAC). A retrospective analysis of 54 consecutive patients receiving endovascular therapy from 2014 to 2020 was performed. The data collected included baseline characteristics, angiographic results, perioperative complications, immediate aneurysm occlusion, clinical outcomes, follow-up at discharge and after 6 months, hospitalisation costs, and inpatient length of stay. Patients were categorised into the SAC group and the non-SAC group. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify risk factors related to clinical outcomes. Of the 54 patients harbouring RIAs with ICH, 22 $(40.74\%)$ and 32 $(59.26\%)$ patients were subject to SAC and non-SAC treatments, respectively. Postoperative rebleeding ( $1[4.5\%]$ and 3 $[9.3\%]$ in SAC and non-SAC groups, respectively, $p{>}0.05$ ) and Hunt-Hess grade (IV-V) lesions ( $13.6\%$ vs. $40.6\%$ , $p{=}0.067.$ ) did not differ between the two groups. In total, 10 $(45.5\%)$ patients treated with SAC received a Fisher scale score of 0–3 compared with 6 $(18.8\%)$ patients treated with non-SAC methods $\langlep=0.035\rangle$ . Compared with the non-SAC group $(7/21.9\%)$ , the rate of wide-necked aneurysms was increased in the SAC group $(11/50\%)$ $(p=0.031)$ . No differences in poor outcomes $(\mathrm{mRS}>2)$ were noted between the SAC and non-SAC groups $(p>0.05)$ . Multivariate analysis revealed that ischaemic complication events $(p=0.016)$ represent the only independent risk factor for adverse outcomes, and a trend towards unfavourable clinical outcomes was noted for patients who smoke $(p=0.087)\$ . SAC is a safe and efficient treatment for RIAs combined with ICH when dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) is used in the perioperative period. In addition, SAC should be preferentially used in wide-neck RIAs. Ischaemic complications are a risk factor for poor clinical outcomes. Given the small sample size and retrospective bias of this study, these findings should be further verified in a study with a larger sample size or a randomised controlled trial (RCT).

# Introduction  

Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH) accompanied by intracranial haematoma (ICH) is a common clinical manifestation in ruptured intracranial aneurysms (RIAs), occurring in $10{-}38\%$ of SAH cases [1]. Previous studies showed that ICH exacerbated the prognosis with a mortality rate of 21 to $100\%$ ; therefore, it is important to provide aetiological treatments and haematoma removal to improve clinical outcomes [1-3].  

It is widely accepted that aneurysmal ICH is always associated with high morbidity and mortality [1, 4]. Based on mass effect, RIAs patients with ICH may experience higher intracranial pressure and poor clinical outcomes compared with patients with SAH alone [1, 4] with mortality rates ranging from 36 to $50\%$ according to the available literature [1].  

RIAs patients with ICH usually require emergency haematoma removal and aneurysm obliteration, especially patients with haematoma volumes greater than 30 millilitres (ml) or CT midline deviations greater than 1 cm (cm) [1, 2, 5-7]. Generally, simultaneous microsurgical treatment of RIAs and haematoma is proposed to improve outcomes for selected patients in emergency situations [3]. Given the development of endovascular technology, endovascular approaches have been used in the treatment of RIAs and are recognised as a feasible and safe option [2, 3, 5-7]. However, some cases of RIAs combined with ICH may undergo stentassisted coiling (SAC), and whether dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) increases the incidence of haematoma enlargement and worsens the prognosis remain unclear. A combination of endovascular and microsurgical approaches has been applied to RIAs patients with haematoma volumes greater than $30\mathrm{ml}$ to achieve immediate endovascular embolization of RIAs and further promote haematoma evacuation [3].  

According to clinical experience, most neurointervention physicians choose coiling alone or balloon-assisted coiling of RIAs combined with ICH to avoid haematoma enlargement. ICH often leads to acute cerebral infarction, hydrocephalus, epilepsy, and eventually multiorgan failure. Stent placement in RIAs patients with ICH requires sufficient and long-term DAPT [2, 5-8]. An insufficient antiplatelet regimen may enhance the incidence of thrombotic complications, whereas an excessive antiplatelet regimen may enhance the incidence of haematoma enlargement. [2, 5-8]. To date, evidence to verify the clinical efficacy and safety of SAC for patients with RIAs combined with ICH is lacking [1, 4]. Thus, we designed a single-centre study to assess the clinical efficacy and safety of SAC in the treatment of RIAs with ICH compared with coiling alone or balloon-assisted coiling (non-SAC).  



# Materials and methods  



# Patients  

All patients with informed consent were obtained. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. Of 141 RIAs patients who presented with ICH at the time of admission from 2014 to 2020, 79 $(56.0\%)$ were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) pseudoaneurysm or intracranial artery dissection at the time of admission; (2)  

other concurrent brain lesions, especially those contributing to the haematoma, such as arteriovenous malformations, cerebral thrombosis, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, arterial stenosis occlusion combined with thrombosis, or cerebral lesions; (3) haematoma volume greater than $30\mathrm{ml}$ or CT midline deviation greater than $1~\mathrm{cm}$ ; (4) complete absorption of spontaneous haematoma at admission; (5) incomplete information on baseline characteristics; and (6) no time or date of follow-up. The haematoma was identified based on CT or MRI angiography imaging examination. Haematoma volume was calculated using the formula ABC/2 excluding perihematomal oedema, where A represents the longest diameter of the haematoma measured by CT, B represents the diameter $90^{\circ}$ to A, and C is an approximation of the number of CT slices with bleeding multiplied by the slice thickness [9].  

According to the ABC/2 method, postoperative haematoma enlargement was defined as a greater than $33\%$ increase in the volume of the ICH over $^{24\textrm{h}}$ , considering that less than $33\%$ change in haematoma volume may reflect CT imaging variability rather than actual haematoma enlargement [10].  

Finally, 54 patients $(38.3\%)$ met the inclusion criteria. A flow chart of the patient selection process is shown (Fig. 1), and the baseline characteristics of enrolled patients with RIAs presenting with ICH are summarised in Table 1.  



# Clinical data evaluation  

Age, sex, clinical history, clinical presentation, the location of RIAs (anterior or posterior), aneurysm morphology (saccular and others), the size of the aneurysm (mm), the number of aneurysms (single or multiple), presence of a wide neck, operation time, external ventricular drainage (EVD), hospitalisation expenses, and inpatient length of stay were included in the analysis.  

The Glasgow coma scale (GCS), Hunt-Hess (H–H) grading system, and modified Fisher grading scale were used to evaluate patients in the perioperative period. The $\mathrm{H-H}$ grade was categorised as I-III (the patient had a mild headache or somnolence and mild neurological rigidity) and IV-V (stupor, moderate-to-severe hemiparesis, early diencephalic rigidity and dysfunction of the autonomic nervous system, deep coma, or agonal state). The GCS score was classified as 9–14 (mild disturbance of consciousness) and 3–8 (moderate-to-severe disturbance of consciousness, coma). Modified Fisher grades were classified as 0–2 (small amount of bleeding with superficial location) and 3–4 (large and thick amount of bleeding with deep location). A wide-necked aneurysm exhibited a neck diameter greater than $4\mathrm{mm}$ or a dome-to-neck ratio $<2$ , as measured by CTA or DSA.

# Endovascular procedures and antiplatelet regimen  

Endovascular procedures were performed by two senior interventional neuroradiologists with extensive expertise in aneurysm therapy. All patients with RIAs were treated by endovascular procedures within $^{24\textrm{h}}$ under general anaesthesia and systemic heparinization. Based on real-time protocols of DSA, microcatheters were superselected to enter the aneurysm or the distal parent artery. Heparinized saline was continuously flushed into the microcatheter. According to the location and morphology of RIAs, the two senior interventional neuroradiologists determined whether SAC was used. For patients with RIAs treated by SAC, $300~\mathrm{mg}$ compound aspirin ( $100~\mathrm{mg},$ /tablet) and $300~\mathrm{mg}$ clopidogrel ( $75~\mathrm{mg}\prime$ tablet) were orally administered before general anaesthesia. Disposable hydrochloride tirofiban $(6~\mathrm{ml})$ was injected intravenously at the beginning of stent placement and maintained for $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ . Then, clopidogrel $(75~\mathrm{mg/day)}$ was administered for at least two months postsurgery, and $100\mathrm{mg}$ aspirin was administered daily for at least 5 months. The patients were transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) promptly for $24-48\mathrm{~h~}$ of vital sign monitoring after the operation. A head CT scan was performed to exclude haemorrhage enlargement or other complications (Figs. 2 and 3).  



# Clinical outcomes and follow‑up index  

Each patient was followed from the time of diagnosis to the last visit by either telephone or outpatient visits. The mRS score and GOS were also used to evaluate clinical outcomes at discharge and 6 months. The mRS score and GOS score at the final follow-up were dichotomized as a favourable outcome $(\mathrm{MRS}\leq2$ or $\mathrm{GOS}\geq4.$ ) or poor outcome $(\mathbf{MRS}\geq3$ or ${\mathrm{GOS}}\leq3.$ ). Perioperative complications included postoperative rebleeding, haematoma expansion, and ischaemic complications.  

Radiological outcomes, including immediate aneurysm occlusion and follow-up angiographic results, were evaluated by CT, MRI, or DSA for all patients receiving endovascular treatment. Immediate aneurysm obliteration was classified as complete occlusion (Raymond I), residual neck (Raymond II), or residual sac (Raymond III), according to Raymond’s score results. The presence of a residual neck or sac was defined as incomplete occlusion. Follow-up angiographic results were assessed for complete occlusion, and patients underwent recanalization after 6 months.  



# Statistical analysis  

Categorical variables were assessed as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations (SD). Fisher’s exact test or the $\chi^{2}$ -test was performed for categorical data analysis, as appropriate. Student’s $t$ test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test was employed for continuous variable analysis. Baseline characteristics, such as age, sex, clinical presentation, aneurysm baseline characteristics, perioperative complications, initial angiographic obliteration results, and clinical outcomes, were included in univariate analyses. The independent association between potential risk indicators and the mRS score at the last follow-up was assessed using univariate logistic analysis. Factors with $p$ values less than 0.1 were selected for inclusion in the multivariate logistic regression model to evaluate independent predictors of poor mRS at the last follow-up. OR and $95\%$ CI values were calculated. $P<0.05$ indicated a statistically significant difference. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS V.22.0 (IBM SPSS; Armonk, New York, USA).

SAH may represent another cause of ischaemic complications. Kocur et al. [27] reported that acute SAH is an independent predictor of thromboembolic complications during the endovascular treatment of RIAs patients. Previous studies also identified a significant difference in the rates of ischaemic complication events between RIAs patients and non-RIAs patients [27, 28, 40]. This difference correlated with limited use and inadequate coverage of DAPT as well as vasospasm and antiplatelet resistance leading to hypercoagulability [27]. Blood compression from the subarachnoid space promotes the release of procoagulant tissue factors into the systemic circulation, leading to thrombin activation and subsequent aggregation of fibrin and platelets [27].  

A comprehensive review of the literature by Harrod et al. [41] concluded that the burden of SAH or large clots within the subarachnoid pool are factors that influence the occurrence, severity, and distribution of clinical vasospasm. In addition, several groups have identified that repeated SAH results in a more intense and rapid onset of vasospasm [27, 41].  

In all, DAPT does not increase the risk of haematoma expansion for RIAs patients with ICH treated by SAC and does not promote poor outcomes. Smoking history and ischaemic complications are independent risk factors for poor outcomes in RIAs patients. Multicentre trials with larger sample sizes are needed for verification.  



# Limitations  

This retrospective study shares the limitations of all retrospective investigations. The two senior neuroradiologists could have biased the selection of patients and the management modalities recommended for RIAs patients with ICH. Moreover, the sample size and follow-up duration are insufficient to draw conclusions based on comparisons of the SAC group and non-SAC group.  



# Conclusion  

SAC is safe and effective for the treatment of RIAs combined with ICH. Dual antiplatelet therapy does not increase the risk of haematoma expansion or postoperative rebleeding in the preoperative period. The use of SAC is preferred for wide-neck RIAs. Ischaemic complications are a risk factor for poor clinical outcomes. However, due to the small sample size and retrospective bias, these findings must be further verified in studies with a larger sample size or RCTs.
