# Novel application of latent class analysis to outcome assessment in traumatic brain injury with multiple injury subtypes or poly-TBI

Tianqi Xiao, BS,1 John J. Francis, BS,1 Douglas Gunzler, PhD,3,4 Mary Joan Roach, PhD,4,5   
and Michael L. Kelly, MD1  

Departments of 1Neurological Surgery, 3Population and Quantitative Health Sciences, and 5Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, Case Western Reserve University School of Medicine, MetroHealth Medical Center, Cleveland, Ohio; 2Department of Neurological Surgery, Cleveland Clinic Foundation, Cleveland, Ohio; and 4Population Health Research Institute, Center for Health Policy & Research, MetroHealth Medical Center, Cleveland, Ohio  

OBJECTIVE  The aim of this study was to stratify poly–traumatic brain injury (poly-TBI) patterns into discrete classes and to determine the association of these classes with mortality and withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST).  

METHODS  The authors performed a single-center retrospective review of their institutional trauma registry from 2018 to 2020 to identify patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI). Patients were included if they had moderate to severe TBI, defined as Glasgow Coma Scale score ≤ 12 and Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) head score $\geq3$ , and the presence of more than one TBI subtype. TBI subtypes were defined as subdural hemorrhage (SDH), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and epidural hemorrhage (EDH). Latent class analysis was used to identify patient classes based on TBI subtypes and Rotterdam CT (RCT) scores. The authors then evaluated class membership in relation to categorical outcomes of in-hospital mortality and WLST by using Lanza et al.’s method.  

RESULTS  A total of 125 patients met inclusion criteria for poly-TBI. Latent class analysis yielded 3 poly-TBI classes: class $\uparrow\cdot$ –mixed; class 2–SDH/SAH; and class 3–EDH/SAH. Class 1–mixed had a higher likelihood of SDH, SAH, and ICH, and a lower likelihood of EDH. Class 2–SDH/SAH had a higher likelihood of only SDH and SAH. Class 3–EDH/SAH had a higher likelihood of EDH and SAH, and a lower likelihood of SDH and ICH. Class 1–mixed was relatively more likely to have an RCT score of 2. Class 2–SDH/SAH was relatively more likely to have an RCT score of 2, 3, and 4. Class 3–EDH/ SAH had a higher likelihood of an RCT score of 3, 4, and 5. Class 1–mixed had significantly lower mortality $(\chi^{2}=7.968;\upmu$ $=0.005)$ and less WLST $(\chi^{2}=4.618$ ; $\mathsf{p}=\mathsf{0}.032)$ than Class 2–SDH/SAH. Class 2–SDH/SAH had the highest probability of death (0.612), followed by class 3–EDH/SAH (0.385) and class 1–mixed (0.277). Similarly, class 2–SDH/SAH had the highest WLST probability (0.498), followed by class 3–EDH/SAH (0.615) and class 1–mixed (0.238).  

CONCLUSIONS  Distinct poly-TBI classes were associated with increased in-hospital mortality and WLST. Further research with larger datasets will allow for more comprehensive poly-TBI class definitions and outcomes analysis.

One way to characterize multiple TBI subtypes is through latent class analysis (LCA); this is a statistical analysis technique that can be used to identify subgroups based on a set of baseline indicators within each participant.7 For example, Sinha and colleagues used LCA to identify groups of patients who presented with COVID-19, in whom there was an increased risk of developing acute respiratory distress syndrome.8 In that study, investigators demonstrated that patients with lower blood pressures as well as a unique biochemical pattern on serum laboratory work composed of elevated inflammatory markers, troponins, and creatinine were at an increased risk of death compared to other patients. These authors suggested that clinicians could use admission laboratory work to identify patients at high risk of death from COVID-19, and accordingly provide increased clinical monitoring.  

LCA can be similarly used to identify patients at higher risk of TBI-related mortality in those with multiple injury subtypes of TBI, which is also called poly-TBI. In our study, we used LCA to identify poly-TBI injury classes. We then tested the association between these poly-TBI classes and clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that unique polyTBI classes are associated with in-hospital mortality and withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST) in patients with moderate to severe TBI.  

# Methods  

Patient Population   
A single-center retrospective review of all patients with multiple TBI subtypes or poly-TBI was performed between January 1, 2018, and October 1, 2020. Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients 14 years of age or older; presentation with multiple TBI subtypes (EDH, SDH, SAH, IVH, and ICH); and moderate to severe TBI, defined as GCS score of $\leq12$ and an Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) head score of $\geq3$ . Patients were excluded if they did not have multiple TBI subtypes, had a GCS score of $\geq13$ or higher, had an AIS head score of 2 or 1, or if they were $<14$ years of age. Patients managed surgically and those managed nonsurgically were included in this study. We defined SAH as having any blood within the subarachnoid space, between the pia and arachnoid layers.9 This is represented by hyperdensity within the subarachnoid space on CT imaging.9 TBI subtype and the RCT score were documented in the initial history and physical examination consultation note by the on-call neurosurgeon after review of the initial admission head CT. The documentation of the TBI subtype and RCT score was then extracted via chart review by our institution’s trauma registrars and included in the registry along with all other standard trauma variables recorded in the database according to the National Trauma Data Bank data dictionary. Our study group then extracted the TBI subtype and RCT variables along with all other standard trauma registry variables such as GCS score, Injury Severity Score (ISS), and AIS head scores. The factors that comprise the ISS are the body region–specific AIS scores. These AIS scores are produced by trauma registrars via chart review and entered into the registry after the patient has been discharged from the hospital. As such, the AIS score and the ISS are not available at the time of injury. In contrast, the variables used in our LCA (i.e., TBI subtype and RCT score) are available at the time of injury along with the initial head CT and could serve as a prognostication tool at the time of injury. We also obtained demographic variables such as age, sex, and race. The RCT score was used to define the radiographic severity of TBI. This score is based on the presence or absence of 4 different categories. The score and mortality associated with each score are listed in Table 1, based on Maas et al.’s study which highlighted the RCT score’s utility.10 To comply with HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) guidelines that protect private health information, patients with ages above 89 years had their ages changed to 90 in the dataset. The RCT scores and TBI subtypes were collected based on the initial CT head scan. Main outcomes included in-hospital mortality and WLST. WLST was defined as the removal of life-sustaining interventions such as mechanical ventilation and vasoactive medication.11  

# Statistical Analysis  

We reported descriptive statistics for our sample as the mean (SD) for continuous measures and the number of patients (percentage) for each category for discrete measures. LCA was used to identify clusters of similar data based on a set of markers: TBI subtypes (EDH, SDH, SAH, and  

ICH) and RCT scores. These variables were used because they permit classification based on TBI subtypes and they account for severity of injury. These variables are foundational and would be available to surgeons prior to treatment, in contrast to other severity metrics used in trauma such as the ISS. Other variables could potentially be included in future models but may lead to detection of a higher number of classes that are not necessarily clinically meaningful; e.g., too specific. LCA is an application of mixture modeling using ordered-categorical indicators.7,12 A maximum likelihood estimator with robust standard errors was used, allowing for inclusion of patients with incomplete data via a full information maximum likelihood procedure.13,14 As a result, all available observations were included in the analyses and missing data were handled under the missing at random assumption (i.e., data missing dependent on data at hand).  

In LCA, a single group model was the reference to which models of increasing clusters where compared (e.g., class 2 model was compared to class 1 model; class 3 model was compared to class 2 model). We evaluated multiple indices when selecting the optimal solution including Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC) and corrected AIC (AICc), entropy, and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test.7,15 Once the best model was determined, the membership probability for each patient to be in each class was estimated and patients were classified to the cluster in which they had the highest membership.16 We then assessed the membership of the clusters as they relate to mortality and WLST by using the method described by Lanza et al. for categorical distal outcomes.17 A chi-square test was used to distinguish each class. All LCA models were conducted in MPlus 8.6.18 Additional regression analysis was not performed due to limitations in sample size.  

Finally, we characterized the demographic and clinical attributes of the patient clusters by using descriptive statistics. We also used either the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test or the chi-square test where appropriate to test for differences across the clusters on these attributes. The R program in the RStudio environment was used for data management, graphical displays, and post hoc analyses (Venables and Smith) and the MPlusAutomation package automated LCA estimation and interpretation.19,20 A twosided alpha of 0.05 was considered significant for all tests.

# TBI Subtype Latent Classes  

After LCA analysis, there were 3 classes that minimized AIC, AICc, aBIC, near minimum BIC, near maximum entropy, and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test. The average classification probability was very good (the likelihood of being in cluster 1 if the model “said” you were in cluster $1\mathrm{was}\geq0.96$ for every cluster).  

Three classes were identified after LCA based on TBI subtype and RCT score (Table 3). Each class was then renamed based on TBI subtype predominance. An example of each class is represented with CT images in Fig. 1. Class 1 was termed mixed due to a relatively equal distribution of TBI subtypes including SDH, SAH, and ICH. Class 2 was named SDH/SAH due to a predominance of SDH and SAH in this group, and class 3 was termed EDH/ SAH due to a predominance of EDH and SAH subtypes in this group. Table 4 shows the demographic characteristics within each class. There were no significant differences in sex or race among the classes. Significant differences were found across classes for age $(\mathtt{p}=0.004)$ , maximum head AIS score $(\mathrm{p}<0.001)$ , GCS score $(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{p}}<0.001)$ , and any surgical intervention $(\mathtt{p}<0.001)$ . Further analysis with a specific measure of craniectomy procedures showed similar results $(\mathtt{p}<0.001)$ , with class 3–EDH/SAH $(58.3\%)$ having the highest percentage, followed by class $2{-}\mathrm{SDH/SAH}$ $(23.8\%)$ and class 1–mixed $(4.3\%)$ . Classes were similar in distribution for mechanisms of injury, with a fall being most common, followed by other mechanisms of injury, MVC, and GSW.  

Higher probabilities denote an increasing likelihood of each TBI subtype within each class. Class 1–mixed had a relatively higher likelihood of SDH (0.69), SAH (0.91), and ICH (1.00), and no likelihood of EDH (Table 3). Class 2–SDH/SAH had a relatively higher likelihood of SDH (1.00) and SAH (1.00) and did not have EDH (0.00) or ICH (0.00). Class 3–EDH/SAH had a relatively higher likelihood of EDH (1.00) and SAH (0.769), but relatively lesser likelihoods of SDH (0.462) and ICH (0.462).  

# RCT Score Subtype Latent Classes  

Class 1–mixed was relatively more likely to have an RCT score of 3 (0.58), and relatively less likely to have an RCT score of 4 (0.15), 5 (0.12), 2 (0.09), or 6 (0.07), and no likelihood of an RCT score of 1. Class 2–SDH/SAH had a wider distribution of possible RCT score values, with an RCT score of 3 (0.33), 4 (0.21), and 5 (0.21) being the relatively most likely, and 6 (0.17) and 2 (0.07) being relatively less likely. There was no RCT score of 1. Class 3–EDH/ SAH had a relatively higher likelihood of an RCT score of 2 (0.39), 4 (0.31), 3 (0.23), or 1 (0.08), with no RCT score of 5 or 6. # Chi-Square Analysis  

Chi-square analysis was performed to evaluate for differences between classes in terms of in-hospital mortality and WLST. Class 2–SDH/SAH had the highest mortality probability (0.612; Table 5), followed by class 3–EDH/ SAH (0.385), and class 1–mixed (0.277), respectively. There was a significant difference observed in mortality across the classes overall $(\mathtt{p}=0.016$ ; Table 6). Class $1-$ mixed had a lower mortality rate than class 2–SDH/SAH $(\mathtt{p}=0.005)$ .  

Similar results were found for WLST. Overall, class $2-$ SDH/SAH had the highest probability of WLST (0.498; Table 5), followed by class 3–EDH/SAH (0.385) and then class 1–mixed (0.238), respectively. There was no significant difference among the classes overall $\mathbf{\sigma}_{\mathrm{{p}}}=0.099$ ; Table 6). Class 1–mixed demonstrated a significantly lower probability of WLST than class 2–SDH/SAH $\mathbf{\tilde{p}}=0.032)$ .  

# Discussion  

# Key Findings  

Our study demonstrates a novel adaptation of LCA to identify unique phenotypes of poly-TBI, each with unique class features across several clinical and radiographic parameters. Our results show that poly-TBI classes represent unique phenotypes of injury as defined by RCT scores, GCS scores, age, and radiographic TBI-subtype at presentation. These poly-TBI classes are associated with different in-hospital mortality and/or WLST outcomes.  

# Interpretation  

Our analysis showed a unique association with mortality and WLST in class 2–SDH/SAH compared to other classes of poly-TBI. The poly-TBI classes are distinguished by radiographic and clinical features of TBI, which suggest that certain radiographic features of injury manifest themselves in association with specific clinical and demographic features of age and GCS score. For example, patients with class 2–SDH/SAH had a similar GCS and lower RCT score than the ones with class 3–EDH/ SAH, but patients with class 2–SDH/SAH were also significantly older (58 vs 35 years). The constellation of these factors in a class 2–SDH/SAH cohort with TBIs accounts for the higher mortality rate observed in the class 2–SDH/ SAH population. In comparison, patients with class $3-$ EDH/SAH presented with similarly lower admission GCS scores but demonstrated a comparatively higher survival rate, probably due to timely surgical evacuation and lower burden of underlying brain injury,21 which is demonstrated by the higher surgical rate among patients with class 3–EDH/SAH. In contrast, SDH with underlying SAH (class 2–SDH/SAH) may reflect a more severe underlying brain injury that persists despite alleviation of mass effect from SDH evacuation. Last, although patients with class 1–mixed lesions tended to have a higher number of TBI subtypes involved (including both SDH and SAH) and an older average age, patients with this type of injury pattern tended to cluster around a higher average GCS score and demonstrated the lowest mortality rate.

# Significance  

The association of unique poly-TBI classifications with mortality and WLST can help in neurosurgical decisionmaking and prognostication. Multiple key decisions during hospitalization are made following severe TBI including the decision to pursue neurosurgical intervention, tracheostomy/percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy, and/or WLST. The identification and development of poly-TBI classes can help to better standardize injury classification and, in turn, outcome assessment and decision-making in circumstances that are often challenging and time sensitive. To our knowledge, our study is the first to quantify the combinatorial effects of multiple TBI subtypes on overall outcome, which allows for more specific prognostication in the setting of severe poly-TBI. For example, a poly-TBI class framework would allow for rapid prognostication in the case scenarios presented in Table 7, where patient A (i.e., class 1–mixed), patient B (class $2\mathrm{-SDH/SAH)}$ , and patient C (class 3–EDH/SAH) would have an estimated inhospital mortality rate of $26\%$ , $61\%$ , and $41\%$ , respectively. Future research and further delineation and validation of poly-TBI classes will help to guide neurosurgical decisionmaking for patients with these injuries.  

# External Validity  

Our results are consistent with previous studies. Wong and colleagues found that cerebral contusions in conjunction with SAH were not associated with worse outcomes among patients with TBIs.22 This is consistent with our findings for patients with class 1–mixed lesions who were more likely to have intracerebral contusions and also had better outcomes. As in the Wong study, we found that patients with both an SAH and an SDH were positively associated with unfavorable outcomes, which is similar to outcomes in our class 2–SDH/SAH population. Interestingly, the most common TBI subtype among patients in our poly-TBI cohort was SAH $(92.8\%)$ , which is consistent with previous reports suggesting that traumatic SAHs are associated with a higher severity of injury.8  

In 2019, Rau and colleagues performed a similar study.23 They investigated factors associated with mortality rates among patients with concurrent SAHs and at least one other hemorrhage. They found that patients with multiple subtypes had increased mortality risk relative to patients with only an SAH. Interestingly, they found that the OR increased with each hemorrhage (e.g., 2 ICHs had an OR of 4, whereas 3 ICHs had an OR of 8.9). Interestingly, these results suggest that as more hemorrhages occur, the risk for mortality is higher. However, we found that the number of hemorrhages does not necessarily correlate with the risk of mortality, given that class 1–mixed had a lower risk of mortality in comparison to class $2-$ SDH/SAH. One possible explanation for this discrepancy in findings is the definition of groups. It may be possible that the SAH/SDH mechanism of injury may lead to more catastrophic outcomes than the other 2 hemorrhage patterns, or even 3 hemorrhage patterns. The Rau study only examines SAH “and one diagnosis,” regardless of the type of specific hemorrhage. Thus, this may lead to less severe mechanisms of injury being incorporated into the same category of SDH/SAH lesions, altering the risk of mortality that we found in our study. Another consideration is that historically, SDH has often been associated with a higher mortality rate in comparison to other hemorrhages.24 Just as one type of hemorrhage (i.e., injury subtype) has a higher mortality association, it is possible that the SDH/SAH injury pattern is a specific, devastating injury pattern that is associated with higher mortality.

In one study, Kerezoudis and colleagues conducted an investigation to determine factors influencing mortality and morbidity using the National Trauma Data Bank registry for patients aged 65 years and older.25 They found that among co-occurring hemorrhages, SAH was the most common $(21\%)$ , twice the frequency of ICH $(10\%)$ . This is consistent with our results in that SAH was present in $92.8\%$ of cases in our study. Additionally, they found that GCS score was the strongest predictor of mortality. Our results were somewhat similar, as the class 2–SDH/SAH, the group with the highest mortality rate, had a significantly different GCS score (median 3) in comparison to class 1–mixed (median 6). There was a similar study in which Powers and colleagues investigated factors associated with mortality among patients with any ICH.26 Notable findings included lower GCS score, older age, and higher ISS being associated with a higher mortality rate. These results are consistent with our findings. Interestingly, the authors sought to create a model that predicted the probability of mortality and were able to obtain significant findings. For example, patients in “grade I” had a significantly lower probability in comparison to “grade IV.” This is a somewhat similar outcome to our approach; however, we used LCA as an approach rather than regression.  

# Limitations  

Our study has several limitations; it was a single-center and retrospective study, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Due to limitations in the trauma registry, we did not address a number of variables that are known to affect mortality in TBI such as hypotension and comorbidities.27 The trauma registry does not contain detailed information about medications including anticoagulation, and so these data were not available for analysis. Future studies that include anticoagulation status would further enhance the discriminatory power of LCA. Additionally, SAH was the most common TBI subtype in our study and the severity of SAH can be heterogeneous. Due to sample size limitations, we did not perform further analysis of SAH severity. A future multicenter study with a larger sample size could allow for analysis of SAH severity. Although we included the RCT scale, which includes factors such as midline shift and cisternal effacement, the RCT scale does not measure other known predictors of outcome in TBI including SDH thickness and contusion volume.22 There have been other approaches to identify prognostic factors associated with mortality. For example, Murray et al. performed an analysis to identify factors that may influence predictors of mortality.28 They found that adding a pupil reactivity score to GCS may better help predict 6-month mortality in addition to radiographic features such as those used in the RCT. The proposed pupil GCS (GCS-P) featured scores ranging from 0 (no reactivity) to 2 (reactivity in both eyes), and subtracting any deficits from the GCS score (e.g., GCS score of 1 for no reactivity). In future studies, it would be interesting to determine if incorporating pupil reactivity into the GCS score may help better define possible classes among patients with multiple hemorrhages compared to the standard GCS. Finally, our study did not explicitly account for the presence of diffuse axonal injury (DAI). Our study used the findings available on initial CT head scans. The diagnosis and classification of DAI is accomplished most effectively with MRI. Patients with TBI in our institution undergo CT head scans only in the setting of acute TBI. An MRI scan is only performed in a delayed fashion from the time of injury. Future studies may use LCA to see how the diagnosis of DAI affects poly-TBI classes and outcomes.  

# Conclusions  

LCA can identify unique classes of patients with polyTBI, which are associated with in-hospital mortality and WLST. Further studies with larger sample sizes across multiple institutions are needed to further delineate polyTBI classes and their associations with clinical outcomes.
