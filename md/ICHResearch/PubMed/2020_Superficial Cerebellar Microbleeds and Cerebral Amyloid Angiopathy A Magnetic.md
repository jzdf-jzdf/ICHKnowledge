# Superficial Cerebellar Microbleeds and Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy Magnetic Resonance Imaging/Positron Emission Tomography Study  

Hsin-Hsi Tsai, MD; Marco Pasi, MD; Li-Kai Tsai, MD, PhD; Ya-Fang Chen, MD; Yu-Wei Chen, MD; Sung-Chun Tang, MD, PhD; M. Edip Gurol, MD, MSc; Ruoh-Fang Yen, MD, PhD; Jiann-Shing Jeng, MD, PhD  

Background and Purpose—The differentiation between cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and hypertensive small vessel disease in primary intracerebral hemorrhage is mainly based on hemorrhagic neuroimaging markers in the supratentorial regions, and the cause for cerebellar microbleeds remains unknown. Our aim was to investigate whether superficial cerebellar microbleeds are more likely to be related to CAA rather than hypertensive small vessel disease.  

Methods—Two hundred seventy-five consecutive patients with intracerebral hemorrhage were retrospectively reviewed from a prospectively maintained hospital-based stroke registry. Eighty-five $(33.1\%)$ patients had cerebellar microbleeds and were categorized into superficial (gray matter, vermis), deep (white matter, deep nucleus, cerebellar peduncle), or mixed type based on the location of cerebellar hemorrhagic lesions. Amyloid imaging was obtained using 11C-Pittsburgh Compound B–positron emission tomography in a subgroup of patients. The associations between cerebellar microbleed locations and the type of small vessel disease (CAA versus hypertensive small vessel disease) based on distribution of supratentorial hemorrhagic lesions as well as other magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography markers were analyzed.  

Results—The presence of cerebellar microbleed was independently associated with supratentorial microbleed and lacunar infarcts (both $P{<}0.01$ ). Strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds were significantly related to CAA–intracerebral hemorrhage, cortical superficial siderosis and high-grade enlarged perivascular space in centrum semiovale (all $P{<}0.05$ ); deep or mixed cerebellar microbleeds were related to hypertension and deep microbleed (all $P{<}0.05$ ). In multivariable models, superficial cerebellar microbleeds were independently associated with CAA–intracerebral hemorrhage $(P{=}0.03)$ ). Of 33 patients assessed by amyloid positron emission tomography, cerebral and cerebellar amyloid load (standardized uptake value ratio) was higher in patients with superficial cerebellar microbleeds compared with deep/mixed cerebellar microbleeds (cerebrum standardized uptake value ratio [reference: cerebellum] $1.33{\pm}0.24$ versus $1.05{\pm}0.09\$ , $P{<}0.001$ ; cerebellum standardized uptake value ratio [reference: pons] $0.58{\pm}0.08$ versus $0.51{\pm}0.09$ , $P{=}0.03$ ).  

Conclusions—Patients with strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds are associated with a clinicoradiological diagnosis of CAA as well as increased cerebral and cerebellar amyloid deposition on Pittsburgh Compound B–positron emission tomography, suggesting underlying CAA pathology. (Stroke. 2020;51:202-208. DOI: 10.1161/STROKEAHA. 119.026235.)

In the current study, we aimed to test the hypothesis that superficial cerebellar microbleeds are more likely to be related to CAA rather than hypertensive SVD in patients with primary ICH. We further tested the association between strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds and higher amyloid load in both cerebral and cerebellar cortices.  

# Methods  

# Data Availability  

Any data not published within the article are available by request within the National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH). Such dataset will be shared on request from a qualified investigator.  

# Patient Selection and Data Collection  

For this cross-sectional analysis, we collected patients with available magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; including T1-weighted, T2-weighted, susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences) from (1) a prospective registry consisting of patients diagnosed with primary ICH at NTUH from September, 2014 to March, 2018; and (2) patients referred from other hospitals and participated in the MRI and amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) study at the NTUH stroke clinic as previously described in detail (Figure 1).10 Patients with primary cerebellar ICH were excluded $(\mathrm{n}{=}12)$ ).10 Patients were categorized as probable or possible CAA-ICH based on the strictly lobar distribution of supratentorial hemorrhagic markers according to the modified Boston criteria.4 Patients were categorized as hypertension-ICH if the hemorrhage was exclusively located in deep brain regions (basal ganglia [BG], thalamus, pons); patients with a combination of mixed lobar and deep bleeds were categorized as Mixed-ICH. A subgroup of 93 patients without overt cognitive impairment also received a $^{11}\mathrm{C}$ -PiB PET to determine brain amyloid retention. As previously described, baseline clinical data collection was collected retrospectively through standardized review of medical records.6,10,11 This study was performed with the approval of and in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Review Board (201404065MIND) at NTUH and with the written informed consent obtained from all participants or their family members.  

# MRI Analyses  

Images were obtained using 3T MRI scanners (Siemens Verio, TimTrio, or Biograph mMR). SWI acquisition was performed with a $\mathrm{T}2^{\ast}$ -weighted gradient echo sequence with flip angle $15^{\circ}$ , repetition time/echo time $:=28/20\mathrm{ms}$ , matrix numbe ${\scriptstyle=221\times320}$ , field of view ${\it\Delta\phi}=23{\it\Delta\phi}$ cm, slice thicknes $_{\mathrm{{s=1.6}}}$ mm. SWI, and minimum intensity projection images were acquired by in-line post-processing of magnitude and phase images, and these postprocessed images were used for the evaluation of the imaging findings. Other conventional imaging protocols included T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging.

Cerebellum microbleeds were evaluated using the SWI sequences and T1-weighted multiplanar reconstruction images for determining the exact anatomic location. For each patient with at least one cerebellar microbleed, the cerebellar microbleed type was categorized into superficial (gray matter, vermis), deep (white matter, deep nucleus, cerebellar peduncle), or mixed type based on the location of cerebellar microbleeds.5,12  

The presence and number of supratentorial microbleeds were evaluated on SWI as previously described.13 Cortical superficial siderosis was defined as homogenous curvilinear signal loss on SWI outlining the superficial layers of the cerebral cortex.14 Enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS) were defined as sharply delineated structures on T2-weighted imaging, measuring ${<}3~\mathrm{mm}$ following the course of perforating or medullary vessels.15 The number of EPVS (on the side of the brain with more severe involvement) was counted in BG and centrum semiovale, and we prespecified a dichotomized classification of EPVS degree as high (score ${>}20$ ) or low (score ${\le}20$ ).15 Lacunes were evaluated in the supratentorial region, and defined as round or ovoid, subcortical, fluid-filled (similar signal as cerebrospinal fluid) cavity, between $3\mathrm{mm}$ and $15\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter, according to Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging criteria.16,17 White matter hyperintensities volume was calculated using an in-house semiautomated algorithm. After registering the T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery volume to the T1-weighted volume, a minimum intensity threshold of $1.5\mathrm{SDs}$ was applied.18,19 The resulting clusters that fell on the white matter were considered, and their volume was calculated. All results were manually verified to ensure their accuracy.  

# PET Analyses  

$^{11}\mathrm{C}$ -PiB was synthesized in NTUH, and PET images (Discovery ST, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) were acquired for 30 minutes ${\approx}40$ minutes after injection of $10\mathrm{mCi}^{11}\mathrm{C}.$ PiB. The interval between the acquisition of PET and MRI scans were within 90 days in all patients. PET data were reconstructed with ordered set expectation maximization and corrected for attenuation. The PET data were semiquantitatively analyzed and expressed as standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) of regions of interest using cerebellar cortex as the reference region (global cerebral SUVR [gSUVR; cerebellum ref]), same as our group previously described.10,11 For the purpose of this study, we further calculated cerebral cortex and cerebellar cortex SUVRs using pons as the reference region (gSUVR [pons ref] for cerebral cortex; global cerebellar SUVR [gcSUVR;  pons ref] for cerebellar cortex).  

# Statistical Analyses  

Categorical variables are presented as percentages, and the continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median (interquartile range) depending on their distribution. In univariable analyses, we compared baseline demographic, clinical, and neuroimaging variables between patients with and without cerebellar microbleeds. A multivariable logistic regression model was built to investigate the independent associations between presence of cerebellar microbleed(s) and relevant neuroimaging markers of SVD (presence of microbleed, severe BG-EPVS, white matter hyperintensities volume, and presence of lacune) with further adjustment for age and ICH category. Within patients who had cerebellar microbleed(s), we compared those with different cerebellar hemorrhagic distribution. The independent association between superficial type cerebellar microbleed and CAA-ICH was investigated in a multivariable logistic regression model with the following covariates: age, presence of microbleed, severe centrum semiovale-EPVS, severe BG-EPVS, and presence of cortical superficial siderosis. For PiB PET analyses, SUVRs were compared between (1) CAA-ICH versus non-CAA ICH (hypertension-ICH and mixed-ICH combined) and (2) superficial type and deep/mixed type cerebellar microbleed using independent sample $t$ test, and the associations were also investigated after adjusted for age. The amyloid uptake values were presented in figures using box and whisker plots, with box edges representing interquartile range from the first to the third quartile and the bars representing the upper and the lower extremes. The κ statistic was calculated to evaluate the inter-observer reliability for cerebellar hemorrhagic distribution pattern. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22 (SPSS, Inc, Chicago, IL). All tests of significance were 2-tailed with a threshold for significance of $P{<}0.05$ .  

# Results  

A total of $257\mathrm{ICH}$ patients (36 CAA-ICH, 100 hypertensionICH, 121 mixed-ICH) were included in the analyses (Figure 1). Eighty-five $(33.1\%)$ patients had cerebellar microbleeds. The comparison between patients with and without cerebellar microbleeds are shown in Table  1. Patients with cerebellar microbleeds were older and more frequently associated with Mixed-ICH when compared with patients without cerebellar microbleeds $(P{<}0.001\$ ). They also showed higher frequency of several neuroimaging SVD markers, including supratentorial lobar and deep microbleeds, white matter hyperintensities volumes, lacunes, and high-degree EPVS in BG (all $P{<}0.05$ ). In the multivariable logistic regression full model including all of these variables, the presence of cerebellar microbleeds remained independently associated with the existence of supratentorial lobar microbleeds (odds ratio, 5.7 $[95\%$ CI, 1.6–20.8]; $P{=}0.008$ ), supratentorial deep microbleeds (odds ratio, 6.1 $[95\%$ CI, 2.0–18.9] $P{=}0.002$ ), and supratentorial lacunes (odds ratio, 2.4 $[95\%$ CI, 1.3–4.4]; $P{=}0.005$ ).  

Cerebellar microbleeds were found in $36.1\%$ (13/36) of CAA-ICH, $15\%$ (15/100) of hypertension-ICH and $47.1\%$ (57/121) of patients with mixed-ICH. The interrater agreement for evaluating the distribution of cerebellar microbleeds using binary category (superficial versus deep/mixed) was excellent $\scriptstyle(\mathrm{k=0.85}$ $[95\%$ CI, 0.69–1.00]). Superficial type cerebellar microbleeds were identified more commonly in CAAICH compared with both deep ICH and mixed-ICH $(33.3\%$ , $4.8\%$ and $16.3\%$ , respectively, $P{<}0.05$ for both comparisons).

# Discussion  

Our study shows that strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds are related to CAA imaging features, whereas deep or mixed location cerebellar microbleeds are more likely related to hypertensive SVD in an Asian primary ICH population. Using amyloid PET imaging, we found that the cortical amyloid load in patients with strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds was higher when compared to patients with microbleeds in deep or mixed cerebellar locations, providing evidence that the superficial cerebellar microbleeds may be a consequence of underlying CAA pathology.  

Chronic hypertension is the commonly accepted cause for primary cerebellar hemorrhage, and most of the hemorrhages arise near the dentate nucleus and subcortical white matter, which are considered deep in location.1,2 There are some reports with corresponding pathological confirmation that CAA might also be an important cause for cerebellar hemorrhages, and cerebellar type CAA-ICH is mostly located near the cortical surface.2,20 Our study confirmed this observation by showing that most of the patients with CAA-ICH with cerebellar involvement show strictly superficial type of bleeding pattern in cerebellum. In hypertension-ICH or mixed-ICH, which are considered consequences of chronic hypertensive SVD,21 both the superficial and deep regions of cerebellum are affected. The superficial type cerebellar microbleeds are not a pathognomonic marker for CAA-ICH, as around $17\%$ of patients with mixed-ICH also presented with strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds. Although the majority of Mixed-ICH is attributed to hypertensive SVD,6 the presence of strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds in mixed-ICH may indicate underlying coexisting CAA. However, deep cerebellar microbleeds were rarely found in CAA-ICH $(2.8\%)$ , and its presence could suggest presence of hypertensive SVD. The diagnostic utility of cerebellar microbleed distribution for CAA versus hypertensive SVD still remains uncertain and might be worth to replicate our findings in larger cohorts with pathological data. Most importantly, whether to include strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds into the Boston criteria or not requires further independent validation and a specifically designed study.

Cerebellar microbleeds were found in about one-third of our primary patients with ICH, which was high compared with the previous report in whites $(13\%)$ .7 It should be noted that the rates of hypertensive SVD in this study was significantly higher than in Western cohort,12 and it may affect the generalizability of our results. In the current study, patients who had cerebellar microbleeds were associated with higher frequency of several neuroimaging SVD markers, especially supratentorial microbleeds, white matter hyperintensities and lacunes. This finding suggests that patients with ICH with cerebellar microbleeds may suffer from a more severe underlying microangiopathy with extensive parenchymal damage when compared with those with merely supratentorial hemorrhagic lesions. Whether these patients are prone to have symptomatic cerebellar ICH was not investigated in the current cross-sectional study and should be a focus of future research.  

An important strength of this study is the use of amyloid PET imaging, which enabled us to confirm the cerebral and cerebellar amyloid burden in vivo. In the majority of amyloid imaging studies, the cerebellum, which is assumed to be amyloid naive, has been used as the reference region to calculate relative tracer uptake in the target region (target-to-cerebellum ratio). However, cerebellar amyloid may be present in CAA and sometimes in Alzheimer disease.2,22 An alternative method using target-to-pons ratio has been proposed and been proved reliable.23,24 To investigate the amyloid burden in cerebellar cortex and to avoid underestimating the cortical amyloid load in patients with CAA, we calculated cortical PiB SUVR using pons as the reference region. Both methods (using either cerebellum or pons as the reference to calculate global cortical PiB SUVR) showed higher cerebral cortical amyloid retention in patients with strictly superficial cerebellar microbleed compared with deep/mixed cerebellar microbleeds. We further calculated the cerebellar-to-pons PiB SUVR and identified a higher cerebellar PiB uptake in the strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds. These findings further support our hypothesis that strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds might be related to CAA. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is also the first one to use in vivo amyloid imaging to show the higher cerebellar cortical amyloid deposition in patients with CAA.  

Our study has some limitations. First, the cause of SVD was determined by neuroimaging criteria using clinical MRI instead of pathological data. However, the presence of strictly lobar ICH/microbleeds showed high accuracy for CAA in radiological/pathological validation studies.3,4 The underlying microangiopathy in Mixed-ICH could be controversial but has been shown mainly owing to hypertensive SVD in our own population and other cohorts.6,21 Second, similar to other studies using in vivo amyloid imaging on patients with CAA, we cannot exclude increased PiB values related to amyloid plaque in addition to vascular amyloid deposition.25 To minimize the effect of Alzheimer type pathology on our results, we did not include patients with any degree of dementia. Finally, our analysis was limited by the relatively small subgroup of patients who had amyloid imaging but the finding of rather robust associations between superficial cerebellar microbleeds and markers of vascular amyloid supported this association.

Confirming these findings in larger cohorts that also include pathological support would certainly increase the confidence in the proposed cause-effect relationships.  

In conclusion, our study showed independent associations between strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds and the diagnosis of CAA in patients with primary supratentorial ICHs. In 33 patients with amyloid imaging, elevated PiB uptake was also associated strictly superficial cerebellar microbleeds. These findings suggest vascular amyloid as an important contributor to physiopathological processes that may result in superficial cerebellar microbleeds and hypertensive SVD as the main cause of deep/mixed cerebellar microbleeds. If confirmed in other independent cohorts, the distribution of cerebellar microbleeds could become a novel image marker of the predominant cerebral microangiopathy in older adults who are suspected to have SVD.
