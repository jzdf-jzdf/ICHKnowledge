# Prolonged infusion of linezolid is associated with improved pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) profiles in patients with external ventricular drains  

Wenjun Zhao $^{1,2}\textcircled{\scriptsize{10}}$ & Lingti Kong3 & Chenchen Wu4 & Xiaofei Wu2  

Received: 20 March 2020 / Accepted: 11 August 2020 / Published online: 18 August 2020   
$\copyright$ Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2020  

# Abstract  

Objective We previously investigated the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) parameters of routine linezolid infusions (1 h) in patients with external ventricular drains (EVD). The aim of the study was to determine whether extended linezolid infusions $200\mathrm{mg/h}$ for $^{3\textrm{h}}$ ) were more efficacious than short linezolid infusions ( $600\mathrm{{mg}/h}$ for $^{1\textrm{h}}$ ).  

Methods We collected cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma samples from 10 patients who received linezolid infusions after cerebral hemorrhage surgery with EVDs. Linezolid concentrations were measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). A Monte Carlo simulation was used to measure the probability of target attainments (PTA) and the PK/PD indexes at four minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC).  

Results When the same dose $(600\mathrm{mg})$ ) was given as an extended infusion $^{3\textrm{h}}$ , linezolid reached its maximum concentrations in the plasma and CSF at $3.00\mathrm{h}$ and $4.40\mathrm{h}$ , respectively. The mean penetration of linezolid in CSF was $41.31\%$ . Using the parameter of $\mathrm{AUC}_{0-24}\mathrm{h}/\mathrm{MIC}\geq100$ , the plasma PTA provided good coverage at $>90\%$ when MIC was $\leq1~{\upmu}{\mathrm{g/mL}}$ , while the values were 0 in CSF. Using the parameter $\%T\left(\mathrm{time}\right)>\mathrm{MIC}\geq85\%$ , the PTA in both the plasma and CSF provided good coverage when MIC $\leq2$ $\upmu\mathrm{g/mL}$ . Compared with routine infusions, prolonged infusion times (3 h) showed increased PTA of linezolid.  

Conclusions Prolonged infusion times increased the concentration of linezolid in the plasma, leading to improved therapeutic outcomes. However, this improvement did not exist in CSF. Lastly, the PK/PD indicator $\mathrm{AUC/MIC\geq100}$ may be used to achieve improved outcomes in patients with critical infections.  

Keywords Linezolid $\cdot$ External ventricular drains $^{*}$ Prolonged infusion $\cdot$ Cerebrospinal fluid $^{*}$ Pharmacokinetics . Pharmacodynamics $\cdot$ Monte Carlo simulation  

# Introduction  

Intracranial infections are a common occurrence after neurosurgery with incidence rates ranging from 3 to $5\%$ [1].  

External ventricular drains (EVDs), also known as ventriculostomy catheters, are often used for postneurosurgery treatment and commonly associated with intracranial infections [2]. Currently, EVDs are associated with approximately $10\%$ (range $=5{-}23\%$ ) of intracranial infections in the clinic [3–5]. Intracranial infections can prolong hospital stays substantially, thereby increasing the associated medical costs and negatively impacting the overall prognosis of patients [6, 7].  

Antibiotics are the first-line treatment for intracranial infections in the clinic [8]. However, most intravenous antibiotics have limited efficacy due to their inability to bypass the blood–brain barrier (BBB), leading to suboptimal accumulation of the drug in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [8]. Even the drugs that penetrate the BBB often lack the retentiveness needed to reach the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) required for therapeutic effects [9–11]. Besides the lack of effective treatments, the number of post-surgical infections continues to rise globally. For example, coagulase-negative Staphylococcus and Staphylococcus aureus are two pathogens commonly associated with post-neurosurgical meningitis [12]. In addition, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) has become of increasing concern due to the lack of effective treatment options [6, 13]. The increasing prevalence of MRSA has become a major challenge for hospitals around the world [14, 15].  

Linezolid, an oxazolidinone antibiotic, is commonly used for the treatment of infections resulting from highly resistant gram-positive bacteria [16, 17]. Having a different mechanism than vancomycin, linezolid only functions in the translation system at the initial stage, thereby inhibiting the binding of mRNA and ribosomes while also blocking the formation of the 70S initiation complex inhibition of bacterial protein synthesis [18, 19]. Because of the high penetration rate in CSF and the low rates of adverse reactions, current protocols recommend the use of linezolid as an alternative to vancomycin in critically ill patients with multidrug-resistant gram-positive bacteria [20, 21], such as MRSA pneumonia [22, 23].  

In a previous study, our group investigated the effects of short-term linezolid $600~\mathrm{{mg/h}}$ for $1\mathrm{~h~}$ on the probability of target attainment (PTA) in plasma and CSF samples from patients with EVDs [24]. However, to increase the efficacy of antibiotics, one must consider prolonging the infusion of time-dependent antimicrobials, which has been done with meropenem, vancomycin, and linezolid in the clinic [22, 25, 26]. The aim of the current study was to determine whether extended infusions $(3\textrm{h})$ of linezolid resulted in higher anti-infective concentration than shorter (1 h) routine infusions.  

# Material and approaches  

# Study approval  

The research program was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College (Anhui Province, China) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients or legal representatives were notified and required to provide written informed consent before participating in this study.  

# Patient selection  

Patients with cerebral hemorrhages and EVDs, who were treated in the Department of Emergency Intensive Care at the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College between May 2018 and January 2019, were recruited for this study. Inclusion standards were the following: age $>18$ years, the presence of EVD for the postoperative cerebral hemorrhage, and received intravenous linezolid because of stroke-associated pneumonia and/or intra-cerebral infections; linezolid was sensitive in drug sensitivity test.  

# Linezolid infusions and sample collection  

Linezolid (Zyvox, Fresenius Kabi Norge AS, Halden, Norway) was given as $_{3-\mathrm{h}}$ infusions of $200~\mathrm{{mg/h}}$ every 12 h. A micro-pump was used to give the infusions. CSF samples from the EVDs $\mathrm{(0.2~mL)}$ and blood samples were drawn by using direct venipuncture, indwelling catheter, or direct-draw vacuum before the first infusion and at 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, 6.0, 8.0, and $12.0~\mathrm{h}$ after the first infusion. Next, the blood samples were centrifuged $3000~\mathrm{rpm}$ , 5 min, room temperature), and $0.2~\mathrm{mL}$ of the separated plasma and CSF samples were stored at − $80~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for future analysis.  

# Chemical substances and reagents  

Linezolid criterion $(99.58\%)$ was acquired from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Chloramphenicol criterion $(99.8\%)$ was purchased from the National Organizations for Food and Drug Control (Beijing, China). Linezolid for injection (Zyvox $\bigotimes_{-}^{}$ was purchased from Fresenius Kabi Norge AS (Oslo, Norway). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade acetonitrile was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Milli-Q water (Millipore, Milford, MA, USA) was used for this study.  

# Linezolid assay and sample arrangement  

First, $0.4~\mathrm{mL}$ acetonitrile and $20~\upmu\mathrm{L}$ internal standard (chloramphenicol, $1~\mathrm{mg/mL}$ ) were added into the plasma and CSF $\mathrm{(0.2mL},$ samples. The samples were mixed and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for $5\mathrm{min}$ , and then $10\upmu\mathrm{L}$ of the organic layer was injected into the HPLC system. HPLC was used to determine linezolid concentrations using methods previously described [24]. Briefly, the linezolid in the organic layer was analyzed using an Agilent Technologies 1290 HPLC system (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Samples were extracted on a Hypersil BDS C18 column $(2.1\times100\mathrm{mm}$ , $3.5~\upmu\mathrm{M})$ at $30~^{\circ}C$ using a mobile phase of water:acetonitrile $(80{:}20,\mathrm{v/v})$ , with a flow rate of $0.8\mathrm{mL/min}$ and detection wavelength of $254~\mathrm{nm}$ . The limit of quantification was $0.20~{\upmu}{\mathrm{g/mL}}$ , and the linearity was validated from 0.20 to $20.0~\upmu\mathrm{g/mL}$ for the plasma and CSF samples. Control samples with high, medium, and low concentrations of linezolid (10.0, 2.00, and $0.20~\upmu{\bfg}/\mathrm{mL}$ were used, and the method showed satisfactory repeatability and reproducibility with intra-day $(n=5)$ ) and inter-day $\mathit{\Omega}_{n=5}$ , 3 days) precision of less than $11.4\%$ , $12.2\%$ , $8.3\%$ , and $10.6\%$ for the plasma and CSF samples, respectively. The system showed excellent accuracy, ranging from 88.2 to $112\%$ and 92.3 to $111\%$ for the plasma and CSF samples, respectively.

# Pharmacokinetics  

Several pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters, including peak concentration $(C_{\mathrm{max}})$ , peak time $(T_{\mathrm{max}})$ , area under the curve $(\mathrm{AUC}_{0-t})$ , half-life $(T_{1/2})$ , total clearance (CL), and the volume of distribution (Vd), were determined for the noncompartmental model using WinNonlin 6.1 (Certara, Princeton, NJ, USA).  

# Monte Carlo simulation  

As linezolid is a long-acting antimicrobial agent that shows time-dependent activity [13, 27–30], the value of the area under the curve/minimum inhibitory concentration (AUC/ MIC) and the time at which the plasma concentration of drug exceeds the MIC $\%T>\mathrm{MIC})$ ) were used as the PK/PD (pharmacodynamic) parameters for the current study.  

In our previous study $600\mathrm{{mg}/h}$ for 1 h), $\mathrm{AUC}_{0-24}\mathrm{h}^{\mathrm{/MIC}}\geq$ 59.1 and $\%T>\mathrm{MIC}\geq40\%$ were used as the parameters [24]. However, recent investigations have suggested that an optimal antibacterial effect may be achieved when $\mathrm{AUC}_{0-24}\mathrm{~h/MIC\geq~}$ 100 and $\%T>\mathrm{MIC}\geq85\%$ [29–32], so we reassessed data by using the new standard in the present study [23, 24].  

Ten thousand–subject Monte Carlo simulations were carried out using Crystal Ball (Oracle, Redwood City, CA, USA) to approximate the PTAs of $600\mathrm{-mg}$ linezolid with 1.0- and $3.0\mathrm{-h}$ infusion time, respectively. The typical MICs were considered according to EUCAST guidance during the simulations, ranging from 1.0 to $8.0\upmu\mathrm{g/mL}$

# Discussion  

Intravenous vancomycin is the first-line treatment for MRSA infections, yet the presence of vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (VRSA) affects its clinical efficacy on Staphylococcus aureus [18, 19, 33, 34]. Hence, linezolid has become an alternative to vancomycin due to its unique molecular mechanism [35–39]. Linezolid is currently recommended for the treatment of infections caused by MRSA, when the vancomycin MIC $\mathrm{is}\geq1~\upmu\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{mL}$ [40].  

Pharmacokinetic parameters will show diverse differences due to changes in dose, infusion time, and study subjects. For example, the Vd of linezolid in plasma reported in previous studies ranges from 41.9 to $101.3\mathrm{L}/\mathrm{kg}$ [40, 41], and the peak concentration in CSF ranges from 3.20 to $10.8~{\upmu{\mathrm{g/mL}}}$ [40, 42], indicating the need for separate studies for different types of patient groups. A preliminary study from our group has calculated pharmacokinetic parameters $(T_{1/2},T_{\mathrm{max}},C_{\mathrm{max}}.$ , AUC, CL, and $\mathrm{Vd}$ ) to determine the PTAs with a Monte Carlo simulation, which was accomplished by 1-h linezolid infusions $(0.6~\mathrm{g}/12~\mathrm{h})$ . Our findings revealed that extending the duration of intravenous infusions may improve the therapeutic effects in patients with drug-resistant infections [24]; the aim of the study was to determine whether extended linezolid infusions $(200~\mathrm{{mg/h}}$ for $^{3\textrm{h}}$ ) were more efficacious than short linezolid infusions $600\mathrm{{mg}/h}$ for $^{1\textrm{h}}$ ).

In the plasma, with $\mathrm{AUC/MIC}\geq100$ as the PK/PD target, we found that (i) both routine infusions and extended infusions were $\geq85\%$ , if the MIC was $\leq1~{\upmu}{\mathrm{g/mL}}$ ; (ii) the $200\mathrm{mg/}$ h for $^{3\mathrm{h}}$ regimen was $\geq50\%$ and the $600\mathrm{mg/h}$ for 1 h regimen was only $20.32\%$ , if the MIC was $\leq2~\upmu{\mathrm{g/mL}}$ ; and (iii) both of them were $<1\%$ , if the MIC was $\leq4\upmu\mathrm{g/mL}$ . Using $\%T{>}\mathrm{MIC}$ as the PK/PD target, the results of both two infusion times were $\geq85\%$ if the MIC was $\leq2~\upmu{\mathrm{g/mL}}$ . However, only extended infusions $200~\mathrm{{mg/h}}$ for $3\mathrm{~h~}$ ) could achieve $\geq85\%$ if MIC was $\leq4~\upmu\mathrm{g/mL}$ . The experiments mentioned above indicate that extending the infusion time from 1 to $^{3\textrm{h}}$ could improve the probability of PTA reaching the standard. A previous study has confirmed that the $^{2-\mathrm{h}}$ infusion regimen is more appropriate to achieve the safety and efficacy, relative to $0.5\mathrm{h}$ and $^{4\mathrm{h}}$ [26]. Combined with the results of this study, we suggest that $^{3\textrm{h}}$ may be a more suitable time.

In the CSF, with $\mathrm{AUC/MIC}\geq100$ as the PK/PD target, we found that both routine infusions and extended infusions were nearly zero if the MIC was $\geq1~\upmu{\mathrm{g/mL}}$ , indicating that it is difficult to achieve good results in intracranial infection using intravenous linezolid therapy at $600~\mathrm{mg}/12~\mathrm{h}$ . This result is consistent with the previous meta-analysis [43]. The dosage increases and intrathecal administration may be required to achieve optimal results in the future. With $\%T>\mathrm{MIC}$ as the PK/PD target, both results were $\geq85\%$ if the MIC was $\leq2~\upmu\mathrm{g}/$ mL. Next, the $200\mathrm{mg/h}$ for $^{3\mathrm{~h~}}$ regimen was $93.1\%$ , while the $600~\mathrm{mg/h}$ for $^{1\textrm{h}}$ regimen was only $49.10\%$ when the MIC was $\leq4~\upmu\mathrm{g/mL}$ .  

It is worth mentioning that the mean penetration of linezolid in CSF decreased from 56.81 (1 h) to $41.31\%$ $(3\textrm{h})$ . It seems that prolonging the infusion time is not conducive to the treatment of intracranial infection. However, the AUC was $42.26\mathrm{h}\upmu\mathrm{g/mL}$ at $^{3\mathrm{~h~}}$ and $45.10\mathrm{h\:\upmug/mL}$ at $^{1\textrm{h}}$ in CSF; there is no significant difference. Meanwhile, the AUC was 102.30 $\pm22.67\mathrm{{h}\upmu{g/mL}}$ $(3\mathrm{~h})$ and $79.39\pm29.26\mathrm{h\:\upmug/mL}$ (1 h) in plasma, indicating that the AUC increased significantly when the infusion time was prolonged to $^{3\textrm{h}}$ , which would be beneficial to the treatment of bloodstream infection. Therefore, the drug exposure in CSF did not decrease when the infusion time was prolonged, but it was an illusion due to the exposure increase in plasma, and this result is consistent with the AUC/ $\mathrm{MIC}\geq100$ as the PK/PD target results between routine infusions and extended infusions in the CSF. However, when used $\%T>\mathrm{MIC}$ as the PK/PD target, prolonging infusion time could improve the probability of PTA to some extent.  

In summary, we found that PTA was more difficult to reach the standard when $\mathrm{AUC/MIC}\geq100$ was used as a parameter. However, PTA was simpler to achieve when $\%T>\mathrm{{MIC}>}$ $85\%$ was used as a parameter in CSF. Our findings demonstrate that $\mathrm{AUC/MIC}\geq100$ should be used as an indicator of PK/PD, which may lead to better clinical outcomes in patients with critical or highly resistant infections. Future studies are needed with larger patient populations to examine the clinical efficacy and side effects of patients who undergo linezolid infusions.
