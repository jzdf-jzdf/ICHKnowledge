OBJECTIVE: Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) is a common cause of brain injury in preterm infants. Fresh human milk (HM) contains stem cells (SCs) that could potentially be delivered via intranasal HM (IHM). In this IHM pilot study, we describe outcomes. STUDY DESIGN: Infants ${<}33$ weeks gestation with IVH were given IHM until maximum 28 days of age. Short-term neurologic outcomes and follow-up testing were compared to historic HM-fed infants. Longitudinal outcomes were plotted using linear mixed models. Weighted G-computation quantified treatment effects. Propensity score models calculated inverse probability weights for IVH grade, gestational age, and sex.  

RESULT: 37 infants $(35.1\%$ grade 3-4 IVH) were compared to 191 historic controls ( $17.8\%$ grade 3-4 IVH). Post-hemorrhagic ventricular dilatation was common $(25.7\%$ IHM patients). Most weighted outcomes, although not significant, favored IHM at 4-12 and 18 months corrected age.  

CONCLUSION: This phase 1 study suggests powered trials of IHM for brain injury are needed. CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRY NAME: clinicaltrials.gov identifier NCT04225286

# INTRODUCTION  

Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) is a common cause of brain injury in preterm infants. Preterm infants with IVH are at increased risk of death, short-term morbidities during Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admission, and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment and disability [1–3]. IVH damages immature, undifferentiated progenitor cells, leading to hypomyelination and grey matter loss with potential resultant suboptimal motor and developmental outcomes [4, 5]. Periventricular hemorrhagic infarction (grade IV IVH) is associated with development of cavitation (porcencphalic cysts) [6]. There currently are no therapies to treat IVH associated brain injury. Care models focus on prevention of IVH through targeted care bundles and monitoring for IVH occurrence and sequelae [3]. If severe IVH occurs, symptomatic management, including for posthemorrhagic ventricular dilation (PHVD) by shunt placement or lumbar puncture, may be necessary. These procedures carry their own risks and do not address underlying brain injury.  

Stem Cells (SCs) have shown promise as potential therapy for brain injury. In animal models of IVH, SCs have shown neuroprotective effects, reducing the degree of observed injury and PHVD [2, 7–9]. SCs delivered through different routes, including intranasally, have shown uptake into the neural system, neuroprotective effects, and improvement in outcomes in animal models of brain and spinal cord injuries [4, 5, 10]. Trials of SCs in human infants with brain injury have focused on SCs isolated from sources such as bone marrow and umbilical cord blood [2, 5, 9, 11], where significant sample processing is required. Fresh human milk (HM) has been identified as a source of viable pluripotent SCs that can be more easily collected and can form neuronal cells in culture [12–14]. HM also contains bioactives and anti-inflammatories, including factors that have shown therapeutic promise in animal models of spinal cord injury [15, 16]. Human milk feedings are associated with reduced brain injury and neuroprotective effects in the preterm population [17]. There is evidence that HM SCs are able to cross the blood-brain barrier, and in animal models these pluripotent SCs have been found in blood and brain tissue of suckling mice [4, 10, 15, 16]. It has been hypothesized that the high vascularity and link to the brain through the olfactory bulb of the nasopharynx is one route for this spread [4, 13], facilitated by tpresence of milk in the nasopharynx as part of physiologic feeding. As typical feeding processes are altered by the use of feeding tubes in the NICU, administration of fresh HM directly into the nasopharynx has been hypothesized as a way to deliver this same effect [13]. A small German nonprotocolized case series using intranasal HM administration in preterm infants with IVH reported a signal towards improvement in short-term morbidities [13], supporting the possibility of this route of administration and neuroprotective effects, but no longterm outcomes were collected.  

Our non-powered phase I trial aimed to determine whether fresh HM could be safely and feasibly delivered intranasally as SC therapy to preterm infants with IVH. Secondary objectives included collecting short-term outcomes to NICU discharge and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes to 18 months corrected age to compare to recent historic HM-fed controls to assess for associations.  

# MATERIALS/SUJECTS AND METHODS Population  

Recruitment for this non-randomized prospective trial (clinicaltrials.gov NCT04225286; Canadian New Frontiers grant NFRFE-2018-01610) took place between July 2020 and September 2021 at two tertiary NICUs in Toronto, Canada. Provincial research ethics board approval (Clinical Trials Ontario ID 1911) was obtained and accepted by participating sites. Written informed consent was obtained from parents for infants for the prospective cohort; data for a historic comparison of parent milk-fed preterm infants with IVH admitted to the same NICUs in the 1.5 years prior to study initiation was obtained from electronic medical record with an ethics board exception for retrospective data use without consent. Inclusion criteria included preterm infants born under 33 weeks gestation with any grade IVH (Papile classification [3]) identified on head ultrasound in the first ten days of age and a lactating parent. Exclusion criteria included known disorders associated with neurodevelopmental impairment, infants with planned redirection of care, choanal atresia or nomalies that would not allow for intranasal treatment, surgical conditions for which intranasal HM was felt to be contraindicated by the clinical team, parents with lactation contraindications or who did not initiate lactation, or a lactating parent unable provide fresh HM (unable/unwilling to pump in-NICU or have fresh HM delivered by designee at least once/day for 3 days within 3 hours of pumping AND located $>30$ km from study NICUs and therefore ineligible for study courier services.)  

# Clinical intervention  

The primary objective of this study was to determine safety and feasibility of intranasal HM administration in a preterm population with IVH [18]; this pilot study was not powered for outcomes. The study protocol has been previously described [18], but in brief, an initial HM dose of $0.2\:\mathrm{ml}$ was administered into one nostril of enrolled infants within 3 hours of pumping by a trained clinician using a standard plastic oral syringe inserted just into the nasal vestibule. Pumping support and transportation of milk by methods such as taxi was used to allow for fresh milk to be administered within 3 hours of pumping when the pumping parent was not present. If tolerated, the dose was increased to $0.4\mathsf{mL}$ given before and after nursing care, ideally twice daily for a goal of 1.6 mL/day. Administration was started as soon as possible after recruitment (latest day 10 of age) and continued whenever fresh milk was available through 28 days of age or transfer from NICU, whichever came first.  

The dosing was justified based on published ranges of HM SC concentrations ( $10,\dot{0}00-13\times10^{6}$ cells/mL.) [9, 19] A 1 mL daily HM dose would meet animal model study dosing and animal model effectiveness thresholds $(5\times10^{5}$ cells) even with a 10-fold lower cell concentration [9]. Although HM of preterm parents likely has higher SC concentrations [20] some may fall below effective ranges, therefore we targeted a dose of 1.6 mL/day, the daily dose in the previous case series of intranasal HM [13].  

# Outcomes  

Secondary objectives were to describe frequency of in-NICU clinical outcomes associated with IVH (PHVD, periventricular leukomalacia and/or porencephalic cysts on head ultrasound, need for medical/ neurosurgical intervention [i.e. lumbar puncture, reservoir, or shunt] for PHVD, or seizures). To compare intervention infants to historic controls, we collected standard NICU outcomes (necrotizing enterocolitis, retinopathy of prematurity stage 3 or greater, bronchopulmonary dysplasia at 36 weeks, culture-positive bloodstream infection, death, and NICU length of stay). Finally, infants were seen at NICU neurodevelopmental follow-up clinic visits as standard of care (unrelated to the study). From these clinic records, long-term neurodevelopmental assessments were collected. At 4, 8, and 12 months corrected age, clinic visits recorded AIMS (Alberta Infant Motor Scale) [21] score and percentile rank based on corrected age (delay: score $<10^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile rank); PFMAI (Posture and Fine Motor Assessment of Infancy; age-based score categorized into mean vs at risk/motor delay [2 standard deviations or greater from mean],) [22] results of hearing and vision tests, weight, head circumference, and continued HM feeding (yes/no). Given the Covid-19 pandemic, some infants were unable to be seen in-person and completed virtual visits. In these cases, formal testing was unable to be performed, and these infants, based on parent report and Ages and Stages Questionnaires (ASQ-3) [23], were categorized for possible fine and gross motor delays (no concern vs monitor/concern). Formal testing results (AIMS and PFMAI) were reported separately, and these informal fine and gross motor delay concerns were also combined with formal testing results [“fine or gross motor delay/monitor”]. At 18 months, assessments included a neurological exam (including results of hearing and vision testing and whether the infant was sitting and walking), Bayley Scales of Infant Development III (BayleyIII, motor, language, and cognitive scales, with moderate/severe impairment in each category defined as score $>~2$ standard deviations below the mean of 100 (score ${<}70^{\circ}$ ) [24], as well as M-CHAT (modified checklist for autism in toddlers; pass/monitor/fail) [25].  

# Statistical analysis  

All statistical analyses assumed a significance level of $5\%$ and were conducted using R v4.0.3. Clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were characterized using median and interquartile range; dichotomous or polytomous variables were characterized using frequencies. We quantified the average treatment effect between cohorts for each outcome (shortterm outcomes and and longer-term outcomes at 4, 8, 12 months and 18 months, as described previously) using weighted G-computation. In the context of externally controlled studies, G-computation has been demonstrated as a preferable approach producing relatively unbiased estimates [26, 27]. To quantify the treatment effect, we computed two weights that accounted for confounding baseline characteristics and missingness. To account for confounding baseline characteristics, as we assumed a-priori that parents of infants with more severe IVH would be more likely to consent to the study, we used a propensity score model and calculated inverse probability weights that adjusted for potential confounders (IVH grade, gestational age, and sex.) The covariate imbalance was assessed using standardized mean differences. We computed weights based on missingness using stabilized weights. We obtained the probability of missing using a logistic regression model. Treatment effects from weighted G-computations were presented by odds ratios, $95\%$ CIs, and $p$ values.  

# RESULTS  

37 eligible preterm infants were enrolled. Three infants $(8.1\%)$ died from causes common in preterm infants (necrotizing enterocolitis, sepsis) during the study period. 14 infants had administration stop prior to 28 days, including the 3 infants who died, 3 infants who had care later withdrawn, and 8 infants who were transferred to another hospital. Study infants received a median of 17 [IQR 9, 20] days of intranasal milk treatment; 36 $(97\%)$ received at least 3 days (our minimum target). 191 preterm infants with IVH were included as historic controls; 21 infants $(11.0\%)$ died before discharge. Patient characteristics of intervention and control group participants are shown in Table 1. Groups were similar in gestational age and birthweight. However, there were more infants with severe IVH (Grades 3–4) in the intervention group.

Short-term in-NICU morbidity data are shown in Table 2. There was no significant difference in PHVD. One infant in each group required surgical intervention for PHVD. The intervention group was noted to have significantly more periventricular leukomalacia (PVL) (OR: 12.50 [1.35, 115.55], $p=0.03;$ ) and necrotizing enterocolitis (OR: 2.27 [1.12, 4.63], $p=0.02\$ when weighted for confounding baseline characteristics of IVH grade, birth gestational age, and sex.  

Longer term outcomes for the 4, 8, and 12 months corrected age follow-up clinic visits are shown in Table 3. Intervention infants were less likely to have fine motor delays on PFMAI (OR 0.35 [0.14, 0.90], $p=0.03\mathrm{.}$ ) and were more likely to have prolonged exposure to parent HM after NICU discharge (OR 2.15 [1.04, 4.46], $p=0.04)$ ). This safety and feasibility study was not powered for outcomes, but most weighted ORs favored the intervention group but were not statistically significant. Eighteen month corrected age results are shown in Table 4. Again, although unpowered for outcomes and not statistically significant, six of eight weighted ORs for neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18 months favored the intervention group despite additional unweighted risks (beyond the weighted IVH grade, gestational age, and sex) for worse outcome such as necrotizing enterocolitis [28] and bronchopulmonary dysplasia [29]. Unweighted median and interquartile range Bayley III language, cognitive, and motor composite scores were also calculated for each group. Language composite: 85 (74–96) for intervention vs 89 (72–102) for historic; cognitive: 90 (85–100) vs 90 (80–100); motor: 84 (79–94) vs 91 (74–95). All longterm data points had missing data (see $N$ in tables) given the Covid-19 pandemic with in-person clinic closures, which affected historic controls more given the timing of their 18 month corrected age with the height of the pandemic. PFMAI testing at 4 months had more missing data in both cohorts than other parameters due to both the pandemic as well as differences in standard testing schedules at the two NICUs (ie one NICU did not test PFMAI at 4 months), with data only available for $48.4\%$ of surviving intervention patients and $19.4\%$ of historic controls.  

# DISCUSSION  

IVH is an important cause of brain injury in preterm infants, but available therapies that target the actual injury are currently lacking. SCs have been shown to have neuroprotective and regenerative benefits, and their presence in fresh HM suggests a potential modality of brain injury treatment [1, 5, 8]. In this pilot study, the intervention group had significantly more high-grade IVH, and overall more morbidities, with higher rates of necrotizing enterocolitis and non-significantly higher rates of bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy of prematurity, and infection, although not higher rates of in-NICU death. Given that there are no current treatments and severe IVH is associated with poor outcomes, we presumed that parents of infants with more severe (grades 3-4) IVH would be more likely to consent for a firstof-its kind study. Indeed this was true, but as we were aware of the imbalance in IVH severity in the cohort, we used weighting to control for this. Short-term morbidities related to IVH weighted for degree of IVH, birth gestational age, and sex (but not the above diagnoses such as necrotizing enterocolitis) did not show differences in PHVD or need for surgical therapy. PVL and porencephalic cysts were more common in the intervention group, despite weighting for degree of IVH. These findings are in contrast to Keller’s retrospective study in Germany, in which 16 subjects with severe IVH who received IHM outside of a research study were compared to 15 subjects who did not receive IHM, and reported lower rates of PHVD $(71\%$ vs $91\%$ and moderate/large porencephalic cysts $(21\%$ vs $58\%$ ) [13]. Our cohort had overall lower rates of PHVD (as a whole and when just including grade $3{-}4\mathrm{\sf~IVH})$ than the Keller study, but we did not see lower rates in the intervention group. Of note, the intervention group was twice as likely to have PHVD on initial ultrasound prior to study entry $15.4\%$ vs $8.8\%$ ), perhaps signifying more symptomatic, larger IVH. Finally, we included any porencephalic cysts in our analysis, unlike the “moderate to large” cysts included by Keller, so it is difficult to compare our data.  

Despite the risk factors of relatively high rates of PVL and porencephalic cysts in the intervention group compared to our historic cohort, although not statistically significant, long-term outcomes, especially categorized 18 month Bayley cognitive scores, as well as sitting and walking all favored the intervention group. Unweighted cognitive Bayley scores were the same in both groups despite markedly different rates of severe IVH, which was a surprising and exciting finding. There were no statistical differences in autism screening (MCHAT) between groups. Study intervention infants were nearly twice as likely to still be receiving parent milk at 4 and 8 months corrected age, which could also potentially independently improve brain injury and outcomes, confounding our results [17, 30]. This large difference suggests that perhaps teaching parents more about HM and HM SCs (as was done in the consent process of our study) may be motivating for long-term lactation, which has previously been reported in a Polish cohort [31]. It is unclear why long-term outcomes favored the intervention group when short-term outcomes like PHVD did not, but one could posit that if HM bioactives and SCs were indeed acting to reduce brain injury, that time was needed to see any effects.  

Limitations to this study include the small sample size of participants, as it was a safety and feasibility study, so not powered for clinical outcomes. In addition, this study took place during the early COVID-19 pandemic, which led to challenges in study recruitment and milk transport due to family and staff visitor restrictions, as well as large challenges in follow-up clinic participation as many clinic appointments were virtual without standardized testing. For example, some of the outcomes that could only be assessed in-person, such as PFMAI or Bayley testing, had $78\%$ and $67.5\%$ missing data between the two groups (with missing data skewed towards the historic cohort due to their age during the height of pandemic lockdowns). The only previously reported use of IHM by Keller had no long-term follow-up data reported [13]. In addition, due to reduced parent presence and in some cases tolerance to the full volume intranasal administration in the smallest subjects, not all study subjects received the goal dose of 1.6 mL/day [18] and duration varied with day of recruitment and visitation/milk availability. In addition, parent milk likely contains a range of SC concentrations as well as bioactives [4, 20]. With these variations of HM SC concentration and milk availability, we had attempted to maximize SC dose a-priori with a long duration of administration up to day 28 of life. Therefore, unlike in animal studies that utilize a single known SC dose, in this pragmatic study, subjects presumably received a range of SC “doses” via HM, along with the bioactive, antiinflammatory milieu found in fresh HM [32]. Given the small sample size, we did not attempt to correlate potential dose to outcomes, but the question of how much and for how long to potentially give intranasal milk remains a question. There currently are no reported animal or pre-clinical models of IHM of which we are aware, which may be a helpful next step to suggest duration of therapy as well as prove pathological and functional improvements. Given the overall tolerance by preterm infants and acceptance by families and staff [18], the relatively easy availability of fresh HM, and the suggestion of potentially improved outcomes in a high-risk population, eventual larger, multicenter trials to study efficacy would be ideal. IHM therapy could also be explored for other sources of neonatal brain injury, such as hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy.

Preterm infants with IVH treated with fresh intranasal HM showed potential for improved long-term outcomes in this nonpowered intervention study. Future pre-clinical and human studies are needed to better understand how intranasal HM can impact short- and long- term outcomes for this high-risk population that currently has few treatment options.
