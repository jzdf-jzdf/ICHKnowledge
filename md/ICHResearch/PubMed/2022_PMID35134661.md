# Effect of remote ischemic preconditioning on cerebral oxygen saturation in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage: Secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial

# ABSTRACT  

Remote ischemic preconditioning (RIPC) can ameliorate cerebral vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia and improve neurological outcomes in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). Monitoring of regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rScO2) during the critical phase after aSAH can help detect ischemia and assess the effect of RIPC intervention. We investigated the effect of RIPC on rScO2 in patients with aSAH. Our study was a single-center, prospective, parallel-group, randomized pilot trial. After approval by institutional ethics committee, consenting patients $\left(\mathtt{n}=25\right..$ ) with aSAH presenting within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ of ictus and scheduled for surgical clipping of cerebral aneurysm were randomized 1:1 to true RIPC (inflation of upper extremity blood pressure cuff thrice to $30\mathrm{mmHg}$ above systolic blood pressure for $5\mathrm{min}$ or sham RIPC (inflation of blood pressure cuff thrice to $30\mathrm{mmHg}$ for $5\mathrm{min}$ ). In this secondary analysis, our outcome measures assessed by a blinded observer were incidence of cerebral oxygen desaturation (COD) during 7–10 days after ictus and Glas­ gow outcome scale extended (GOSE) at discharge. The incidence of COD (decrease in $\mathrm{rScO2}>20\%$ from baseline) was lower in the RIPC group $(15.4\%$ versus $33.3\%$ ; $\mathbf{p}=0.378$ . The absence of ipsilateral COD resulted in a higher mean GOSE (estimate 1.15, $\mathbf{p}=0.015$ ). The RIPC group had a higher mean GOSE compared to sham group (estimate 0.8, $\mathbf{p}=0.027)$ . This pilot trial demonstrated that RIPC has the potential to prevent COD in patients with aSAH. Larger trials with cerebral oxygenation as the primary outcome are needed to confirm our findings.  



# 1. Introduction  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is a potentially lethal consequence of intracranial aneurysm. Cerebral vasospasm and ischemia after aSAH contribute significantly to poor neurological out­ comes.[1] Focal ischemic preconditioning using brief periods of parent vessel occlusion followed by reperfusion during aneurysm surgery has shown to ameliorate brain tissue hypoxia.[2] Attempts to explore noninvasive, safe, and pre-emptive neuroprotective techniques have paved the way for investigating the clinical utility of remote ischemic pre­ conditioning (RIPC) in patients with aSAH.[3] Recent trials of RIPC in patients with cerebrovascular pathologies have shown promising results in terms of protection from cerebral ischemia[4] and transient improvement in cerebral hemodynamics with preservation of neuronal cell function.[5]  

The role of RIPC in improving cerebral oxygenation [6,7,8]in nonneurological settings has been demonstrated in earlier studies. Decrease in regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rScO2) as assessed using near infra-red spectroscopy (NIRS) technique can indicate cerebral hypoperfusion.[9] Monitoring of rScO2 has evolved as a crucial component of vasospasm surveillance [10] and assessment of response to angioplasty in the management of patients with aSAH.[11]

Reduction in cerebral cortical oxygen saturation by $3.9{-}6.4~\%$ from baseline was found to have $100\%$ sensitivity and $85.7\%$ specificity for diagnosing vasospasm.[12] We have previously demonstrated favorable long-term outcome in patients with good grade [World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) 1 and 2] aSAH and a tendency for vasospasm attenuation with RIPC.[13] In this study, we explored if RIPC reduces the incidence of cerebral oxygen desaturation (COD) and whether this translates into better outcomes. We also studied if an as­ sociation exists between COD and cerebral vasospasm. We hypothesized that RIPC minimizes the occurrence of cerebral hypoperfusion and COD, with resultant improvement in neurological outcome.  



# 2. Methods  



# 2.1. Study setting and participants  

After approval from the ethics committee of the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), Bengaluru, and regis­ tration with the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2017/06/ 008724), this prospective, parallel-group, single-center, pilot random­ ized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted from June 2017 to January 2019. The detailed trial methodology is published earlier.[3] In brief, consenting adult patients with WFNS 1 or 2 of aSAH scheduled for surgical clipping of the ruptured intracranial aneurysm during the study period were enrolled based on pre-defined patient inclusion criteria. An independent investigator performed a six-block randomization using computer-generated random sequencing. The study participants were allocated in a 1:1 ratio to receive either true RIPC or sham RIPC inter­ vention. The enrolled patient, treating physician, outcome assessor, and data analyst were all blinded to the study interventions. This is a sec­ ondary analysis of an RCT for which the primary outcome data have already been published. [13]  



# 2.2. Data collection  

The demographic and clinical data were recorded for all patients at baseline. Patient’s heart rate and blood pressure (BP) during interven­ tion and daily rScO2 through bifrontally placed NIRS sensors were monitored from the day of admission till 7 to 10 days after the ictus. The rScO2 was monitored every $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and at each time, the average of three readings over a period of five minutes was recorded. The rScO2 was measured bilaterally using the NONIN EQUANOX7600 cerebral oxim­ eter (Nonin Medical Inc., Plymouth, MN). The cerebral vasospasm was assessed by insonating the middle cerebral artery (MCA) on both the sides using transcranial Doppler (TCD) (Compumedics DWL USA Inc., California). The data regarding Glasgow outcome score extended (GOSE) at hospital discharge was collected from patient records.  



# 2.3. Study interventions  

The method of administration of RIPC (true or sham) included inflating upper limb BP cuff three times for $5\mathrm{min}$ to $30\mathrm{mmHg}$ above systolic BP in the true RIPC group and inflating BP cuff three times for 5 min to $30\mathrm{mmHg}$ in the sham RIPC group. All patients received assigned true or sham RIPC sessions every $^{48\mathrm{~h~}}$ from the day of recruitment till 7 to 10 days after ictus or until discharge, whichever was earlier.  



# 2.4. Outcome assessment  

The principal investigator, blinded to the group allocation and intervention, monitored the 24 hourly rScO2 measurements to diagnose COD.  



# 2.5. Statistical analysis  

Data was analyzed using R software version 3.5.3. packages and data representation were “rmcorr”,” ggplot2” and “lmerTest”. Correlation between rScO2and mean flow velocity data was conducted using repeated measures correlation. Agreement between presence of COD and presence of TCD based vasospasm was conducted using kappa test. Association between ipsilateral COD and GOSE was conducted using Mann Whitney $U$ test and linear regression (to account for confounding influence of group). Association of ipsilateral and contralateral rScO2 trends and intervention groups was performed using linear mixed effect modeling. Unstructured covariance structure was assumed for the same. $\mathrm{P}<0.05$ was taken as a level of statistical significance.  



# 3. Results  

Ninety patients admitted to NIMHANS with aSAH during the study period were screened for possible recruitment in the study. Amidst them, only 31 were suitable for recruitment satisfying our predefined inclusion criteria. A CONSORT flow diagram depicting the flow of patients into the study is published earlier. [3] The reasons for exclusion of 59 pa­ tients were: presentation beyond $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ of ictus $(\mathtt{n}=14)$ , poor WFNS grade at presentation $(\mathtt{n}=11$ ), poor trans-temporal window for inso­ nation using TCD $({\bfn}=13)$ , posterior circulation aneurysm $\left(\mathtt{n}=9\right)$ , endovascular coiling planned as the definitive treatment $(\mathtt{n}=9)$ , older than 65 years $(\mathtt{n}=2)$ , and absence of consent for participation in the study $(\mathtt{n}=1)$ ). Of the 31 eligible patients, consent was withdrawn in 3 patients following trial recruitment and 1 patient was discharged from the hospital before administration of pre-defined minimum number of study interventions (3 sessions of true/sham RIPC). Two patients reconsidered their choice of definitive treatment and opted for endo­ vascular coiling after the institution of one session of RIPC as study intervention. Thus, 25 patients completed all the study interventions as per randomization with 13 in the true RIPC group and 12 in the sham RIPC group.  

Baseline demographic parameters were comparable between the groups, as presented in the previously published paper.[13] No adverse effects (local arm bruising, cutaneous erythema, or limb edema) were noted in patients who received the RIPC interventions, as reported earlier.[13] Acom aneurysms were the most common diagnosis in both the groups. There was no difference in the distribution of aneurysm location between the groups $(\mathtt{p}=0.581)$ ).  

The baseline bilateral rScO2 values were similar in both the groups. None of the 25 patients had a baseline rScO2 value below 50 suggestive of cerebral hypoxia.  



# 3.1. Association of cerebral oxygen saturation trends with intervention group  

Due to small sample size, change of rScO2 over study time points was assumed to be linear. Side of surgery was added as covariate. Distribu­ tion of $\mathrm{rScO2}$ on the ipsilateral and contralateral sides over the study time points between the two groups can be observed in Fig. 1. The incidence of ipsilateral COD as determined by a $20\%$ reduction in rScO2 values compared to baseline [14] on any day throughout the measure­ ment period on either side was $15.4\%$ (2 patients) in the true RIPC group and $33.3\%$ (4 patients) in the sham RIPC group with a p value of 0.378. The RIPC group was found to have higher rScO2, with a mean difference (standard error) of 3.51 (1.12), and $\mathbf{p}=0.005$ . In aggregate, rScO2 was found to reduce by $0.77\%$ for each day after the ictus.  



# 3.2. Association between cerebral oxygen saturation and MCA flow velocity  

Analysis showed a poor negative correlation between rScO2 and MCA flow velocity with coefficients of $-0.175$ ( $95\%$ CL −0.323, $-0.18\$ ; $\mathbf{p}=0.029.$ ) and $-0.112$ ( $95\%$ CL $-0.266$ , 0.047; $\mathbf{p}=0.163)$ for ipsi­ lateral and contralateral sides, respectively. We also found no statisti­ cally significant agreement between incidence of TCD based vasospasm and incidence of COD $\mathbf{(kappa=0.33}$ , $\mathbf{P}=0.157.$ ). The COD was found in 6 $(24\%)$ patients, while TCD based vasospasm was found in 5 $(20\%)$ patients and concordance between the two was seen in 20 $(80\%)$ patients.

# 3.3. Association of cerebral oxygen desaturation and neurological outcome  

Ipsilateral COD was found to be significantly associated with poor GOSE at discharge; median [interquartile range] GOSE of 7 (7–7) in those with no COD and 4 (1–6) in those with $\operatorname{COD}(\mathbf{p}=0.005)$ . Associ­ ation of pooled incidence of COD (ipsilateral or contralateral) with GOSE at discharge, was found to be trending towards significance $\left(\mathbf{p}=\right.$ 0.061). The discharge GOSE was7 (7–7) in those with no COD (pooled) and 5 (1.75–6.75) in those with COD (pooled). Distribution of GOSE between COD statuses can be viewed in Fig. 2. Linear regression analysis showed that absence of ipsilateral COD resulted in a higher mean GOSE (estimate 1.15, $\mathbf{p}=0.015$ ). The RIPC group was found to have a higher mean GOSE at discharge compared to sham group (estimate 0.8, $\mathbf{p}=$ 0.027).  



# 4. Discussion  

We observed higher mean rScO2 values with RIPC in our study. There are only a few reports on the effect of RIPC on cerebral oxygen­ ation. One study noted improvements in cutaneous tissue oxygen satu­ ration and arterial capillary blood flow by $29\%$ and $35\%$ respectively with RIPC in healthy volunteers.[15] In another study involving patients with unilateral MCA stenosis ${\tt(n=10.}$ ) and healthy volunteers ${\left(\mathtt{n}=24\right)}$ , tissue oxygenation index (TOI) of the RIPC arm and the brain was assessed with NIRS technique. The authors reported a steady decline during RIPC followed by transient overshoot above the baseline after reperfusion in the arm TOI. However, no difference in brain TOI was seen before and after RIPC in both the groups. [16] An animal model study showed that RIPC performed before hypothermic circulatory ar­ rest conserved the cerebral oxygen tension during circulatory arrest which in clinical practice can translate to possibly prolonged safe operating time.[6]

A recent RCT demonstrated an increase in cerebral oxygenation and attenuation of ischemia–reperfusion injury in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty when RIPC was performed before application of tourniquet to the operative site.[7]Another RCT evaluating the effect of 10-minutes of lower limb RIPC administered thrice after the induction of anesthesia, demonstrated higher rScO2 levels after beach chair posi­ tioning for shoulder surgeries with fewer intraoperative COD episodes in patients receiving RIPC as compared to the control group.[8]  

None of our 25 patients had baseline cerebral hypoxia $({\bfr}Sc{\bfO}2<$ $50\%$ ) following aSAH. The incidence of COD as determined by a $20\%$ reduction in rScO2 values compared to baseline on any day spanning the measurement period was lower in the true RIPC group. Though not statistically significant in our pilot study, this difference in the incidence of COD between the true and sham RIPC groups can be clinically important if replicated in a larger trial, as even small benefit gained from this simple intervention can be critical in this vulnerable population of patients with aSAH.  

Improvement in rScO2 may be due to increase in pulmonary oxygenation as seen in the studies by Chung et al and may be mediated through the upregulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase and enhanced release of endothelial nitric oxide (NO). [6,7] This may further explain the preservation of cerebral oxygenation secondary to NOinduced improvement in cerebral perfusion attributable to RIPC. We too observed a concomitant reduction in the incidence of vasospasm (implying preservation of cerebral perfusion after aSAH) in patients receiving RIPC that might have contributed to improved neurological outcomes.[13] This explains the potential mechanism by which RIPC helps in maintenance of cerebral oxygenation and perfusion in patients with aSAH. Alternate protective mechanisms of RIPC proposed in aSAH patients include a combination of neuro-humoral and immunological pathways causing an altered cellular gene expression, nuclear methyl­ ation process and a resultant regulation of the cell cycle and immuno­ logical sequel.[17]  

In our study, 5 out of 25 patients demonstrated cerebral vasospasm on TCD evaluation while 6 out of 25 patients developed COD as assessed using NIRS technique. However, only 3 of these 6 patients had associ­ ated vasospasm. 2 patients had only TCD vasospasm with normal rScO2, and 3 had only COD with normal TCD findings.  

A pilot study involving six patients by Naidech et al. demonstrated discordance between rScO2 and angiographic or TCD based vasospasm. [18] Yokose et al. also reported similar findings in three patients with delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), who had all shown COD, as against TCD which remained normal in one. [19]  

The diagnostic accuracy of rScO2 monitoring was compared with TCD assessment and found to be better in predicting DCI in 46 patients with severe angiographic vasospasm, although this difference was not statistically significant (AUROC difference $=0.044$ , $\mathbf{p}=0.571$ , $95\%$ CI: −0.108–0.195). [20] The discordant findings in $20\%$ of patients in our study could be explained by the difference in intracerebral components monitored by the NIRS and the TCD. Vasospasm may not have been severe enough to cause COD in two out of five patients who demon­ strated high mean flow velocities, fulfilling the vasospasm criteria. Also, the NIRS is a regional cerebral oxygen saturation monitor. Isolated monitoring of the bifrontal areas may have precluded the diagnosis of COD in non-frontal areas. The COD in 2 out of 20 patients without vasospasm could have occurred due to other causes of DCI unrelated to vasospasm, namely, dysregulated cerebral vascular response, cortical spreading depolarizations, microvascular thrombosis, higher cerebral metabolic rate or ischemic or reperfusion injury as observed in 2 out of 9 patients in the case series reported by Maslehatyet al. [21]  

Earlier reports demonstrated the failure of rScO2 monitoring to reliably predict neurological outcome in patients with cerebral venous thrombosis,[22] patients who were brain dead,[23] and those with re­ turn of spontaneous circulation after cerebral hypoxia during cardio­ pulmonary resuscitation.[24] A systematic review of adult cardiac surgical patients demonstrated low-level evidence for linking NIRS technique based perioperative COD to postoperative neurologic com­ plications.[25]  

Studies evaluating the effect of rScO2 on neurological outcomes in patients with aSAH are scarce. In a study conducted by Burzynska et al., 38 patients with aSAH were monitored for the timing and duration of cerebral desaturation episodes (CDE) lasting for at least $30\mathrm{min}$ . An increased frequency and duration of $\mathrm{r}S\mathrm{c}O2<60\%$ , that occurred in $44\%$ of patients with severe aSAH, correlated with poor short-term outcome as demonstrated by low GOS scores (median GOS grade 3), in compar­ ison to those without CDE (median GOS grade 5).[26] In our study too, patients who developed COD were found to have poorer outcomes. Patients with intact ipsilateral rScO2 survived to lead better productive lives as reflected by their higher GOSE at hospital discharge. Preserva­ tion of neuronal oxygenation is integral for the sustenance of neuronal activity and function. This implies that despite the myriad complex biochemical and molecular changes that occur in the disease course following aSAH, an uncompromised cerebral oxygenation can serve to instill hope for a non-compromised recovery. These findings also high­ light the role of rScO2 in the multimodal neuromonitoring armamen­ tarium involved in the care of these patients.  

This is a first study to compare the effect of RIPC on rScO2 in patients with aSAH undergoing microsurgical clipping and to evaluate the impact of COD on the neurological outcomes. However, this study has certain limitations. The small sample size and secondary analysis nature of the study restricts the generalizability of the findings to wider pop­ ulation. The rScO2 monitoring was performed only once every $24\mathrm{{h}}$ for a prolonged period. Continuous monitoring would have more likely captured the episodes of COD making the assessment of impact on outcome more accurate. The regional nature of cerebral oxygenation detection using the NIRS monitor may preclude the inference about the global cerebral oxygenation status. The presence of extravascular blood may preclude the accuracy of rScO2 in the assessment of cerebral oxy­ gen saturation in patients with SAH. Despite these limitations, this study provides some insights into the interplay between COD, cerebral vaso­ spasm, and neurological outcome.  



# 5. Conclusions  

From our preliminary findings, it appears that RIPC has a protective role in the prevention of COD in patients with aSAH. The occurrence of COD after aSAH is associated with poor neurological outcome. RIPC is an easy, non-invasive, reproducible intervention with potential benefits and can be included as an adjunct to the standard aSAH management protocol. Larger RCTs with cerebral oxygenation as the primary outcome are needed to confirm our findings for its routine applicability in clinical practice.
