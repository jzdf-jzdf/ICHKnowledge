# Abstract  

Background: We performed this analysis to identify the rates, predictors and associated outcomes of unexpected neurosurgical evacuation in a multicenter randomized clinical trial, Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage (ATACH)-2.  

Methods: The ATACH 2 trial determined the efficacy of antihypertensive treatment in patients with spontaneous supratentorial intracerebral hemorrhages (ICHs) with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of ${\ge}5$ and intraparenchymal hematoma volume of $\textless60\mathrm{cm}3$ on initial computed tomographic (CT) scan. We determined the proportion of ICH patients requiring unanticipated surgical evacuation and identified baseline factors associated with evacuation.  

Results: Among the 992 subjects analyzed, 44 $(4.4\%)$ subjects required unanticipated surgical evacuation of hematoma. The proportion of subjects with initial GCS score of 13 or less was significantly higher among those who required surgical intervention ( $43.2\%$ vs $26.8\%$ , $\mathrm{p}{<}0.001\$ ). In the logistics regression analysis, hematoma volume $\geq18~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ (odds ratio [OR], 4.3, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI], 2.1-8.8) and right sided hematoma (OR, 2.8, $95\%$ CI 1.3-5.9) were significantly associated with surgical intervention. Age, location, GCS score strata, and allocated treatment (intensive versus standard systolic blood pressure (SBP) reduction) were not associated with surgical intervention.  Among the 44 patients who underwent surgical evacuation, death or disability at 3 months post randomization was seen in 32 $(73\%)$ of 44 subjects.  

Conclusions: In the large cohort of ATACH-2 subjects with good grade ICH, the rates of unanticipated surgical evacuation were low and was associated with a relatively high rate of death or disability at 3 months.  

# INTRODUCTION  

The guidelines from the American Heart Association (AHA)/American Stroke Association (ASA) states that the usefulness of surgery for most patients with supratentorial intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is not well established (Class IIb; Level of Evidence A) [1] but may be considered in deteriorating patients as a life-saving measure (Class IIb; Level of Evidence C). The possibility of surgical intervention at later intervals after presentation has direct relevance to intensity of monitoring and neurosurgical involvement particularly in patients who are good grade and do not have large hematomas with significant midline shift. Late surgical intervention in ICH and the factors surrounding it are not well described. This is important issue and knowledge of such processes would help clinicians caring for these patients to make clinical decisions and help advance care and understanding of this disease. To this end we isolated a cohort of previously observed patients, using a highly valued cohort from a large multicenter randomized clinical trial, Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage (ATACH)- 2, who did not need early surgical evacuation to determine the rate of late transition to emergent surgery. The goal was to identify some associated factors potentially contributing to the observed outcomes. This information would help develop clinical information valuable for future study of ICH with late deterioration and grant insight to clinical conditions as they exist today.  

# METHODS  

The methodology of this trial has been reported previously.[2-4] The trial was conducted the trial at 110 sites in the United States, Japan, China, Taiwan, South Korea, and Germany. The trial determined the efficacy of intensive versus standard antihypertensive treatment that was initiated within 4.5 hours after symptom onset and continued for the next 24 hours in patients  

with spontaneous supratentorial ICHs. Briefly, subjects included those aged $\geq18$ years with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of ${\ge}5$ at the time of arrival in the emergency department and with an intraparenchymal hematoma volume of $<60~\mathrm{cm}3$ on initial computed tomographic (CT) scan. Subjects were excluded if there was a previously known neoplasms, arteriovenous malformation, or aneurysms, or ICH considered to be related to trauma. Other exclusion criteria included ICH located in infratentorial regions such as pons or cerebellum, or extensive intraventricular hemorrhage blood completely fills one lateral ventricle or more than half of both ventricles. Subjects who were considered candidates for immediate surgical intervention by the neurosurgery service were excluded. Subjects with living wills which precluded aggressive intensive care unit (ICU) management were also excluded. Any patient screened, but not randomized, was tracked on the screen failure log. Potential subjects not enrolled for pending "immediate hematoma evacuation" were otherwise only captured on screen failure logs, along with other reasons for exclusions.  

The goal of treatment was to reduce and maintain the hourly minimum systolic blood pressure(SBP) in the range of 140 to $179\mathrm{mmHg}$ in the standard-treatment group and in the range of 110 to $139\mathrm{mmHg}$ in the intensive-treatment group throughout the period of 24 hours after randomization. After randomization, intravenous infusion nicardipine was administered and if the systolic blood-pressure level was higher than the target, despite infusion of the maximum dose of nicardipine for 30 minutes, a prespecified second agent (intravenous labetalol, diltiazem or urapidil) was used. A CT scan of the head was obtained at 24 hours after the initiation of treatment. Baseline and 24-hour CT scans were forwarded to the core image analysis center for analysis of the site of hemorrhage, the presence or absence of blood in the ventricles, and the volume of the parenchymal hematoma. Serious adverse events were systematically reported up to 3 months after randomization. Non-serious adverse events were systematically reported up to day 7 or hospital discharge, whichever came first. Neurological deterioration was defined as a decrease of 2 or more points on GCS [corrected for intubation by scoring grid] or an increase of 4 or more points on NIHSS score that was not related to sedation/hypnotic use and was sustained for at least 8 hours). Hematoma expansion (defined as an increase of $23\%$ was calculated using baseline and 24 hours (between 18 and 30 hours post-randomization with non-missing time) CT scans.  

ATACH 2 did not have a stringent policy for surgical intervention. The practice of surgical evacuation in ATACH 2 was expected to be based on principles outlined in ASA/AHA and European Stroke Initiative (EUSI) guidelines.[5, 6] The determinations were made independently by the individual neurosurgeons at the participating sites. Some standardization was provided by an independent oversight committee (IOC) [7] which monitored the adequacy of adherence to the protocol and the principles and intensity of overall care at all sites.  

Follow-up after discharge included telephone contact at 1 month and in-person clinical evaluation at 3 months. During the telephone interview, the site staff obtained information regarding serious adverse events and deaths. Modified Rankin scale was ascertained at the 3- month visit by a qualified investigator who was blinded to randomization, treatment, or inhospital clinical treatment of the patient.  

# Statistical analysis  

We compared the demographic and clinical characteristics of subjects who did and did not require surgical evacuation using chi-square test for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. We adjusted for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction. We dichotomized the initial hematoma volume $\mathbf{\left(\ge18~cm3\right)}$ versus $\mathbf{<18~cm3}$ ) based on optimal relationship between hematoma volume and surgical evacuation identified by plotting of receiver operating curve (ROC). We entered variables of interest into a logistic regression model to identify predictors of un anticipated surgical evacuation. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. released 2015.  

# RESULTS  

A total of 299 $(3.5\%)$ of 8,532 subjects who were screened for inclusion in the trial were excluded because of the urgent need of surgical evacuation. Among the 1,000 subjects randomized, complete data were available for 992 subjects. Among the 992 subjects analyzed, 44 $(4.4\%)$ subjects required unexpected surgical evacuation of hematoma (Table 1). The mean age ± standard deviation (SD) of subjects who required surgical evacuation was $57.3{\pm}11.6\$ years; 25 $(56.8\%)$ were men. The mean time interval between randomization and surgical evacuation was 1.6 days (±SD 3.7 days).  

Subjects who required surgical evacuation were less likely to have previous history of stroke or transient ischemic attack and more likely to be active cocaine users. There were no significant differences in mean SBP between subjects who did or did not require surgical evacuation. The proportion of subjects with initial GCS score of 13 or less was significantly higher among those who required surgical intervention ( $43.2\%$ vs $26.8\%$ , $\mathsf{p}{=}.01$ ). The proportion of subjects with initial hematoma volume of ${\ge}18~\mathrm{cm}3$ ( $68.2\%$ vs $27.1\%$ ) as measured at local site was significantly higher among those who required surgical evacuation. There was a higher  

proportion of subjects with right sided parenchymal ICH and those with basal ganglionic hemorrhages among those who received surgical intervention. In the logistics regression analysis, hematoma volume $\geq18~\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ (odds ratio [OR], 4.3, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI], 2.1-8.8) and right sided hematoma (OR, 2.8, $95\%$ CI 1.3-5.9) were significantly associated with surgical intervention. Age, location, GCS score strata, and allocated treatment (intensive versus standard SBP reduction) were not associated with surgical intervention.  

The rate of neurological deterioration was significantly higher (25 of 44 subjects versus 96 of 948 subjects, $\mathtt{p}{<}0.001$ ) but not rate of hematoma expansion (20 of 44 subjects versus 456 of 948 subjects, $\mathrm{p}\mathrm{=}0.8$ ) within 24 hours higher among subjects who required surgical evacuation. The proportion of subjects requiring intraventricular catheter and mechanical ventilation was significantly higher among subjects who required surgical evacuation.  

Among the 44 patients who underwent surgical evacuation, death or disability at 3 months post randomization was seen in 32 of 44 $(72.7\%)$ subjects. Eight of 44 $18.2\%)$ subjects had died within 3 months. Among the 948 patients who did not undergo surgical evacuation, death or disability at 3 months post randomization was seen in 331 of 948 $(34.9\%)$ subjects. Fifty five of 948 $(5.8\%)$ ) subjects had died within 3 months.  

# DISCUSSION  

# Summary of findings  

A very low rate of $4.4\%$ for unexpected neurosurgical evacuation was observed in the ATACH 2 multicenter clinical trial involving good grade patients with ICH. The time interval between randomization and surgical evacuation was 1.6 days (±SD 3.7 days). The rate of surgical  

evacuation was significantly higher among patients with an initial GCS score of 13 or less and those with initial hematoma volume of ${\ge}18\mathrm{cm}3$ . The proportion of patients with neurological deterioration was significantly higher among those who required surgical evacuation. Three fourths of the patients who underwent surgical evacuation experienced death or disability at 3 months. There were no significant differences in initial mean SBP between subjects who did or did not require surgical intervention. Part of this lack of relationship may be due to inconsistent relationship between initial SBP and variables such as neurological deterioration and hematoma expansion.[8-10] The study provides some insight into the characteristics of patients chosen for surgical treatment and provides evidence for the poor outcome of patients managed in this manner.  

# Principles of surgical evacuation in ATACH 2 trial  

# ATACH 2 protocol recommended following the principles outlined in ASA/AHA and EUSI  

guidelines.[5, 6] The AHA/ASA guidelines in 2007 [5] had stated that routine evacuation of supratentorial ICH within 96 hours of ictus is not recommended (Class III, Level of Evidence A) with possible exception for patients presenting with lobar clots within 1 cm of the surface. No clear evidence at present indicates that ultra-early craniotomy improves functional outcome or mortality rate. The updated guideline from the AHA/ASA in 2015 [1] recommended that for most patients with supratentorial ICH, the usefulness of surgery is not well established (Class IIb; Level of Evidence A). A policy of early hematoma evacuation is not clearly beneficial compared with hematoma evacuation when patients deteriorate (Class IIb; Level of Evidence A). Supratentorial hematoma evacuation in deteriorating patients might be considered as a lifesaving measure (Class IIb; Level of Evidence C). The EUSI guidelines in 2006 had

recommended surgical evacuation in ICH was superficial (hematoma is 1 cm or less from surface and does not reach the basal ganglia) or if there is neurological deterioration in consciousness (GCS 9-12 to ≤8.[6] EUSI guidelines recommended in 2014[11] that there is no evidence to support surgical intervention on a routine basis to improve outcome after supratentorial ICH in comparison with conservative management, but early surgery may be of value for patients with a GCS score 9–12 (moderate strength recommendation). The recommendation was based on meta-analysis of individual patient data, based on 2186 patients recruited in 8 out of 14 randomized clinical trials published 1985–2012 before Surgical Trial in Intracerebral Hemorrhage (STICH) II, found that surgery seemed effective in patients with a higher consciousness level [GCS score 9–12] and in patients who were randomized to surgery within eight-hours of ICH symptom onset.[12] The Japanese guidelines in 2009 recommended surgical evacuation in patients with putaminal hemorrhage if hematoma volume $>31~\mathrm{cm}3$ with moderate neurological deficits and severe mass effect. Stereotactic evacuation was recommended in patients with altered level of consciousness.[13]  

# Comparison with previous studies  

A total 226 $(6.9\%)$ of 3,233 patients and 83 of 372 patients with large ICH volume $(>30\mathrm{mL})$ ) underwent surgical evacuation with 7 day after randomization in a pooled analysis of the pilot Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage Trials (INTERACT)1 $\mathbf{\rho}_{\mathrm{n}}=$ 404) and INTERACT2 $\mathbf{\tilde{n}}=2\mathbf{,}839\mathbf{\tilde{\rho}}$ ) which involved patients with ICH randomized within 6 hours of symptom onset.[14] Significant predictors of those patients who underwent surgery were younger age, severe neurological deficit (lower GCS score, and NIHSS score $\geq15$ ), baseline  

ICH volume $>30\mathrm{cm}3$ , and intraventricular hemorrhage. The INTERACT 2 trial excluded patients with very high likelihood of death within $^{24\mathrm{h}}$ in patients with high severity based on investigator judgement, if they were in a deep coma (defined as a GCS score of 3 to 5), if they had a massive hematoma with a poor prognosis, or if early surgery to evacuate the hematoma was planned.[15]  Of the 529 patients with a spontaneous supratentorial ICH within $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ of symptom onset in STICH randomized to initial conservative management, 140 $(26\%)$ underwent surgery after an initial period of observation (median period 60 hours, interquartile range 27–99 hours).[16] The main reasons for these patients requiring surgical evacuation were rebleeding and neurological deterioration. Twenty-one percent of 291 patients randomized to initial medical management in STICH II ultimately underwent surgery, with the most common reason described as patient deterioration[17] after 46 hours (21·7–81·9; 5·0–266·5) of randomization. Comparison of patients in the initial conservative treatment group who had surgery with those who did not demonstrated that they were more likely to undergo surgery if they had a paralyzed limb at randomization, lower GCS score, or larger hematoma. The higher rate of surgical evacuation in STICH and STICH 2 was probably attributable to high proportion of patients with initial GCS $<13$ and larger mean hematoma volumes compared with ATACH 2 trial.  

# Limitations and strengths of the current study  

The main strength of our study is that the data was collected prospectively and is part of the ATACH 2 study which represents a large cohort of ICH patients. The post hoc analysis examined subsets of the data accrued in the setting of ATACH 2 trial in order to answer clinically relevant questions that were not addressed in the pre-specified data analysis plan. Such analysis may identify groups of patients who may respond differently (undergo surgical evacuation) than other groups. [18, 19] The predictors of surgical evacuation identified are based on observed associations. We were unable to control for bias due to variation in regards to patient selection for surgical evacuation between sites. An analysis of the screening logs from the STICH network of 3893 ICH patients in 42 centers demonstrated variations in practices with greater agreement regarding surgical evacuation in patients who were younger, had a lower GCS score, and had a lobar hematoma. [20] The small number of patients did not allow identification of predictors of favorable outcomes in those who underwent surgical evacuation (to avoid type II error). [21] We acknowledge that the subgroup hypothesis or the direction of effect was not pre-specified for this analysis. Therefore, these are exploratory data analyses for hypothesis generation and extrapolation of these results to individual patients may be premature.  

# Future research  

Death or disability at 3 months post randomization was seen in 32 $(73\%)$ of the 44 patients who underwent surgical evacuation. The rate of death or disability was higher than the rates observed in minimally invasive catheter evacuation followed by thrombolysis (MISTIE) trial. [22] A total of $56\%$ of patients in the MISTIE group had achieved a mRS score of 0–3 at 365 days post randomization even though the patients had an initial GCS score of 14 or less and initial hematoma volume was ${\ge}30\mathrm{cm}3$ as per protocol. The MISTIE protocol required placement of a cannula through a burr hole into the hematoma followed by repeated aspiration and administration of thrombolytics through the cannula. The MISTIE approach may offer a better intervention compared with standard surgical evacuation in patients with ICH who require unplanned surgical evacuation. There also appeared to be a preference towards right sided intraparenchymal hematomas to be selected for surgical intervention which may not be based on objective evidence. Infarction of the dominant (left) hemisphere has often been used to exclude patients from receiving hemicraniectomy to avoid poor quality of life due to aphasia. [23] However, in a meta-analysis found that the benefit of hemicraniectomy in patients with large infarction for preventing death or moderate-to-severe disability was similar regardless of the presence or absence of aphasia at baseline. [24] Further trials or prospective observational studies using comparative effectiveness research leveraging variability in care and surgical management are needed.  

# Conclusion  

In the large cohort of ATACH-2 subjects with ICH, the rate of unexpected surgical evacuation was low but was associated with a relatively high rate of death or disability at 3 months. This study was designed to build future studies and cannot be used to make clinical decisions today outside of the studied cohort.
