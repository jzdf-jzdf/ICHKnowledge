# Diagnostic Sensitivity of Plasma Endothelin-1 for Cerebral Vasospasm After Subarachnoid Hemorrhage, a Multicentre Double-Blind Study  

Judith Bellapart1,2,3 $\textcircled{1}$ , Fatima Nasrallah4, James Winearls5,   
Melissa Lassig-Smith1,2,3, Janine Stuart1,2,3, Rob Boots6, Craig Winter7,

# Abstract  

The purpose of this study is to establish the diagnostic sensitivity of Endothelin-1 for risk stratification and screening of clinical vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage.  

This is a multicentre, observational study, correlating daily blood Endothelin-1 with clinical variables. Binary logistic regression used to examine if Endothelin-1 levels could be used to predict clinical vasospasm. Bivariate modelling used to explore associations between patient characteristics and vasospasm. A Receiver Operating Curve used to explore cut-off values for Endothelin-1. Sensitivity and specificity was used to validate the cut-point found in the pilot study. A total of 96 patients were enrolled over two years. Median Endothelin-1 was higher for patients who experienced clinical vasospasm except for day-5, where median endothelin for patients without vasospasm was higher (3 $\therefore6~\mathsf{IQR}=5.3\AA,$ ), compared to patients with vasospasm $3.31\mathrm{QR}=8.5\$ ) although differences were not significant. The Receiver Operating Curve analysis confirmed that day-5 Endothelin-1 was not a good indicator of vasospasm, with an area under the curve of 0.506 ( $95\%$ CI: 0.350-0.663, $\pmb{\mathscr{p}}=0.93\pmb{8})$ ). The levels of Endothelin-1 in blood do not discriminate patients who may develop symptomatic vasospasm. The high variability in Endothelin-1 levels, aligns with the pathophysiological variability of most biomarkers, decreasing their ability to predict a clinical event.  

# Keywords  

vasospasm, endothelin, subarachnoid hemorrhage, biomarkers  

# Introduction  

Spontaneous aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH) is a common cause of stroke among young populations. There are currently no measures to modulate the natural history of this disease nor to predict those patients who will develop vasospasm. Although SAH morbidity and mortality has been directly correlated to the initial thickness of the clot and the development of clinical vasospasm;1-7 the pathogenesis of cerebral vasospasm remains unclear. Previous studies using Endothelin (ET) antagonists8-10 demonstrated a relation between the expression of the Endothelin receptors and the development of cerebral vasospasm, but such relation was not causal. Studies demonstrated a significant reduction on vasospasm with ET1 blockade using Clazosentan but those failed to demonstrate outcome benefit.10 In addition, levels of ET1 in plasma have been used as a daily biomarker predicting patients that over the following days do develop neurological deficits secondary to cerebral vasospasm.11-14 Juvela et al.15,16 reported a significant correlation between plasma levels of ET1, delayed ischemic neurological deficits (DIND) and cerebral stroke related to vasospasm. In these studies, the investigators described a peak level of ET1 in plasma on day-5 after bleed and proposed its applicability as a screening marker. Subsequently, a pilot study17 replicating Juvela’s methodology, demonstrated comparable results; it found that a plasma threshold of ET1 of $3.2\pm0.8~\mathrm{pcg/mL}$ on day-5 following SAH was significantly associated with the development of symptomatic vasospasm, but the study was not powered to assess diagnostic utility. Following same methodology, this current study is powered to assess the diagnostic sensitivity of plasma ET1 in patients with SAH, with the aim of identifying a biomarker that could be used as screening tool to identify patients at risk of developing clinical vasospasm; a biomarker that could also be used as a tool to rationalise resources and allocate patients in areas of higher clinical monitoring and to offer the possibility of establishing early interventions that improve neurological outcomes.  

# Material and Methods  

The primary objective was to investigate the ability of ET1 in plasma as a biomarker that discriminates patients with SAH at higher risk to develop clinical vasospasm.  

This study was approved by the Australian Institutional Ethics’ Committee. Inclusion and management of patients complied with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki’s recommendations. The manuscript was prepared according to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.  

# Study Participants and Protocol  

Patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) with SAH were recruited consecutively in two tertiary university teaching hospitals’ ICU (The Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital and The Gold Coast University Hospital, Queensland, Australia). Patients were daily screened for eligibility by each site’s research coordinators; following waiver of consent or informed consent by surrogate decision makers, patients were enrolled and followed until ICU discharge. Data was collected and entered the study database by local research coordinators. Cerebral vasospasm was defined following standard radiological criteria, reported by radiologists and clinical criteria by each primary intensivist, both specialists were blinded to ET1 levels.  

# Hypothesis  

A specific threshold in the plasma concentration of ET1 may be a predictor of cerebral vasospasm, preceding the development of symptoms and prior to the establishment of cerebral infarct.  

# Aims of the Study  

Primary endpoint: Establishing the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of plasma ET1 for the prediction of clinical cerebral vasospasm.  

Secondary endpoint: Defining the time lag between plasma levels of ET1 and clinical vasospasm, upon establishment of the primary endpoint.  

# Variables  

Qualitative variables included: Fisher grade on admission (utilising the non-modified Fisher scale as classified prior to the implementation of the updated Fisher classification); World Federation Neuro Surgical (WFNS) classification on admission; daily Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) and the description of any new neurological deficit. Clinical vasospasm was defined as a new neurological deficit coinciding to a vascular territory with radiological vasospasm and not attributed to any other pathology (infarct, hemorrhage, encephalopathy, seizures, other extracranial processes). Radiological vasospasm was defined as a new vascular narrowing of $<25\%$ of the vessel lumen diameter (defined as “mild” radiological vasospasm) or 25 to $50\%$ (“moderate”) or $>50\%$ (“severe”).18 Both recruiting centres utilised Computerised Angiography (CTA) and Digital Subtracted Angiography (DSA) for the diagnosis and monitoring of radiological vasospasm and the confirmation of clinical vasospasm. The choice of the specific investigation was dependant upon the foreseeable need of endo-arterial vasodilation or the need to exclude the presence of small vessel vasospasm.  

Quantitative variables included: Daily plasma concentrations of ET1 over the first 7 days after bleed.  

# Eligibility Criteria  

Inclusion criteria: All SAH patients admitted to Intensive Care during the first $^{36\mathrm{~h~}}$ after initial bleed.  

Exclusion criteria: Patients admitted ${>}36\mathrm{h}$ after initial hemorrhage; pregnancy; traumatic SAH; patients deemed unlikely to survival for more than 7 days.  

# Et-1 Assay in Plasma  

Blood samples were collected daily from an arterial indwelling catheter, centrifuged at 2850 R.P.M (1505 G) for $10\mathrm{min}$ (Spintron-GT25, Victoria, Australia) and the supernatant stored at $-80~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ according to the assay’s manufacturer recommendations. Plasma ET1 was measured using the R&D System (DET-100-Quantikine, ELISA, Minneapolis, USA) as per manufacturer’s instructions. All assays were completed using the same batch, with individual samples analysed in triplicate (and averaged to determine the result). Absorbance was measured at $450\mathrm{nm}$ for $30\mathrm{min}$ after the addition of substrate and stop solution. Unknowns and quality controls values were estimated using a four-parameter logistic fit calibration curve. All analyses were completed by one analyst at the School of Medical Science, Griffith University, in a blinded fashion.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Following the results of our pilot study in which ET1 in plasma significantly discriminated patients developing clinical vasospasm at a level of $3.3\mathrm{mcg/dL}$ on day 5 after bleed; in this multicentre study the sample size calculation estimated that a total of 88 subjects would be needed to detect an area underneath the curve (AUC) of at least 0.7 with $90\%$ power and level of significance of 0.05 and assuming that a $25\%$ of patients admitted with SAH would experience clinical vasospasm. Therefore, to account for missing data or patient withdrawal our aim was a sample size of 100 patients.  

ET1 was described using medians, minimum and maximum. ET1 was log transformed, the geometric means back transformed, a $95\%$ confidence intervals were used to plot descriptive statistics between vasospasm-groups. Demographic variables between groups were explored using chi-square or independent t-tests. Fishers exact tests were used if the assumptions of chi-square were not met. Assumptions of normality between groups and equal variance for t-tests were explored using descriptive statistics and plots. Binary logistic regression was used to examine if ET1 levels could be used to predict clinical vasospasm. Logistic regression requires 10 cases per predictor variable; therefore, modelling was limited due to the small number of patients experiencing vasospasm. Bivariate modelling was used to explore associations between patient characteristics and vasospasm. The odds (OR) of clinical vasospasm for day-5 ET1 were adjusted by patient characteristics and one variable at a time to examine confounding. A Receiver Operating Curve (ROC) curve was then used to explore cut-off values for ET1 at day $^{-5}$ , with AUC and $95\%$ confidence intervals reported. Sensitivity and specificity were used to validate a cut-point of $3.3\mathrm{pg/ml}$ found in the previous pilot study. Missing data was explored for patterns using chi square and t-tests. Missing data was deleted pairwise in analyses, with a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation to impute ET1 data. ET1 analysis was stratified by day, therefore no repeated measures were used, however, to account for multiple comparisons a significance level of $p^{<0.001}$ was used.  

# Results  

During the study period a total of 110 patients with SAH were admitted to the participating ICUs. Of these, 100 patients met study eligibility criteria and 96 $(96\%)$ were enrolled in the study of which 1 patient’s samples were not available. Patients’ demographics are shown in a supplemental table. Similar characteristics were observed between vasospasm groups (Table 1) with patients experiencing vasospasm aged 57.9 (SD 9.6) compared to patients without 58.6 (SD 12.9) $p{=}0.799$ . Median ET1 was higher for patients who experience vasospasm throughout the seven-day period, except for day-5, where median ET1 for patients without vasospasm was slightly higher $(3.6~\mathrm{pg/ml}$ , $\mathrm{IQR}=5.3\$ ), compared to patients with vasospasm $(3.3~\mathrm{pg/ml}$ , $\mathrm{IQR}=8.5$ ) shown in Table 2, although such difference was not statistically significant. There was a larger variation in ET1 ranges for patients without vasospasm, with narrower ranges observed in patients who had vasospasm (Figure 1). The log transformed data, showed a similar pattern in the data, with mean ET1 patients with vasospasm slightly higher on day 1, 2 and 6, with very similar means observed between vasospasm groups between days 3 to 5 (Figure 2) with day-5 ET1 geometric means of 2.9 $95\%$ CI: 2.0, 4.1) for patients without vasospasm and 3.1 $95\%$ CI: 1.7, 5.5) for patients without vasospasm.

In general, although not statistically significant, patients with higher WFNS $(\mathrm{OR}=2.790$ , CI: 0.816-9.538, $p{=}0.102;$ ), Fisher grade $(\mathrm{OR}=1.321\$ , CI: 0.481-3.629, $p=0.590)$ and females $(\mathrm{OR}=1.495\$ , CI:0.447-5.005, $p{=}0.514$ ) tended to have greater odds of a having vasospasm (Table 1). # Discussion  

In this study we have demonstrated that the temporal pattern as well as the average plasma concentrations of ET1 during the first seven days after SAH, do not predict the development of neurological deficits due to cerebral vasospasm.  

This is a multicentre study following a pilot study17 which demonstrated that levels of ET1 in plasma were higher among patients who developed clinical vasospasm as opposed to patients without it; with a difference that was statistically significant. This pilot study also demonstrated that an ET1 of $3.3~\mathrm{pcg/mL}$ in plasma on day 5, had a ROC with a sensitivity and specificity of $78\%$ for the diagnosis of clinical vasospasm, but these results were not reproduced in this larger study.

This multicentre study was performed to further investigate the pilot data with an adequate sample size and to ascertain the diagnostic sensibility of ET1 as a predictor biomarker for cerebral vasospasm.  

Currently typical practice is for patients to have a diagnostic subtracted angiography for the confirmation of cerebral vasospasm, but this is often triggered by the presence of neuroworsening (a terminology defined as a new and sustained drop in GCS of two or more points). This study was designed to evaluate ET1 as a biomarker capable to discriminate patients developing clinical vasospasm prior to the development of symptoms, enabling early treatment. Furthermore, with current trends towards early discharge from ICU, we hypothesised that patients could benefit of a biomarker that discriminated those at higher risk of developing cerebral vasospasm to assist in the decision of where to allocate these patients, while rationalising resources.  

Our results are consistent with previous investigations that show a trend to higher mean levels of ET1 in plasma among patients who develop clinical vasospasm. However, this trend was not significant and may reflect limited statistical power. The median ET1 among both groups was also higher within the vasospasm group, but with a considerable variability in ET1 levels; the cut-off ET1 of $3.3\mathrm{pcg}/\mathrm{mL}$ on day-5 after SAH had a ROC curve with a $50\%$ sensitivity and $47\%$ specificity for the diagnosis of clinical vasospasm which is too low to be utilised in clinical practice. In addition, our results also show a consistent variability within baseline levels of ET1, without the capacity to establish a narrow baseline threshold on day 1 after bleed.  

The main limitations of this study relate to the nature of ET1 as biomarker and its demonstrated variability, it’s non-linear expression and possibly the difficulty of stablishing clinically meaningful cut-offs. Although this study was powered to find a 0.7 area underneath the curve, as there were only 23 patients who developed clinical vasospasm, such small sample could have been insufficient to capture such variability.  

Despite ET1 being the focus of research for two decades, with significant efforts in the development of ET1-antagonists for the prophylaxis of vasospasm and multiple observational studies investigating the role of ET1 in the development of vasospasm, it is plausible that its significance (if present) could require trials of larger sample size. It is also noteworthy that we limited our study to 7 days due to practical limitations and longer observation time could have demonstrated significant differences or even non-captured patterns of expression. Additional limitations relate to not examining the presence of cerebral infarcts, rebleed or refractory hypertension which could have account for potential confounders to baseline levels of ET1; or long term clinical and functional outcomes associated with ET1 levels, as these may have other prognostic value other than for acute vasospasm.

# Conclusion  

Plasma levels of ET1 during the first seven days after SAH, do not predict the risk of developing clinical vasospasm.
