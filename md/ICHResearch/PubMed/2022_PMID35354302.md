# Prophylactic Therapies for Morbidity and Mortality After Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis of Randomized Trials  

Mojtaba Dayyani , MD; Behnam Sadeghirad , PharmD, MPH, PhD; James C. Grotta , MD; Samira Zabihyan , MD; Saba Ahmadvand , MD; Yuting Wang, MD; Gordon H. Guyatt, MD; Sepideh Amin-Hanjani , MD  

BACKGROUND: Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is associated with high mortality and morbidity. We aimed to determine the relative benefits of pharmacological prophylactic treatments in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage by performing a network meta-analysis of randomized trials.  

METHODS: We searched Medline, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, ProQuest, and Cochrane Central to February 2020. Pairs of reviewers independently identified eligible trials, extracted data, and assessed the risk of bias. Eligible trials compared the prophylactic effects of any oral or intravenous medications or intracranial drug-eluting implants to one another or placebo or standard of care in adult hospitalized patients with confirmed aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. We used the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach to assess the certainty of the evidence.  

RESULTS: We included 53 trials enrolling 10 415 patients. Nimodipine likely reduces all-cause mortality compared to placebo (odds ratio [OR],0.73 $[95\%$ CI, $0.53\substack{-1.00}]$ ; moderate certainty; absolute risk reduction (ARR), $-3.35\%$ ). Nimodipine (OR, 1.46 $[95\%$ CI, $1.07\mathrm{-}1.99]$ ; high certainty; absolute risk increase, $8.25\%$ ) and cilostazol (OR, 3.73 $[95\%$ CI, 1.14–12.18]; moderate certainty; absolute risk increase, $23.15\% $ ) were the most effective treatments in improving disability at the longest follow-up. Compared to placebo, clazosentan ( $10\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg}$ ; OR, 0.39 $[95\%$ CI, 0.22–0.68]; high certainty; ARR, $-16.65\%\cdot$ ), nicardipine (OR, 0.48 $[95\%$ CI, $0.24\mathrm{-}0.94]$ ; moderate certainty; ARR, $-13.70\%)$ ), fasudil (OR, 0.55 $[95\%$ CI, $0.31\substack{-0.98}$ ; moderate certainty; ARR, $-11.54\%)$ , and magnesium (OR, 0.66 $[95\%$ CI, 0.46–0.94]; high certainty; ARR, $-8.37\%$ ) proved most effective in reducing the likelihood of delayed cerebral ischemia.  

CONCLUSIONS: Nimodipine and cilostazol are likely the most effective treatments in preventing morbidity and mortality in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. Clazosentan, nicardipine, fasudil, and magnesium showed beneficial effects on delayed cerebral ischemia and vasospasm but they were not found to reduce mortality or disability. Future trials are warranted to elaborately investigate the prophylactic effects of medications that may improve mortality and long-term functional outcomes, such as cilostazol and clazosentan.

Cerebral vasospasm is among the most common complications of aneurysmal SAH (aSAH) with nearly $70\%$ of aSAH patients developing angiographic vasospasm.5 Vasospasm usually begins after day three of the bleed, reaching a peak at days 7 to 8, typically abating in 7 to 10 days.6,7 Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) is a multifactorial ischemic event after aSAH,8 widely known as clinical or symptomatic vasospasm, occurs in $17\%$ to $40\%$ of patients with aSAH and worsens clinical outcomes.9–11  

Investigators have tested multiple pharmacological interventions for preventing mortality and DCI and improving neurological and functional outcomes after aSAH that have been summarized in several systematic reviews using conventional meta-analytic methods.12–16 These reviews have been largely restricted to comparing the effects of a single drug to placebo with no attempt to assess their comparative effectiveness. To clarify their relative merit, we conducted a comprehensive systematic review and network meta-analysis of trials addressing therapeutic benefits of pharmacological treatments in patients with aSAH.  

# METHODS  

We followed the PRISMA extension statement for reporting of systematic reviews incorporating network meta-analysis (NMAs) of health care interventions,17 and registered our protocol with PROSPERO (The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews).  

# Data Sharing  

All data are freely available within the Supplemental Material  

# Search Strategy and Study Selection  

To identify potential eligible studies, we searched MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses database, and the Cochrane Central Register of  

Controlled Trials from their inception to February 2020, with no language restrictions (Supplemental Material, section 1) as well as Google Scholar, clinicaltrials.gov and controlled trials. com, and key scientific journals in the fields of Neurology and Neurosurgery. We also sought eligible studies through review of reference lists of eligible trials and relevant reviews.  

Pairs of reviewers (M.D., S.A., W.Y., and S.Z.) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full-text articles of records retrieved through the searches. Reviewers resolved disagreements by discussion or in consultation with a third reviewer (B.S.). We included randomized trials that compared the prophylactic effects of any oral or intravenous medications or intracranial drug-eluting implants (including but not limited to calcium channel antagonists, statins, NSAIDs, rho-kinase inhibitors, and phosphodiesterase inhibitors), to one another or placebo or standard of care in adult hospitalized patients $(\geq18$ years) with confirmed aSAH. We excluded studies that focused on the treatment of cerebral vasospasm and those medications that are no longer available or no longer indicated for the condition based on the recommendations of the American Heart Association Guideline on the treatment of aSAH.18  

# Data Abstraction and Risk of Bias Assessment  

Using a standardized form, pairs of reviewers (M.D., S.A., and W.Y.) independently extracted data from eligible studies including participant and trial characteristics, details of interventions and comparators, and reported outcomes and their definitions. Patient-important outcomes included all-cause mortality, disability at the longest follow-up, and DCI; we also considered the surrogate outcome, cerebral vasospasm.  

Cerebral vasospasm was defined as detection of vasospasm by either digital subtraction angiography or transcranial Doppler (any peak systolic middle cerebral artery velocity [120 cm/s] and a Lindegaard ratio of ${>}3$ ). Acceptable definitions for DCI were the development of new, focal neurological deficits, and/or a decreased level of consciousness of at least two points on the Glasgow Coma Scale after other possible causes of deterioration have been excluded or a new infarct revealed by follow-up brain computed tomography.7,19  

Eligible disability measures included modified Rankin Scale or Glasgow Outcome Scale, evaluated at the longest follow-up. For the modified Rankin Scale, we considered modified Rankin Scale scores of 1 and 2 as favorable, and scores of 3 to 6 as unfavorable and for or Glasgow Outcome Scale measurements 4 and 5 as favorable and 1 to 3 as unfavorable.  

Two reviewers (M.D. and S.A.) assessed the risk of bias (RoB) independently and in duplicate using a modified Cochrane RoB instrument, disagreements resolved by adjudication of a third reviewer (B.S.).20,21 The RoB tool addresses the following potential sources of bias: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of study participants, blinding of healthcare providers, blinding of data collectors and outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data (missing data or loss to follow-up $>10\%$ of randomized population were considered as high RoB), and other potential sources of bias.  

# Data Synthesis and Statistical Methods  

We calculated odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding $95\%$ CIs for all outcomes. Also, we calculated the absolute treatment effects of the network estimates by multiplying the relative risk and its confidence interval with the estimated baseline risk. For each outcome, we used median risk in the placebo group of included randomized trials as the baseline risk. Initially, we performed conventional pairwise meta-analysis using a DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model for all comparisons with 2  randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or more. We assessed heterogeneity between RCTs for each direct comparison with visual inspection of the forest plots and the I2 statistic. We performed meta-regression using the Knapp-Hartung modification of the variance regardless of observed heterogeneity to assess effect modification of receiving additional vasospasm treatment, duration of treatment, inadequate allocation concealment and blinding, and being at RoB due to missing participant data $(>10\%$ missing data). For all direct comparisons, we assessed small study effects using Harbord test when 10 or more RCTs were available.22  

When the same treatment was used with different dosages or was delivered via different routes (eg, oral or intravenous), the decision to consider them as separate nodes or combined them as a single node in the network meta-analysis was based on clinical input, evidence from trials directly comparing the alternatives, and results of an expanded analysis that allowed comparison of treatment effects. We then performed frequentist NMA to synthesize the available evidence from the entire network of trials using the methodology of multivariate meta-analysis assuming a common heterogeneity parameter.23,24 We confirmed the coherence assumption in the entire network using design-by-treatment model (global test) as described by Higgins et al.25 We also used node splitting method to assess the presence of incoherence between direct and indirect estimates of the effect.26,27  

We estimated ranking probabilities using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve, mean ranks, and rankograms.28,29 We used metaprop command in Stata, which applies the variance-stabilizing Freeman-Tukey double arcsine approach to calculate overall risk for the placebo group in each outcome, then used network estimates compared with placebo to calculate absolute risks. We used Stata (StataCorp‚ release 15.1. College Station, TX) for all data analyses.  

# Assessing Certainty of the Evidence  

We used the approach suggested by the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach for rating the certainty of evidence for each network estimate.30–32 To rate the certainty of network estimates, both direct and indirect comparisons are considered. Initially, we rated the certainty in direct estimates according to the traditional Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation guidance.30 Then, rated the certainty in indirect estimate, with a focus on the dominant lowest order loop.31 Ratings of the certainty of estimates for direct and indirect estimates included RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. We judged imprecision using the network estimate and rated down for imprecision if the $95\%$ CI excluded the null effect.  

Network estimate certainty started as the higher of the direct and indirect evidence; however, the relative contribution of direct and indirect evidence to the network estimate was considered when rating the certainty. We considered rating down the certainty of network estimate if we detected incoherence between the indirect and direct estimates (also known as local inconsistency).31,32  

# Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation Approach to Summarize Results From NMA  

To optimize the interpretation of NMA results, we applied a novel minimally contextualized approach in which we categorized interventions—from most to least effective—based on the effect estimates obtained from the NMA and their associated certainty of evidence. For each outcome, we created categories of interventions as follows: category 1, the reference intervention (placebo) and interventions with no evidence of difference compared to placebo, referred to as among the least effective; category 2, interventions superior to the reference treatment, but not better than any other of the interventions superior to placebo, termed inferior to the most effective, but superior to the least effective; category 3, interventions that proved superior to at least one category 2 intervention, termed among the most effective. We then divided all categories into 2 groups: those with moderate- or high-certainty evidence relative to placebo, and those with low-certainty or very low–certainty evidence relative to placebo.33,34  

# RESULTS  

# Description of the Evidence  

We identified 2110 citations through literature search, of which 53 studies involving 10 415 patients proved eligible.35–87 We excluded 4 trials46,49,71,81 from the analysis that became one-arm observational studies after interventions were collapsed into common nodes. Figure  1 presents details of the study selection process and reasons for exclusion. We attempted to contact authors of 4 trials for additional information but received no response.  

Among the 53 included trials, median of the mean age for patients was 53.2 years (interquartile range [IQR], 50.0–56.0 years), and on average, $65.0\%$ of the enrolled patients were females (IQR, 58.3–70.0). Table S1 summarizes the characteristics of the participants.  

The majority of trials (37 of 53) treated patients for $\geq14$ days; in 5 trials patients received the intervention for ${}^{27}$ days.  

Calcium channel antagonists (nimodipine, nicardipine, and magnesium sulfate) were the most common studied interventions (25 studies), followed by statins (simvastatin, pravastatin, atorvastatin, pitavastatin, and atorvastatin) (12 studies), clazosentan (5 studies), cilostazol (3 studies), and fasudil hydrochloride (3 studies). The remaining 5 studies addressed erythropoietin (2 studies), meloxicam (1 study), eicosapentaenoic acid (1 study), and dantrolene (1 study).  

Additional vasodilator (nimodipine or fasudil) was commonly used in both arms of trials of magnesium $(85\%)$ , cilostazol $(67\%)$ , and statins $(58\%)$ . Additional vasodilator was also used in both arms of all trials of clazosentan, erythropoietin, meloxicam, and dantrolene.

${\mathsf{Of}}$ the 53 trials, 51 $(96\%)$ adequately generated their randomization sequence, and 25 $(47\%)$ were judged to be at low RoB for allocation concealment; 17 trials $(32\%)$ were judged to be at high RoB for inadequate blinding of participants, 20 $(37\%)$ for inadequate blinding of study personnel, and 21 $(39\%)$ for inadequate blinding of outcome assessors. Thirteen studies $(24\%)$ reported $\geq10\%$ missing outcome data. Table S3 provides details of RoB assessments.

# Delayed Cerebral Ischemia  

The 47 studies reporting DCI enrolled 9991 patients (Figure 2B), and provided 25 direct comparisons, for 13 of which 2 or more studies were available for conventional pairwise meta-analysis; of these, 3 comparisons had substantial heterogeneity $(|^{2}\geq50\%)$ (Table S9). The design-by-treatment interaction model showed no evidence of incoherence (Figure S4); however, we observed incoherence between direct and indirect estimates (ie, the difference between direct and indirect comparison was statistically significant) for the comparison of nimodipine and placebo. We, therefore, used the direct estimate of effect from conventional meta-analysis, over the network estimate, for the comparison of nimodipine versus placebo; this showed high-certainty evidence of reduction in DCI (OR, 0.53 $[95\%$ CI, 0.39–0.72]; ARR, −12.15 $[95\%$ CI, $-6.75$ to −16.66]).

# Cerebral Vasospasm  

The 34 studies reporting cerebral vasospasm enrolled 4005 patients (Figure S1) and provided 23 direct comparisons, 9 of which had ${\ge}2$ studies available for conventional pairwise meta-analysis; of these, 3 demonstrated substantial heterogeneity $(1^{2}\geq50\%)$ (Table S11). Statistically significant global or loop-specific incoherence proved not present (Figure S5).  

Nimodipine (OR, 0.59 $[95\%$ CI, 0.36–0.97]; ARR, $-13.10\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $-0.74$ to $-24.81]$ ) and magnesium sulfate (OR, 0.61 $[95\%$ CI, $95\%$ CI, $0.41-0.92]$ ; ARR, $-12.27\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $-2.04$ to $-21.86]$ ) proved among the most effective in reducing cerebral vasospasm compared to placebo (moderate certainty, Figure  3, Table, and Table S5).  

Clazosentan may result in reduction of cerebral vasospasm compared with placebo (for $10~\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg}$ infusion: OR, 0.18 $[95\%$ CI, $0.10\substack{-0.33}$ ; ARR, $-38.15\%$ $[95\%$ CI, −26.71 to $-45.96]$ , for $5\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{kg}$ infusion: OR, 0.24 $[95\%$ CI, 0.13–0.45]; ARR, $-33.14\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $-19.68$ to −42.85], and for $2\mathrm{mg}/\mathrm{kg}$ infusion: OR, 0.32 $[95\%$ CI, $0.16{-}0.65]$ ; ARR, $-27.37\%$ $[95\%$ CI, −10.69 to −39.97] low certainty). Nicardipine (OR, 0.37 $[95\%$ CI, $0.17-$ 0.79]; low certainty; ARR, $-24.20\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $-5.82$ to −39.05], and cilostazol (OR, 0.39 $[95\%$ CI, $0.19\substack{-0.79}]$ ; low certainty; ARR, $-23.01\%$ $[95\%$ CI, $-5.82$ to −37.26]) may reduce cerebral vasospasm (low certainty, Figure 3, Table, and Table S5).  

# Additional Analysis  

Tables S12 and Table S13 and Figures S6 through S10 provide ranking probabilities and surface under the cumulative ranking curve values for all outcomes. Results of sensitivity analysis for different nimodipine formulations were not statistically significant and are provided in Tables S14 through S17. We performed meta-regression to explore the impact of allocation concealment, blinding, missing participant data, length of follow-up, additional prophylactic treatment, and duration of prophylactic treatment and found no statistically significant coefficients for any of the outcomes.  

# DISCUSSION  

In this network meta-analysis of randomized trials of prophylactic treatments after aSAH, we found moderate to high-certainty evidence that nimodipine and cilostazol are among the most effective prophylactic treatments (Figure  3). Nimodipine as the most effective treatment showed benefit in reduction of all-cause mortality, DCI, and cerebral vasospasm and improved function. With the exception of mortality, cilostazol demonstrated similar beneficial effects. Higher doses of clazosentan, nicardipine, fasudil, and magnesium showed moderate to high certainty evidence of improvement in DCI, but they were not found to reduce mortality or disability (Table and Figure 3).  

Most guidelines on management of aSAH recommend all patients receive calcium channel blockade with nimodipine.88,89 The American Heart Association guideline18 and European Stroke Organization Guideline90 recommendations emphasize that patients with aSAH receive only nimodipine, with European Stroke Organization Guideline recommending against using magnesium.90 In addition, there are no clear guidance or recommendations for or against using cilostazol and other prophylactic medications (statins, endothelin-1 antagonists, and phospholipase A2 inhibitors) citing uncertainty of their effects or ongoing trials.18,90  

To our knowledge, there are no prior NMA of pharmacological treatments for prevention of cerebral vasospasm secondary to aSAH. The most recent systematic review summarizing the evidence for management of cerebral vasospasm and DCI after aSAH only searched Scopus for English articles and did not explore comparative effects of different treatment options or consider the overall certainty of evidence.91  

This systematic review and NMA represent the most comprehensive synthesis of data from English and non-English RCTs for currently available prophylactic pharmacological therapies of cerebral vasospasm secondary to rupture of intracranial aneurysms. Further strength of our review includes reporting all 4 clinically essential outcomes related to patients with aSAH, which provides a holistic view of clinical decisionmaking based on all possible clinical dimensions. We used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach for evaluating the certainty in NMA effect estimates and reported our findings using a novel, transparent, and intuitive presentation of the results that ranks the treatments based on the magnitude of effects and the certainty of the supporting evidence.  

Limitations of our review include the small number of studies directly comparing active treatments to one another rather than to placebo. The limited amount and quality of included studies may have impacted the overall confidence in the meta-analysis results and conclusions and reduced the power of our analysis in addressing the comparative effects of treatments. Furthermore, the evidence to inform effectiveness of most interventions proved of low or very low certainty.  

We observed noticeable variability in patients' characteristics, treatment delivery, and cointerventions (eg, rescue therapy and treatment with clipping/coiling) among the included trials. We found no evidence of effect modification for length of follow-up, additional prophylactic treatment, and duration of prophylactic treatment, yet the small number of closed loops and studies limits the power of these analyses. Although we specified explicitly a priori criteria for our outcomes of interest, studies included in our analysis varied in their definition of DCI and vasospasm; however, given the minimal observed statistical heterogeneity, these variabilities may not have an important impact on our findings. All the above suggest our conclusions may be impacted by intransitivity and need to be cautiously interpreted. Further details on distribution of potential effect modifiers among trials are provided in the Figures S11 through S15.  

Pharmacodynamic studies indicate that nimodipine has a more significant effect on cerebral arteries than other arteries. This may be due to its highly lipophilic structure compared with other calcium channel blockers that allow it to cross the blood-brain barrier and reaches the brain and cerebrospinal fluid.92,93 The vasodilatory function of cilostazol can be justified by increased cAMP levels in cells that can antagonize the influx of calcium ions, inhibiting the contraction of vascular smooth muscle cells and leading to vasodilatation.94 Compared with other phosphodiesterases III inhibitors like milrinone, cilostazol has a longer half-life, making it a better choice in terms of administration and maintaining a steady-state plasma concentration. Although the networks of treatments for DCI and cerebral vasospasm were better connected and involved more randomized trials, networks of functional outcomes were, except for cilostazol and nimodipine, sparse and supported by low-certainty to very low–certainty evidence. Thus, future trials investigating the preventive effects of alternative medications would provide useful information.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

Our results suggest nimodipine and cilostazol are likely the most effective treatments in preventing morbidity and mortality in patients with aSAH. Clazosentan, nicardipine, fasudil, and magnesium showed beneficial effects on DCI and vasospasm but they were not found to reduce mortality or disability. The limited amount and quality of included studies as well as variability in delivery of prophylactic therapies may have impacted our findings. Future randomized trials should prioritize investigating the effects of agents that may improve mortality and long-term functional outcomes (such as cilostazol and clazosentan).
