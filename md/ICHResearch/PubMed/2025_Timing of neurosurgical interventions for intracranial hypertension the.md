# Purpose of review  

The aim of this study was to highlight clinical considerations regarding medical versus surgical management of elevated intracranial pressure (ICP), describe limitations of medical management, and summarize evidence regarding timing of neurosurgical interventions.  

# Recent findings  

The optimal ICP management strategy remains elusive, and substantial practice variations exist. Common risks of medical treatments include hypotension/shock, cardiac arrhythmias and heart failure, acute renal failure, volume overload, hypoxemia, and prolonged mechanical ventilation.   
In traumatic brain injury (TBI), recent randomized controlled trials (RCT) did not demonstrate outcome benefits for early, prophylactic decompressive craniectomy, but indicate a role for secondary   
decompressive craniectomy in patients with refractory elevated ICP. A recent meta-analysis suggested that when an extraventricular drain is required, insertion $24\mathsf{h}$ or less post-TBI may result in better outcomes. In large ischemic middle cerebral artery strokes, pooled analyses of three RCTs showed functional outcome benefits in patients less than 60 years who underwent prophylactic DC within less than $48{\mathsf{h}}$ . In intracranial hemorrhage, a recent RCT suggested outcome benefits for minimally invasive hematoma evacuation within less than $24\mathsf{h}$ .  

# Summary  

More data are needed to guide ICP targets, treatment modalities, predictors of herniation, and surgical triggers; clinical decisions should consider individual patient characteristics, and account for risks of medical and surgical treatments.

# INTRODUCTION  

When Nils Lundberg developed the technique for continuous invasive intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring in the 1950 s, the ability to measure this physiologic target represented a major milestone in neurocritical care [1&]. Subsequent work found that intracranial hypertension represented an underrecognized phenomenon after severe acute brain injury (SABI) and that ICP elevations were associated with worse clinical outcomes [2,3]. In 2019, the Seattle International Severe Traumatic Brain Injury Consensus Conference (SIBICC) delineated a tiered algorithm for management of intracranial hypertension in traumatic brain injury (TBI) [4]. These tiers include gradually escalating medical treatments, as well as placement of an external ventricular drain (EVD), and secondary decompressive surgery for refractory intracranial hypertension. The optimal timing of neurosurgical interventions in SABI remains controversial. # PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF INTRACRANIAL HYPERTENSION  

After SABI, a complex cascade of multiple systemic and neuronal mechanisms contributes to development of cytotoxic or vasogenic cerebral edema and intracranial hypertension [5–7]. Elevated ICP can compress vital brain structures and affect cerebral blood flow via direct vascular compression or by impacting cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) [8]. The Monro-Kellie hypothesis states that within a fixed cranial vault composed of brain parenchyma $(80\%)$ , blood $(10\%)$ , and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF, $10\%)$ ), volume increases in one compartment necessitate a compensatory decrease in another to maintain a constant ICP [9]. The dynamic components (1c3e8rebral edema, blood volume, CSF volume/flow) within each compartment represent treatment targets [10]. However, when ICP remains refractory, decompression to open the inflexible box formed by the cranium may be necessary to prevent cerebral herniation and progression to death by neurologic criteria [11].  

# GENERAL APPROACH TO INTRACRANIALHYPERTENSION  

It is widely recognized that intracranial hypertension is associated with poor outcomes [2,3]. However, there are insufficient data to inform optimal ICP targets, treatment triggers, or the best treatment modality, and current guidelines are often based on limited evidence [12–14]. ICP-lowering treatments may be based on clinical signs, radiographic findings, and ICP monitoring. Individualized ICP treatment thresholds accounting for cerebral compliance and perfusion remain to be determined. Based on a single-center study of 459 patients with severe TBI using sequential chi-squares to test ICP values [15], the 2016 Brain Trauma Foundation (BTF) guidelines changed the recommended treatment threshold from 20 to $22\mathrm{mmHg}$ [11,12]. These guidelines also state that “any chosen threshold must be closely and repeatedly corroborated with the clinical exam and imaging in the individual patient”. Nowadays, there is growing consensus that, ICP should not just be seen as a number but rather as part of a pathophysiological process; the concept of “cumulative ICP $\mathrm{dose}^{\prime\prime}$ based on time and intensity of insult is gaining traction [16–19].  

Data regarding impact of ICP monitoring has been mixed $[20,21,22^{\bf^{*}\cdot},23$ ]. The BEST-TRIP randomized controlled trial (RCT) comparing a clinical algorithm versus an ICP-monitoring-based approach showed no difference in clinical outcomes [20], although methodological concerns have been raised [24]. A large prospective observational SABI cohort showed higher therapy intensity levels (TIL), lower withdrawal of life-sustaining treatments, and lower 6-month mortality in patients who received ICP monitoring [21]; however, a TBI cohort using propensity-matching demonstrated associations between ICP monitoring and poorer neurologic recovery, more medical therapies, longer duration of mechanical ventilation and increased complications $[22^{\mathbf{s}\mathbf{\overline{{]}}}}$ .  

Substantial practice variations have been reported regarding utilization of ICP monitoring and management $[21,25,26^{\bf*\bf}]$ . In a recent survey across 160 centers in 30 countries examining cerebral edema management, $84\%$ reported institutional practice variations, $52\%$ reported absence of standardized protocols, and triggers for hyperosmolar therapy and timing of decompressive surgery varied widely $[26^{==}]$ .

The SIBICC algorithm for TBI suggests starting with treatments that confer less risk, with stepwise escalations towards more invasive therapies (Fig. 1) [4]. The initial interventions (“tier $0^{\prime\prime}$ ) include basic measures such as head of bed elevation, fever prevention, and analgesia/sedation to treat agitation/pain. Tier 1 encompasses hyperosmolar therapy and analgesia/ sedation to treat ICP, hemodynamic augmentation to preserve a CPP target of $60{-}70\mathrm{mmHg}$ , mechanical ventilation to modulate partial pressure of carbon dioxide ( $pa\mathrm{CO}_{2},$ goal $35{-}38\mathrm{mmHg}$ , and EVD placement. Tier 2 comprises neuromuscular blockade, mild hypocapnia ( $\mathrm{{\cdot}}p\mathrm{{a}}\mathrm{{CO}}_{2}$ goal $32{-}35\mathrm{mmHg}$ ), as well as individualized mean arterial pressure (MAP) and CPP goals. Tier 3 includes pentobarbital/thiopental coma, $p\mathrm{aCO}_{2}$ goal $30{-}32\mathrm{mmHg}.$ , mild hypothermia, and secondary decompressive craniectomy.  

In general, in the absence of a compressive mass lesion contributing to an ICP crisis, medical interventions can be utilized before escalating to surgical treatments. However, while thought to be less invasive, major side effects can occur with medical management [27]. When debating the optimal timing of surgical interventions, evaluation of the effectiveness of medical treatment and vigilant monitoring for its complications are critical. One potential advantage of early neurosurgical treatments such as CSF diversion or decompressive surgery is that they often allow for de-escalation of medical therapies [21,28].  

# LIMITATIONS AND RISKS OF MEDICAL MANAGEMENT  

Systemic insults have been associated with worse neurologic outcomes [29]. When high TIL are utilized, medical treatments often need to be continued more than 7 days, with accumulating potential for iatrogenic risk. Common medical complications include hypotension/shock, volume overload, hypoxemia, ventilator-associated pneumonia, acute renal failure, and cardiac arrhythmias (Table 1, Fig. 2).  

Hypotension due to sedation, treatment with mannitol, or neurogenic heart failure is comm1o3n9, and has been widely associated with poor outcomes in SABI [5,29,30,31&], likely due to impaired cerebral perfusion and exacerbation of secondary brain injury. The traditional concept of hypotension (MAP ${<}65$ or SBP ${<}90\mathrm{mmHg}$ ), may be inadequate in patient with neurologic injury. Recent data suggest that age-based SBP targets of more than 100 or $110\mathrm{mmHg}$ are associated with better outcomes in TBI [32]. Also, adequate oxygenation and ventilation are important cornerstones of SABI management [33].

# Sedation and analgesia  

High doses of propofol can often result in hypotens1i4o0n. Propofol-related infusion syndrome (PRIS) is a rare but serious complication encountered with prolonged infusions, particularly at high doses.  

Midazolam can be utilized as an alternative with less hemodynamic effects; however, sedative effects are long-lasting, and common complications include worsening delirium, tachyphylaxis, and benzodiazepine withdrawal [34,35]. Increasing vasopressor requirements can pose limits on sedation. A norepinephrine equivalent of more than $0.2\upmu{\mathrm{g/}}$ $\mathrm{kg/min}$ can predispose patients to cardiac arrhythmias and bowel ischemia [36]. Vasopressin may be utilized as a norepinephrine-sparing agent but can contribute to hyponatremia and thereby worsen cerebral edema [37,38&]. High doses of opioids for analgesia can cause systemic complications such as bowel ischemia, electrolyte derangements, ileus, and contribute to intracranial hypertension via increased abdominal pressure [39].

# Hyperosmolar therapy  

Hyperosmolar therapy has been shown to effectively reduce ICP; however, several systemic and neurologic side effects have been described. Acute renal failure can be observed with mannitol due to direct nephrotoxic effects [40], and with hypertonic saline in the setting of renal artery constriction in response to hypernatremia and modulation of renal blood flow due to hyperchloremia [41,42]. Hyperosmolar therapy can also contribute to excessive extravascular fluid, resulting in pulmonary edema and worsening hypoxemia or exacerbation of heart failure. Cerebral maladaptation is of particular concern in malnourished chronically hyponatremic patients who are at elevated risk for developing central pontine myelinolysis (CPM) [43]. There are conflicting data on outcomes associated with hyperosmolar states $[44,45^{\bf*\bf*}]$ . Cerebral parenchyma may increase production of osmoles to adapt to a hyperosmolar state [46]. An acutely impaired blood brain barrier can allow for osmoles to equilibr1a4t1e between intracellular and extracellular compartments, resulting in an accumulation of exogenous osmoles within the injured parenchyma and paradoxical cerebral edema [47]. There are limited data to specify limits for serum sodium and osmolality, though expert consensus suggests Na more than $155\mathrm{mEq/l}$ and $\mathrm{Osm}{>}320\mathrm{mOsm}/1,$ , or an osmolar gap less than $20\mathrm{mOsm/l}$ [4,48]. In general, hyperosmolar therapy is considered no longer effective at these values due to loss of the osmotic gradient and may potentially be harmful [48].  

# Hyperventilation  

While transient hyperventilation is effective in acute ICP crises, prolonged hypocapnia for ICP control is controversial and thought to be detrimental due to concerns about cerebral vasoconstriction or rebound intracranial hypertension when shifting back to normocapnia. Myocardial blood flow can be affected, and arrhythmias have been reported [49]. Typically, induced hypocapnia is considered a short-term bridge to a neurosurgical intervention. Both hypocapnia and hypercapnia have been associated with worse outcomes in SABI $[50-52,53^{\bullet\bullet}]$ , and concerns around elevated ICP have to be balanced with considerations around cerebral perfusion. While SIBICC specifies $pa\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ target ranges based on tiers, major and rapid fluctuations in $pa\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ should be strictly avoided [54].  

# SURGICAL INTERVENTIONS: INDICATION AND TIMING  

Decisions around surgical interventions are largely driven by radiographic findings and the clinical examination. Acute focal lesions with clinically significant mass effect and signs of impending herniation are triggers for prompt “primary” decompression [55]. Mass lesions can be extradural, intradural, intraparenchymal, or a combination thereof [56].  

In cases with diffuse cerebral edema and no imminent clinical/radiographic signs of herniation, medical interventions can be attempted first; the necessity and timing of a “secondary” neurosurgical intervention are often determined based on response to medical therapies and evolution of neurologic examination/imaging. Potential advantages of an early neurosurgical procedure include neuroprotection (by preventing further cerebral compression and secondary ischemia) and the ability to deescalate medical ICP treatments, while disadvantages mainly revolve around the surgical risks of the procedure, including bleeding, surgical site infection, alteration in CSF flow, hydrocephalus, s1u42nken flap syndrome, complication around cranioplasty, thromboembolic events, and peri-operative risks [57–61].  

# Decompressive surgery  

Neurosurgical interventions to allow for relief of mass effect include decompressive craniectomy, craniotomy, and evacuation of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). The most common indication for decompressive craniectomy is acute subdural hematoma (ASDH) [62,63]. A recent RCT comparing primary decompressive craniectomy (bone flap left off, to account for brain swelling postoperatively) versus craniotomy (bone flap replaced) in ASDH demonstrated a trend towards better outcomes at 12 months with the latter; more patients in the craniotomy group (albeit relatively few) required additional surgery within 2 weeks, while more wound infections were noted in the decompressive craniectomy group $[64^{\bf=\bf4}]$ . The authors concluded that if the bone flap can be replaced without compression of the brain, surgeons may consider doing so, as opposed to performing a preemptive decompressive craniectomy. However, these findings may not be relevant for resource-limited or military settings, where preemptive craniectomy is often used owing to the absence of advanced ICU facilities for postoperative care.  

Two landmark RCTs in TBI investigated the utility of secondary decompressive craniectomy. The DECRA-RCT evaluated decompressive craniectomy as an early neuroprotective intervention, comparing bifrontal decompressive craniectomy versus and standard medical management versus medical management alone (Table 2). Patients were randomized within $72\mathrm{h}$ if their ICP exceeded $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ for more than $15\mathrm{min}$ in $^{1\mathrm{h}}$ . The surgical group had a higher rate of patients with bilaterally unreactive pupils; after adjusting for this imbalance posthoc, rates of unfavorable outcomes were not different between treatment arms [28]. The RESCUE-ICP RCT investigated the effectiveness of decompressive craniectomy (bifrontal or unilateral) as a last-tier therapy for sustained, refractory intracranial hypertension. Patients were randomized to decompressive craniectomy versus continuing medical therapy with barbiturates if ICP exceeded $25\mathrm{mmHg}$ for more than $^{1\mathrm{h}}$ despite tier 1–2 interventions. At 6 months, patients with decompressive craniectomy had significantly lower mortality, but higher rates of vegetative states while severe disability, rates of moderate disability and good recovery were similar [65]. At the 2-year follow-up, the decompressive craniectomy group was found to be more likely to recover over time; for every 100 individuals treated surgically rather than medically, 21 additional patients will survive at 24 months: six dependent, seven independent at home, eight independent outside their home [66]. This trial had a high rate of crossover $(37\%)$ from the medical to the surgical arm, with results based on an intention-to-treat analysis; one would expect that this would have diluted the observed treatment effect. Following these trials, the 2020 BTF guidelines recommended that secondary decompressive craniectomy be performed for late refractory ICP elevations, but not as an early preventive measure [11]. Overall, the data confirm that there is a role for secondary, last-tier decompressive craniectomy in TBI, before using barbiturates. Given the heterogeneity of TBI, decisions about exact timing should be made on a caseby-case basis.

In acute ischemic stroke, life-threatening malignant cerebral edema occurs in up to $10\%$ of patients with large middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarcts, typically manifests between poststroke day 2–5 [67,68], and has a case fatality of up to $80\%$ [69,70]. Scores and risk factors to predict cerebral edema have been described (infarct volume, evolution of infarct and shift on subsequent imaging, lack of revascularization, poor collateral flow, and basal cisternal effacement) [71–73]. Regarding decompressive craniectomy timing, pooled analyses of three RCTs (DESTINY, DECIMAL, HAMLET) demonstrated a mortality benefit (78 versus $29\%$ ) and functional outcome benefit (NNT four for survival with modified Rankin Scale, $\mathrm{mRS}{\leq}3$ , two for $\mathrm{mRS}{\le}4$ ) with prophylactic early decompressive craniectomy within $48\mathrm{h}$ in patients between 18 and 60 years with good premorbid baseline [74–77]. Of note, HAMLET enrolled patients up to $96\mathrm{{h}}$ poststroke, and mortality or functional outcome benefits were not seen in the $48{-}96\mathrm{h}$ subgroup [77]. The subsequent DESTINY-2 RCT investigated prophylactic decompressive craniectomy within $48\mathrm{h}$ poststroke in patients 61–82 years of age; while mortality and severe disability were reduced in the decompressive craniectomy group, most survivors in both arms had substantial disabilities (none survived with mRS 0–2, only $6\%$ with mRS 3) [78]. These findings emphasize that quality of life and goal-concordant care are critical factors in decisions about operative management.  

Sizable cerebellar infarcts can become lifethreatening due to brainstem compression and obliteration of the fourth ventricle, necessitating urgent suboccipital decompressive craniectomy and/or EVD placement. Because patients can rapidly deteriorate, vigilant neuromonitoring is warranted, especially if the infarct volume is more than onethird of the cerebellar hemisphere, or if effacement of the fourth ventricle is noted [79–83].  

In hemorrhagic stroke, a large multicenter RCT (STICH) comparing early $(<24\mathrm{h})$ hematoma evacuation versus initial conservative management did not show differences in functional outcomes, although major limitations included high crossover $(26\%)$ and randomization based on equipoise, with a large proportion of screened patients who underwent hematoma evacuation being excluded from the analysis [84]. Many clinicians wrongly interpreted these findings as evidence that there is no role for hematoma evacuation in spontaneous ICH, failing to recognize that unconscious patients in a life-threatening condition were not randomized but managed according to best medical practice. A follow-up study comparing early $(<12\mathrm{h})$ hematoma evacuation with initial conservative management in superficial lobar ICH of $10{-}100\mathrm{ml}$ (STICH-2) found a trend towards better outcomes with early hematoma evacuation [85]. The SWITCH-RCT investigated prophylactic decompressive craniectomy less than $72\mathrm{h}$ of deep ICH $40–80\mathrm{ml}$ without hematoma evacuation and found weak evidence that decompressive craniectomy plus medical management may be superior to medical management alone [86]. The ENRICHRCT demonstrated better functional outcomes in patients with early $(<24\mathrm{h}$ from time last known well) minimally invasive hematoma evacuation, with the benefit mostly attributed to the subgroup with lobar hemorrhages [87].

# External ventricular drainage  

An EVD can be utilized as an ICP treatment modality via CSF diversion (Fig. 3) and a tool for ICP monitoring. EVD placement is widely considered an effective, life-saving treatment for acute obstructive hydrocephalus. With regards to ICP monitoring in TBI with diffuse pathology, practice variations have been described regarding use of EVD versus an intraparenchymal probe (IPM) as the initial monitor [25]. While EVD is associated with substantial risks (e.g., tract hematoma and infection/ventriculitis), it is thought to be the more durable monitor and allows for CSF diversion, which may decrease the need for other ICP treatments; a small prospective study showed sustained ICP reduction, related physiologic improvements, and decreased TIL [88,89]. An RCT from China comparing EVD versus IPM as the initial monitor demonstrated lower incidence of refractory ICP, lower decompressive craniectomy rates, and higher 6-month-survival in the EVD group; however, patients randomized to the IPM group could not receive a subsequent EVD [90]. Meta-analyses did not find differences in mortality but suggested fewer complications in patients with IPM and decreased stay in the ICU [91]. A retrospective analysis of the Citicoline Brain Injury Treatment RCT found higher mortality and worse neuropsychological outcomes in patients who received EVD as the first ICP monitor [92]. In general, IPM is more commonly chosen as the initial monitor given lower risks, with conversion to EVD when ICP remains persistently elevated despite initial medical treatments [25]. The data for early EVD placement for CSF drainage are conflicting [89,93,94], with a recent individual patient data meta-analysis merging several large TBI datasets (trials and observational studies) suggesting lower odds of death or severe disabilities with EVD placement less than $24\mathrm{h}$ [93]. A pragmatic consideration is that later EVD placement can be more challenging due to extrinsic compression of the ventricular system in the context of persistently elevated ICP. EVD placement can also allow for confirmation of IPM values when considering escalations to higher TIL.  

# CONCLUSION  

The timing of prophylactic neurosurgical treatme1n4t5s has become more consolidated in recent years. While thought to be neuroprotective in younger patients with large MCA strokes, a trial of early prophylactic decompressive craniectomy in TBI did not show improved outcomes. Another RCT demonstrated benefits from secondary, last-tier decompressive craniectomy in TBI. The evidence to guide clinical decisions is limited; major challenges of surgical trials include large crossover, institutional variations in surgical practice, heterogeneity of injury patterns (especially in TBI), enrollment contingent on equipoise [28,65,84,85], and lack of patient/family-centric outcomes. Guidelines based on expert consensus provide a valuable framework [4], but cannot substitute thoughtful, individualized management. Future studies are needed to better predict which patients will develop clinically significant cerebral edema. A shift away from a purely time-based paradigm may allow for precision medicine accounting for cerebral perfusion, compliance, and clinical, cumulative impact of ICP. Meanwhile, clinical decisions should require thoughtful interdisciplinary discussions, ongoing multimodal neuromonitoring, evaluation of response to medical treatments, and balancing risks and benefits of medical and surgical treatments in the individual patient.
