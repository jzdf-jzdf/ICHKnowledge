# Subacute Neurological Improvement Predicts Favorable Functional Recovery After Intracerebral Hemorrhage: INTERACT2 Study  

Shoujiang You , MD, PhD\*; Danni Zheng, BPharm, PhD\*; Xiaoying Chen , PhD; Xia Wang , PhD; Menglu Ouyang , MPH, PhD; Qiao Han, MD; Yongjun Cao , MD, PhD; Candice Delcourt , MD, PhD; Lili Song , MD, PhD; Cheryl Carcel , MD; Hisatomi Arima , MD, PhD; Chun-Feng Liu , MD, PhD; Richard I. Lindley , MD; Thompson Robinson , MD; Craig S. Anderson , MD, PhD; John Chalmers , MD, PhD; for the INTERACT Investigators  

BACKGROUND: The frequency and prognostic significance of subacute neurological improvement (SNI) on 90-day outcomes after acute intracerebral hemorrhage are unknown.  

METHODS: Secondary analyses of participant data from the INTERACT2 trial (Second Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage Trial). SNI included any, moderate, significant, and substantial neurological improvement defined as $\geq1$ , ${\ge}2,{\ge}3$ , and ${\ge}4$ points decrease, respectively, on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale from 24 hours to 7 days after intracerebral hemorrhage. Logistic regression models were used to assess associations of SNI and death or major disability (modified Rankin Scale score of $3\mathrm{-}6\mathrm{.}$ ), major disability (modified Rankin Scale scores, 3–5), and death alone at 90 days. Data are reported as odds ratios and $95\%$ CIs.  

RESULTS: Of 2571 patients included in analyses, 1492 $(58.0\%)$ ), 1057 $(41.1\%)$ , 731 $(28.4\%)$ ), and 490 ( $19.1\%)$ patients experienced any, moderate, significant, and substantial SNI (24 hours to 7 days) after intracerebral hemorrhage, respectively. After adjustment for key confounders, any SNI was associated with $49\%$ , $25\%$ , and $65\%$ reduced odds of death or major disability (odds ratio, 0.51 $[95\%$ CI, 0.42–0.63]), major disability alone (odds ratio, 0.75 $[95\%$ CI, 0.63–0.90]), and death (odds ratio, 0.35 $[95\%$ CI, $0.24\substack{-0.50}].$ ), respectively. Moderate, significant, and substantial SNI were also significantly associated with decreased odds of death or major disability at 90 days. The relationship between any SNI and study outcomes was consistent in most subgroups, including age and baseline hematoma volume. Early intensive blood pressure-lowering treatment did not increase the odds of SNI.  

CONCLUSIONS: SNI from 24 hours to 7 days is common after intracerebral hemorrhage and predicts a lower likelihood of death or major disability.

Beyond the hyperacute phase, a series of neuropathologic changes occur in the brain involving blood-brain barrier damage, brain edema, iron deposition, and cytotoxic cascade reactions that could result in secondary brain injury8 and late neurological deterioration.9–11 Recent studies have highlighted that around $10\%$ of ICH patients experience delayed neurological deterioration, which is also a key determinant of long-term stroke outcomes.9–11 Therefore, while much focus has been on the ICH treatments in the acute phase, clinical trials investigating the therapeutic benefit of novel therapeutic interventions not only targeting secondary brain injury processes but also promoting neurological recovery beyond this initial period are becoming increasingly important.  

To the best of our knowledge, little data exist on the significance of neurological improvement beyond the initial 24 hours after ICH. We hypothesize that most ICH patients experience stable or improved neurological impairment after the acute phase and that any measurable neurological improvement from 24 hours to 7 days is associated with a favorable stroke outcome. Herein, we aimed to characterize subacute neurological improvement (SNI) from 24 hours to 7 days and its association with 90-day clinical outcomes in participants of the INTERACT2 trial (Second Intensive Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage Trials).5 We also evaluated the prognostic significance of SNI across important subgroups defined by age, baseline hematoma volume, early hematoma expansion, and early neurological deterioration.  

# METHODS  

# Study Design  

Individual deidentified participant data used in these analyses can be shared by request from any qualified investigator after approval of a protocol and signed data access agreement via the Research Office of The George Institute for Global Health, Australia.  

INTERACT2 was an international, multicenter, open, blinded end point assessment, randomized controlled trial, as described elsewhere and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT00716079).5,12 In brief, a total of 2829 participant patients with spontaneous ICH ${<}6$ hours of onset) and elevated systolic BP $(150{-}220\mathsf{mm}\mathsf{Hg})$ ) without a clear indication or contraindication to randomized treatment and no massive ICH or requirement for early decompressive surgery were randomly assigned to intensive (systolic BP target $<140\mathrm{mm}\ Hg$ within 1 hour) or guideline-recommended (systolic BP target $\mathtt{<}180$ mm $\mathsf{Hg}$ ) BP management. The study protocol was approved by the appropriate ethics committee at each participating site, and written informed consent was obtained directly from the patient or an appropriate surrogate. This article followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guideline (Supplemental Material).13  

# Outcome Variables and Definitions  

Demographic characteristics and clinical variables were recorded at the time of presentation, with neurological severity measured on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores at baseline, 24 hours, and at Day 7 (or earlier on discharge from the hospital). These assessments were undertaken by investigators at each center who were not involved in the clinical management of patients and blinded to the treatment allocation.  

SNI was defined by a change in NIHSS scores between 24 hours and day 7: any improvement, moderate improvement, significant improvement, and substantial improvement were defined as $\geq1$ , ${}^{\ge2}$ , ${\ge}3$ , and ${\ge}4$ points (24-hour NIHSS minus 7-day NIHSS), respectively.  

The primary outcome was death or major disability (modified Rankin Scale scores, 3–6). Secondary outcomes were major disability (modified Rankin Scale scores, 3–5) and allcause death alone at 90 days.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean (SD) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and number $(\%)$ for categorical variables; between-group comparisons were made using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or $\chi^{2}$ test. Associations of any, moderate, significant, and substantial SNI on 90-day outcomes (death or major disability, major disability, and death) were determined in logistic regression models. Also, the effect of early intensive BP-lowering treatment compared with standard treatment on any, moderate, significant, and substantial SNI was assessed by logistic regression models. Crude and multivariable logistic regression models were developed with known confounders, including age, sex, baseline NIHSS score, recruitment in China, baseline hematoma volume (logtransformed), antithrombotic use, intraventricular hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, BP-lowering treatment, hematoma location, and history of stain use, ischemic stroke, and acute coronary syndrome. To assess the robustness of prognosis for any SNI on 90-day outcomes, associations were analyzed in multivariate adjusted models stratified by age $(\ge65$ versus ${<}65$ years), median baseline hematoma volume $(\geq1~1$ versus $<11$ mL), absolute hematoma expansion at 24 hours $(\ge6$ versus ${<}6$ $\mathrm{m}\mathsf{L})^{2}$ and early neurological deterioration $(\ge4$ -point increase in scores on the NIHSS or ${}^{\ge2}$ -point decrease on the GCS from baseline to 24 hours).4,9 Data are reported with odds ratios (OR) and $95\%$ CIs. All analyses were performed with SAS software (version 9.3).  

# RESULTS  

Among 2829 INTERACT2 participants, 2571 ICH patients (mean [SD] age, 63.2 [12.7] years; 975 $[37.9\%]$ female) who were alive within 7 days and had available data were included (patient flowchart in Figure S1). There were 1492 $(58.0\%)$ ), 1057 $(41.1\%)$ , 731 $(28.4\%)$ , and 490 $(19.1\%)$ patients who experienced any, moderate, significant, and substantial SNI, respectively, from 24 hours to 7 days after ICH. The distribution of NIHSS score changes from 24 hours to 7 days after ICH is shown in Figure S2. The baseline characteristics of ICH patients with and without any SNI are shown in Table 1. Compared with patients without any SNI, those with any SNI were more often recruited outside of China, had less severe ICH (lower NIHSS and higher GCS scores), had less withdrawal of active treatment in the first 7 days, had greater use of antithrombotic agents and statins, had more deep location but less lobar ICH, and had less intraventricular hemorrhage extension.  

At 90 days, 1300 $(50.6\%$ ) experienced death or major disability, 1139 $(44.3\%)$ had major disability, and 161 $(6.3\%)$ had died. There was a statistically significant difference in the distribution of modified Rankin Scale scores between subjects with and without any SNI $(P<0.0001$ ; Figure 1). After adjustment for age, sex, baseline NIHSS score, baseline hematoma volume, hematoma location, and other key confounders, those with any SNI had significantly $49\%$ , $25\%$ , and $65\%$ lower odds of death or major disability (OR, 0.51 $[95\%$ CI, 0.42–0.63]), major disability (OR, 0.75 $[95\%$ CI, $0.63\substack{-0.90}]$ ), and death (OR, 0.35 $[95\%$ CI, 0.24–0.50]) compared with patients without any SNI (Figure 2). Compared with patients without moderate SNI, those with moderate SNI were also associated with a $46\%$ $25\%,$ and $57\%$ reduced odds of death or major disability (OR, 0.54 $[95\%$ CI, 0.45–0.66]), major disability (OR, 0.75 $[95\%$ CI, $0.63\substack{-0.91}],$ ), and death (OR, 0.43 $[95\%$ CI, 0.29–0.64]) after adjustment for key confounders. Similar findings were observed for significant and substantial SNI, which were also associated with reduced odds of death or major disability at 90 days (Figure 2).  

Significant associations between any SNI and death or major disability, major disability and death alone, were observed across most subgroups by age, baseline hematoma volume, 24-hour hematoma expansion, and early neurological deterioration. There was an interaction between any SNI and early neurological deterioration on the outcome of major disability at 90 days (Pinteraction= 0.001). Any SNI was associated with reduced risk of major disability only in those without early neurological deterioration (OR, 0.65 $[95\%$ CI, 0.54–0.80]), but not in patients with early neurological deterioration (OR, 1.55 $[95\%$ CI, 0.93–2.59]; Table 2).

# DISCUSSION  

These post hoc analyses of the INTERACT2 database, involving mainly patients with mild-to-moderate ICH severity, have shown that more than half of them experienced SNI from 24 hours to 7 days. Any SNI, defined as a decrease of $\geq1$ point in NIHSS scores, was significantly associated with lower odds of death and major disability at 90 days. Furthermore, we observed that the association between any SNI and decreased odds of death or major disability was consistent across a broad range of early clinical characteristics, including hematoma volume, age, early hematoma expansion, and early neurological deterioration. There was no association between early intensive BP-lowering treatment and SNI.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to investigate the significance of SNI in patients with acute ICH. Neurological status is often unstable early after an ICH, with a higher likelihood of neurological deterioration within the initial 24 hours.4,9,14,15 This is largely due to early hematoma growth and existing or new intraventricular hemorrhage expansion.3,16,17 Studies indicated that $6.4\%$ to $19.7\%$ of ICH patients may experience neurological deterioration beyond the first 24 hours,4,9–11 which suggests most patients remain stable neurologically or experience improvement beyond the first 24 hours post-ICH. However, the frequency of neurological improvement from 24 hours to 7 days is unknown.  

NIHSS and GCS remain the most widely used scales for evaluating neurological deficits after ICH. Compared with the GCS score, which primarily reflects the level of consciousness, the NIHSS is a comprehensive scale that assesses various key components of neurological deficits, making it more effective in detecting changes in neurological improvement.18,19 Therefore, in the present study, we used the changes in NIHSS score to measure SNI. The present study also indicates that any, moderate, significant, and substantial SNI were associated with a reduced risk of death or major disability. Specifically, individuals with any SNI had $49\%$ , $25\%$ and $65\%$ lower odds of death or major disability, major disability alone, and death alone, respectively. Existing randomized controlled trials in ICH, such as intensive BP lowering and hemostatic therapy, target early hematoma expansion,5–7 but most of the findings to date have not shown any effects that translate into improved stroke outcomes.5–7 Our finding may have implications for clinical practice and in the design of future ICH trials to examine the neuroprotective,20,21 antioxidative,22 anti-inflammatory,23,24 and anti-edema effects25 of various drugs and compounds in the entire acute phase from symptom onset to 7 days after ICH.  

In the subgroup analysis, we showed that having any SNI was associated with reduced odds of death and major disability and was consistent across subgroups by age and baseline hematoma volume, which are known key predictors of 90-day outcome in ICH.26,27 Early hematoma expansion and early neurological deterioration are crucial predictors of death and disability after ICH,3,4,9,28 even in those with early hematoma expansion and early neurological deterioration, and we found any SNI remained significantly associated with a decreased risk of death.  

We found that early intensive BP-lowering treatment was not associated with SNI. These findings may help to explain why this intervention reduces early hematoma expansion but does not clearly reduce the risk of death or major disability in the trial.  

The strengths of our analysis were the use of a large and well-characterized clinical trial population managed across a broad range of health care settings, adjustment for multiple potential confounders, and assessment of several standardized outcome measures. However, our results are to some extent influenced by selection bias, as patients with large hematomas or who required early neurosurgery were purposely excluded, as were those who were ineligible through having normal (or low) systolic BP levels at presentation. Thus, participants in the INTERACT2 cohort tended to have mild-to-moderate ICH and a relatively small hematoma volume, raising issues about the generalizability of these findings to all ICH patients. Also, another limitation is that the INTERACT study did not measure neurological deficits using scales such as the FOUR Score and Coma Recovery Scale. The omission of these assessments may limit our understanding of the full spectrum of neurological function in ICH patients. Future trials would benefit from including such measures to enable more comprehensive evaluation of ICH recovery and its implications for patient outcomes. Finally, residual confounding is possible in an observational study, despite our efforts to adjust for several important potential confounders in the multivariable models.  

In summary, we showed a high frequency and important prognostic influence of SNI in mild-to-moderate ICH patients: more than half of these ICH patients experienced neurological recovery from 24 hours to 7 days, and having any SNI was significantly associated with a reduced likelihood of death or disability. Our findings may help to understand the importance of the surrogate measure of neurological status beyond 24 hours after ICH, and this could influence practice and clinical trial design as well as provide information for clinicians in the counseling of families.
