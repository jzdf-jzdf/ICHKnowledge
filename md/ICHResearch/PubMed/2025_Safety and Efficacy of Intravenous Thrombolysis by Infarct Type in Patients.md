BACKGROUND: About $25\%$ of patients with acute ischemic stroke have lacunar infarct on follow-up imaging. In this secondary analysis from the AcT (Alteplase Compared With Tenecteplase) trial, we assessed if there is variation in safety or efficacy of intravenous thrombolysis by infarct type in patients with no visible occlusion. We also determined if this effect differed between tenecteplase and alteplase.  

METHODS AND RESULTS: This is a secondary analysis from the AcT trial. Lacunar infarct was defined as single, $\le15\mathrm{mm}$ , and in regions supplied by perforating arterioles on 24-hour imaging. Outcomes included symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage at 24 hours, any hemorrhage on imaging, and 90-day modified Rankin Scale score. Mixed-effects regression models adjusted for age, sex, stroke severity, thrombolytic type, and onset-to-needle time were used. Of 1577 patients, $456/29.9\%$ had no visible occlusion and interpretable follow-up imaging (magnetic resonance imaging: $41.2\%$ . Of these 93 $(20.4\%)$ (magnetic resonance imaging: $62.3\%$ had lacunar infarct, 171 $(37.5\%)$ (magnetic resonance imaging: $67.4\%$ had nonlacunar infarct, and 192 $(42.1\%)$ (magnetic resonance imaging: $7.3\%$ ) had no visible infarct. Four patients $(2.3\%)$ in the group with nonlacunar infarct and none in the groups with lacunar infarct and no visible infarct developed symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage. Any intracranial hemorrhage was highest (21; $12.4\%$ in the group with nonlacunar infarct. There was no significant interaction between thrombolytic type and treatment outcomes.  

CONCLUSIONS: In the AcT trial, safety of intravenous thrombolysis was similar across all infarct types in patients with no visible occlusion but functional outcomes were worst in patients with nonlacunar infarct. Safety and functional outcomes after intravenous thrombolysis were better in the groups with lacunar infarct and no visible infarct as compared with the overall trial population.

A recent systematic review and metaanalysis found that the quality of evidence supporting the efficacy of thrombolysis in patients with LI is very low.3 Contrary to this, the benefit of thrombolysis in patients who have nonlacunar infarct (NLI) without vessel occlusion is also unknown. In a post hoc secondary analysis of the ENCHANTED (Enhanced Control of Hypertension and Thrombolysis Stroke Study) trial, the outcomes of patients with lacunar infarct $({\le}20\mathsf{mm}$ , in territory of penetrating arterioles, and without corresponding vessel occlusion) were compared with those with NLI. Patients with NLI were less likely to have excellent outcome (mRS score $0{-}1$ at 90 days) compared with those with LI ( $37.4\%$ versus $69.4\%$ ; adjusted odds ratio [OR], 0.60 $[95\%$ CI, 0.47–0.77]).5 In addition, there was a higher likelihood of symptomatic intracerebralhemorrhage (SICH; SITS-­MOST [Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke-­Monitoring Study]) in the group with NLI $2.3\%$ versus $0.2\%$ ; adjusted OR, 0.09 $[95\%$ CI, 0.01–0.63]). However, it is important to note that in this study, the group with NLI also included those with visible vessel occlusion.  

Tenecteplase is a second generation of tissue plasminogen activator with higher specificity for fibrin and a longer half-life, preventing the need for a 1-hour infusion that occurs with alteplase.6 Tenecteplase $(0.25\mathsf{mg}/\mathsf{kg})$ is now increasingly replacing alteplase for treatment of AIS within 4.5 hours of symptom onset in North America, Europe, and Australia.7–9 Given the pharmacological differences between tenecteplase and alteplase and the different pathophysiology of LI compared with NLI, it is unknown whether there are any differences in the safety and efficacy of tenecteplase as compared with alteplase varying on infarct type.  

The aim of the current study is to compare the safety and efficacy profiles of intravenous thrombolysis given to patients with LI, NLI, and no visible infarct (NI) on follow-up imaging in a subset of patients with no visible occlusion. Further, we aimed to assess whether these outcomes differed by thrombolytic type (tenecteplase versus alteplase) in the subpopulations with LI, NI, and NLI.  

# METHODS  

# Study Population  

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The present study was an observational cohort design and a secondary analysis of the AcT (Alteplase Compared With Tenecteplase) trial. The trial methods and inclusion and exclusion criteria of the trial have been reported previously.10,11 In summary, the AcT trial was a phase-3, prospective, open-label, registry-linked, randomized, controlled trial with blinded outcome assessment in 22 primary and comprehensive stroke care centers across Canada, involving patients with AIS eligible for thrombolysis according to 2018 Canadian Best Practice guidelines.12 The trial recruited patients presenting with AIS symptoms within 4.5 hours. Participants were randomized to receive either intravenous tenecteplase (0.25 mg/kg of body weight) or alteplase (0.9 mg/kg of body weight). Follow-up was done between 90 and 120days using standardized telephone interview by blinded outcome assessors. In this substudy, patients who had an available and interpretable computed-tomography angiography (CTA) at presentation, no visible occlusion (NVO) on CTA, and who received thrombolysis within 4.5 hours (symptom onset to needle time within 4.5 hours) were included. The trial was regulated by Health Canada and approved by research ethics boards at all participating sites. The trial used deferred consent procedures wherever approved by local research ethics boards.  

# Imaging Analysis  

An independent imaging core laboratory with 4 to 20 years of experience read all imaging data (baseline noncontrast CT, CTA, digital subtraction angiography, and follow-up scans). The imaging core laboratory, which included authors F.B., N.S., B.M., and M.A., was blinded to treatment allocation, clinical outcomes, and follow-up imaging (when reading baseline imaging). As is standard of care, noncontrast CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was assessed at 24 hours after administration of thrombolysis for any evidence of intracranial hemorrhage. First, patients with NvO on presenting CT-angiogram were identified. In all patients with NVO, 24-hour CT scans as well as MRI (when available) were examined by 2 of the authors (A.Z.S. and N.S.) to determine the existence of acute infarct, size of infarct, and location of infarct. If there was disagreement about whether an infarct existed, the decision was cross-referenced by the decision of the core laboratory. LIs were defined as those infarcts that were single, $\le15\mathrm{mm}$ in size, and located in the corona radiata, caudate head, lentiform nucleus, internal capsule, thalamus, or pons.13,14  

# Outcomes and Measures  

Safety outcomes included were SICH within 24 hours, mortality, and evidence of any intracranial hemorrhage on follow-up imaging as per the Heidelberg Bleeding classification.15 SICH was defined as any intracerebral hemorrhage that was temporally related to and directly responsible for a worsening of a patient’s neurological condition and in the investigator’s opinion was the most important factor for the neurological worsening.16 Functional outcomes included mRS17 scores 0 to 1, mRS 0 to 2, ordinal mRS at 90 to 120 days, return to baseline function/prestroke status, and length of stay in hospital. The mRS is a 7-point ordinal scale that is the most common method of measuring disability in stroke trials. The scale goes from 0, which signifies no disability, to 6, which signifies death. Return to baseline functioning is a pragmatic outcome measured in the AcT trial and was defined as the patient perceiving that they were able to return to all the activities in which they engaged before the event. Length of hospital stay was defined as the number of days from the patient’s admission to their discharge, assessed post hoc.  

# Statistical Analysis  

Baseline characteristics and imaging variables were compared between the groups with LI, NLI, and NI using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies with percentages and quantitative variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or means±SDs as appropriate per distribution of data. The Fisher exact test and Wilcoxon rank sum test or $t$ test were used for categorical and continuous variables respectively as appropriate per distribution of data. Adjusted analyses were performed using mixed effects models adjusted for age, sex, baseline stroke severity (NIHSS score), thrombolytic type (alteplase versus tenecteplase), and stroke onset-to-needle time as fixed effects variables and participating site as random effects variable. Further, we tested for interaction between thrombolytic type (alteplase versus tenecteplase) and infarct type on functional outcomes using multiplicative interaction terms. For adjusted analyses of outcomes, mixed effects Poisson regression for binary response variable (with no offset) was used adjusting for sex, baseline stroke severity measured using the NIHSS score, and thrombolytic type (alteplase versus tenecteplase) as fixed effects and enrolling site as the random-effects variable (assuming normal distribution) to account for clustering of data within sites. We used the robust sandwich estimator of variance. The ordinal mRS score was analyzed using mixed effects ordinal logistic regression (meologit function in Stata, which fits a cumulative logit model), adjusted as described previously. The Brant test was used to check for proportional odds assumption. All models were tested for the following assumptions: (1) normality of residuals using Shapiro Wilk test, (2) homogeneity of variance of residuals using Breusch test for homoscedasticity, and (3) linearity using scatterplots for quantitative predictors. We did not check for sphericity, because we did not use repeated measures in the analysis.  

There were no missing data, so we did not run an imputation analysis. All analyses were considered exploratory. Statistical significance was defined as 2-tailed $P{\le}0.05$ . Analyses was performed using Stata/ MP version 16.0 (Stata Corp.).

# DISCUSSION  

In this post hoc secondary analysis of the AcT trial, as compared with patients with Nl on follow-up imaging, patients with LI receiving intravenous thrombolysis had similar safety and efficacy outcomes. However, patients with NLI had the highest rates of any ICH and lowest rates of excellent functional outcome (mRS score 0–1) at 90 to 120 days. There was no difference in safety or efficacy between tenecteplase and alteplase.  

In the present study, patients with $\sfNI$ on follow-up imaging had the highest rate of excellent outcome and lowest rate of hemorrhagic transformation. However, it is difficult to conclude whether patients with $\sfNI$ on follow-up imaging truly did not have any infarct or ischemia to begin with as the majority $(92.7\%)$ of patients in this group had a CT instead of MRl for 24-hour follow-up imaging. CT is not very sensitive in detecting infarcts that are small, multiple, and deep in location or in the posterior fossa, especially in the hyperacute period.18 Thus, infarcts such as these may remain undetected on 24-hour imaging with CT. In addition, small infarcts tend to have lower risk of hemorrhagic transformation and a higher rate of excellent functional outcome.19,20

The fact that the group with NLls on follow-up imaging had NVO on baseline imaging could have been the result of a variety of different mechanisms. One likely possibility is that the vessel spontaneously recanalized before CTA and therefore, the imaging did not show any evidence of vessel occlusion.21 Other reasons include the presence of small, distal occlusions that are not visible on CTA, a shower of emboli from a proximal source in absence of intracranial occlusion, or an ipsilateral significant carotid stenosis leading to decreased perfusion and ischemia in absence of intracranial occlusion. Rates of excellent outcome seen in the group with NLI $(33.9\%)$ are similar to those seen in previous published data.5  

Interestingly, the results of the study demonstrated that almost half of patients with LI had an excellent outcome at 90 to 120 days with a no significant increase in intracranial hemorrhage. This is similar to the results seen in previous studies.5,22,2 3 Clinical examination (lacunar syndrome such as “"dysarthria-clumsy hand" and NIHSS score ${<}7$ ) can increase the likelihood that a patient presenting with AIS will have LI.3 However, these examination findings may overlap with cortical stroke syndromes $15\%$ to $20\%$ of the time and have poor specificity for identifying LI syndromes.3,24 Therefore, the stroke physician does not truly know when administering thrombolysis whether the patient will have LI. There is uncertainty regarding efficacy of intravenous thrombolysis for this stroke subtype for different reasons.3 First, lacunar strokes are often mild, thereby decreasing the benefit to risk ratio of intravenous thrombolysis. Second, lacunar strokes often occur in patients who have concomitant small vessel disease and leukoaraiosis, which may put patients at higher risk of intracranial hemorrhage. Finally, the pathophysiology of these infarcts is different from NLIs. Pathological studies have demonstrated arteriosclerosis with medial hyalinization, dense adventitial fibrosis, and foamy macrophages in the vessel wall of in small vessels in patients with lacunar stroke.2 Fibrinogen, von Willebrand's factor, and D-dimer levels are lower in the blood of patients with lacunar stroke versus that of patients with nonlacunar stroke.25 Why then would a drug that acts by breaking up a fibrin and platelet-rich clot be effective? One possibility is that the patients in the study who seemed to have an LI actually had a vessel occlusion. The vessel recanalized late enough to still cause a subcortical infarct but early enough to not cause cortical or other infarcts. This would be less likely in our study as we considered only those patients as LI who did not have a vessel occlusion on baseline  

CTA. Another reason for the benefit of thrombolysis in this population is that up to $5\%$ of lacunar strokes could be caused by cardioembolism,26 which may be readily amenable to thrombolysis. Lastly, the pathophysiology of lacunar stroke is not entirely understood, and it is possible that thrombolytic therapy does have mechanisms of removing occlusions in small, perforator vessels that need further elucidating.  

The present study did not demonstrate any differences between the safety and efficacy of tenecteplase versus alteplase for the groups with NLI, LI, or NI, which was not surprising. One would expect tenecteplase to have higher recanalization rates for vessel occlusion given its higher fibrin specificity and reduced binding affinity to plasminogen activator inhibitor.27 Indeed, some evidence from phase 2 studies does show that tenecteplase may have higher rates of recanalization compared with alteplase but this was not confirmed in phase 3 studies.28 The study also demonstrated that clinical outcomes in subgroups did not differ between thrombolytic groups.29 Lastly, the thrombolytic subgroups in the group with LI were small and not powered to detect a significant difference in outcomes.  

# LIMITATIONS  

One of the main limitations of the present study is that most of the patients did not have MRI scans. Compared with MRI, CT scans have much lower sensitivity for detecting infarction, especially small deeper infarcts such as lacunes.30 Given that $92.7\%$ of the group with NI did not have MRI, it is possible that infarcts in the group with NI were missed owing to the limitations of CT. This could have led to the results of the groups with LI and NI to look similar. Nevertheless, the images were read by a core laboratory consisting of experienced stroke neurologists and neuroradiologists who went through the scans to adjudicate the presence of infarct on follow-up imaging. Another limitation of the study is that two thirds of the population with LI received tenecteplase and one third received alteplase. As tenecteplase may have a lower rate of ICH,31 this could have affected the analysis and the comparison to the group with NI, which had a higher proportion of patients that received alteplase. Additionally, there is no placebo arm and hence we cannot comment on whether outcomes in each of the subgroups differed by administration of intravenous thrombolysis. Finally, this is a post hoc secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial, thus the results are only hypothesis generating.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

In this secondary analysis of the AcT trial, we demonstrate that intravenous thrombolysis has similar safety and efficacy outcomes in patients with LI as compared with those with NI. Patients with NLI have higher but nonsignificant rates of symptomatic hemorrhage and worse outcomes compared with the other 2 groups. There was no effect modification by thrombolytic type on outcomes in this patient population.
