# ABSTRACT  

Background: Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) are common in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients. The presence of CMBs increases the risk of hemorrhagic transformation in AIS patients, and it is also closely associated with cognitive decline and even dementia. At present, there exist different opinions on the independent risk factors for CMBs, and there is no consensus on whether there are gender differences in -post-stroke CMB. Therefore, this study sought to investigate gender heterogeneity in the influencing factors for CMBs by studying male and female AIS patients.  

Methods: This was a China-based, Single-center, retrospective review of data from 482 AIS inpatients at the Neurology Department of Hebei General Hospital (NCT05882123). Both demographic and clinical data were collected from the study subjects. Different head magnetic resonance imaging sequences were used to assess the subjects’ CMBs, white matter lesions, and old lacunar infarcts (LI). Various statistical methods, including the $t\cdot$ -test, $\chi^{2}$ test, and logistic regression, were used to analyze the gender heterogeneity of the influencing factors for CMBs in AIS patients.  

Results: When compared with the male AIS patients, the female AIS patients were older and had higher total cholesterol, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, ApoA, ApoB, and fibrinogen levels. The female AIS patients also had higher National Institute of Health Stroke Scale scores and hypertension disease composition ratios. By contrast, the proportions of female AIS patients with a history of smoking and a history of alcohol consumption were both lower than the corresponding proportions of male AIS patients. These differences were all statistically significant $(p<.05)$ . There were no statistically significant differences in the incidence and severity of CMBs between the male and female AIS patients $(\chi^{2}~=~0.851$ , 3.092, $p>.05)$ . The univariate and multivariate stepwise logistic regression analyses confirmed that age $({\mathsf{OR}}=1.074\$ , $95\%$ CI: 1.013–1.139, $p=.016)$ and old LI ( $\mathsf{\{OR=}}$ 4.295, $95\%$ CI: 1.062–17.375, $p=.041$ ) were independent risk factors for comorbid CMBs in the female AIS patients, while blood glucose $\mathrm{\Delta}OR=0.692.$ $95\%$ CI: 0.494–0.968, $p=.031$ ) was an independent protective factor for comorbid CMBs in the female AIS patients. However, these factors were not found to be independent risk or protective factors for comorbid CMBs in male AIS patients.  

Conclusion: There are gender differences in the influencing factors for CMBs in AIS patients. Age, old LIs, and blood glucose are independent risk or protective factors for comorbid CMBs in female AIS patients, although they are not associated with the risk of developing CMBs in male AIS patients.

# 1. Introduction  

Stroke is the leading cause of death in China. Indeed, the estimated prevalence, incidence, and mortality rate of stroke in China were $2.6\%$ , 505.2 per 100,000 person-years, and 343.4 per 100,000 person-years, respectively, among adults aged 40 years or older in $2020^{1}$ . The signs of cerebral small vessel disease on conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) include recent subcortical infarcts, white matter changes, lacunes, prominent perivascular spaces, cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), and atrophy . As one of the markers of cerebral small vessel disease, CMBs have attracted widespread attention. CMBs occur due to the destruction of vascular walls and plasma exudation caused by cerebral microvasculopathy resulting in hemosiderosis3. CMBs can be detected by means of MRI, T2-weighted gradient-recalled echo (T2-GRE), and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). Among these techniques, SWI can detect CMBs with greater sensitivity4.  

CMBs are more common in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients than in healthy individuals, although various studies have shown that the incidence of CMBs in AIS patients varies considerably, ranging from $20\%-60\%$ . The specific cause of such a high level of variability remains unclear. It may be due to the type of stroke, infarction site, or differences in the risk factors for CMBs. Evidence has shown that the presence of CMBs increases the risk of hemorrhagic transformation in AIS patients, particularly at the start of anticoagulant treatment5 and it is also closely associated with poststroke depression, cognitive decline, and even dementia6,7. As a consequence, it is particularly important to analyze the risk factors for CMBs in AIS patients and thereby to identify the cause of the variability in CMB detection rates.  

Currently, the generally recognized independent risk factors for CMBs include age, history of hypertension, history of stroke, white matter lesions (WMLs), and old lacunar infarcts (LI). However, there are also other factors that damage cerebral capillaries, such as diabetes, lipid levels, and uric acid. Different studies offer different perspectives on these additional factors. Moreover, previous studies have conducted indepth examinations of gender heterogeneity in diseases such as tumor, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease, showing that gender heterogeneity exists in all of these diseases8,9. Yet, there is a noticeable lack of gender heterogeneity studies on CMBs. Due to the physiological differences between male and female individuals, there are gender differences from the clinical level to the molecular level in relation to stroke. In fact, studies have shown that there are differences in ischemic stroke between different genders, especially in terms of lacunar infarction. Although the severity of lacunar stroke is similar between males and females, more elderly women than men experience this type of stroke, and long-term hospitalization, obesity, arterial hypertension, and age are all risk factors for women. Male peripheral vascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, renal dysfunction, and heavy smoking may be more closely associated with lacunar stroke in men10. In addition, different stroke mechanisms in male and female patients may result in differences in ${\mathsf{CMB}}{\mathsf{s}}^{11}$ . Most prior studies found no relationship between gender and the risk of poststroke ${\mathsf{CMBs}}^{12,13}$ although some have shown that male AIS patients are more prone to CMBs than female patients14. Yet, there is no consensus on whether there are gender differences in post-stroke CMB, and further study is required to determine whether gender differences exist in relation to post-stroke CMBs. Hence, this study sought to investigate gender heterogeneity in the influencing factors for CMBs by studying male and female AIS patients, which could provide references for the prevention, early individual diagnosis, and treatment of CMB patients of different genders.  



# 2. Materials and methods  



# 2.1. Study subjects  

In this retrospective study, the data of 482 patients with AIS who were admitted to the Department of Neurology of Hebei Provincial People’s Hospital from January 2014 to December 2015 were continuously collected to investigate gender heterogeneity in the influencing factors for CMBs by studying male and female AIS patients (NCT05882123).  

Inclusion criteria are as follows: all participants aged 18 years and older, and must meet the diagnostic criteria for AIS as presented in the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke $2014^{15}$ . They must be in good physical condition and have the ability to cooperate with MRI examinations; and they must have underwent SWI. Exclusion criteria are as follows: MRI contraindications present, such as cardiac pacemaker, cardiac stents, or metal implants. Head MRI or carotid artery ultrasound suggests the presence of severe stenosis or occlusion in the intracranial or extracranial segments of the cerebral arteries; and severe comorbid diseases, such as cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, and renal insufficiencies. Among them, 252 patients with AIS were excluded in this study due to exclusion criteria. 230 patients with AIS finally were enrolled in this study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and internationally accepted standards of Good Clinical Practice. Detailed descriptions of the study designs can be found in their primary reports.  

The detailed research process is shown in the research flow chart of Figure 1.  



# 2.2. General information  

The demographic data and detailed clinical data collected include sex, age, history of smoking and alcohol consumption, body mass index (BMI), the score of the National Institute of Health stroke scale (NIHSS), usage of antiplatelet drugs, as well as a history of hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, hyperlipidemia, past ischemic strokes, and TOAST subtypes [Large-artery atherosclerosis (LA), Cardioembolism (CE), Small-artery occlusion Lacunar (SA), Acute stroke of other determined etiology (Others)].  



# 2.3. Laboratory tests  

After admission, the patients venous blood was collected on an empty stomach at 6-7 am the next morning of the following day for total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), lowdensity lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), uric acid, fibrinogen, and urine protein tests. The normal values, as follow, for laboratory tests in Hebei General Hospital were used to determine if there were abnormalities in the aforementioned markers: TC: 2.8– 5.7 mmol/L, TG: 0.56–1.7 mmol/L, LDL: $1.68{-}4.53\mathrm{mmol/L},$ HDL: $0.78{-}1.55\mathrm{mmol/L}$ , VLDL: $0.28{-}0.85\mathrm{mmol/L}$ , uric acid: $155-$ 428ummol/L, fibrinogen: $2{-}49/\mathsf{L}$ , and urine protein: negative.  



# 2.4. Head MRI scan  

A $3.0\top$ Signa HD superconducting magnetic resonance imaging scanner manufactured by the General Electric Company, USA, was used to carry out cross-sectional T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and SWI scans. Two experienced radiologists who were blinded to the patients’ medical history interpreted the various MRI sequence images. The chief physician of the radiology department reviewed the images when there were differences in opinion between the two radiologists.

# 2.5. Definition of WML and imaging evaluation  

WML refers to T2WI or FLAIR hyperintensities, T1WI isointensities or hypointensities, and when cavities are absent, which are different from cerebrospinal fluid signals3. Age-related white matter change (ARMC) scale16 was used as a reference for WML scoring, and the temporal lobe, occipital lobe, parietal lobe, frontal lobe, infratentorial region, and basal ganglia were scored. A total score from zero to 30 points was obtained by adding up the various scores and ranges.  



# 2.6. Definition of old LI and imaging evaluation  

Old LI are circular or oval-shaped cavities with a diameter of $3{-}15\mathrm{mm}$ that are located at the subcortical region and filled with signals identical to those for cerebrospinal fluid. These are associated with an old infarction in the blood-supplying regions of penetrating arteries and appear as cerebrospinal fluid-like hypointense signals in the center surrounded by a hyperintense ring in the FLAIR sequence.  



# 2.7. Definition of CMBs and imaging evaluation  

CMBs are circular or oval-shaped signal-free homogeneous lesions with clear borders and surrounded by brain parenchyma on the T2-GRE or SWI sequence. CMBs are $2{-}10\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter; T1 and T2 weighted images do not show hyperintensities at the corresponding sites3. The microbleed anatomical rating scale (MRAS)12,13 was used to count the number of CMBs in the infratentorial (brain stem, cerebellum), deep (basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, external capsule, corpus callosum, deep and periventricular white matter), and brain lobes (temporal lobe, parietal lobe, frontal lobe, occipital lobe, and insular lobe) regions. Two deputy chief physicians were responsible for individual scoring, and the mean value was used as the final number of CMBs. The final score was used to classify the CMBs into four severity grades: Grade 0, none; Grade 1, 1–5 CMBs; Grade 2, 6–15 CMBs; and Grade $3,>15{\mathsf{CMBs}}^{17}$ .  



# 2.8. Statistical analysis  

SPSS 21.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Normally distributed quantitative data were represented using mean $\left(\bar{\mathsf{x}}\right)\pm$ standard deviation (s), and intergroup comparison was carried out using a t-test. Skewed distributed quantitative data were represented using median $\left(\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{d}}\right)$ or inter-quartile range (QR), and intergroup comparison was carried out using the Mann-Whitney $U$ test. Qualitative data was expressed as a percentage $(\%)$ , and intergroup comparison was carried out using the $\chi^{2}$ test. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to analyze the correlation of two ordinal variables. Logistic regression analysis was used for multivariate analysis. A difference of $p<.05$ was considered statistically significant.

# 3.2. Comparison of clinical data between the male and female AIS patients  

When compared with the male AIS patients, the female AIS patients were older and had higher TC, TG, HDL, LDL, ApoA, ApoB, and fibrinogen levels. The female patients also had higher NIHSS scores, and a higher proportion of them had hypertension. By contrast, the proportions of female AIS patients with a history of smoking and a history of alcohol consumption were both lower than the corresponding proportions of male AIS patients. # 3.3. Comparison of CMB-related characteristics between the male and female AIS patients  

There were 160 males and 70 females enrolled in this study. The overall incidence of CMBs in the male AIS patients was $47.50\%$ (76/160), while the distribution of the CMB sites in the males was as follows: infratentorial, $11.84\%$ (9/76); deep region, $18.42\%$ (14/76); brain lobes, $22.37\%$ (17/76); and mixed, $47.37\%$ (36/76). # 4. Discussion  

In this study, the overall incidence of CMBs in AIS patients was $48.26\%,$ which is slightly higher than the $23.2\%$ reported in previous studies18. The cause of this difference may be the fact that the enrolled subjects were inpatients of older age, with poor vascular foundation, and with more underlying diseases, or that SWI was employed for the detection of CMBs, which has a higher detection rate than $\mathsf{GRE-T}2^{\ast}\mathsf{W}|^{19}$ This study further found that, when compared with the nonCMB group, the CMB group included patients who were older and had higher WML scores, and it also included a higher proportion of patients with hypertension, old LIs, a history of cerebral infarction, and a history of a cerebral hemorrhage. By contrast, the CMB group included a lower proportion of patients with hyperlipidemia as well as patients with lower TG and VLDL levels. These findings are fairly consistent with the generally recognized independent risk factors for the development of CMBs.  

The different stroke mechanisms in male and female patients may lead to differences in their respective ${\mathsf{CMB}}{\mathsf{s}}^{11}$ . This study found that the incidence of CMBs in male and female AIS patients was $47.50\%$ and $51.43\%$ respectively, which indicates that the risk of developing CMBs in AIS patients is not related to gender. This finding is consistent with the results of most prior studies20,21. When compared with the male AIS patients, the female AIS patients in this study were older and had higher TC, TG, HDL, LDL, ApoA, ApoB, and fibrinogen levels. They also had higher NIHSS scores. Moreover, a higher proportion of the female AIS patients had hypertension, while the proportions of the female AIS patients with a history of smoking and a history of alcohol consumption were both lower than the corresponding proportions of the male AIS patients. An earlier study found that women differ from men in terms of the distribution of the risk factors for stroke as well as the stroke subtype, severity, and outcome .  

This study demonstrated that gender heterogeneity exists in the influencing factors for CMBs in AIS patients. More specifically, with regard to the CMB predictive factor analysis for the male AIS patients, it was found that the CMB grade is closely associated with age, TC, LDL, ApoB, history of cerebral infarction, history of cerebral hemorrhage, old LIs, and use of antiplatelet drugs. Yet, the multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that these factors cannot serve as independent risk or protective factors for comorbid CMBs in male AIS patients.  

In terms of the CMB predictive factor analysis for the female AIS patients, it was found that the CMB grade is closely associated with age, blood glucose, hyperlipidemia, TG, VLDL, and old LIs. In addition, the multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that age and old LIs are independent risk factors for comorbid CMBs in female AIS patients, whereas blood glucose is an independent protective factor for comorbid CMBs in female AIS patients. In other words, older female AIS patients with old LIs have a higher risk of developing CMBs, while female AIS patients with higher blood glucose have a lower risk of developing CMBs. Age is widely recognized as an independent risk factor for developing CMBs, which may be due to the association between CMBs and age-related small vessel disease. The cortex-subcortex and deep white matter regions are located at the distal end of blood circulation, where collateral circulation is poor. As a result, the small arteries in these sites tend to develop ischemic changes with age, which results in intravascular endothelial damage and CMBs22. Moreover, hypertensive vasculopathy and cerebral amyloid angiopathy also show important correlations with age. However, this study found that the female AIS patients were older than the male AIS patients, while age was found to be an independent risk factor for comorbid CMBs in the female AIS patients but not in the male AIS patients. This difference may be due to agerelated changes in hormone levels. Although sex hormone levels, including testosterone for males and estradiol for females, decrease with age in both sexes, males experience fairly slow sex hormone decreases, whereas females experience drastic decreases before and after menopause and to a greater degree than those experienced by males23,24. Furthermore, estrogen has some protective effects in relation to cerebro-cardiovascular disease. Estrogen decreases vascular oxidative stress and protects vascular endothelial cells through the regulation of NADPH oxidase expression and function, as well as through antioxidant enzymes25. During the late stage of menopause, the estrogen level drastically decreases, which activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and sympathetic nervous system, causing vascular endothelial dysfunction26. Therefore, age is a more important risk factor for the development of CMBs in female AIS patients than it is in male AIS patients.  

Previous studies have come to differing conclusions regarding whether or not diabetes is associated with CMBs. For instance, one meta-analysis found that a history of diabetes is a risk factor for CMBs, whereas another found that it is a protective factor for ${\mathsf{CMB}}{\mathsf{s}}^{27}$ . In this study, we did not find an association between diabetes and comorbid CMBs in AIS patients, although we did find that blood glucose is an independent protective factor for CMBs in female AIS patients. That is, female AIS patients with higher blood glucose have a lower risk of developing CMBs. However, further large-scale multicenter studies are required to overcome this inconsistency in the research findings.  

It is widely accepted that hyperlipidemia increases the risk of developing both coronary heart disease and ischemic stroke. This is because lipid deposition in large and medium blood vessels can result in atherosclerosis. Yet, researchers have differing opinions on whether blood lipid abnormalities can affect capillary structure and function. Some researchers have found that a low serum TC level and low LDL level are associated with CMBs28, and further, that HDL elevation and TG reduction may both increase the risk of developing CMBs in general, albeit without increasing the risk of developing deep ${\mathsf{CMB}}s^{29}$ . This study found that, while there was no difference between the proportion of the female AIS patients with hyperlipidemia and that of the male AIS patients, the female AIS patients had higher TC, TG, HDL, LDL, ApoA, and ApoB levels. Additionally, the multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that the female AIS patients with comorbid hyperlipidemia tended not to develop CMBs. Blood lipids are ubiquitous in various tissues in the human body, among which nervous tissues have an extremely high content. TC and TG are the basic components of the cell membrane, and they play vital roles in regulating cell membrane function and maintaining cell membrane stability and fluidity30,31. Low TC and TG levels cause the cerebral artery endothelium to become thinner, cell permeability to increase, and erythrocyte fragility to increase32,33. This causes microaneurysms, vascular leakage, and hemolysis, thereby causing CMBs34. Moreover, a low TG level has been found to negatively correlate with vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors and the plasminogen activator inhibitor, which may result in a tendency toward bleeding35. In AIS patients, ischemic and hypoxic damage in brain tissues and free radical production in brain cells disrupt the cell membranes, causing changes in many protein functions, including the ion channels, pump channels, and transporter dysfunction, in addition to the blockage of transmembrane nutrient transport. Under such circumstances, TC exerts protective antioxidant effects30. When the TC level decreases, its protective antioxidant effects on brain tissue are weakened, resulting in CMBs. Estrogen can significantly decrease both TC and LDL, increase HDL, and inhibit LDL oxidation to improve blood lipid levels. Postmenopausal women lack this protective mechanism, meaning that their blood lipid levels significantly increase to become higher than those of men. Therefore, a reasonable blood lipid level is vital to the maintenance of cerebral small vessel wall integrity, indicating that excessive intensive lipid-lowering treatment should be avoided in AIS patients with low blood lipid levels.  

To sum up, the mechanism of gender heterogeneity in AIS patients with CMBs is not yet clear, although it may be related to the following: (1) biological factors, such as gonadal hormones and genetic differences; (2) psychological factors, such as gender-specific symptom profiles; (3) social factors, such as gender-specific exposure to stressors; and/or (4) personality traits, such as those associated with susceptibility to stress and a tendency to experience negative emotions. However, the results of this study demonstrated that the effects of various factors related to CMBs are associated with gender differences, suggesting that gender-specific intervention measures can be formulated to prevent and treat AIS. Thus, the focus of clinical interventions should differ in males and females. For male AIS patients, the focus should be on ordinary lifestyle improvements, such as smoking cessation, limiting alcohol consumption, engaging in moderate exercise, and weight control. By contrast, for female AIS patients, especially those who are postmenopausal, regular physical examinations should be carried out and attention should be paid to risk factors such as blood lipids and blood glucose, which should serve to decrease the risk of developing CMBs.  



# 5. Conclusion  

In conclusion, we found there to be gender differences in the influencing factors for CMBs in AIS patients. More specifically, age, old LIs, and blood glucose were found to be an independent risk or protective factors for comorbid CMBs in female AIS patients, although these factors were not found to be associated with the risk of developing CMBs in male AIS patients.
