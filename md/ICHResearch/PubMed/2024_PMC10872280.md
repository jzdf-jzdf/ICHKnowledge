# Abstract  

Background: Observational studies suggest magnesium may have hemostatic effects. Field Administration of Stroke Therapy-Magnesium (FAST-MAG) was a pragmatic clinical trial of magnesium sulfate administered pre-hospital for acute clinical stroke syndromes and included intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) patients. Exploratory secondary analysis by treatment group found no reduction in hematoma expansion (HE) associated with magnesium treatment in ICH but did not consider serum magnesium levels achieved. We analyzed FAST-MAG ICH data for associations between serum magnesium level and hematoma expansion (HE) and early neurologic deterioration, accounting for groupwise biases.  

Methods: HE was defined as hematoma volume increase $\ge3\mathrm{mL}$ within 24 hours and early neurologic deterioration as $\geq1$ point Glasgow Coma Scale decline from arrival to hospital day 4. Comparing treatment and placebo groups confirmed biased availability of neuroimaging data. Therefore, HE and neurologic deterioration were analyzed stratified by treatment and placebo groups using univariate tests and adjusted logistic regression.  

Results: Spontaneous ICH was present in 381 patients. Placebo patients had fewer serial neuroimaging studies available (123 $[65.4\%]$ versus 145 $[75.1\%]$ , $\mathtt{p}{=}0.038,$ ). Necessary data were available in 104 magnesium and 85 placebo treated patients (age 64.9 [13.0] years, $67.7\%$ male). In the magnesium group, higher magnesium level was associated with less HE (aOR 0.64 per mg/dL; $95\%$ CI 0.42, 0.93) and less neurologic deterioration (aOR 0.54 per mg/dL; $95\%$ CI 0.33, 0.82). In the placebo group, magnesium level was not associated with either HE or neurologic deterioration.  

Conclusions: Magnesium may exhibit a hemostatic effect that was only observable in the FAST-MAG magnesium treatment group. Equipoise should be maintained and specific trials are needed.

Observational studies suggest a hemostatic role for magnesium in hemorrhagic stroke consistent with magnesium’s biological role in coagulation and platelet function.1–3 The Field Administration of Stroke Therapy-Magnesium (FAST-MAG) clinical trial investigated the neuroprotective effects of magnesium sulfate versus placebo administered by emergency medical services for acute stroke syndromes.4 Given the pre-hospital design, patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) were enrolled. FAST-MAG was a pragmatic trial; sites were at liberty to collect and report supplemental data elements, such as serum magnesium levels and neuroimaging data, but were not obligated to do so. A recent descriptive report of FAST-MAG ICH data suggested no difference in initial hematoma volumes or hematoma expansion (HE) between patients receiving magnesium versus placebo.5 That report did not evaluate serum magnesium levels, the likely biologically relevant factor, nor account for potential imbalances in supplemental data availability introduced by the pragmatic design. We undertook an analysis to identify associations between serum magnesium level, rate of HE, and incidence of early neurologic deterioration using stratification by therapeutic group to account for imbalances in data availability between groups.  

# Methods  

Details of FAST-MAG have been published previously and are summarized in the Supplement.4  

We received FAST-MAG “CORE” and HE databases from the FAST-MAG publication committee. These databases were merged using site and patient-level codes. Our primary endpoint was HE within 24 hours of hospital arrival with secondary endpoint of early neurologic deterioration ( $\geq1$ point decrease in Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] from arrival to hospital day 4), both assessed by adjusted logistic regression. Only patients with both arrival serum magnesium level and serial neuroimaging could be included. We excluded extra-axial intracranial hemorrhages $(\mathrm{n}{=}2)$ ) and patients with surgical hematoma evacuation before repeat neuroimaging (4 magnesium and 6 placebo patients). We determined if treatment groups differed by availability of serial neuroimaging or magnesium levels. If significant differences in data availability were identified, we subsequently stratified analysis by treatment group to account for imbalance in data availability.  

We considered HE to occur when hematoma volume on repeat imaging increased $\ge\mathrm{mL}$ in 24 hours. Dowlatshani et al. used the method of Youden to identify $3\mathrm{mL}$ as the optimal cutoff maximizing HE sensitivity and specificity. Statistical approach details are in the Supplement.  

# Results  

Spontaneous ICH was present in 381 $(22.4\%)$ patients enrolled in FAST-MAG, including 193 $(50.7\%)$ magnesium and 188 $(49.3\%)$ placebo randomized patients (Supplement). Serum magnesium levels were available in 143 $(74.1\%)$ magnesium and 131 $(69.7\%)$ placebo treated patients. Time from last known well to magnesium level assessment was 9.37 (1.75, 17.88) hours. Placebo patients had significantly fewer serial neuroimaging studies available (123 $[65.4\%]$ versus 145 $[75.1\%]$ , $\mathtt{p}{=}0.038,$ ). Time from last known well to first neuroimaging study was 1.47 (1.21, 1.87) hours and time from initial to repeat neuroimaging was 16.7 (11.3–22.3) hours; neither time differed between treatment groups. There were 104 $(55.0\%)$ magnesium and 85 $(46.7\%)$ placebo treated ICH patients who met criteria for further analysis (Table 1, Supplement). Given the difference in neuroimaging availability, subsequent analyses were stratified by treatment group, according to our methodology.  

# Magnesium therapy group  

Median initial serum magnesium level was $3.70\left(3.20,4.53\right)\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , ranging from 1.40 to $7.20\mathrm{mg/dL}$ . Initial hematoma volume was not correlated with magnesium level (Spearman correlation $-0.16$ , $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p=0.10}}$ ). HE $\scriptstyle\geq3.0\mathrm{mL}$ occurred in 44 $(42.3\%)$ patients. Patients with HE had significantly lower magnesium levels (3.50 [2.88, 3.90] versus 3.80 [3.48, 4.63] $\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , $\mathrm{p}{=}0.007.$ ). After adjustment, higher magnesium level was independently associated with lower odds of HE (Table 2, supplement, adjusted OR 0.64 per mg/dL; $95\%$ CI 0.42, 0.93; $\mathtt{p}{=}0.024,$ ).  

GCS score on hospital day four was available in 93 $(89.4\%)$ ) patients. Day four median GCS was 14 (8, 15) with change from admission of 0 $(-5,0)$ points, though 45 $(48.4\%)$ patients experienced neurologic deterioration ≥1 GCS points. Change in GCS was significantly associated with magnesium level (Spearman correlation 0.27, $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p=0.009}}$ ), and those without compared to with neurologic deterioration had significantly higher magnesium levels (4.00 [3.50, 4.78] versus $3.50[2.90,4.00]\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p=0.002}},$ ). Adjusting for ICH score, higher magnesium level remained associated with lower odds of neurologic deterioration by hospital day four (adjusted OR 0.54 per mg/dL; $95\%$ CI 0.33, 0.82; $\mathtt{p=}0.007.$ ).  

In the magnesium therapy group, a magnesium level cut-point of $\mathrm{\yen4.3mg/dL}$ maximized the negative predictive value for HE $(78.6\%)$ and for neurologic deterioration $(75\%)$ .  

# Placebo therapy group  

Median initial serum magnesium level was $2.00\left(1.90,2.20\right)\mathrm{mg/dL}$ , ranging from 1.10 to $4.2\mathrm{mg/dL}$ . Initial hematoma volume was not correlated with magnesium level (Spearman correlation 0.04, $\mathrm{p}{=}0.74$ ). HE $\scriptstyle\geq3.0\mathrm{mL}$ occurred in 35 $(41.2\%)$ patients. Placebo group patients with HE did not differ in magnesium levels (2.10[1.90, 2.25] versus 2.00 [1.90, 2.18] mg/dL, $\mathtt{p=}0.47.$ ). Even after adjustment, magnesium level was not associated with lower HE in the placebo group (Supplement, adjusted OR 1.80 per mg/dL; $95\%$ CI 0.37, 9.26; $\mathrm{p}{=}0.46,$ ).  

GCS score on hospital day four was available in 78 $(91.8\%)$ ) of patients. Day four median GCS was 15 (9, 15) with change from admission of $0\left(-5,0\right)$ points, though 31 $(39.7\%)$ patients experienced neurologic deterioration ≥1 GCS points. Change in GCS score was not associated with magnesium level in the placebo group (Spearman correlation 0.05, $\mathtt{p=}0.68$ ).  

# Discussion  

While initial hematoma volumes and rate of HE did not differ between magnesium and placebo groups based on available supplemental data from the FAST-MAG trial, the neuroimaging necessary to evaluate HE was available less often in the placebo group. This may confound direct comparison of HE effects between treatment groups; in particular, observation bias may exist. A stratified analysis detected an association between serum magnesium level and both HE and early neurologic deterioration in patients randomized to magnesium sulfate but not to placebo. Several explanations are possible. FAST-MAG was designed to detect a modified Rankin Scale difference in an ischemic stroke population. Post-hoc analyses of small subgroups are vulnerable to type 1 and 2 errors. Unmeasured patient-level variables related to HE may also have influenced investigators to obtain supplemental data elements like serial neuroimaging and magnesium levels. Alternatively, the narrower range of magnesium levels in the placebo group than magnesium group may have impacted the ability to detect an effect. Moreover, magnesium may exhibit a threshold effect, such that hemostatic effects could only be appreciated in the magnesium treated patients where sufficiently high magnesium levels were more frequently achieved. A threshold effect would be consistent with prior observational data.2 The magnesium therapy group data suggests $4.3\mathrm{mg/dL}$ may represent a threshold target in ICH.  

Drawing firm conclusions about therapeutic effects from subgroup analyses may be challenging, especially when availability of necessary data is imbalanced. While our analysis is consistent with a threshold mediated effect of magnesium on hemostasis, type 1 error is possible. Our results suggest that the FAST-MAG trial neither excludes nor confirms an effect of serum magnesium on hemostasis. This question requires specifically designed trials and equipoise should be maintained.
