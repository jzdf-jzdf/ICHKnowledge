# Hemorrhagic Transformation in Noncardioembolic Acute Ischemic Stroke: MRI Analysis From PACIFIC-STROKE  

Chih-Hao Chen , MD, PhD; Ashkan Shoamanesh , MD; Pablo Colorado , MD; Feryal Saad , MD; Robin Lemmens , MD, PhD; Gian Marco De Marchis , MD, MSc; Valeria Caso , MD, PhD; Lizhen Xu, PhD; Laura Heenan, MSc; Jaime Masjuan , MD, PhD; Hanne Christensen , MD, PhD; Stuart J. Connolly , MD; Pooja Khatri , MD, MSc; Hardi Mundl , MD; Robert G. Hart , MD; Eric E. Smith, MD, MPH  

BACKGROUND: In the phase 2 PACIFIC-STROKE trial (Proper Dosing and Safety of the Oral FXIa Inhibitor BAY 2433334 in Patients Following Acute Noncardioembolic Stroke), asundexian, an oral factor XIa inhibitor, did not increase the risk of hemorrhagic transformation (HT). In this secondary analysis, we aimed to investigate the frequency, types, and risk factors of HT on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).  

METHODS: This was a secondary analysis of the PACIFIC-STROKE trial. Patients with mild-to-moderate acute noncardioembolic ischemic stroke were randomly assigned to asundexian or placebo plus guideline-based antiplatelet therapy. Brain MRIs were required at baseline $(\leq120$ hours after stroke onset) and at 26 weeks or end-of-study. HT was defined using the Heidelberg classification and classified as early HT (identified on baseline MRI) or late HT (new HT by 26 weeks) based on iron-sensitive sequences. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to test factors that are associated with early HT and late HT, respectively.  

RESULTS: Of 1745 patients with adequate baseline brain MRI (mean age, 67 years; mean National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, 2.8), early HT at baseline was detected in 497 $(28.4\%)$ ). Most were hemorrhagic infarctions (hemorrhagic infarction type 1: $15.2\%$ ; HI2: $12.7\%)$ while a few were parenchymal hematomas (parenchymal hematoma type $1:0.4\%$ ; parenchymal hematoma type 2: $0.2\%$ ). Early HT was more frequent with longer symptom onset-to-MRI interval. Male sex, diabetes, higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale large $(>15\mathrm{mm})$ infarct size, cortical involvement by infarct, higher number of acute infarcts, presence of chronic brain infarct, cerebral microbleed, and chronic cortical superficial siderosis were independently associated with early HT in the multivariable logistic regression model. Of 1507 with follow-up MRI, HT was seen in 642 $\phantom{-}42.6\%)$ ) overall, including 361 patients $(23.9\%)$ ) with late HT (new HT: 306; increased grade of baseline HT: 55). Higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, large infarct size, cortical involvement of infarct, and higher number of acute infarcts predicted late HT.  

CONCLUSIONS: About $28\%$ of patients with noncardioembolic stroke had early HT, and $24\%$ had late HT detectable by MRI.   
Given the high frequency of HT on MRI, more research is needed on how it influences treatment decisions and outcomes.

emorrhagic transformation (HT) of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is frequent and often asymptomatic but can lead to neurological deterioration.1 HT can occur spontaneously during the course of AIS, particularly in embolic strokes, but interventions such as thrombolysis, thrombectomy, or anticoagulation during the acute phase can exacerbate HT. Since the introduction of intravenous thrombolysis, HT has been classified as hemorrhagic infarct (HI) or parenchymal hemorrhage (PH), based on the size and mass effect of hemorrhage within the infarct and surrounding tissues.2 The presence of HT often prompts a delay in the initiation of antithrombotics after stroke.3,4

The incidence of spontaneous HT varies depending on the population studied, methods, timing of detection, and different sets of definitions. Risk factors for HT following thrombolysis or thrombectomy have been extensively studied, leading to the development of various prediction scores. These risk factors include increasing age, hypertension, hyperglycemia, higher stroke severity, and early signs of ischemia on computed tomography.1,5 HT has been reported in $10\%$ to $15\%$ of the patients with cardioembolic stroke who undergo neuroimaging performed within 48 to 72 hours after stroke onset, although most were not treated with reperfusion therapies.6–8  

Nevertheless, the prevalence, natural course, and factors influencing HT in acute noncardioembolic, mild-tomoderate stroke, or strokes not undergoing reperfusion therapy remained less studied.9,10 Additionally, previous studies of HT, including those defining HI and PH, were most often computed tomography–based, while the prevalence of HT using the more sensitive susceptibilityweighted imaging (SWI) on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has not been thoroughly investigated.9,11,12  

In the PACIFIC-STROKE phase 2 clinical trial (Proper Dosing and Safety of the Oral FXIa Inhibitor BAY 2433334 in Patients Following Acute Noncardioembolic Stroke; URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT04304508), the use of asundexian, a novel oral factor XIa inhibitor, in patients with acute noncardioembolic ischemic stroke, in addition to background antiplatelet therapy, did not increase the risk of major or minor bleeding, including intracranial hemorrhage or HT.13 As part of the trial, all enrolled patients were required to undergo an MRI scan at baseline (within  

120 hours of the index stroke event) and at a 26-week follow-up. Consequently, all patients had MRI data available with hemosiderin-sensitive sequences capable of detecting HT in the acute phase (referred to as early HT) and any incident HT, not seen on the baseline scan, in the chronic phase (referred to as late HT). The current study is an observational cohort study based on a secondary analysis of the PACIFIC-STROKE trial and is not a prespecified analysis.13 We hypothesized that the frequency and risk factors of early HT and late HT at 26 weeks may differ. In the present study, our aim is to investigate the frequency and types of HT among patients with noncardioembolic stroke and identify the clinical and neuroimaging risk factors associated with HT.  

# METHODS  

# Design and Participants  

This is an observational cohort study based on the Phase 2, randomized controlled trial PACIFIC-STROKE (Figure 1).13 Study data cannot be shared.  

Patients were eligible if they had acute noncardioembolic ischemic stroke and were 45 years old or greater and could be randomized to either the new drug asundexian or placebo in addition to a regular antiplatelet regimen within 48 hours of symptom onset. The trial protocol was approved by an institutional review board at each site, and participants provided written informed consent. For more details, please refer to the methods section of the Supplemental Material.  

# MRI Protocol  

MRIs were performed at each study site using their standard MRI machines. The protocol for the study MRI required the following sequences: fluid-attenuated inversion recovery, diffusion-weighted imaging, apparent diffusion coefficient, T1- and T2-weighted imaging, and an iron-sensitive sequence. Sites were allowed to choose the iron-sensitive sequence according to their local acute stroke protocol, which could include SWI, susceptibility-weighted angiography, or ${\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{2}}^{\star}$ gradient-recalled echo imaging.  

# Definition of HT  

HT was identified based on susceptibility-weighted sequences and defined as hypointensity in the infarct area on ironsensitive sequences or a lesion with signal characteristics consistent with an acute hematoma. Types of HT were classified in accordance with the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification, as hemorrhagic infarction type 1 (HI1), type 2 (HI2), parenchymal hematoma type 1 (PH1), type 2 (PH2; Figure 2). Two radiologists classified the degree of HT independently, blinded to clinical outcomes and treatment assignment, and in the case of discrepancy, the case was adjudicated by a third reader. Each patient was assigned an HT category; if a patient had multiple infarcts, then the category was assigned based on the maximum HT (PH1>PH2>HI2>HI1) in any of the infarcts. Furthermore, patients were classified into (1) no HT, (2) HI1, (3) HI2, and (4) any PH (PH1 and PH2 were collapsed into a single group given there were few PHs) groups. Early HT was assessed on the baseline MRI, and late HT was assessed on the 26-week MRI. Late HT was defined by either the appearance of new HT in a patient with no HT at baseline, or an increase in the grade of HT in a patient with a lower grade HT at baseline (Figure 1).

# Other Neuroimaging Markers  

Terms and definitions for brain infarcts, white matter hyperintensity,14 cerebral microbleeds (CMBs),15 and cortical superficial siderosis (cSS) were derived from the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging.16 Infarct lesions were classified by timing (acute versus chronic), size $(>15$ versus $\le15\mathrm{mm}$ axial diameter), and locations affected. Chronic infarcts should have cerebrospinal fluid-like signals on T1- and T2-weighted images and included typical lacunes based on the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging criteria,16 as well as cortical or large subcortical $(>15\mathrm{mm})$ infarcts. The locations of infarcts were classified into cortical, subcortical structures of the cerebrum, brainstem, and cerebellum; and the numbers of lesions within each location were documented. Furthermore, we have subcategorized chronic brain infarcts into the following mutually exclusive categories: (1) presence of prior cortical or large infarcts only; (2) presence of prior small subcortical infarcts only; (3) presence of both cortical or large and small subcortical infarcts.  

# Statistical Analysis  

The demographics, past medical history, characteristics of index stroke events,17 and neuroimaging features were compared between patients with and without HT. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to test independent baseline factors associated with early HT. The covariates in the full model included variables that were clinically important or reached a significant level of 0.20 in the univariate analysis. The final reduced model was built from the full model by using a backward stepwise selection procedure, with variable retention (stay) set at $R{<}0.05$ , forcing age, large infarct size, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), asundexian exposure, and time from stroke onset/last known well to MRI in the model. The model for late HT was specified in a similar fashion. Notably, the final reduced models were derived from the full models through the backward stepwise selection procedure, wherein a subset of variables demonstrating a statistically significant association $^{\prime}P{<}0.05)$ with the outcome of interest were retained, providing a focused representation of the key predictors of early and late HT, respectively. A $P$ value of ${<}0.05$ was considered statistically significant. A complete case approach was employed, including only subjects with complete data. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. There were no subgroup or sensitivity analyses. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). The study adhered to the STROBE guidelines (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology).18  

# RESULTS  

# Frequency of Early HT and Its Risk Factors  

There were 1808 patients randomized, of whom 1745 had adequate baseline MRI (done at $51.6{\pm}33.3$ hours after stroke onset) and 1507 had follow-up MRI with hemorrhage-sensitive sequences. A study flow diagram is shown in Figure 1.

At baseline MRI, pooling all doses of asundexian and placebo, there was no HT in 1248/1745 $(71.5\%)$ , HI1 in 266/1745 ( $15.2\%$ ), HI2 in 221/1745 ( $12.7\%\rangle$ ), PH1 in 7/1745 $\left(0.4\%\right)$ , and PH2 in 3/1745 $(0.2\%)$ ). The frequency of early HT increased with longer onset-to-MRI interval $(<12$ hours: 32/141 $[22.7\%]$ , 12 to 24 hours: 45/189 $[23.8\%]$ , 24–48 hours: 141/551 $[25.6\%],>48$ hours: 279/864 $[32.3\%]$ ; Ptrend ${<}0.001$ ; Figure 3A). In addition, the distribution of early HT on the baseline MRI, stratified by asundexian dose and whether the study MRI was clinically initiated (in which case it preceded asundexian exposure) or study initiated (in which case it was obtained up to 72 hours after the first asundexian dose) is shown in Figure 3B. In patients who had MRIs done after randomization, the asundexian dose was not associated with the baseline early HT category.  

Patient characteristics of those with and without early HT are shown in Table 1. In the multivariable-adjusted model, male sex (adjusted OR [aOR], 1.52 $[95\%$ CI, 1.17–1.97]), diabetes (aOR, 1.36 $[95\%$ CI, 1.05–1.77]), higher NIHSS score (aOR, 1.08 $[95\%$ CI, 1.03–1.14]), large $(>15\mathrm{mm})$ infarct size (aOR, 2.35 $[95\%$ CI, $1.81-$ 3.06]), cortical involvement of infarct (aOR, 1.58 $[95\%$ CI, 1.16–2.14]), higher number of acute infarcts (aOR, 1.04 $[95\%$ CI, 1.01–1.07]), presence of chronic brain infarcts (including prior cortical or large infarct, aOR, 3.94 $[95\%$ CI, 2.68–5.79]; prior small subcortical infarct, aOR, 2.20 $[95\%$ CI, 1.62–3.00], and both cortical or large and small subcortical infarct, aOR, 3.04 $[95\%$ CI, $2.16\mathrm{-}4.27]\mathrm{\rangle}$ ), presence of CMB (aOR, 1.40 $[95\%$ CI, 1.08–1.80]), presence of cSS (aOR, 4.84 $[95\%$ CI, 1.18–19.79]), and use of SWI or susceptibility-weighted angiography sequences versus gradient-recalled echo sequences (aOR, 1.85 $[95\%$ CI, 1.44–2.37]) were associated with early HT (Table 2).  

# Frequency of Late HT and Its Predictors  

Follow-up MRIs were done at a median of 26.0 weeks (range, 0.3–45.6 weeks) after randomization. In patients with available follow-up MRI, HT was seen in 642/1507 overall. This included 361 $(23.9\%)$ late HT, of which 306 patients had new HT and 55 had worsened grades of HT. Late HT was predominantly due to the new appearance of HI1 (133/361, $36.8\%$ ) or HI2 (171/361, $47.4\%)$ ), and less commonly increased area of HI1 (14/361, $3.9\%$ ) or HI2 (41/361, $11.4\%\rangle$ ). There was 1 new PH1 that occurred after baseline, in the placebo group and 1 new PH2 in the asundexian 20 mg group. The distributions of new HT categories were comparable between different asundexian dosage groups and placebo based on the randomization assignment (Figure 3C).

To investigate the timing of late HT, we examined 368 patients who underwent both $13\textdegree$ and 26-week MRI scans, which were used for safety assessment in part A of the trial. The types of late HT in these 9 patients were 2 HI1, 5 HI2, and 2 worsening grades of previous HI. Three of these late HT $(33\%)$ ) occurred on placebo (Table S2). No cases of PH developed between 13- and 26-week MRI scans.  

# DISCUSSION  

Based on the post hoc analysis of the PACIFIC-STROKE trial, we found that about $28\%$ of patients with noncardioembolic stroke had detectable early HT by MRI within 5 days of onset, while an additional $24\%$ had new late HT visible on the follow-up MRI done by week 26. The longer the time interval between stroke onset and MRI, the more HT could be detected with almost half of HT estimated to occur within 55 hours from stroke onset. Factors associated with HT included larger infarct size, higher stroke severity, and preexisting vascular brain lesions including chronic brain infarcts and CMB. The risk factors of HT found in the current study may provide clues for further investigation on the prevention of HT.  

Previous studies have predominantly focused on HT in patients who underwent reperfusion therapy, which may include a significant proportion of individuals with cardioembolic stroke.1 Reperfusion therapy and atrial fibrillation are well-known predictors of HT.5 Moreover, prior studies commonly relied on computed tomography scans to identify HT, despite their inferior sensitivity in detecting hemorrhage when compared with hemosiderin-sensitive sequences on MRI.19 Therefore, our study is one of the largest to date on the prevalence and natural progression of HT detected by MRI in patients with noncardioembolic stroke. In 1 study comparing the effects of dabigatran to aspirin in acute noncardioembolic stroke, it was found that an asymptomatic petechial HT developed in $5.6\%$ of patients on the day 30 MRI.9 The higher rate of HT detection in our study $(\approx28\%$ by day 5) may be attributed to the inclusion of patients with more severe strokes in the PACIFIC-STROKE trial (median NIHSS score 1 in the dabigatran trial while the mean NIHSS score was 2.8 in the PACIFIC-STROKE trial).

HT usually develops when the increased vascular permeability caused by ischemia takes place at around day 3 to 7 after stroke onset.20 This is consistent with our observations that the proportion of HT increased with longer onset-to-MRI interval. In addition, ${>}97\%$ of the HT identified in our study were HI. HI is usually considered asymptomatic and has not been associated with clinical worsening in other studies.21 HI is believed to result from diffuse low-pressure bleeding originating from necrotic brain tissue, which does not lead to the formation of any parenchymal hematoma or mass effect.22 Conversely, PH is considered high-pressure bleeding and possibly triggered by reperfusion into large severe ischemic tissue.22 Since the majority of our patients had small-vessel occlusion type of strokes and reperfusion therapy was only applied to a small proportion $(<15\%)$ ) of patients, it was not surprising that PH rarely occurred. In future analyses, we plan to investigate the association between HT grade and stroke outcomes in the PACIFIC-STROKE trial.  

In clinical practice, physicians may postpone the initiation or discontinue the ongoing antithrombotics, especially anticoagulants, when HT is detected.3,23 In the Early versus Late Initiation of Direct Oral Anticoagulants in Postischemic Stroke Patients with Atrial Fibrillation trial, the presence of PH was an exclusion criterion for early initiation of direct oral anticoagulant after ischemic stroke.24 The observation that asundexian use, a selective anticoagulant, did not increase the risk of HT is reassuring. In the PACIFIC-Stroke trial, asundexian did not increase major and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding in patients with acute noncardioembolic ischemic stroke.13 This novel class of antithrombotic is hypothesized to selectively inhibit clot progression without interfering with hemostasis activated by the tissue factor pathway.25 This can explain the low bleeding risk and favorable safety profile observed in several clinical trials using asundexian and other factor XI/XIa inhibitors.26–28  

The risk factors of HT found in the present study could be grouped into 3 categories: patient characteristics, manifestations of acute infarcts, and preexisting brain lesions. Patient characteristics included male sex and diabetes. Male sex has been associated with HT in both patients with thrombolysis-treated and nonthrombolyzed ischemic stroke.10,29,30 It is hypothesized that estrogen may protect endothelial cells, thereby preserving the integrity of the blood-brain barrier.30 Hyperglycemia or diabetes has been consistently reported as risk factor for symptomatic hemorrhage after thrombolysis or thrombectomy.31–33 Hyperglycemia during acute stroke is associated with elevated activity of matrixmetalloprotease-9, which may increase the permeability of the blood-brain barrier.34 Among the patient characteristics, concurrent antiplatelet use or intravenous thrombolysis did not increase the risk of HT. This observation could potentially be explained by the inclusion of patients with overall mild, noncardioembolic stroke in the current study.  

Regarding the manifestations of acute infarcts, patients with higher NIHSS scores, large infarct size $(>15\mathrm{mm})$ , more diffusion-weighted imaging lesions, and cortical involvement by infarcts were associated with risk of HT. Large or multiple diffusion-weighted imaging lesions in noncardioembolic strokes are usually caused by proximal arterial stenosis with artery-to-artery embolism, or etiologies other than small vessel occlusion. This infarct pattern often involves the cerebral cortex. Furthermore, higher NIHSS scores usually result from larger infarcts. These findings suggest that having larger or more ischemic lesions increases the risk of HT due to the presence of more damaged brain tissue susceptible to reperfusion injury.1  

Preexisting vascular brain lesions, including chronic brain infarcts, CMB, and cSS, are usually caused by cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) and covert (ie, clinically unrecognized). In SVD, endothelial dysfunction and blood-brain barrier leakage are key pathomechanisms, and they also contribute to the hemorrhagic manifestation of SVD.35 A meta-analysis showed that the presence of neuroimaging markers of SVD was associated with an increased risk of HT after thrombolysis.36 Our observations are generally consistent with previous findings, although we did not observe significant associations between white matter hyperintensity and HT. The reason for this discrepancy might be that our patients had milder white matter hyperintensity, with a median Fazekas score of 1. Among the patients with CMBs, half of them had CMBs only in lobar locations. Additionally, cSS was associated with early HT. The presence of lobar-only CMB, along with cSS, is highly suggestive of cerebral amyloid angiopathy.37 It has been reported that underlying cerebral amyloid angiopathy can predispose to thrombolysisrelated HT, as the retention of cerebral amyloid $\upbeta$ -protein appeared higher in patients with HT.38 Therefore, thoroughly evaluating neuroimaging markers of cerebral amyloid angiopathy may help explain the presence of HT, even in patients with noncardioembolic stroke who have not received revascularization treatments.  

In the present study, late HT occurred in one-fourth of the patients and was predominantly composed of new HT rather than increased area or severity of existing HT. In 1 study, which included patients with AIS and repeated computed tomography scans at specific intervals, about $40\%$ of HT occurred in the first week, while $50\%$ occurred during the second week.20 This observation was compatible with the distribution of early and late HT in our study. Therefore, it was not surprising that risk factors of late HT largely concurred with that of early HT. However, markers of SVD were not predictive of late HT in our study. This suggests that HT occurring in the late phase may be attributed to the tissue necrosis of the index stroke itself, rather than being promoted by an underlying fragile cerebral microvascular environment.  

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a post hoc analysis and the findings should be regarded as exploratory for hypothesis generation. Second, there were only a few cases with PH, thereby limiting further analyses on the risk factors of PH. However, this finding was also reassuring regarding the safety of asundexian use in noncardioembolic stroke, even when combined with guideline-based antiplatelet therapy. Third, both SWI/susceptibility-weighted angiography and $\mathsf{T}2^{\star}\cdot$ gradient-recalled echo sequences were allowed in the study, and the detection rate of HT may differ because of the sensitivity of different imaging protocols. However, this was a pragmatic trial that reflected the realworld application of imaging modalities. Furthermore, we have adjusted for MRI sequence type in the multivariable analysis. Finally, we did not analyze the effects of HT on clinical outcomes, such as functional status or recurrent stroke. However, most of the HT was HI, which rarely causes clinical deterioration according to previous studies. There were no participants who discontinued the medications because of HI. The impacts of HT on clinical outcomes will be explored in further analyses of PACIFIC-STROKE data.  

In conclusion, this post hoc analysis of the PACIFICSTROKE trial reveals that HT is commonly detected on MRI in noncardioembolic stroke, with ${\approx}28\%$ of patients experiencing early HT and an additional $24\%$ showing late HT by the 26-week follow-up. Risk factors for HT include larger infarct size, higher stroke severity, cortical involvement of infarcts, male sex, diabetes, and preexisting brain lesions associated with cerebral SVD. The range of independent risk factors for MRI-detected HT implies a more complex pathophysiology than solely reperfusion of damaged tissue. These findings highlight the need for a comprehensive evaluation of neuroimaging markers to assess HT risk in stroke patients. Further research should explore the impact of HT on clinical outcomes and targeted preventive measures.
