# Left atrial appendage closure in patients with intracranial hemorrhage: the clock is ticking — to take the bull by the horn or wait for a randomized controlled trial  

Editorial commentary regarding “Left atrial appendage closure in patients with intracranial hemorrhage” by Garg and colleagues  

Alberto Alfie1 $\circledcirc$ · Patrick Müller2  

Received: 7 March 2022 / Accepted: 8 March 2022 / Published online: 11 March 2022   
$\circledcirc$ The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2022  

One of the primary goals of atrial fibrillation (AF) management is the prevention of thromboembolic events and balance the risk of bleeding events due to oral anticoagulation (OAC) therapy. Intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a challenging clinical scenario in AF management, because there is lack of evidence of randomized controlled trials (RCT) for determining the best clinical strategy to prevent further thromboembolic and bleeding occurrence. This high-risk population was not included in the landmark RCT (PROTECT AF, PREVAIL) due to the need of long-term OAC therapy. These RCTs were designed to compare the safety and efficacy of left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) versus long-term oral anticoagulation (OAC) with warfarin for stroke prevention in non-valvular AF [1, 2].  

The recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Garg and colleagues highlighted the safety and efficacy of LAAO in AF patients with prior ICH based on seven observational studies which is currently recommended with a weak indication in the AF guidelines [3].  

The 2020 ESC AF guidelines recommend resuming OAC with novel anticoagulation agents (NOAC) after ICH with a class IIa indication, whereas LAAO is recommended with a class IIb indication [4]. The use of NOAC after ICH is justified by a better outcome compared to vitamin K antagonists (VKA) in RCTs. The clinical decision to use NOAC versus LAAO strategy is based on patient characteristics that must consider the presence of ICH reversible causes (i.e., uncontrolled hypertension, smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, concomitant antiplatelet therapy, sympathomimetic drugs, low LDL/triglycerides) and ICH irreversible causes (i.e., older age, male sex, Asian ethnicity, chronic kidney disease, cerebral disease) [4]. For the above-mentioned reasons, a tailored approach is warranted.  

In contrast to PROTECT AF and PREVAIL which used VKA in the control arm, the recent PRAGUE-17 trial compared LAAO versus NOAC and demonstrated non-inferiority of LAAO regarding the primary composite outcome [5]. This RCT differed from the prior ones in that different commercial LAAO devices were used. Here, antithrombotic therapy after LAAO was dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) but based on patient risk profile, OAC therapy was indicated nearly in half of the patients undergoing Watchman implantation.  

In patients with prior ICH, short-term OAC therapy after LAAO may increase the risk of recurrent ICH, but on the other hand, forgoing OAC therapy may increase the risk of device-related thrombus (DRT) formation. In the present meta-analysis by Garg and colleagues, the minority of patients were implanted with a Watchman device $(32\%)$ , which per manufacturer requires short-term OAC therapy. However, OAC therapy rate after Watchman device implantation was not high $(78\%)$ , and surprisingly the incidence of DRT was low. This is also supported by other studies. There are two studies that examined with this scenario, the ASAP study, a non-randomized study in eligible patients for LAAO and contraindication for OAC and the EWOLUTION, a prospective real-world registry of Watchman device implantation. In the ASAP study, DAPT was prescribed with a low incidence of ischemic stroke ( $1.7\%$ /year) and an acceptable DRT rate $(4\%)$ , which is in line with the results of PROTECT AF $(4.2\%)$ [6]. In EWOLUTION, only $37\%$ of patients were using OAC post-Watchman device implantation. There was no correlation between the different drug strategies and incidence of DRT during follow-up, with a total DRT incidence of $4.1\%$ [7]. In the present meta-analysis, $68\%$ of patients were implanted with an Amplatzer cardiac plug or Amulet device, which only require DAPT. Despite this observation, it is hard to assume that the low incidence rate of DRT was related to the type of implanted devices used, because as mentioned before, there was no difference in DRT formation related to Watchman postimplantation drug strategies.  

When comparing the benefits of OAC therapy versus LAAO, it is important to consider the follow-up period. Whereas LAAO is associated with an initial increased risk of stroke and bleeding events post-implantation, PREVAIL and PROTECT-AF 5-year follow-up results demonstrated significant lower incidence of major bleeding, hemorrhagic stroke, and disabling/fatal stroke with a $23\%$ reduction in all-cause of death in the LAAO arm. Therefore, long-term follow-up is warranted to address outcomes when comparing OAC versus LAAO. Of note, the PRAGUE-17 trial showed a $45\%$ reduction of clinically relevant bleeding events in the LAAO arm compared to NOAC after a 4-year follow-up.  

It is relevant to compare the incidence of ICH in RCT comparing VKA versus NOAC and its relationship with age. In this meta-analysis, the mean age was 74 years old. In this regard, the efficacy and safety analysis of ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial and the ARISTOTLE trial in elderly population highlight valuable information [8, 9]. In post hoc analysis of ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial (patients $>75$ years), ICH incidence was $0.5\%$ per year in the edoxaban arm versus $1.2\%$ in the warfarin arm. The ARISTOTLE trial, which compares apixaban versus warfarin in patients of 75 years, showed ICH incidence of $0.43\%$ per year in apixaban arm versus $1.29\%$ per year in the warfarin arm. It is noteworthy to mention that the present meta-analysis demonstrated very low incidence of recurrent ICH after LAAO with an annual rate of $0.05\%$ [3]. Comparisons are difficult from the statistical point of view, because these studies were not powered to make this statistical analysis, but to put it in numbers: ICH incidence was 1 out of 200 patients per year using edoxaban or apixaban, whereas when performing LAAO ICH incidence was 1 out of 2000 patients per year. These findings would mean a tenfold absolute risk reduction of ICH.  

Recently, the PINNACLE FLX trial investigated the newgeneration Watchman FLX closure device [10]. This device was improved in its design to ensure a better anchorage, reduce peri-device leak, and reduce metal exposure in proximal aspect to reduce incidence of DRT. This trial showed $0.5\%$ incidence of primary safety outcomes at 7 days as compared to $2.2\%$ in PREVAIL. The PINNACLE FLX trial observed no peri-device leak $>5~\mathrm{mm}$ and the annual incidence of DRT was $1.75\%$ . The device-related improvements combined with a longer follow-up period may have a better outcome compared to OAC and should be investigated in future studies.  

The STROKECLOSE trial comparing LAAO versus OAC in patients with AF and prior ICH is still under recruitment [11]. The present meta-analysis by Garg and colleagues brings some new light in clinical decision making, and highlights LAAO as a compelling scientific-based strategy in patients with AF and prior ICH.  

The clock is ticking and we have to make a decision: do we take the bull by the horns or do we continue to wait for a randomized controlled trial?
