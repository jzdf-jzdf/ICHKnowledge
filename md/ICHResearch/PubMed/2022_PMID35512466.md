# Effect of High-Frequency Repetitive Peripheral Magnetic Stimulation on Motor Performance in Intracerebral Haemorrhage: A Clinical Trial  

Jiaqia ${\mathrm{Ke}},^{\mathrm{a},\#}$ Jiana Wei,ad,# Bofang Zheng,? Tian Tan,a Wenbin Zhou,a Xiaopei Zou, Huihui Zou,? Haoxuan Zeng,a Guoping Zhou, Lukui Chen, and Xianju Zhou,a  

Objectives: The aim of the randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial was to explore the efficacy and safety of HF-rPMS synchronosly applied to the axilla (stimulating the brachial plexus) and the popliteal fossa (stimulating the tibial nerve and common peroneal nerve) in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage on rehabilitation of motor functions. Materials and Methods: Patients with intracerebral haemorrhage in the early period were recruited and randomly assigned to the HF-rPMS group or the sham rPMS group. The two synchrous coils of magnetic stimulation in the two groups were respectively applied to the axilla and the popliteal fossa of the affected limb. But the sham group received the ineffective rPMS and only heard the sound as occured in the HF-rPMS group. Clinical outcomes included the change of Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) scale and Medical Research Council (MRC) scale before and after HF-rPMS. Results: Of 76 eligible patients, 30 were included and only 26 patients completed this study. The diferences on the improvement of the upper extremity FMA $(\mathrm{P}{=}0.012)$ , the lower extremity FMA $(\mathrm{P=0.001}),$ ), the proximal MRC of upper extremity $(\mathtt{p}=0.043)$ , the proximal MRC of lower extremity $(\mathtt{p}{=}$ 0.004) and the distal MRC scores of lower extremity $(\mathtt{p}{=}0.008)$ between the the HFrPMS group and sham rPMS group were statistically signifcant. Conclusions: Synchrous HF-rPMS intervention at the axilla and the popliteal fossa significantly improved motor function and proximal muscle strength of upper and lower limb of patients in acute or early subacute phase of intracerebral hemorrhage.

# Introduction  

Suffering from stroke, patients often exhibit motor deficits contralateral to the side of the lesion, such as impaired motor function and poststroke spasticity1. Motor dysfunction seriously affects their daily life and work ability. It is essential for stroke patients to promote the recovery of motor deficits. However, drugs and physiotherapy, which used in stroke patients, have a limited impact on functional recovery.  

Repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation (rPMS), the non-invasive and less painful magnetic stimulation of nerves/muscles, is an emerging approach developed for neuromodulation in movement disorders.23 Similar to neuromuscular electrical stimulation that has been widely applied to treat motor deficits caused by brain or nerve disorders, rPMS can be used to promote motor function recovery. However, compared with electrical stimulation by recruiting cutaneous receptors, rPMS can stimulate deep neural and muscular tissues painlessly, produce proprioceptive signals during muscle contraction, thereby affecting plasticity of the nervious system and improving the sensorimotor system. Also, rPMS is more efficient to drive M1 plasticity and sensorimotor improvements by massive inflows of proprioceptive information. Current studies have shown that rPMS can affect the excitability of the sensory motor cortex of healthy individuals and the motor cortex of stroke patients.6 It was reported that rPMS can change brain plasticity of patients, increase the muscle strength of the ankle joint and relieve muscle spas ticity in acute phase? and chronic phase of stroke,8-10 without obvious side effects.? However, its beneficial effects on rehabilitation of motor function in stroke patients was uncertain. At present, most of the hot spots that rPMS were applied over were local muscles (such as extensor wrist muscles and flexor muscles), that is, rPMS stimulated muscles and excited the innervated local nerves, caused muscle contraction and thus changed the excitability of the sensory motor cortex, thereby alleviating spasm and muscle weakness. But the evidence level of those studies was not $\ h\mathrm{{gh},^{9}}$ and the best stimulation parameters and location were still unclear. In order to increase the efficiency of stimulation and obtain a better effect, we turned the attention to the sites in whose depth there were superficial and concentrated peripheral nerve trunks or plexus, such as the axilla (in its depth, there was the brachial plexus, including many nerve trunks) and the popliteal fossa (in its depth the sciatic nerve was divided into the tibial and common peroneal nerves). We speculated that HF-rPMS would significantly improve stimulation efficiency and increase excitability of the motor cortex by synchrously stimulating the two sites with figure-of-eight coil. Our unpublished work demonstrated that the motor cortical excitability in healthy subjects reflected by the percentage change of MEP amplitude is improved after 10 min HF-rPMS, without any significant adverse effects, suggesting that HF-rPMS may be a potential effective and safe method to promote the movement of limbs. In addition, our unpublished previous exploratory trial, in which 3 patients with intracerebral hemorrhage and 3 patients with cerebral infarction voluntarily receiving rPMS treatment were compared in the change of muscle strength and we found that rPMS improved upper limb muscle strength more significantly in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage than in patients with cerebral infarction, implicated that compared with the acute or early subacute intracerebral infarction, the effect of HFrPMS on limb motor functions in patients at acute or early subacute intracerebral haemorrhage might be more significant. Therefore, we assumed that HF-rPMS may significantly promote the motor function of the upper and lower limb in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage at the acute phase by synchronous stimulation at the axilla and the popliteal fossa.  

# Material and Methods  

# Participants  

We recruited patients who were 18-70 years old, within 8 weeks of first-ever intracerebral haemorrhage onset. Additionally, they were conscious and had stable vital signs. Patients were ineligible if the intracerebral hemorrhage was attributable to head injury; if they had other central nervous system diseases (such as ischaemic stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, subdural hemorrhage); if they were combined unstable arrhythmia, fever, infection and epilepsy; if they were severe aphasia or cognitive impairment leading to poor cooperation. Also, patients with contraindications for magnetic stimulation or severe limb orthopedic disease were ineligible. This study was permitted by our hospital ethics committee. Written signed informed consent was obtained from all participants.  

# Trial Design and Treatments  

This a single-center, randomized, double-blind, shamcontrolled study. During this trial, we explored the safety and efficacy of daily HF-rPMS for 10 consecutive days on limb motor recovery. The thirty eligible patients were randomly divided into the HF-rPMS group and the sham rPMS group. Each patient performed conventional treatment and received HF-rPMS or sham rPMS once a day for 10 consecutive days. Clinical efficacy was assessed by the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA) and Medical Research Council (MRC) scale.  

# rPMS protocol  

rPMS was applied by two CCY-IV magnetic stimulator (YIRUIDE Inc, Wuhan, China) with a $70{\cdot}\mathrm{mm}$ figure-ofeight coil. For each stimulation session, rPMS was applied at a frequency of $20~\mathrm{Hz}$ and delivered for $1~\mathsf{sec}$ ,with an interval of 19 sec between stimuli. In total, we delivered 1800 pulses (a period of $30~\mathrm{min}$ stimulation per day) at each stimulation site. The intensity was individually set to $40{-}60\%$ output intensity of the stimulator) that evoked signifcant movement of the affected limbs but not produced any uncomfortable responses. Also, the center part of two figure-of-eight coils was respectively placed at the center of the axilla and the popliteal fossa (Figure 1). rPMS was simultaneously delivered by the two coils. For patients in the sham group, the coil was placed at the site away from the axilla or the popliteal fossa. Thus, the stimulation was ineffective, and failed to elicit any significant limb movement. But the sound produced by the stimulator was present to the patients.

# Conventionaltreatments  

All participants received daily routine treatment, such as patients in the acute phase receiving mannitol dehydration to reduce intracranial pressure, blood pressure control, and neuroprotection, while patients in the early stages of recovery received blood pressure control, neuroprotection, and 30min routine physical therapy and 30min acupuncture by professional therapists.  

# Clinical outcome evaluation  

Primary outcome measures was motor function of the hemiparesis limbs. The upper extremity Fugl-Meyer Assessment (UE-FMA) and the lower extremity FuglMeyer Assessment (LE-FMA) were mainly used for evaluationl1. The UE-FMA had a total of 66 points and the LE-FMA had a total of 34 points, with higher score indicating better motor function. The secondary outcome measures was muscle strength of the shoulder abduction, elbow flexion/extension, wrist flexion/extension, hip flexion, knee flexion/extension, and ankle dorsal flexion. The Medical Research Council (MRC) scale was a reliable measurement1 to examine muscle strength. It had 6 points, ranging from 0 to 5, higher score meaning greater muscle strength. The clinical outcome assessments were carried out by a neurological physician who had a good experience in these scales and was blinded to study assignment at the baseline and at the end of stimulation.  

# Statistical analysis  

SPSS 22.0 software was performed for statistical analysis. Data were expressed as median [IQR]. Student's t-test and the Mann-Whitney test was used for comparing normally distributed parameters and parameters that exhibited skewed distribution pattern, respectively. Categorical data was compared by the Fisher's precision probability test. Most of the data were not normally distributed. The time points (Baseline and after HF-rPMS intervention) were used as the within-group factors; the HF-rPMS group and the sham rPMS group were used as the between-group factor. The P-value less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.  

# Results  

# Baseline characteristics of participants  

Seventy-six  intracerebral  hemorrhage  patients  with hemiparesis were consecutively recruited between April 2019 and March 2021 from Department of Neurology at Integrated Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine of Southern Medical University. Eventually thirty patients Were assessed for eligibility. No significant adverse effects were observed during the treatment period in the HFrPMS group or in the sham rPMS group. There were two patients discontinued in each group during the treatment. As a result, 26 patients completed treatment (Figure 2).  

After analyzing the demographic and clinical characteristics of 26 patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (most of them were due to hypertension except one in the rPMS group due to intracranial aneurysm), we found that no significant differences were observed in age, sex, affected hemisphere, lesion side, time since intracerebral haemorrhage, Fugl-Meyer assessment of the upper extremity, Fugl-Meyer assessment of the lower extremity, the Medical Research Council scale and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) $(\mathrm{P}{>}0.05)$ between the two groups (Table 1).

# Discussion  

This study was to explore whether simultaneous HFrPMS at the axilla and the popliteal fossa had a specific impact on motor deficits in the acute or early subacute intracerebral haemorrhage. Results showed that motor function and muscle strength of both the upper limb and lower limb were improved by simultaneous HF-rPMS, as suggested by clinical outcomes.  

Previous studies reported the similar result that motor function could be improved by rPMS.713 However, there is evidence that rPMS relieved muscle spasm, but had no obvious effect on muscle strength recovery,14 different from our result. This difference might be related to the fact that the stimulation parameters (including frequency, pulse number, session and duration), stimulation site were different. Notably, in previous studies only one muscle was stimulated, but in our study, mutiple muscles were simultaneously excited by stimulating their innervated nerves. More importantly, to enhance the synergy, we simultaneously stimulated the nerves in both upper and lower limbs, which might extensively induce an increase of transsynaptic excitability in sensory areas, contributing to a better recovery of sensorimotor functions. What's more, differences from the previous reports that investigated no motor recovery after rPMS might be due to the type of stroke.15 Further studies need to address the relationship between the type of stroke and motor recovery by using HF-rPMS. Additionally, the intervention time point was also an important factor. Consistent with time point in the previous studies that confirmed the effects of rPMS on motor recovery,7,13 the patients, in our study, were in the acute or early subacute phase after cerebral hemorrhage. That is, at that time the HF-rPMS might be more effective. Firstly, previous studies showed that the greatest gain of recovery tended to occur within the first 3 to 6 months after stroke, especially the first 4 weeks post-stroke.16 During the period, patients who received some form of training like physical or occupational therapy showed faster improvement with better final outcome compared  to those control therapy.17 Secondly, although activation of the ipsilesional hemisphere and inactivation of contralesiona hemisphere were reported to promote motor recovery, lost functions of the ipsilesional hemisphere might compensate by the contralesional hemisphere during the first weeks after stroke.1819 As some studies provided, HF-rPMS could induce an increase of excitability in the contralesional hemisphere,8,20 probably  compensating for the  lost function of the ipsilesional hemisphere and contributing to better functional recovery in the acute or early subacute phase stroke.

In clinical practice, the change scores of UE-FMA (near 9 points) and LE-FMA (near 8 points) in the HF-rPMS group was close to the threshold of minimally clinical important diference.21,22 2 Although no signifcant advantages of the synchronous rPMS were observed in the Wrist-FMA score and the distal MRC scores of upper extremity, only reflecting the change of the motor function and the muscle strength of wrist in our study, the changeable scores are less associated with executing function than hand. And the motor function of hand was improved by simultaneous HF-rPMS. Besides, such improvement was achieved in a relatively short period of stimulation (10 days), suggesting that synchrous HF-rPMS might play an important role in fast and effective rehabilitation for motor function. In a word, it indicated that the HF-rPMS group gained better improvements in motor function and muscle strength, leading to stronger confidence of patients and improving their initiative of limb function exercise.  

This study has the following limitations. First, this study was still a small, single-center clinical trial, remained to be confirmed by large-scale multicenter studies. Second, there was a lack of neuroelectrophysiological data supporting the improvement of neural and muscular tisses. Third, it was unclear if synchrous HF-rPMS was superior to HF-rPMS alone on upper limbs or lower limbs. Finally, whether synchrous HF-rPMS was effective in the chronic intracerebral hemorrhage stage need to be confirmed in a future randomized control study.  

# Conclusion  

Our study provided a promising approach that synchrous HF-rPMS intervention at the axilla and the popliteal fossa significantly improved motor function and proximal muscle strength of upper and lower limb of patients at acute or early subacute intracerebral hemorrhage. These results further required a larger sample to be confirmed and exploring the potential mechanisms of motor function improvements.
