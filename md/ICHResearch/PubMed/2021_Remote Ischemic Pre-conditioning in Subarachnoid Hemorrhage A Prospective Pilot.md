# Remote Ischemic Pre‑conditioning in Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: A Prospective Pilot Trial

and Ihab Dorotta1

# Abstract  

Background:  Cerebral injury from aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is twofold. The initial hemorrhage causes much of the injury; secondary injury can occur from delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI). Remote ischemic preconditioning (RIPC) is a mechanism of organ protection in response to transient ischemia within a distant organ. This pilot trial sought to apply RIPC in patients with aSAH to evaluate its effect on secondary cerebral injury and resultant outcomes.  

Methods:  Patients were randomized to the high-pressure occlusion group (HPO) or the low-pressure occlusion group (LPO). Lower extremity RIPC treatment was initiated within 72 h of symptom onset and every other day for 14 days or until Intensive Care Unit (ICU) discharge. In HPO, each treatment consisted of 4 five-minute cycles of manual blood pressure cuff inflation with loss of distal pulses. LPO received cuff inflation with lower pressures while preserving distal pulses. Retrospectively matched controls were also analyzed. Efficacy of treatment was measured by total days spent in vasospasm out of study enrollment days, hospital and ICU length of stay (LOS), cerebral infarction, one and six month modified Rankin score, and mortality.  

Results:  The final analysis included 33 patients with 11 in each group. Patient demographics, aneurysm location, admission airway status, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), modified Rankin score, Hunt and Hess score, modified Fisher Score and aneurysm management were not significantly different between groups. Hospital and ICU LOS was shorter in LPO compared to the control $\textstyle(p=0\cdot0468$ and $p=0.0409_{,}$ , respectively). Total vasospasm days/study enrollment days, cerebral infarction, one and six month modified Rankin score, and mortality were not significantly different between the groups.  

Conclusions:  This pilot trial did demonstrate feasibility and safety. The shortened LOS in the LPO may implicate a protective role of RIPC and warrants future study.  

Keywords:  Cerebral vasospasm, Delayed cerebral ischemia, Transcranial doppler  

# Background  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) accounts for only $5\%$ of all strokes but has a disproportionally high morbidity and mortality [1]. Average mortality has been reported to be $51\%$ with $46\%$ of survivors suffering long-term cognitive impairment and one-third requiring long-term care [2]. While the initial hemorrhage from the aneurysm causes much of the injury, secondary injury can occur from vasospasm which may lead to delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI). This pathological vasoconstriction of small and large cerebral vessels typically occurs between day 4 and 10 following aneurysm rupture [3].  

Murry et  al. initially described ischemic conditioning in 1986, demonstrating that brief episodes of induced coronary ischemia protected the myocardial vascular territory from a subsequent prolonged ischemic event [4]. This method of organ protection eventually expanded to a noninvasive method of using a blood pressure cuff to briefly stop blood flow to an extremity to confer ischemic protection to distant organs such as the heart, kidney and brain [5]. Remote Ischemic Preconditioning (RIPC) has been tested for multiple organs and illnesses with promising preliminary data.  

The exact mechanism of organ protection is not completely established but preclinical data and clinical trials have demonstrated the ability of RIPC to improve local and systemic endothelial function and microcirculation [6, 7]. Meng et al. found RIPC prevented recurrent stroke in patients with intracranial stenosis [8]. aSAH patients treated with RIPC were observed to have reduced middle cerebral artery mean velocity, decreased lactate/pyruvate ratios and glycerol levels which indicated cerebrovascular vasodilation with protective effects lasting 2 days following RIPC [9]. Given the predictable time course for developing vasospasm after aSAH and recent evidence about RIPC’s potential vasodilatory effects on cerebral vasculature this trial evaluates if RIPC can decrease the incidence of vasospasm and hence morbidity when applied soon after initial aneurysmal rupture.

The cuff was inflated to a pressure $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ higher than the systolic blood pressure, measured by the patient’s arterial line or upper limb blood pressure cuff, for HPO and it was inflated to $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ below the systolic blood pressure for LPO. The adequacy of inflation was confirmed by the absence of pulse in the ipsilateral pedal artery as detected by Doppler ultrasonography for HPO and given lower pressure for LPO the perfusion was confirmed by palpation or Doppler detected pulse.  

Patients were continuously monitored for pain, hemodynamic instability and neurologic changes. RIPC session was held for significant discomfort, blood pressure or heart rate change of greater than $20\%$ , oxygen saturation below $92\%$ , acute drop in GCS by greater than 2 points or increase in intracranial pressure (ICP) above $20\mathrm{mmHg}$ .  

A third group of retrospectively matched cohort was also analyzed. A total of 11 patients were included in this group. Decision to add this group as the control was based on observations by the research team that mottling was present despite palpable pulses in the LPO patients. LPO was initially intended to be the sham treatment group. Due to the concern of possibly delivering ischemic conditioning to both groups, a decision to add a retrospective cohort was made. This group was admitted during the same time period as the other groups that met inclusion criteria but were not enrolled in the study due to greater than $^{72\textrm{h}}$ interval between onset and time for potential first RIPC treatment.  

# Methods  

# Study Design  

A prospective, single center pilot trial was designed to evaluate the effects of lower-limb RIPC on patients with aSAH. Approval was obtained from our University’s Institutional Review Board (#5150015) and listed on Clinicaltrials.gov $\#\mathrm{NCT}02381522$ prior to enrollment. Patients were enrolled in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) from March 2015 to December 2017. Inclusion criteria included patients at least 18  years old with admission diagnosis of aSAH. Exclusion criteria included known peripheral vascular disease, lower extremity neuropathy, lower extremity vascular surgery, and delay in first treatment of greater than $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ after onset of symptoms.  

A total of 22 patients were randomized into the highpressure occlusion group (HPO) or the low-pressure occlusion group (LPO), in a 1:1 allocation. Each group received up to six sessions of lower limb RIPC. The first RIPC session commenced within $^{72\mathrm{~h~}}$ of symptom onset and continued every other day of enrollment up to day 14 of admission or ICU discharge. Each session of RIPC consisted of four 5-min cycles of lower limb ischemia  

# Outcome Measurements  

Outcomes measured were total days spent in vasospasm out of study enrollment days. Vasospasm was determined by daily transcranial Doppler ultrasonography (TCD) performed from Day 2 of admission till Day 14 or discharge from the ICU. Study enrollment days were considered from admission to ICU discharge or maximum of 14  days. Vasospasm episode was considered positive if the patient had moderate or severe vasospasm in the middle cerebral artery. Doppler velocities $150{-}200~\mathrm{cm/s}$ was moderate vasospasm and $>200~\mathrm{cm/s}$ was severe vasospasm. Other outcomes included modified Rankin Score (mRS) at 1 and 6 months, cerebral infarction determined by CT and/or MRI, ICU length of stay (LOS), hospital LOS, and overall mortality. All $\mathrm{mRS}$ were obtained by one physician that was not part of the research team. This provider was asked to follow the flow diagram questionnaire used by Bruno et al. [10].  

# Sample Size  

As this is a pilot study and there is paucity of data, sample size was not based on hypothesis testing. A sample size of 11 patients/group was used based on suggestion in literature for pilot studies noting at least 10 patients/ group be used [11, 12].  

# Randomization and Masking  

Prior to study enrollment a simple randomization list was created by the statistician using the Wei’s Urn design to assign patients to HPO or LPO treatment groups. Upon consents being obtained, a treatment was assigned based on the sequential number on the list by research coordinator.  

Blinding was strictly maintained with patient, healthcare provider, data collectors and outcome adjudicators not aware of study intervention.  

# Statistics  

Continuous data were analyzed for normal distribution and those showing significant deviation from normality were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges. These were analyzed using the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis Test to determine differences between groups with results presented as median; 25th to 75th percentile range. Categorical outcomes were summarized using counts and percentages. The Fisher’s exact test was used to detect differences between categorical outcomes. The Dunn’s multiple comparison test was used for posthoc analysis of continuous variables, and the pairwise comparison test was used for post-hoc categorical variables. All statistical analysis was conducted in R version 3.5.1.

# Outcomes  

Cerebral vasospasm was diagnosed by TCD. Days of vasospasm were not significantly different between the three groups.  

Cerebral infarction was defined as new cerebral infarcts noted on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain that were not present on admission or immediately after aneurysm coiling or clipping. Infarcts were not statistically different between groups. Baseline mRS was obtained by history and was not statistically significant across the three groups. Differences in mRS at one and six months were also not statistically significant between the three groups.  

Hospital LOS was shorter for LPO group (16; 10 to 19  days) compared to the Control group (23; 20 to 27  days; $p=0{\cdot}0400\$ ). ICU LOS was also shorter in LPO group (14; 6 to 15  days) compared to the Control (17; 14 to 21  days; $p{=}0{\cdot}0354;$ . In both the HPO and LPO groups, one patient died within 6  months, while there were no deaths in the control group.  

Feasibility of performing treatments was determined by evaluating how often study protocol intervention was interrupted. The study protocol was held once for two different patients in the HPO group because of patient discomfort and never for any LPO patients (Table 2). The intervention was never interrupted due to hemodynamic or neurological changes. No signs of tissue damage or permanent vascular injury was noted in any patient.  

# Discussion  

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage has been an attractive model for therapeutic research due to its predictable timeline of secondary injury. Multiple therapeutic interventions have been trialed in attempts to reduce vasospasm and DCI. The few that have become standard of care include nimodipine, euvolemia and hypertension [13–15]. Despite these interventions, we are far from eliminating the considerable morbidity and mortality associated with the secondary injury of aSAH. The pathophysiology of vasospasm is unclear; however, DCI has been known to occur with or without radiological evidence of vasospasm. This is thought to be due to a multitude of mechanisms contributing to a neuroinflammatory response consisting of microthrombosis, cortical spreading depolarizations, and cerebral vascular dysregulation [16].

The mechanism by which RIPC confers organ protection is also not well understood but evidence to date shows its involvement in neural, humoral, and immunological pathways [17, 18]. The complex interplay between these pathways is thought to be vital in organ protection [19]. In aSAH, induction of RIPC causes cellular level gene expression and DNA methylation changes leading to coordinated cell cycle and inflammatory responses [20]. RIPC has also been shown to cause transient cerebral vasodilation with brain microdialysis demonstrated reduction in lactate-pyruvate ratio and glycerol for up to $54\mathrm{~h~}$ in aSAH patients [9]. RIPC also appears to prolong PT and INR values in humans while maintaining within the normal range, without hemorrhagic complications [21]. The potential for these pathways to counteract pathological mechanisms of aSAH has led some authors to believe this to be an attractive adjunctive treatment option.  

In preclinical trials, preconditioning before the induction of SAH showed reduced vasospasm, and prevented neurological deterioration [22–26]. However, given the weak clinical correlation between vasospasm and DCI it was not a surprise that this study did not show a difference in the incidence of vasospasm between treatment groups [27, 28]. The present study also did not show a statistically significant difference in cerebral infarction although there was increased number of patients with cerebral infarction in the Control group. This may explain the statistically lower ICU and hospital LOS in the LPO group compared to the Control group. It was noted that a greater number of patients in the HPO and LPO group improved from poor to good mRS by six months compared to the Control group. This finding is similar to a study published by Laiwalla et  al. describing the effects of RIPC on neurological protection after aSAH. Although results of that study did not reach statistical significance, it did demonstrate a trend toward lower incidences of death and stroke, with a higher incidence of patients without disability at discharge (mRS 0) [29].  

There have been multiple feasibility and safety trials for RIPC with few specifically targeting aSAH [30–34]. Our study had 1 treatment cycle held in two different HPO group patients due to patient discomfort. Both times the patients were able to resume treatment after a brief break. We also noted minimal to no change in vital signs, ICP or mental status during the treatments. None of the patients in this study were noted to have temporary or permanent neurovascular injury, muscular injury, deep venous thrombosis or worsened cerebral hemorrhage. Similar findings have been documented in other studies and with longer cuff inflation times [30]. One limitation of this study is that the Control group was studied retrospectively. To increase the validity of the data, the Control group patients were treated during the same time period as treatment groups and qualified for the study but were not enrolled due to not being able to implement a possible first RIPC treatment within $^{72\mathrm{h}}$ of onset. The nature of retrospective data collection lends itself to selection bias and hence limits our ability to make conclusions based on this data. Another limitation of the study is the small sample size. Although a sample size can be calculated based on vasospasm incidence, vasospasm may not correlate well with DCI and outcome. We believe that future studies focusing on DCI and neurological outcomes would be appropriate to evaluate RIPC’s clinical impact.
