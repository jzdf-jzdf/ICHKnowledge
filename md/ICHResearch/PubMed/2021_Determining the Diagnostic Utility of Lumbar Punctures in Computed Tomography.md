# Determining the Diagnostic Utility of Lumbar Punctures in Computed Tomography Negative Suspected Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: A Systematic Review and Metaanalysis  

Michelle Kameda-Smith1, Mohammed Aref1, Youngkyung Jung2, Huphy Ghayur3, Forough Farrokhyar4,5 - BACKGROUND: While headache is a common neurologic symptom, subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a rare and potentially catastrophic cause of sudden-onset severe headache. The utility of the imaging modalities and interventional procedures are central to the investigation of the causes of headache; however, they are not without their limitations, risks, and complications.  

- METHODS: A meta-analysis in accordance with the Preferred Reporting for Systematic Reviews and Metaanalysis guidelines was conducted searching PubMed, EMBASE, and Google Scholar. Patients investigated for suspected subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) with a negative computed tomography (CT) and positive lumbar puncture (LP) and final diagnosis of SAH were included. The sensitivity of LP in the context of a negative CT and vsubsequent imaging confirming the cause of SAH (computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, digital subtraction angiography [DSA]) was quantified. The pooled data were analyzed using the DerSimonian-Laid random effects model.  

- RESULTS: Four studies with 2782 patients who presented with headache suspicious for SAH were included with an initial negative CT report and a subsequent LP to rule out SAH. All included studies had an observational prospective cohort design. A combined pooled proportion of 0.383 (0.077, 0.756); 0.086 (0.007, 0.238); and 0.22 (0.04, 0.49) for LPD, DSAD, and DSA/computed tomography angiography $^+$ investigations were estimated with a $95\%$ confidence interval.  

- CONCLUSIONS: The current clinical workflow of an LP after a negative CT head for a patient presenting with a sudden-onset severe headache is observed to have a high enough proportion to warrant its continued use despite the sensitivity of modern CT scanners of $297\%$ .  

# INTRODUCTION  

W hile headache is a common neurologic symptom, subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a rare and potentially catastrophic cause of sudden-onset severe   
headache. Approximately $g/\mathbf{\pi}00,000$ cases occur per year world  
wide, with intracranial aneurysms accounting for $85\%$ of cases of   
aneurysmal SAH (aSAH). The combined morbidity and mortality   
can range up to $50\%$ with a third of survivors remaining   
dependent and $20\%-30\%$ admitted to the hospital with good   
initial clinical grade, having poor outcomes.2  

The consequence of missing the diagnosis of diffuse SAH whether secondary to a ruptured intracranial aneurysm or ruptured vessel can potentially be fatal secondary to the local and systemic complications that can ensue. It has been estimated that fatal outcomes in the acute phase after an aSAH range between $32\%$ and $67\%$ . The work-up and timeliness of key investigations is crucial in the diagnosis and identification of the etiology of SAH. The American Heart Association and American Stroke Association recommended with level B evidence that acute diagnostic workup should include a noncontrast head computed tomography (CT). If nondiagnostic, it should be followed by lumbar puncture (LP).4 While CT scans are provided with minimal risk to the adult population, the LP procedure is not always straightforward, especially in patients with larger lumbar habitus or degenerative spinal diseases. It is also not without complication including persistent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak and postural hypotension.12-16 Moreover, the complications associated with a false-positive LP or symptoms that provide high suspicion for SAH with vascular etiology lead to further investigations, such as CT cerebral angiograms and further conventional digital subtraction angiography (DSA), which carries a $\mathrm{r}\%-3\%$ , $0.9\%$ , and $0.5\%$ risk of hypersensitivity,17 contrast-induced nephropathy,18 and permanent stroke,19 respectively. Here, we aim to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to resolve the utility of an LP in the context of a negative noncontrast CT head in the diagnosis of aSAH.  

# MATERIAL AND METHODS  

A systematic review and meta-analysis were performed in accordance with the Meta-analysis Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement, and Cochrane Handbook.  

# Criteria for Considering Studies  

Eligible sources included only randomized controlled trials and prospective observational studies. Our population of interest is adult patients with confirmed diagnosis of aneurysmal SAH.  

One index test was evaluated in this study: lumbar puncture (LP). The role of LP in detecting xanthochromia as a surrogate marker is to indicate aSAH secondary to a vascular malformation requiring neurosurgical or neurointerventional management. Studies examining participants with SAH secondary to aneurysmal rupture were included. Reference standards will include any study confirming the diagnosis of a ruptured aneurysm following the CT image and/or lumbar puncture. These confirmatory tests will include angiogram testing, such as conventional angiogram, CT angiography, and magnetic resonance (MR) angiogram confirming a ruptured aneurysm. Other reference standards may include any validating tests indicating aneurysmal rupture, such as postmortem examination, MR findings, or intraoperative findings.  

# Literature Search Strategy  

A search without language or publication-type restrictions was conducted using the following databases: MEDLINE (PubMed and Ovid), EMBASE, CINAHL, Google Scholar, ISI Web of Science, PsychINFO, BioMed Central, conference abstracts, and The Cochrane Library. Databases were queried from January 1, 1970 through July 17, 2018. The search was updated May 27, 2020. References of studies with potential relevance and review articles were screened manually to identify any eligible resources that were not previously identified. Key words and MeSH terms related to aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (i.e., aneurysm, aneurysmal, non-traumatic, cranial, cerebral, subarachnoid hemorrhage, haemorrhage, bleed, or bleeding) and the diagnosis of subarachnoid bleed secondary to ruptured aneurysm (i.e., diagnostic test, diagnosis, computed tomography, CT, lumbar puncture, xanthochromia, angiogram, angiography, computed tomography angiography [CTA], magnetic resonance angiography, MR imaging) were searched in relevant combinations (Figure 1).  

# Data Collection and Analysis  

Two independent reviewers (MMKS, MA) evaluated the studies for eligibility. Adult patients with SAH secondary to an intracerebral vascular anomaly were included. The diagnostic tests of interest were CT scan and/or lumbar puncture followed by a confirmatory reference standard. We included conferences abstracts, randomized controlled trials, or observational studies (prospective or retrospective). Exclusion criteria included duplicate studies, studies of the pediatric population, studies with overlapping patient data (i.e., the most recent manuscript were included), sample size ${<}\mathbf{I}0$ , traumatic SAH, and studies with lack of data for adequate analysis. Studies not providing adequate information to construct a $2\times2$ table were excluded. Disagreements between the 2 reviewers (MMKS, MA) regarding the decision to include or exclude a study were resolved by consensus and, if necessary, consultation with a fourth reviewer (HG). Data from eligible articles using data abstraction forms included the title, first author, journal, year, study design, number of cohorts, total number of examined patients, gender, mean age, presenting symptoms, timing of CT scan, experience of CT interpreter, CT specifications, LP timing, LP modality of assessment and LP results, number of patients with negative CT findings followed by LP, reference test used to confirm vascular anomaly, reference standards timing, and specification. All data were independently abstracted by the 2 primary investigators (MMKS, MA) and verified for accuracy by the third reviewer (YJ). Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus. Thereafter, 2 reviewers (MMKS, HG) performed quality assessment independently. The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS)- $^{\cdot2}$ tool examines the $4$ domains of patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing to determine if the risk of bias and concerns regarding applicability to the review question is low, unclear, or high among eligible studies. The DerSimonian-Laird random effects model was applied to evaluate the sensitivity of LP in the context of a negative CT for SAH. Pooled proportions with the corresponding $95\%$ confidence interval are reported.

# RESULTS  

From an initial pool of $\tau8,764$ studies, $34^{8}$ full-text studies were analyzed for eligibility. Of these, we included randomized controlled trials and prospective observational studies. Studies with pediatric participants, conference abstracts, or retrospective studies were excluded. Specifically, retrospective studies were excluded given the large discrepancies in data availability and parameters analyzed. Of studies from the same author with an additive study population, only the most recent report was included. Four full-text prospective studies were eligible and included in the final meta-analysis. All studies were prospective database or cohort studies that were published between 2006 and 2015 in an adult patient population. The search was updated May 28, 2020 with no new prospective studies being identified (Figure 1, Table 1).  

Table 1 summarizes the studies included in the final metaanalysis. While investigating CT-negative, LP-positive SAH, the cohorts varied as all but the Bakker et $a\l\mathrm{l}^{20}$ cohorts included only patients presenting with symptoms suspicious for SAH and a negative CT and positive LP. Carstairs et al21 and Watson et al23 included all CT-negative patients, and Perry et al’s22 patients had a CT head for symptoms suspicious for SAH undergoing further imaging and biochemical workup. In all of these studies, the neuroradiologists provided the final results for the CT imaging. The method of identification of blood produces with the CSF acquired via LP differed providing differential results. In fact, Watson et al’s23 study compared various threshold values indicative of SAH (see Table 1).  

Study heterogeneity was almost $\mathrm{_{IOO}\%}$ across the 4 studies, as expected inherent to study design and seen in the variability of proportions of $\mathrm{LP+}$ results (Figure 2). While Bakker et al $\mathbf{S}^{20}$ work may have significantly contributed to the heterogeneity to the meta-analysis (CT-negative, LP-positive SAH), we removed this study to find overall heterogeneity did not improve as heterogeneity varied from $\mathbf{I.}9\%$ in Carstairs et $a\boldsymbol{\mathrm{l}}^{2\mathrm{I}}$ and $98.8\%$ in Bakker et al.20 Table 2 summarizes the pooled proportions of positive and negative results of the studies that estimated these values were calculated using the random effects model to account for between and within study variations. All included studies reported noncontrast CT head, LP, and DSA reports. Only 2 reported CTA results after negative CT. In the context of an initial negative CT head report, the pooled estimates from the $4$ included studies for $\mathrm{LP+}$ , $\mathrm{DSA+}$ , and ${\mathrm{DSA/CTA+}}$ were analyzed (Figure 3, see Table 2). A combined pooled proportion of $0.383$ (0.077, $_{0.756)}$ ); 0.086 (0.007, 0.238); and 0.22 (0.04, 0.49) for $\mathrm{LP+}$ , $\mathrm{DSA+}$ , and $\mathrm{DSA/CTA+}$ investigations were estimated (see Figure 3). Appreciating the divergent approaches taken to assess the utility of LP in the setting of a sudden-onset severe headache to rule out SAH, a range of $0.08\%{-4\cdot2\%}$ SAH false-negative rate using only CT was quantified.  

# DISCUSSION  

Timely recognition of SAH is particularly challenging among patients with normal neurologic examination results and mental status. The estimated rates of missed diagnoses have been reported to range between $5\%$ and $\tau2\%$ .24 One of the recent reported prospective studies of an emergency department population with acute headache has suggested several combinations of high-risk clinical variables (i.e., age ${\geq}40$ years, neck pain, loss of consciousness, and onset of headache with exertion) that, if absent, might allow clinicians to safely forgo further diagnostic testing for SAH after a negative CT. However, some of the current protocols are based on literature from evidence of older literature. Numerous factors need to be considered when determining whether a negative CT is helpful in the clinical decision making process. These factors may include timing of CT after onset of symptoms of SAH, the quality of the CT scanner, and the experience of the interpreter of the CT to be significant influential factors.

The sensitivity of CT in diagnosing SAH is extremely time sensitive. Sensitivity of older CT scanners have reported $93\%{-95\%}$ within the first 24 hours after onset of symptoms. The sensitivity of CT scanners 3 days after onset of symptoms declined to $85\%$ , and after 1 week the sensitivity was only $50\%$ .26 Perry et $a\vert^{27}$ found that the sensitivity of third-generation CT scans in emergency patients read by emergency physicians or radiology trainees to be $92.9\%$ with a specificity of $\mathrm{_{IOO}\%}$ and a negative predictive value of $99{\cdot}4\%$ and positive predictive value of $\mathrm{_{IOO}\%}$ . When the CT was completed within 6 hours of symptom onset, the sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value and positive predictive value was $\mathrm{_{IOO}\%}$ . Horstman et $a{\sqrt{\phantom{-}}}^{28}$ also found that no aneurysm was detected in 12 patients in whom CT was negative and performed within 6 hours after ictus. Furthermore, Backes et $a\mathbf{l}^{29}$ found that the accuracy of a new-generation head CT that is interpreted by an experienced neuroradiologist, sensitivity, specificity and negative predictive value, and positive predictive value of head CT $\leq6$ hours were all $\mathrm{_{IOO}\%}$ . The study identifying a $4{\cdot}2\%$ falsenegative rate of CT for SAH was the oldest study included in our analysis, and despite using a $64$ -slice CT scanner, as is the case with most nontertiary centers, the images were read by a general radiologist. Comparatively, Bakker et $a\l\mathrm{l}^{20}$ included patients initially assessed by both $32$ -slice and $64$ -slice scanners, however, quantifying the lowest rate of false-negative SAH with CT alone.20 Unique to this study, Bakker et al’s study was one of the only studies to include the timing of the CT in their descriptive statistics.20  

A recently published questionnaire reported that emergency physicians felt that $99\%$ to $\mathrm{_{IOO}\%}$ sensitivity was required for a clinical decision to rule out SAH.30 Recent advances in CT technology have improved the accuracy of today’s top-of-theline CT scanners. Yet some protocols rely on older studies using third-generation scanners. In the past decade, the fifthgeneration CT scanners with their multidetector advantages are much faster and able to take multiple images per rotation, leading to thinner slices and less motion artifact and thus better resolution. Studies that report lower sensitivities frequently use older-generation CT scanners with fewer slices instead of newer ${\ttI6-}$ to $64$ -slice scanners and may be reported by a nonspecialist. Lourenco et $a\mathrm{l}^{3\mathrm{I}}$ used only 16-slice CT scanners and reported a sensitivity of CT for SAH to be $97\%$ . Gee et $a\mathrm{l}^{32}$ later validated these results, demonstrating that the sensitivity of 16-slice or better CT scanner in their study was $97.8\%$ . Cortnum et $a\mathrm{l}^{33}$ later reported the sensitivity of modern $64$ -slice multidetector CT scanners in detecting SAH to be $99.7\%$ overall within the first 24 hours after ictus. In 2005, Boesiger and Shiber, using only fifthgeneration CT read by both general and neuroradiologists, found that none of their 170 patients who had a negative CT was found to have a positive LP.34 Hence the quality of the CT scanner, timing of the CT scan, and interpreter of the CT scanner may influence the results of the initial CT significantly.

As for the diagnostic utility of LP in the setting of a CT-negative, sudden-onset severe headache, we found a pooled proportion of $38.3\%$ with a subsequent CTA/DSA positive for a vascular cause of SAH, representing a significantly high enough proportion to warrant an LP. The diagnostic utility in the setting of a negative CT ranges from $\mathbf{I}^{0}/\mathbf{o}-28\%$ ; the variability is owed to the selection bias of referrals made to neurosurgical centers.35 While the low prevalence of SAH combined with the low threshold for a LP may produce a low diagnostic yield, it is a valuable diagnostic tool when reconciling strong clinical suspicions with a negative CT.36 The sensitivity of CT for SAH is initially within $98.7\%-\mathrm{I00\%}$ within 6 hours of onset and drops to $89\%$ afterwards.37,38 Therefore the diagnostic utility of LP is of particular interest for patients presenting outside this window, and ideally after 12 hours from symptom onset, to minimize false negatives on CSF analysis.35 CT may be less sensitive for patients with primarily atypical SAH symptoms (e.g., syncope, nuchal rigidity, seizure) in lieu of the classic “thunderclap headache.” Carpenter et $a\mathrm{l}^{3^{8}}$ further argue for the circumstantial utility of LPs through a symptom-based pretest probability for SAH of $70\%$ justifies a LP, with the caveat that this figure drops to $20\%$ for patients presenting outside the 6-hour window. Pretest probabilities beyond these ranges are indications to proceed directly to angiography, while lower-risk patients do not stand to benefit from invasive testing. Other authors also argue that negative CTs should be followed by an angiography over an LP, owed to aneurysms that go undetected with the latter.35,39

These authors analyzed a subset of $\mathrm{CT-}$ and $\mathrm{LP+}$ patients with subsequent angiography; since these patients were deliberately chosen on the basis of these parameters, the true role of LP with a $\mathrm{CT-}$ is difficult to determine. In Carstairs’ study, patients underwent CT and CTA; LP was done based on the CT results.21 Conversely, Perry et $a\vert^{22}$ and Watson et $a\mathrm{l}^{23}$ aimed to refine LP analysis. The primary outcome of Perry et al’s study was to determine a threshold red cell count to signal clinically significant SAH; as such, CT scans were not a requirement. Some patients underwent LPs without a preceding CT, and angiography was not a requirement.22 The primary outcome of Watson et al $'s^{23}$ study was to determine the utility of CSF ferritin as a marker for SAH; while all recruited patients had negative CTs and subsequent LPs, angiography was not required, much like Perry et al’s study. This poses several challenges for a meta-analysis as 77 of patients in Perry et al $\mathbf{S}^{23}$ did not obtain a CT. Furthermore, LP results did not indicate CT status (i.e., positive, negative, none). In Carstairs et $a\vert^{2\mathrm{{I}}}$ study, 3 patients underwent DSA after a negative CT and LP, secondary to a high degree of clinical suspicion for SAH. In Bakker et al’s study, patients enrolled before 2002 automatically received a DSA while patients enrolled after that year underwent CTA first.20  

Another factor affecting the heterogeneity of these studies may be their diagnostic criteria for SAH. All studies except Perry et al require either a positive CT or angiogram.20,21,23 Perry et al’s22 study employed a more generous definition, where they also included LP (xanthoma or blood in the last tube of CSF) along with postdischarge diagnosis via telephone interviews. The asymmetry between these papers and our goal of determining the diagnostic utility facilitates the observation of trends pertaining to the clinical utility of an LP following a negative CT, however, limits the strength of our conclusions. This is reflective of the need for more rigorous investigation of the clinical utility with uniform clinical criteria for the indication of an LP, threshold for positivity of the CSF sample, and indication for further potentially more invasive investigation with their associated risk of complications.  

# CONCLUSION  

Our meta-analysis suggests the diagnostic utility of LPs in confirming suspected cases of SAH with a negative CT. A large, well-designed study with rigorous methodology is needed to verify the diagnostic utility of LPs. Areas for future investigation should aim to define the rate of concordance between LP analysis methods for SAH (e.g., visual inspection of collected CSF, red cell counts, spectrometry) and aneurysm detection with angiography (e.g., DSA, magnetic resonance angiography, CTA). Future studies may tailor these investigations to patients presenting after 6 hours of symptom onset, since CT has a $\mathrm{_{IOO}\%}$ sensitivity for SAH for patients who present within this time window.
