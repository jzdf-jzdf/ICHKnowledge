# Ticagrelor for the primary prevention of stroke in patients with coronary artery disease: a systematic review and meta‑analysis of randomized controlled trials  

Qi Kong1,2 · Xin Ma1,2 · Xiaoxi Zhao1,2 · Fei Chen1,2 · Chengbei Hou3  

Published online: 27 March 2020   
$\circledcirc$ Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature 2020  

# Abstract  

Coronary artery disease (CAD) patients had a higher risk of first-ever stroke than general population even when they were on antiplatelet treatment. It was unknown whether more potent antiplatelet inhibitor ticagrelor which also provided adenosinemediated protection would improve the primary stroke prevention for CAD. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and CERNTRAL were searched for randomized clinical trials (RCTs) comparing efficacy and safety outcomes of over 30-day use of ticagrelor versus other antiplatelet drugs or placebo in patients with acute or chronic coronary syndrome. RCTs involving patients with any stroke history were excluded. Based on 5 RCTs with 45,843 patients, ticagrelor-involving regimens significantly reduced first-ever strokes (risk ratio [RR] 0.81; $95\%$ confidential interval [CI] $0.71{-}0.94$ ; $\scriptstyle\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ) in comparison to other antiplatelet regimens in CAD, where the benefits in reducing ischemic strokes (IS) (RR 0.80; $95\%$ CI 0.68–0.94; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ) was not canceled out by the increase of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) (RR 1.41; $95\%$ CI 1.05–1.89; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ). According to results of subgroup analyses, the protective effects of ticagrelor on first-ever stroke were more significant with treatment duration of more than 1 year, dosage of $60\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily, and in clinical settings of chronic coronary syndrome. In conclusion, available evidence from aggregate data supported a modest advantage of ticagrelor-involving regimens for the primary stroke prevention in CAD compared with other antiplatelet regimens after the trade-off between reducing IS and inducing ICH, where more benefits might be expected from long-term and low-dose use of ticagrelor among patients with chronic coronary syndrome. Further collaborative meta-analysis of individual participant data from well-designed and statistically-powered trials would be needed to generate high quality evidence on this issue.  

Keywords  Ticagrelor $\cdot$ Primary stroke prevention $\cdot$ Coronary artery disease $^{\ast}$ Antiplatelet $\cdot$ Meta-analysis  

# Highlights  

Aggregate data from five randomized clinical trials involving 45,843 patients with acute or chronic coronary syndrome showed significant associations between ticagrelor treatment and a lower risk of first-ever stroke. The benefits of ticagrelor for primary stroke prevention in patients with coronary artery disease mainly came from treatment for more than 1 year, using $60~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily dose, and in patients with chronic coronary syndrome. Reducing the risk of first-ever stroke significantly, ticagrelor-involving regimens should be considered in antiplatelet treatment for patients with coronary artery disease who had no stroke history. Ticagrelor treatment in patients with coronary artery disease to prevent more first-ever strokes might serve as an efficient way to reduce the growing incidence of stroke attacks.  

# Introduction  

Stroke is a leading cause of functional impairment and mortality worldwide, and nearly $80\%$ of them are first-ever attacks [1]. Having coronary artery disease (CAD) nearly tripled the incidence of first-ever stroke in the Framingham cohort [2], and stroke patients with CAD had worse prognosis [3, 4]. However, the primary prevention of stroke in patients with CAD has not drawn specific attention, with assumption that the antiplatelet strategies tailored for CAD would simultaneously provide effective prevention of their first-ever strokes. But the patients with CAD were still under higher risk of first-ever strokes than the general population, notwithstanding that they regularly received antiplatelet therapy [5, 6]. Furthermore, results from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) had suggested that the efficacy of antiplatelet drugs to prevent stroke was not necessarily consistent with their effects on reducing myocardial infarction (MI) [7, 8]. Therefore, further specific research on antiplatelet treatment for primary stroke prevention in patients with CAD is warranted.  

Considering the enhanced risk of first-ever stroke, especially the ischemic ones, more potent antiplatelet treatment may be more beneficial to the primary stroke prevention in CAD. Besides, as patients with stroke are particularly vulnerable to bleeding complications [9], CAD patients without a history of stroke may be more tolerate to potent antiplatelet treatment than the general CAD patients. In concordance, a meta-analysis of 44,816 patients with CAD ( $(<2\%$ with stroke history) demonstrated a $34\%$ reduction of ischemic stroke (IS) (hazard ratio [HR] .66, $95\%$ confidential interval [CI] 0.54–0.81) in patients treated with the more intensive antiplatelet therapy versus the less intensive one, without increasing the risk of hemorrhagic stroke (HR 0.29, $95\%$ CI 0.64–2.59) [5]. Recently, many potent antiplatelet drugs were new-developed and proved for clinical use in patients with CAD, offering exciting chance to optimize their antiplatelet regimens to prevent more first-ever strokes.  

Ticagrelor is a novel direct-acting and reversible antagonist of adenosine diphosphate receptor $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ , which potently inhibits platelet aggregation. Moreover, ticagrelor can increase blood adenosine concentration, further regulating platelet function, vessel vasodilation, and inflammatory responses [10]. The versatile action mechanism of ticagrelor might be quite favorable for a net benefit in stroke prevention. As preliminary evidence, recent meta-analyses in patients with CAD, cerebrovascular disease or peripheral artery disease found a trend that ticagrelor was associated with lower incidence of combined ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke compared with other antiplatelet regimens $(\mathtt{p}{=}0.05,$ [11, 12]. However, these meta-analyses in patients with various vascular disease either analyzed the effects of ticagrelor on primary and secondary stroke prevention in combination or focused on specific dosage of ticagrelor treatment. Further exploration would be necessary to determine whether ticagrelor treatment was more advantageous to patients with CAD to prevent the first-ever strokes.  

Therefore, in this meta-analysis of RCTs, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of ticagrelor versus other oral antiplatelet agents or placebo for the primary prevention of stroke in patients with acute or chronic coronary syndrome.  

# Methods  

The study was performed in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (see Supplementary materials for the PRISMA checklist).  

# Eligibility criteria of studies  

RCTs that compared ticagrelor with other oral antiplatelet agents (aspirin, clopidogrel, prasugrel) or placebo in patients presented with acute coronary syndrome (ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction, or unstable angina) or chronic coronary syndrome (defined as the 2019 European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommended [13]) were included. Studies of patients with a history of stroke were excluded. Ticagrelor can be administered as monotherapy or in combination with aspirin. Intravenous antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation therapy as co-intervention were not accepted. The clusterrandomized trials and cross-over trials were excluded. The ticagrelor treatment duration and follow-up period of clinical outcomes had to be at least 30 days, to establish a more plausible connection between the use of ticagrelor and the stroke-related outcomes.  

# Outcomes of interest  

The primary outcome was first-ever stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic, fatal or non-fatal). The secondary outcomes were first-ever IS (fatal or non-fatal), intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) (fatal or non-fatal), MI (fatal or non-fatal), allcause death, and major bleeding. We used the definition of outcomes as provided in the original publications.  

# Search strategy  

Eligible studies were identified by systematically searching PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane  

Collaboration Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception to September 2019. No language restriction was applied. The searching terms “ticagrelor”, “brilinta”, “brilique” and “AZD6140” were used (see Supplementary materials for Detailed search strategy). We further checked relevant references from original articles and pertinent reviews. We also searched www.clinicaltrials.gov and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform for completed and ongoing trials.  

# Data extraction and quality assessment  

Two reviewer (KQ and ZX) independently selected potentially eligible studies and performed the quality assessment. The third reviewer (MX) would resolve all the disagreements. Data was extracted using a predefined extraction form. We evaluated the methodological quality according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The judgements about each risk of bias item for each included study and judgements about each risk of bias item across all included studies were recorded. The publication bias would be evaluated by funnel plots when there were more than 10 studies reporting the outcomes.  

# Data synthesis and statistical analysis  

Data was analyzed using the Cochrane Review Manager 5.3 software [14]. The interested outcomes reported as dichotomous data were used for the meta-analysis, with the intention-to-treat principle. Statistical heterogeneity of trial data was assessed by the $\mathrm{I}^{2}$ statistics. If there were multiple intervention groups of a trial being relevant to this review, they would be combined to create a single pair-wise comparison, or split the shared group into two or more groups to include two or more independent comparisons.  

The overall treatment effect was estimated by the pooled risk ratio (RR) with $95\%$ CI using a fixed-effect model (Mantel–Haenszel). A random-effects model was used in cases of $\mathrm{I}^{2}\geq30\%$ . The planned subgroup analysis included different duration of ticagrelor treatment $\leq1$ year and $>1$ year), different dosage of ticagrelor treatment $90~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily and others), and different clinical settings of CAD (acute coronary syndrome and chronic coronary syndrome). These subgroup analyses were conducted using the random-effects models, and the differences between subgroups was tested with $\chi^{2}$ . A sensitivity analysis that only included studies with a low risk of bias was performed. The consistency in outcomes of interest between fixed-effect models and random-effects models was checked.  

# Results  

# Study selection and characteristics  

Systematic search identified a total of 5 RCTs (“The Effect of Ticagrelor on Health Outcomes in diabEtes Mellitus patients Intervention Study [THEMIS]” trial published in 2019 [15], “Rapid Early Action for Coronary Treatment [ISAR-REACT] 5” trial published in 2019 [16], “Prasugrel Versus Ticagrelor in Patients With Acute Myocardial Infarction Treated With Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention [PRAGUE] $18^{,}$ trial published in 2018 [17], the “Prevention of Cardiovascular Events in Patients With Prior Heart Attack Using Ticagrelor Compared to Placebo on a Background of Aspirin-Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction [PEGASUS-TIMI] 54” trial published in 2015 [18], and a trial by Bonello et al. published in 2015 [19]). The study selection process was shown in Supplementary figure 1.  

The selected 5 RCTs involved a total of 45,843 patients. Two trials included high-risk patients with chronic coronary syndrome (with prior MI or concomitant diabetes mellitus) and followed up about 3 years [15, 18]. Another 2 trials included patients with acute coronary syndrome undergoing invasive procedure and followed up 12 months [16, 17]. The last trial included non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome and followed up 1 month [19]. All these RCTs excluded patients with known history of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke.  

All participants in the review received aspirin as a background antiplatelet therapy, and ticagrelor was intended to be taken during the whole follow-up period. Two trials compared ticagrelor (both dosage of $90~\mathrm{mg}$ and $60~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily were administered) with placebo [15, 18], and 3 trials made the comparison between ticagrelor $90~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily and prasugrel $10~\mathrm{mg}$ daily [16, 17, 19]. The detailed description of these studies were listed in Table 1, the baseline characteristics and outcomes of interest were shown in Tables 2 and 3.

Three studies were openlabel RCTs [16, 17, 19] and two studies were doubleblinded [15, 18]. An independent committee adjudicating all efficacy endpoints and bleeding events in a blinded manner was clearly stated in 4 RCTs [15–18]. # Effects of ticagrelor on outcomes  

Compared to the control, ticagrelor was significantly associated with a lower incidence of first-ever stroke (5 studies; RR 0.81; $95\%$ CI 0.71–0.94; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and a reduction in IS (3 studies; RR 0.81; $95\%$ CI 0.69–0.94; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ). MI (3 studies; RR 0.99; $95\%$ CI 0.74–1.33; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}86\%$ ) and all-cause death were similar between ticagrelor group and control group (4 studies; RR 0.98; $95\%$ CI 0.90–1.06; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ). Of note, the effects of ticagrelor on MI showed substantial heterogeneity.  

For the safety outcomes, ticagrelor significantly increased the risk of ICH in comparison to the control (3 studies; RR 1.42; $95\%$ CI 1.06–1.89; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ). But ticagrelor was not related to a higher risk of major bleeding (5 studies; RR 1.48; $95\%\operatorname{CI}0.98–2.22;\Gamma^{2}=80\%)$ , with substantial heterogeneity (Fig. 1).  

# Subgroup analysis  

# Subgroup analysis of ticagrelor treatment duration  

In the “more than 1 year” subgroup, patients treated with ticagrelor had a significantly decreased risk of stroke (RR 0.80; $95\%$ CI $0.69\substack{-0.93}$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and IS (RR 0.80; $95\%$ CI $0.68{-}0.94$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ), but were also more likely to have ICH (RR 1.41; $95\%$ CI 1.05–1.89; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ) than those in the control group. In addition, ticagrelor became significantly associated with less MI (RR 0.83; $95\%$ CI 0.75–0.92; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and more major bleeding (RR 2.13; $95\%$ CI 1.77–2.56; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ) without heterogeneity. The all-cause death was still similar between ticagrelor and control groups.  

In the “1 year or less” subgroup, compared to the control, ticagrelor did not significantly change most interested outcomes, with an exception that the likelihood to experience MI was increased (RR 1.60; $95\%$ CI 1.16–2.19). However, there was only 1 study in this subgroup for the outcomes of IS, ICH, and MI (Fig. 2).  

# Subgroup analysis of ticagrelor treatment dosage  

In the $\mathrm{{}^{66}mg}$ twice daily” subgroup, compared to the control, ticagrelor was significantly related to a lower risk of stroke (RR 0.80; $95\%$ CI 0.67–0.94; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and IS (RR 0.79; $95\%$ CI 0.66–0.94; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ), but also a higher risk of ICH (RR 1.46; $95\%$ CI 1.05–2.03; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ). Different from the main analysis, ticagrelor significantly reduced MI (RR 0.84; $95\%$ CI $0.74\mathrm{-}0.95$ ; $\mathrm{I}^{2}{=}0\%$ ) and caused more major bleeding (RR 2.07; $95\%$ CI 1.69–2.55; $\mathrm{I}^{2}=0\%$ ) without heterogeneity. No significant influence of ticagrelor on the all-cause death was observed.  

In the $^{\mathrm{{66}}}90\mathrm{{mg}}$ twice daily” subgroup, there was no detectable difference for all interested outcomes between ticagrelor and control groups (Fig. 3).  

# Subgroup analysis of different clinical settings of CAD  

Considering that different antiplatelet strategies would be adopted as relevant guidelines recommended in different clinical settings of CAD, we performed the subgroup analysis according to ticagrelor treatment in acute or chronic coronary syndrome. Of note, the “acute coronary syndrome” subgroup included identical studies to aforementioned “1 year or less” subgroup. Meanwhile, studies in the “chronic coronary syndrome” subgroup were same as those in the “more than 1 year” subgroup. Therefore, results of this planned subgroup analysis could be referred to above subgroup analysis of ticagrelor treatment duration.  

# Sensitivity analysis  

In the sensitivity analysis of RCTs only with low risk of bias, findings were largely unchanged. We further used randomeffects models to re-analyze all interested outcomes, and the results were similar to those using fixed-effect models.  

# Discussion  

Pooled data from 45,843 CAD patients with no history of stroke in 5 RCTs showed that ticagrelor had  a statistically significant advantage in preventing first-ever strokes compared with other antiplatelet drugs or placebo. According to subgroup analyses, the beneficial effects of ticagrelor on primary stroke prevention in patients with CAD mainly came from treatment for more than 1 year, using dosage of $60\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily, and in patients with chronic coronary syndrome.  

In a meta-analysis in 2018, ticagrelor was found effective for reducing IS (RR 0.86, $95\%$ CI 0.78–0.95) in patients with

But only the dosage of $90~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily was assessed and the net benefits of preventing stroke were only found in those patients with stroke history [12]. Focusing on CAD patients without a history of stroke and with updated data, our review demonstrated that ticagrelor could significantly reduce first-ever strokes, driven by a reduction in first-ever IS. Though the incidence of ICH was also increased, the protective effects of ticagrelor on overall strokes were not neutralized. As many antiplatelet strategies after CAD had been proved to be effective for the secondary prevention of composite ischemic events, results of this meta-analysis further provided evidence that ticagrelor might be a superior choice for the primary stroke prevention in particular.  

There was no detectable statistical heterogeneity between included studies for above results, predefined subgroup analyses further suggested the patients who could benefit more.  

We found significant associations between ticagrelor and a lower risk of first-ever stroke in the “more than 1 year”, $^{\mathrm{{60}}}\mathrm{{mg}}$ twice daily” and “chronic coronary syndrome” subgroups of patients with CAD. However, the ticagrelor treatment duration, dosage, and clinical settings of CAD were closely intertwined among the included studies. Specifically, RCTs with ticagrelor treatment more than 1 year mainly used dosage of $60~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily, and were all in patients with chronic coronary syndrome. In contrast, RCTs with ticagrelor treatment 1 year or less mainly used dosage of $90~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily, and were all in patients with acute coronary syndrome. As a consequence, the implications of our subgroup analyses should be interpreted within this context where ticagrelor treatment duration, dosage, and clinical settings of CAD might simultaneously contribute to the results of subgroup analyses.  

At first, perhaps the advantages of ticagrelor on primary stroke prevention in CAD would grow with time, and became significant after 1 year treatment. Data from PEGASUS-TIMI 54 trial had shown that prior MI patients without a history of stroke have a persistent risk of ischemic events, and the effect of ticagrelor on preventing ischemic events remained stable during the 3-year follow-up, while the associated risk of major bleeding decreased year by year [20]. Therefore, the potential of ticagrelor to reduce IS might be able to outweigh its chance to induce hemorrhagic stroke with the prolongation of therapy, leading to a gradually reduced incidence of overall stroke. More analyses of relevant studies with long follow-up duration were needed to consolidate this possibility.

On second, maybe $60\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily dosage of ticagrelor treatment would provide more benefits than $90~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily for the primary stroke prevention in CAD. Pharmacological studies had proved that ticagrelor $60\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily could achieve similar level of platelet inhibition to that with  

$90~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily [21], while the $60\mathrm{-mg}$ dose was better tolerated and offered a more attractive safety profile [22]. In consistence, RCT of prior MI patients without stroke history demonstrated that the risk of stroke was significantly reduced with ticagrelor $60~\mathrm{mg}$ bid (HR 0.75; $95\%$ CI 0.57–0.98) rather than with $90~\mathrm{mg}$ bid (HR 0.82; $95\%$ CI 0.63–1.07) in comparison to placebo [18]. Ticagrelor $60\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily had been approved in Europe and America for the secondary prevention of composite ischemic events after prior MI [23, 24]. Our review might offer additional evidence that ticagrelor $60~\mathrm{mg}$ twice a day probably be more conducive to specifically reducing the first-ever strokes in CAD than the dosage of $90\mathrm{mg}$ twice a day. However, these analyses were based on indirect comparisons, more head-tohead studies would be needed.  

Thirdly, ticagrelor-involving regimens were possibly more beneficial to preventing first-ever stroke in chronic rather than acute coronary syndrome. For different clinical settings of CAD, different antiplatelet strategies were recommended by guidelines [23]. Fitting in this antiplatelet treatment framework, all RCTs included in “chronic coronary syndrome” subgroup were comparing ticagrelor plus aspirin dual-therapy with aspirin mono-therapy, while all RCTs included in “acute coronary syndrome” subgroup were comparing two first-line dual antiplatelet regimens (ticagrelor plus aspirin versus prasugrel plus aspirin). Consequently, the protective effects of ticagrelor on first-ever stroke in “chronic coronary syndrome” subgroup might result from advantages of dual antiplatelet therapy to mono-therapy. A recent post hoc meta-analysis manifested that prolonged dual antiplatelet therapy beyond 1 year among stabilized patients with prior MI (a scenario of the chronic coronary syndrome) could prevent more strokes than mono-therapy (RR 0.81, $95\%$ CI 0.68–0.97) [25]. Our meta-analysis of RCTs further implied that ticagrelor plus aspirin dual-therapy might reduce more first-ever strokes compared with aspirin monotherapy in patients with chronic coronary syndrome who even had no prior MI. But the effect of ticagrelor mono-therapy on primary stroke prevention in patients with chronic coronary syndrome remained unexplored.  

When it comes to the neutral effects of ticagrelor on first-ever stroke in “acute coronary syndrome” subgroup, as ticagrelor and prasugrel were both potent third generation of $\mathrm{P2Y}_{12}$ receptor inhibitor, it was understandable that ticagrelor plus aspirin versus prasugrel plus aspirin showed no significant difference in primary stroke prevention. However, a post hoc analysis of RCT comparing ticagrelor with traditional clopidogrel in patients with acute coronary syndrome also did not find a superiority of ticagrelor in preventing first-ever stroke [26]. Taken together, within the guidelinerecommended antiplatelet treatment framework after CAD, benefits of ticagrelor-involving regimens for primary stroke prevention might not be obvious immediately surrounding the acute coronary event, but would be significant in the chronic phase of CAD.  

Apart from aforementioned outcomes of interest that were directly related to primary stroke prevention, MI, major bleeding (defined by Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction [TIMI] criteria in 3 studies [15, 17, 18], and by Bleeding Academic Research Consortium [BARC] criteria in 2 studies [16, 19]) and all-cause death were also important outcomes to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ticagrelor for the preventing first-ever strokes in patients with CAD. We found no significant association between ticagrelor and these outcomes in main analysis, but substantial heterogeneity existed for the effects of ticagrelor on MI and major bleeding. The heterogeneity totally disappeared in subgroup analyses of ticagrelor treatment duration and clinical settings of CAD, indicating that they were the potential source of heterogeneity. Notably, MI was significantly reduced and major bleeding (including ICH, fatal bleeding, and clinically overt signs of hemorrhage associated with a drop in hemoglobin $\mathrm{of}\geq5~\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{dL}$ ) was increased by ticagrelor in “more than 1 year” and “chronic coronary syndrome” subgroups. Therefore, patients belong to these subgroups could also benefit from a reduction of MI in addition to experiencing less first-ever strokes with ticagrelor. But on the other hand, ticagrelor treatment more than 1 year in patients with chronic coronary syndrome reduced IS and MI at great expanse of causing major bleeding (for fewer ischemic events, the number need to treat [NNT] was 111; for more major bleeding events, the number to harm [NNH] was 103), notwithstanding that increased ICH did not cancel out the benefits of ticagrelor in preventing IS (for fewer IS, $\mathrm{NNT}=236$ ; for more ICH, $\mathrm{NNH}=815$ ) and the incidence of all-cause death was unaffected.  

Patients with CAD were high-risk individuals not only for cardiac events but also for first-ever strokes, who should be specifically targeted for effective primary stroke prevention. For the first time, we systematically reviewed data from available RCTs to assess the efficacy and safety of ticagrelor for the primary prevention of stroke in patients with CAD, and generated preliminary evidence that ticagrelor might serve as a promising choice to optimize the antiplatelet strategies after CAD from the aspect of preventing first-ever strokes, especially with treatment more than 1 year and $60~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily in patients with chronic coronary syndrome. As suggested by the narrow CIs, accuracy of our main findings was good, despite that the analytic results in “1 year or less”, $^{\mathrm{{\leftarrow}}90\mathrm{mg}}$ twice daily” and “acute coronary syndrome” subgroups might be less accurate than those in “more than 1 year”, $^{\mathrm{{60}~mg}}$ twice daily” and “chronic coronary syndrome” subgroups (due to a relatively small sample size with fewer interested events in the former subgroups). In accordance, the findings from subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution, given the limited number of studies included in this meta-analysis. Although there was inconsistency in ticagrelor treatment duration, dosage, comparators, and clinical settings of CAD among included studies, no statistical heterogeneity was detected for most interested outcomes except MI and major bleeding. And predefined subgroup analysis explained the source of positive effects, as well as the source of heterogeneity, to some extent. However, the different enrollment criteria of participants, unstandardized definitions of outcomes, various study sites, complex phenotypes of CAD and stroke, and many other confounding factors among the included trials might also contribute to the heterogeneity and affect the results in syntheses. Cautions should be taken in the generalization of our findings. In the very context, meta-analyses with individual participant data, which would allow more detailed analysis and more thorough risk of bias assessment than this review of aggregate data, might be necessary to generate highquality evidence for the changes of guidelines.  

Limitations of this review should be further considered. Firstly, as we aimed to address the potential for ticagrelor to reduce first-ever strokes in patients with CAD, a study would be excluded if any participant in it had a history of stroke. Only 5 RCTs met the stringent selection criteria, and two of them ( $6.3\%$ of the total participants) were open-label in design and one terminated prematurely. Secondly, all the analyses were performed based on reported studies and reported data within studies, which might arise the bias due to missing results in the syntheses. But we did not find additional unpublished results from the trials registers (www. clinicaltrials.gov and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform), four selected RCTs $(99.6\%$ of the total participants) reported outcomes as the protocols published beforehand, and all the five eligible RCTs had specifically reported our primary interested outcome (first-ever stroke). Nonetheless, the publication bias and the reporting bias for our secondary outcomes of interest could not be excluded. Thirdly, we only included RCTs using ticagrelor for at least 30 days, in order to establish a more plausible connection between ticagrelor and the strokerelated outcomes. So the immediate protection against stroke with very short duration of ticagrelor treatment might be underestimated. At last, the intention-to-treat principle was applied in the data syntheses to maintain the benefits of randomization and estimate the effects of assignment to intervention, but the results were likely to be biased, as indicated by the higher rate of permanent treatment discontinuation in the ticagrelor group than the control group in both doubleblinded [15, 18] and open-label [16, 17] RCTs included in this review.  

In the future, more well-designed and adequately powered studies focusing on the application strategies of ticagrelor for primary stroke prevention in CAD would be needed, with an emphasis on evaluating the efficacy and safety of ticagrelor using more than 1 year, $60~\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily, or as monotherapy in reducing the first-ever strokes among patients with chronic coronary syndrome. Meanwhile, more efforts should be put in identifying CAD patients with higher IS risk and lower bleeding risk to improve the benefit-harm profile of ticagrelor therapy. For example, it would be intriguing to know whether ticagrelor could prevent more first-ever strokes in patients with CAD who had asymptomatic cervicocephalic large artery atherosclerosis. Lastly, high-quality meta-analyses with patient-level data from RCTs, as well as post hoc meta-analyses of RCTs and meta-analyses of observational studies, were welcomed on this issue. With all these efforts, further advance in primary stroke prevention and further optimization of antiplatelet therapy in CAD might be expected. It would not only be instrumental in stroke reduction overall, but also be helpful to precisely improve the prognosis of patients with CAD.  

# Conclusions  

Based on aggregate data from 5 RCTs, ticagrelor-involving antiplatelet regimens might be favored in comparison to other antiplatelet regimens for the primary stroke prevention in patients with CAD. Ticagrelor was associated with a reduction of IS and an acceptable increase of ICH, resulting in the net modest protective effects on preventing firstever strokes in CAD. The benefits would possibly be more obvious with long-term and low-dose ticagrelor treatment in patients with chronic coronary syndrome. High quality evidence on this issue remained lacking, and further systematic reviews with individual participant data and well-designed clinical trials with adequate power would be clearly needed.
