# Combined External Ventricular Drainage and Endoscope-Assisted Microsurgery Using the Middle Frontal Gyrus Approach in Severe Ventricular Hemorrhage with Casting of the Fourth Ventricle  

Wu Qi, Zhan Liping, Chen Bo, Li Qiaoyu, Sun Eryi - OBJECTIVE: To investigate combined external ventricular drainage and endoscope-assisted microsurgery using the middle frontal gyrus approach in patients with severe ventricular hemorrhage with casting of the fourth ventricle and patients’ recovery after this treatment.  

- METHODS: Patients with severe ventricular hemorrhage with casting of the fourth ventricle $(n=41)$ were randomly assigned to intervention and control groups. Modified Graeb score was used to assess 3-day hematoma clearance rate before and after surgery, drainage tube extubation time for the 2 groups was compared, and time when blood clot in the fourth ventricle was not blocked with cerebrospinal fluid was compared. Glasgow Coma Scale was used to assess consciousness after surgery; Glasgow Coma Scale scores recorded 1 and 7 days after surgery were also compared. Modified Rankin Scale was used to evaluate patients’ recovery 1 and 6 months after surgery. Hydrocephalus and intracranial infections in patients after surgery were recorded for 90 days.  

- RESULTS: The 3-day hematoma clearance rate was dramatically higher in the intervention group. Modified Graeb score showed that more hemorrhage was delimited in 3 days in the intervention group. The intervention group exhibited significantly reduced length of block of the fourth ventricle and drainage tube extubation time. High Glasgow Coma Scale and modified Rankin Scale scores and significantly low incidence of complications (e.g., hydrocephalus and intracranial infection) were observed in patients in the intervention group.  

- CONCLUSIONS: Combined external ventricular drainage and endoscope-assisted microsurgery using the middle frontal gyrus approach can effectively improve severe ventricular hemorrhage with casting of the fourth ventricle and enhance patients’ neurological function and recovery.  

# INTRODUCTION  

ntracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is associated with high mortality and disability. Based on accumulated reports, half of patients with ICH die within 30 days. Moreover, half of these   
patients die within 2 days, so ICH is a disease characterized by   
significantly high acute mortality.1 ICH spreading into the   
ventricle will increase ventricle system compression, acute   
dilation, and intracranial pressure owing to the significant,   
rapidly developing hematoma, leading to acute obstructive   
hydrocephalus.2,3 In addition, patients with intraventricular   
hematoma (IVH) with blood clots in the third and fourth   
ventricles might form ventricular casts. Furthermore, brainstem   
compression,4 cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation blockage,   
and poor prognosis5 are found in these patients. As a result,   
innovative treatments that enhance prognosis are urgently needed.  

# Key words  

- Endoscope-assisted microsurgical - External ventricular drainage - Middle frontal gyrus approach - Severe ventricular hemorrhage  

# Abbreviations and Acronyms  

CFV: Casting of the fourth ventricle CSF: Cerebrospinal fluid CT: Computed tomography EVD: External ventricular drainage ICH: Intracerebral hemorrhage IVH: Intraventricular hematoma  

The traditional treatment for these patients is combined external ventricular drainage with intraventricular fibrinolysis.4 However, no significant improvement occurred in patients with severe ventricular hemorrhage (SVH) with casting of the fourth ventricle (CFV). Because typical therapies take a long time to remove a hematoma, the brainstem is frequently subjected to continuous compression and permanent damage.5,6 A cerebellar vermis incision was recently used to remove a fourth ventricle hematoma in patients with SVH with CFV; however, injury of the cerebellar vermis was also found after this surgery.7 The cerebellar medulla fissure approach7 was also used; still, it was not successful in removing the hematoma in the body of the ventricle. Some studies used flexible endoscopic aspiration for intraventricular treatment, but this cannot stop the active bleeding.8 Therefore, more efficient approaches are needed in this situation.  

Recently, the endoport-assisted device used in cerebral cavernous malformation,9 ruptured posterior cerebral artery aneurysm,10 and severe $\mathrm{IVH}^{\mathrm{II-T6}}$ has been reported in a number of cases of SVH with CFV. Despite alternative procedures, endoscopic surgery appears to be the most promising treatment for SVH with CFV.12-14 Although endoscopic hematoma evacuation has been widely used to treat this disease, the technique still needs to be further developed.16 In our study, using the middle frontal gyrus approach, we explored the short-term and long-term efficacy of combined external ventricular drainage (EVD) and endoscope-assisted microsurgery in patients with SVH with CFV.  

# MATERIALS AND METHODS  

# Patients  

This study enrolled 41 patients with SVH with CFV. All enrolled patients were treated in our neurosurgery department between 2019 and 2021. We randomly assigned all patients to either an intervention group $(n=\mathrm{\mathrm{~\bf~r~}}6\mathrm{\mathrm{~\bf~\Omega~}}$ , 10 men and 6 women with mean age 61.5 years) or a control group $\mathtt{\tilde{n}}=25$ , 17 men and 8 women with mean ag: 60.96 years). Conventional ventricular puncture and drainage, lumbar cistern drainage, and urokinase lysis were performed in patients in the control group. Patients in the intervention group additionally underwent combined EVD and endoscope-assisted microsurgery using the middle frontal gyrus approach.  

This study strictly complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Study protocols and procedures were approved by our hospital ethics committee. All patients enrolled in this study provided informed consent before the start of the research.  

# Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria  

Patients ranging in age from ${>}\mathbf{I}8$ years old to $<80$ years old with disease onset within 24 hours, dilated hematoma in the fourth ventricle, bleeding into the ventricle, and parenchymal bleeding $<20\mathrm{mL}$ were included in this study. Patients with secondary cerebral hemorrhage caused by hemorrhagic infarction, cerebral hernia, brain tumor, craniocerebral injury, ruptured intracranial aneurysm, or arteriovenous malformation were excluded. Patients with severe renal and hepatic insufficiency and anticoagulationrelated cerebral hemorrhage were also excluded. Additionally, patients who refused to attend follow-up and did not meet the surgical indications were excluded. Finally, lactating or pregnant women with malignant tumors were excluded from this study.  

# Operation  

Neuroendoscopy was used as a monitoring lens to remove ventricular hematoma in the intervention group. After intubation, general anesthesia, supine positioning, and bed elevation to $30^{\circ}$ , an arc-shaped incision about 10 cm long was made around the puncture point $2.5~\mathrm{cm}$ in front of the coronal suture and next to the midline. A bone window with a diameter of $3{\mathrm{cm}}\times3$ cm was formed with a milling cutter. Dural dissection was done by a cross incision, and the middle frontal gyrus cortex was electrocoagulated about $\mathtt{1.5}\mathrm{cm}$ . The puncture sheath tube was quickly withdrawn from the hollow tube core after entering the hematoma cavity, and the hematoma was removed under direct endoscopic vision. A larger range could be observed by slightly adjusting the puncture sheath angle and angle lens. During the removal of hematoma, care was taken to protect important structures. After most of the hematoma was removed, a small amount of bleeding in the surgical area was continuously rinsed with $37^{\circ}$ Ringer solution or temporarily compressed with a small gelatin sponge. Double minimum power electrocoagulation was used when there was definite pulsing bleeding. For thalamic hemorrhage into the ventricle, contusions on the lateral wall of the ventricle were observed during the operation, and the hematoma in the thalamic parenchyma could be sucked out through the damage. The third ventricle and aqueduct were visible, and the intraventricular hematoma was sucked out. The drainage tube was placed into the third ventricle under direct endoscopic vision, and the puncture sheath was slowly withdrawn. While retreating the inside tunnel, the bosearvtion is continued insistently to ensure the tube’s location. The drainage tube was drawn out and fixed beside the incision; the distance from the incision was $\ge3\mathrm{cm}$ , and the drainage device was connected. A head computed tomography (CT) was performed immediately after surgery and again within 24 hours to examine the elimination of the hematoma and the position of the drainage tube as well as to alter the drainage tube’s depth in real time. The other postoperative treatments were the same as the control group.  

The patients in the control group were treated with traditional lumbar cistern and ventricle puncture drainage plus urokinase lysis. The drainage tube position was observed by head CT reexamination within 24 hours after the operation. On the second day after the procedure, if there was no fresh bleeding, 20,000 U urokinase dissolved in $5\mathrm{mL}0.9\%$ saline was injected into the drainage tube, and the drain was opened after 1 hour of suspension 1 or 2 times a day. The head CT scan was repeated daily, and the circulation of CSF in the upper and lower ventricles was smooth. Closure of the drain and extubation were attempted within 7 days. If extubation failed, the drainage was prolonged, or the lateral ventricle was pierced again on the opposite side until the patient could tolerate the drainage tube being closed and extubation was completed. The lumbar cistern was continuously drained after extubation, or a lumbar puncture was done until the CSF was yellowish and clear.  

# Observation Index  

Treatment Outcome of Both Treatments. The clearance rates of these 2 therapeutic approaches were evaluated on a head CT scan 3 days after surgery. The volume was calculated with ImageJ software.17 The clearance rate of hematoma was calculated using the following formula: $\mathrm{\mathrm{~\textit~{~R~}~}}=\mathrm{\Gamma(V_{I}\mathrm{~\mathrm{~-~}~}V_{2})/V_{I}\mathrm{~\times~}\Gamma_{I00}\%}$ , where $\mathrm{V}\mathbf{\tau}$ represents the volume of preoperative hematoma, and $_{\mathrm{V}2}$ represents the volume of postoperative hematoma. In addition, modified Graeb score18 was also obtained to assess the treatment efficacy; this score could also show the IVH volume. The drainage tube extubation times for 2 differentially treated groups of patients were compared. Moreover, length of the block of the fourth ventricle was compared.  

Neurological Deficiency of Both Treatments. At 1 and 7 days after the surgery, consciousness was evaluated using the Glasgow Coma Scale score and compared. Recovery of the patients at 1 and 6 months after surgery was assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score.  

Complications of Both Treatments. Incidence of hydrocephalus within 90 days and intracranial infection within 30 days was recorded during follow-up.  

# Statistical Analysis  

IBM SPSS Version 26.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA) was employed to analyze the data. Count data were represented as rates, and the comparison was conducted using the $\chi^{2}$ test. Measurement values were shown as mean $\pm\mathrm{SD}$ , and the comparison was carried out using a t test. Multiple logistic regression was employed to conduct the multivariate analysis. $\mathrm{~P~}<0.05$ indicated significant difference.  

# RESULTS  

# Baseline Patient Data  

As shown in Table 1, no significant differences in Glasgow Coma Scale score, modified Graeb score, bleeding site, time to surgery,  

age, or sex were found between the patients in the control and intervention groups.  

# Operative Results  

As shown in Table 2, 3 days after surgery, the patients in the intervention group exhibited a significantly high rate of hematoma clearance. The difference was significant compared with the control group. Meanwhile, the modified Graeb score, which showed the volume of ventral bleeding, revealed more hemorrhage was delimited in 3 days in the intervention group (Table 2). Additionally, the patients in the intervention group also exhibited dramatically reduced length of the block of the fourth ventricle and drainage tube extubation time compared with the control group $\left(\mathrm{P}<0.00\mathrm{I}\right)$ ) (Table 2).  

# Recovery Rate  

The evaluations of consciousness and recovery after surgery showed that compared with the control group, the patients in the intervention group exhibited significantly high Glasgow Coma Scale scores $\mathrm{\Delta(P_{\mathrm{~<~}}0.oor)}$ ) (Table 2) and mRS scores $\left(\mathrm{P}<0.00\mathrm{I}\right)$ (Table 2) at 1 and 6 months after surgery, suggesting these patients had better consciousness and recovery.  

# Mortality and Complication Rate  

The intervention group had a lower death rate compared with the control group, but this was not significant. Moreover, the patients in the intervention group exhibited fewer complications, such as intracranial infection and hydrocephalus, suggesting a reduced complication rate in the patients treated with our new approach (Table 2).  

# Illustrative Case  

A patient with thalamic hemorrhage is shown in Figure 1. This 72- year-old patient lost consciousness and was hemiplegic. The right thalamic hemorrhage with casting hematoma was observed in the third, fourth, and anterior horns of the lateral ventricle on CT scan. Endoscopic surgery was performed within 6 hours. The CSF circulation impaired by hematomas was evacuated sufficiently, and a ventricular tube was put in the third ventricle during surgery. CT scan showed that $75{\cdot}22\%$ of the hematoma was removed. Then, urokinase was given 2 days after the surgery, and the clot casting the ventricle almost completely disappeared 3 days after the surgery (Figure 1). The ventricular drainage tube was removed 4 days after surgery. No additional CSF shunt surgery was performed; the 1-month mRS score was 4.

# DISCUSSION  

Ventricular casting produced by a ventricular hemorrhage develops quickly and is characterized by significant morbidity $(85\%)$ and impairment $\left(50\%{-}80\%\right)$ .19,20 Compared with patients without IVH, patients with IVH twice as many poor outcomes.5 Hematoma volume caused by acute CSF circulation blockage could induce toxic metabolic damage to the ependyma, hypertensive hydrocephalus, ventricular wall distention, mechanical disruption, reduced cerebral perfusion, and increased intracranial pressure, leading to rapid neurological deterioration.21 On the other hand, reduced neurotoxicity and relief of obstructive hydrocephalus owing to administration of blood products could efficiently increase the long-term functional outcome and survival of patients with IVH.22 Thus, clinical treatment plans commonly used the reduction of intracranial pressure, brain edema, and dredging up obstrcution of the ventricle.  

Compared with lobar ICH, ICH within deep structures adjacent to the third ventricle is more frequently associated with worse outcomes because the deep nuclei play an important role in neurological functions.23 Neurological deficiency caused by ventricular outflow compression, deep hemorrhages, or both was observed in patients. Furthermore, the benefit of intraventricular thrombolytic therapy may be diminished by the  

CSF flow obstruction within the ventricular system in patients with concomitant IVH.5 Thus, relief of brainstem compression and dredging up obstrcution of the ventricle were chosen as the treatment plan for these situations.  

The traditional surgical methods are unilateral or bilateral lateral ventricle drilling and drainage, followed by intraventricular urokinase injection to dissolve blood clots and continuous and slow drainage of gradually liquefied blood in the intraventricular.24 The advantage of this surgical method is it is associated with minor trauma. The use of intraventricular fibrinolytic agents25 slightly reduced mortality compared with EVD alone. However, no apparent benefit in functional outcome was obtained. In addition, low case fatality $(\mathrm{I}8\%$ vs. $29\%$ ) was observed after ventricular irrigation with alteplase via EVD. In comparison, we also observed a higher percentage of patients with an mRS score of 5 $(\mathrm{{I}}7^{0}/\mathrm{{{0}}}$ vs. $9\%)^{26}$ ; obstruction of the third ventricle by IVH was a significant predictor of higher mRS score at 180 days, whereas obstruction by ICH was not.5 However, this method has many limits and disadvantages. EVD lack of the surgical vision and cannot verfied the location directly under vision, which causes the position of catheterization to be uncertain and easily compromised owing to the drain blockage by a clot. This situation commonly occurs in patients with cast IVH because most of the clot is solid, and only the more superficial fluid component (if any left) of blood-stained CSF remaining in the ventricles can be drained, so the IVH cannot be cleared quickly, and the intracranial pressure cannot be reduced rapidly.18 Drainage tube indwelling time is long, and the injection of urokinase through the drainage tube increases the risk of bleeding and infection. Hematoma in brain parenchyma cannot be cleared. Improvement was observed in some patients after the traditional treatment method. However, no improvement in the disability rate was found in these patients after treatment. In the patients with CFV, ischemic necrosis and insufficient blood supply were caused by brainstem compression and brainstem perforator artery perfusion resulting from IVH.27 These observations indicated that dredging, the third ventricle’s obstruction, and removing the larger amounts of IVH volume might positively affect prognosis, even if it has many limits and disadvantages.

Some researchers have performed the cerebellar vermis incision to remove the fourth ventricle hematoma when dealing with obstruction of the third and fourth ventricles. Patients with acute disease were saved by this operation; however, injury of the cerebellar vermis was also caused by this surgical approach.28 However, consciousness, circulatory, and respiratory dysfunction resulted from the intraoperative injury because of more trauma from this surgical approach. Additionally, complications, including cerebellar ataxia or mutism, may occur owing to the cut of the cerebellar vermis. High complication rates after surgery do not favor the patient’s recovery.27 Although patients’ lives were saved, lifelong disability was also caused by these complications. Some studies used the cerebellomedullary fissure microsurgery approach to treat IVH with CFV.7 However, this approach has many deficiencies. Familiarity with the anatomical characteristics around the fourth ventricle is necessary. A variety of electrophysiological neuromonitoring methods need to be combined during the operation to monitor the patient’s blood supply and brainstem function. Therefore, the operation site needs to be adjusted. Finally, this approach cannot deal with intraventricular hemorrhage.  

A safer operation was conducted by some researchers, who operated under a high-resolution visual field using a flexible neuroendoscope (videoscope).8 However, this type of surgery is difficult to manage, with more hematoma, and it takes a lot of time to suck out the hematoma. At the same time, there is no effective way to stop active bleeding.  

In this study, neuroendoscopy was used as an observation lens, and an endoport was used to remove hematoma in the working channel formed in the frontal horn of the lateral ventricle punctured in the middle frontal gyrus, and urokinase thrombolytic therapy was combined with a drainage tube. Three days of hematoma clearance, recanalization time of the fourth ventricle, and the prognosis of postoperative neurological functions all were good outcomes.  

The middle frontal gyrus approach is a classic approach for treating ventricular hemorrhage. Some researchers are inclined to use the endoscope in the trans-sulcal approach.16 Actually, there remains some doubt about this approach. In this study, we did not select the trans-sulcal approach considering the technical hurdles for surgeons to overcome in the surgery, including the varied anatomy of the cortical bridging veins, the dissection of the pericallosal arteries, and the need for callosal sectioning.29 In the resection of lateral ventricular tumors, lesions in the temporal horn or atrium are more safely approached through cortical incisions.30 Furthermore, the transcortical approach for the resection of ventricular lesions has been proved to be associated with less impairment to working memory than the transcallosal route,31 possibly attributed to less damage to the fornices in the transcortical approach. Theoretically, tubular retractors allow for the gentle spreading of white matter fibers to decrease the number of transected tracts and have been used in minimally invasive corticectomy.32,33 Compared with the transcallosal approach, the corticectomy and puncture via a middle frontal gyrus approach cause less neurological dysfunction in patients with severe ICH.  

On the other hand, neuroendoscopy technology pushes the surgeon’s “eye” close to the lesion for general observation. Furthermore, it operates in the working channel formed by endoport, which has the advantage of dealing with the long surgical path. The advantage of this treatment plan is that it can use neuroendoscopy technology to clear most of the clots in the IVH. In our experience, large blood clots that are difficult to be sucked out at one time can be sucked out gradually after being chewed up, and the surgeon needs to be patient and careful to not damage the ventricle wall. After the hematoma was cleared in the visual field, the structure of the cerebral ventricle wall was observed. After the direction was differentiated, the angle of the working sheath was adjusted to remove the lateral ventricular frontal hematoma. The interventricular foramen, anterior phrenic vein, septal vein, and colliculus veins filled with blood clots could be observed. These veins, especially the colliculus veins, need special protection. As to our experience, the hematoma was aspirated at the same ventricular foramen with a straight head side attractor or a curved one, and the suction force of the attractor was controlled carefully. With pressure change, the hematoma in the third ventricle and contralateral ventricle would gradually move to the ventricular foramen. An operation without visibility to remove the hematoma completely should never be performed. Hematomas in the trigone of the ipsilateral ventricle and the occipital horn can be removed backward along the choroid plexus of the lateral ventricle, and even hematomas in the ipsilateral temporal horn can be removed mainly by adjusting the angle of the working sheath (or changing the angle lens). By changing the angle of operation to conform to the physiological and anatomical characteristics of the ventricle, the dead angle in the surgical field of vision can be eliminated to the maximum extent. Using endoscopic surgery, the operators could quickly clear the hematoma, improve cerebral perfusion, weaken the neurotoxic effects caused by hematoma decomposition products, and reduce extrusion effects on critical cerebral.  

In the case of small artery pulsing bleeding, a fixed endoscopy arm, two-handed operation, bipolar electrocoagulation, and lowpower electrocoagulation can be used to stop blood and reduce the incidence of secondary bleeding. Most of the bleeding on the wound surface can be satisfactorily controlled by flushing with $37^{\circ}$ Ringer solution or temporary compression with a gelatin sponge. Blood usually remains in the temporal and occipital horns after clot removal from the lateral ventricles and frontal horns. Using an endoscope provided a novel approach for clot evacuation extensively and safely and for adequate bleed control from the choroid.34 This application of neurosurgeons significantly increased the rate of hematoma clearance and decreased the rebleeding probability after surgery because the hematoma can be removed under direct vision.  

For cases of thalamic hemorrhage spreading into the ventricle, the portal can often be observed on the lateral wall of the lateral ventricle, through which the hematoma in the thalamus can be sucked out, rather than completely removing the hematoma in the parenchyma of the thalamus, to achieve decompression.  

Finally, some published research indicated that to decrease the risk of posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus, a third ventriculostomy must be performed after removing the clot plugging the aqueduct when using the conventional endoscopic technique.34 However, ETV success varies from $40\%$ to $87\%$ . The overall complication rate is $8\%-\mathtt{I5}\%$ , including bleeding (cortical, ventricular, and cisternal vessels), neural injury (fornix, third cranial nerve, hypothalamus, and midbrain), subdural collections, pneumoventricle, hyponatremia, seizures, delayed awakening, bradycardia, hypothalamic dysfunction (diabetes insipidus, lack of thirst, amenorrhea), and hyperthermia. Late complications include reclosure of the stoma, weight gain, and precocious puberty. In our experience, we chose to put the drainage catheter into the third ventricle to deal with the obstruction of the third and fourth ventricles. The drainage catheter could be placed under direct view, improving placement accuracy and avoiding damage to the choroid plexus. We placed the catheter into the third ventricle and combined urokinase thrombolytic therapy with endoscopic surgery. It performed well in 3 days for hematoma clearance, fourth ventricle recanalization time, and postoperative neurological function prognosis.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

Combined endoscopic and urokinase thrombolytic therapy can swiftly eliminate lateral ventricular hematoma as well as a few third and fourth ventricle hematomas, allowing ventricular circulation access to be restored. This reduces the number of complications and helps patients with neurological deficits.
