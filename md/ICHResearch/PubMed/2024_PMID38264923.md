# Efficacy and Safety of Non–Vitamin-K Antagonis Oral Anticoagulants Versus Warfarin Across the Spectrum of Body Mass Index and Body Weight: An Individual Patient Data Meta-Analysis of 4 Randomized Clinical Trials of Patients With Atrial Fibrillation  

Siddharth M. Patel , MD, MPH; Eugene Braunwald , MD; Jan Steffel , MD; Giuseppe Boriani , MD, PhD; Michael G. Palazzolo , MS; Elliott M. Antman , MD; Erin A. Bohula , MD, DPhil; Anthony P. Carnicelli , MD; Stuart J. Connolly , MD; John W. Eikelboom , MBBS, MSc; Baris Gencer , MD, MPH; Christopher B. Granger , MD; David A. Morrow , MD, MPH; Manesh R. Patel , MD; Lars Wallentin , MD, PhD; Christian T. Ruff , MD, MPH; Robert P. Giugliano , MD, SM; on behalf of the COMBINE AF (A Collaboration Between Multiple Institutions to Better Investigate Non-Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulant Use in Atrial Fibrillation) Investigators  

BACKGROUND: The efficacy and safety of non–vitamin-K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) across the spectrum of body mass index (BMI) and body weight (BW) remain uncertain.  

METHODS: We analyzed data from COMBINE AF (A Collaboration Between Multiple Institutions to Better Investigate Non-Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulant Use in Atrial Fibrillation), which pooled patient-level data from the 4 pivotal randomized trials of NOAC versus warfarin in patients with atrial fibrillation. The primary efficacy and safety outcomes were stroke or systemic embolic events (stroke/SEE) and major bleeding, respectively; secondary outcomes were ischemic stroke/SEE, intracranial hemorrhage, death, and the net clinical outcome (stroke/SEE, major bleeding, or death). Each outcome was examined across BMI and BW. Because few patients had a B $\mathsf{\Omega}\forall1<18.5~\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ $\scriptstyle\mathrm{\stackrel{\prime}{n}}=598,$ ), the primary analyses were restricted to those with a $\mathsf{BM}|\geq18.5\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ .  

RESULTS: Among 58 464 patients, the median BMI was 28.3 (interquartile range, 25.2–32.2) $\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ , and the median BW was 81.0 (interquartile range, 70.0–94.3) kg. The event probability of stroke/SEE was lower at a higher BMI irrespective of treatment, whereas the probability of major bleeding was lower at a higher BMI with warfarin but relatively unchanged across BMI with NOACs. NOACs reduced stroke/SEE overall (adjusted hazard ratio $[\mathsf{HR}_{-}]$ , 0.80 $[95\%$ CI, 0.73–0.88]; $P{<}0.001$ ), with a generally consistent effect across BMI $(P_{\mathrm{trend}}$ across HRs, 0.48). NOACs also reduced major bleeding overall $\left(\mathsf{H}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{adi}},\mathsf{\Omega}\right)$ , 0.88 $[95\%$ CI, 0.82–0.94]; $P{<}0.001$ ), but with attenuation of the benefit at a higher BMI (trend test across BMI $[P_{\mathrm{trend}}],$ 0.003). The overall treatment effects of NOACs versus warfarin for secondary outcomes were consistent across BMI, with the exception of the net clinical outcome and death. While these outcomes were overall reduced with NOACs (net clinical outcome, $\mathsf{HR}_{\mathsf{adj}},$ 0.91 $[95\%$ CI, 0.87–0.95]; $P{<}0.001$ ; death, $\mathsf{HR}_{\mathrm{adj}},$ , 0.91 $[95\%$ CI, $0.86\substack{-0.97}]$ ; $_{P=0.003}$ ), these benefits were attenuated at higher BMI $\cdotP_{\mathrm{trend}},$ 0.001 and $0.08$ , respectively). All findings were qualitatively similar when analyzed across BW.  

CONCLUSIONS: The treatment effect of NOACs versus warfarin in atrial fibrillation is generally consistent for stroke/SEE across the spectrum of BMI and BW, whereas the reduction in major bleeding is attenuated in those with higher BMI or BW.  

Death and the net clinical outcome are overall reduced with NOACs over warfarin, although there remain uncertainties for these outcomes at a very high BMI and BW.

# Clinical Perspective  

# Nonstandard Abbreviations and Acronyms  

# What Is New?  

•	 The effect of non–vitamin-K antagonist oral anticoagulants on stroke and systemic embolism is generally consistent across the spectrum of body mass index (BMI) and body weight (BW) relative to warfarin. •	 Non–vitamin-K antagonist oral anticoagulants reduce major bleeding versus warfarin, primarily in patients with lower BMI and BW, with attenuation of benefit at higher BMI and BW. •	 The reduction in intracranial hemorrhage, the primary safety benefit of non–vitamin-K antagonist oral anticoagulants relative to warfarin, appears preserved across the spectrum of BMI and BW, including in underweight patients $(\mathsf{BMI}<18.5\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ or BW ${<}50~{\ttkg},$ traditionally felt to be at high risk.  

# What Are the Clinical Implications?  

•	 These data provide clinicians with a more comprehensive overview of the efficacy and safety of non– vitamin- $\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}$ antagonist oral anticoagulants relative to warfarin across the range of BMI and BW for a multitude of clinical outcomes and may be useful for shared decision-making with patients about choice of anticoagulant.  

ontemporary guidelines recommend the use of non–vitamin-K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) over vitamin-K antagonists for prevention of ischemic stroke in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF).1,2 Obesity is a well-established risk factor for AF.3 Despite a greater prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors with increasing obesity severity, previous analyses have suggested an obesity paradox in AF, with patients with obesity having lower rates of stroke, bleeding, and even mortality.4,5 Importantly, obesity may also result in reduced drug concentrations of NOACs mediated in part by changes in volume of distribution and drug clearance.6,7 The opposite may occur in underweight patients, with unintended higher drug concentrations in this population. As such, concerns have been raised about the efficacy and safety of NOACs in both obese and underweight patients,8,9 for whom differences in drug concentrations may contribute to higher risk for ischemic stroke and major bleeding, respectively.10–13

Although some post hoc analyses of the NOAC versus warfarin trials in patients with AF have suggested preserved efficacy and safety of NOACs in these populations,5,7,12–15 the number of individuals at the extremes of body mass index (BMI) or body weight (BW) within any of these studies has been relatively modest $(<200$ and $<1100$ patients with a $\mathsf{BMI}<18.5~\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ and $\ge40$ $\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ , respectively, in any single trial). Consequently, equipoise remains about the optimal choice of anticoagulant and dosing in these patient populations, with some society guidelines recommending the use of vitamin- $\cdot\mathsf{K}$ antagonists8,9 or measurement of NOAC plasma levels in those at the extremes of BMI or BW,1,9 whereas others offer no specific guidance.2  

Accordingly, we sought to examine the treatment effect of NOACs versus warfarin across the range of BMI and BW, including those at the extremes, by leveraging the greater sample size and number of events afforded by pooling individual patient-level data across the 4 pivotal NOAC versus warfarin trials in AF.  

# METHODS  

# Study Design and Data Source  

The design and methods of the COMBINE AF (A Collaboration Between Multiple Institutions to Better Investigate Non-Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulant Use in Atrial Fibrillation) database have been previously published.16,17 In brief, COMBINE AF pools individual patient data from RE-LY (Randomized  

Evaluation of Long-Term Anticoagulation Therapy),18 ROCKET AF (Rivaroxaban Once Daily Oral Direct Factor Xa Inhibition Compared With Vitamin K Antagonism for Prevention of Stroke and Embolism Trial in Atrial Fibrillation),19 ARISTOTLE (Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation),20 and ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 (Effective Anticoagulation With Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation–Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 48),21 which assessed dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban, respectively, versus warfarin, in patients with AF. Each trial was approved by the governing institutional review board or ethics committee at each site, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. COMBINE AF is registered with PROSPERO (CRD42020178771), and the current subgroup analysis of BMI and BW was prespecified. Because of data privacy restrictions, data from COMBINE AF are unable to be shared outside of the members of the COMBINE AF executive committee and their institutions.  

The primary analysis was restricted to standard-dose (SD) NOAC treatment, which was defined as any of the following: 150 mg of dabigatran twice daily; $20\mathsf{mg}$ of rivaroxaban (or 15 mg if meeting dose-reduction criteria) daily, $5~\mathsf{mg}$ of apixaban (or $2.5\mathrm{~mg}$ if meeting dose-reduction criteria) twice daily, or 60 mg of edoxaban (or $30~\mathrm{mg}$ if meeting dose-reduction criteria) daily. Because lower-dose (LD) NOACs (110 mg of dabigatran twice daily or 30/15 mg of edoxaban daily) are not approved globally for clinical use in patients with AF, these were separately evaluated in a sensitivity analysis.  

# Outcomes  

Independent clinical event committees for each trial adjudicated clinical outcomes using similar definitions as described within trial-specific clinical event committee charters. The primary efficacy outcome was the composite of any stroke (ischemic, hemorrhagic, or other) or systemic embolic event (stroke/SEE). The primary safety outcome was major bleeding as defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis.22 Key secondary end points for this analysis included ischemic stroke/SEE, intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), death and the net clinical outcome (NCO) composite of stroke/ SEE, International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis major bleeding, and death.  

The intention-to-treat population was analyzed for efficacy outcomes (stroke/SEE, ischemic stroke/SEE, or death). For safety and composite outcomes, the safety population was used as defined by each of the individual trials, which typically included patients receiving at least one dose of study drug who were followed for events occurring between the time of treatment initiation and up to 2 to 3 days after discontinuation of the study drug.  

For the exploratory analysis of mode of death, trial-specific death categories were reviewed by 3 of the authors (S.M.P, C.T.R, and R.P.G.) and harmonized after achieving consensus.  

# Statistical Analyses  

Outcomes comparing NOACs versus warfarin were examined across BMI, which was analyzed both categorically and continuously. For the former, BMI was categorized using commonly accepted thresholds established by the World Health Organization: $^{23}<18.5$ (underweight), 18.5 to ${<}25$ (normal), 25 to ${\it\Omega}_{<30}$ (overweight), 30 to ${<}35$ (class I obesity), 35 to ${<}40$ (class II obesity), and ${\ge}40$ (class III obesity) $\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ . Outcomes were also examined across BW as a continuous variable, with an additional analysis performed for those at low BW $(<50~\mathsf{kg})$ .  

All outcomes were censored at 32 months because of differential follow-up time across trials, with a median follow-up duration (25th to 75th percentile) of 26.6 (18.9 to 32) months. A Cox proportional hazards model stratified by trial to account for between-trial heterogeneity was used to examine each outcome across both BMI and BW, with interaction terms for BMI (or BW) and treatment allocation. The Cox proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, which demonstrated time independence of each outcome. The following covariates for model adjustment were selected a priori based on their established associations with thromboembolism24 and bleeding:25 age (assessed continuously), sex, creatinine clearance (assessed continuously), hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, previous heart failure, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack, aspirin use, thienopyridine use, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory use. Additional covariates were selected using backward selection applied to further identify factors independently associated with death in this data set: race, region, AF type, smoking status (current versus remote or none), previous myocardial infarction, presence of valvular disease, insulin use, beta-blocker use, and previous history of falls. All covariate modeling assumptions, including assessment for collinearity, were verified.  

Between-trial heterogeneity of treatment effect across categories of BMI was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic, Higgins and Thompson I2, and the $\uptau^{2}$ measures. Restricted cubic splines were constructed using 3 to 5 knots to display both the event probabilities by treatment allocation and hazard ratios for each outcome across continuous BMI and BW allowing for nonlinearity. Sensitivity analyses were performed stratifying by sex and excluding patients of non-White race. Hazard ratios with corresponding $95\%$ CIs were reported for the categorical analyses of BMI. All event rates were reported as annualized rates. All P values were 2-sided, and values ${<}0.05$ were considered statistically significant. Linear trend testing was performed to assess the interaction of treatment effect (adjusted hazard ratios $[\mathsf{HR}_{\mathsf{adi}}])$ across the ordinal BMI groups, with $P-$ values for trend $(P_{\mathrm{trend}})$ values ${<}0.05$ considered statistically significant for heterogeneity of treatment effect. Statistical computations were performed with SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).  

# RESULTS  

# Baseline Characteristics  

Among 58 464 patients, the median BMI was 28.3 (interquartile range, 25.2–32.2) $\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ (Figure S1A). In view of the small sample size $(\mathfrak{n}{=}598.$ ) of patients with a $3\mathsf{M}|<18.5~\mathrm{kg/m^{2}}$ , these patients are excluded from the primary analyses and are presented separately. Of the remaining 57 866 patients with a B $\mathsf{M}|\geq18.5~\mathsf{kg}/$ $\mathsf{m}^{2}$ , 22 251 $(38.5\%)$ were obese $(\mathsf{BMI}\geq30\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2})$ , and 2902 $(5.0\%)$ ) had class III obesity $(\mathsf{BM}|\geq40~\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2})$ . $\mathsf{Pa-}$ tients with a higher BMI were younger and more likely to be of White (compared with Asian) race, with a greater prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and previous heart failure, and with a lower prevalence of previous stroke or transient ischemic attack (Table). In addition, those with a lower BMI were more likely to receive a dose-reduced NOAC. No differences in baseline characteristics were observed when further stratifying by randomized treatment (Table S1).  

# Event Probabilities for Outcomes Across BMI for Warfarin  

For patients randomized to warfarin, the probability of stroke/SEE, ischemic stroke/SEE, major bleeding, ICH, the NCO, and death all decreased monotonically with increasing BMI (Figure 1A through 1F).  

# Event Probabilities for Outcomes Across BMI for SD NOACs  

For patients randomized to SD NOACs, the probability of stroke/SEE and ischemic stroke/SEE decreased monotonically with increasing BMI (Figure 1A and 1B). However, in contrast with the relationship observed in warfarin-treated patients, the probability of major bleeding did not differ markedly across BMI for patients randomized to SD NOACs (Figure 1C), whereas ICH decreased monotonically with increasing BMI (Figure 1D). A U-shaped relationship was observed for the NCO and death, with increasing event probabilities for each outcome observed for patients at either end of the BMI range compared with those with a BMI between 30 and $35~\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ (Figure 1E and 1F).

SD NOACs significantly reduced major bleeding overall versus warfarin (annualized rate, $2.95\%$ versus $3.44\%$ ; $\mathsf{HR}_{\mathsf{adj}}$ 0.88 $[95\%$ CI, 0.82–0.94]; $P\mathrm{<}0.001$ ), although this benefit was primarily observed at a lower BMI $(P_{\mathrm{trend}}$ 0.003; Figure 2) because of a progressive decline in the rate of major bleeding with warfarin at a higher BMI. ICH was markedly reduced with SD NOACs versus warfarin overall $.0.36\%$ versus $0.79\%$ ; $\mathsf{HR}_{\mathrm{adj}}$ 0.45 $[95\%$ CI, 0.37–0.54]; $P\mathrm{<}0.001$ ), with a consistent treatment effect across all BMI categories $(P_{\mathrm{trend}}0.72$ ; Figure 2).  

SD NOACs reduced the NCO versus warfarin overall (annualized rate $7.11\%$ versus $7.83\%$ ; $\mathsf{HR}_{\mathsf{adj}}$ 0.91 $[95\%$ CI, 0.87–0.95]; $P{<}0.001$ ), but with effect modification across BMI $(P_{\mathrm{{trend}}}\:0.001\$ ) such that this benefit did not persist in those at a higher BMI (Figure 2). Similarly, SD NOACs reduced death versus warfarin overall (annualized rate, $3.84\%$ versus $4.17\%$ ; $\mathsf{HR}_{\mathrm{adj}}$ 0.91 $[95\%$ CI, $0.86\substack{-0.97}]$ ; $_{P=0.003}$ ), but without a consistent benefit in those at a higher BMI (Figure 2). Each of these findings was qualitatively similar when BMI was analyzed as a continuous variable (Figure S2) and when further stratified by sex (Figure S3) or when excluding those of non-White race (Figure S4).  

There was evidence of moderate between-trial heterogeneity for each outcome, particularly in the normal BMI group (18.5 to ${<}25.0~\mathrm{kg/m^{2}},$ ), except for death, which had little between-trial heterogeneity (Table S2).  

# Causes of Death by Treatment Across BMI  

Given heterogeneity in treatment effect for death across BMI, we further explored the causes of death by treatment in each BMI group (Table S3). No differences between treatments were observed for vascular deaths or fatal bleeding in the BMI $\geq40\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ group; however, there were greater rates of heart failure–related and sudden deaths with SD NOACs versus warfarin in patients with class III obesity (Table S3).  

# Outcomes and Treatment Effect Across BW  

The median BW was 81.0 (interquartile range, 70.0–94.3) kg (Figure S1B). The relationship between BW and clinical outcomes for warfarin- and SD NOAC–treated patients was similar to that observed across BMI (Figure S5). The treatment effect of SD NOACs versus warfarin was consistent across BW for stroke/SEE, ischemic stroke/SEE, and ICH (treatment $P_{\mathrm{interaction}}$ 0.26, 0.41, and 0.87 respectively; Figure S6). However, as observed across BMI, there was attenuation of the safety benefit with SD NOACs versus warfarin for major bleeding at a higher BW $(P_{\mathrm{interaction}}$ 0.04). As well, there were attenuations of the benefits for SD NOACs relative to warfarin for the NCO and death at higher BW $(P_{\mathrm{{interaction}}}<0.001\$ and 0.005, respectively; Figure S6).  

# Comparison of LD NOACs to SD NOACs and Warfarin Across BMI  

LD NOACs versus warfarin resulted in greater risk for ischemic stroke/SEE, lower risk for major bleeding and ICH, and lower risk for the NCO and death with consistent treatment effects across BMI groups (Figure S7).  

Comparing SD NOACs versus LD NOACs, SD NOACs reduced stroke/SEE and ischemic stroke/SEE but increased major bleeding and ICH; these results were consistent across BMI (Figure S8). # Treatment Effect of SD NOACs Versus Warfarin at Low BMI $(<18.5~\mathrm{kg/m^{2}})$ or BW $(<50~\mathsf{kg})$ )  

Compared with the primary analysis population (BMI $\geq18.5~\mathrm{kg/m^{2}},$ ), those with a $\mathsf{BMI}<18.5~\mathrm{kg/m^{2}}$ $\scriptstyle(\nVDash598.$ ) had markedly different baseline characteristics (greater age, more likely to be of Asian compared with White race, a greater prevalence of previous stroke or transient ischemic attack, and lower creatinine clearance), with a greater proportion receiving dose-reduced NOACs (Table S4). Annualized event rates were higher for each outcome irrespective of treatment compared with the primary analysis population (Figure S9). No significant differences were observed with SD NOACs versus warfarin for any of the 6 outcomes in underweight patients, albeit in the context of overall small numbers of events (Figure S9).  

Similarly, patients with a BW ${<}50~\mathsf{kg}$ $(\mathsf{n}{=}1240)$ had higher annualized event rates for each outcome compared with the overall population (Figure S10). In these patients, SD NOACs versus warfarin reduced stroke/ SEE and ICH, without significant differences observed for ischemic stroke/SEE, major bleeding, the NCO, or death (Figure S10).  

# DISCUSSION  

In this patient-level meta-analysis of the 4 pivotal randomized trials of NOACs versus warfarin in 58 464 patients with AF, we found general consistency in the treatment effect of SD NOACs versus warfarin for prevention of stroke/SEE across the spectrum of BMI and BW. We also observed an overall reduction in major bleeding with SD NOACs versus warfarin, with evidence of treatment heterogeneity such that there was an attenuation of this benefit for patients at a higher BMI or BW. Moreover, our findings illustrate that the marked reduction in ICH, which is the established primary safety benefit of SD NOACs over warfarin,26 is preserved across the spectrum of BMI and BW including in underweight patients $(\mathrm{BM}|<18.5~\mathrm{kg/m^{2}}$ or BW ${\tt<}50~{\ttkg}.$ ) traditionally felt to be at high risk. However, we observed heterogeneity of treatment effect for the NCO composite and for death across BMI and BW, with uncertainty of treatment benefit of SD NOACs over warfarin for these outcomes in patients with class III obesity.  

# Treatment Effect Across Body Mass: Previous Findings and Current Results  

Several previous analyses from the individual randomized trials have examined heterogeneity of treatment effect for NOACs versus warfarin across BMI or BW. In RE-LY, no differences were observed in the risk of stroke/SEE with randomization to dabigatran versus warfarin in subgroups of obese patients (defined as $\geq100~\mathsf{kg},$ ) compared with nonobese patients.18 In ARISTOTLE, the efficacy of apixaban for stroke/SEE and ischemic stroke/SEE appeared to be consistent across BMI groups, albeit with an attenuation in the benefit with respect to major bleeding for those at a higher BMI $(\ge35$ $\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2})$ .5 In ROCKET AF, the treatment effect of rivaroxaban versus warfarin on stroke/SEE was consistent across body mass up to a BMI of ${\geq}30~\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ ; however, there appeared to be a nonsignificant but numeric increase in ischemic strokes, as well as an increase in major and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding at a higher BMI with rivaroxaban $(P_{\mathrm{interaction}}0.11$ and 0.01, respectively).15 Although an analysis of the ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 trial demonstrated no differences for stroke/SEE or major bleeding by BMI, there appeared to be a signal for a greater risk of ischemic stroke/SEE with the higher-dose regimen of edoxaban versus warfarin at a BMI $\geq40\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ , albeit in the context of a very low number of events in both treatment arms (8 versus 2).12 Notably, analyses of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic data from ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 demonstrated no differences in peak or trough edoxaban concentrations or in inhibition of endogenous anti–factor Xa activity across either BMI or BW; thus, the excess in thrombotic events did not appear to be a result of subtherapeutic edoxaban concentration or diminished anticoagulant effect.12,13  

It is important to note that the numbers of patients at the extremes of BMI or BW in these previous individual trial analyses are modest $(<200$ and $<1100$ patients in any individual trial with a $\mathsf{BM}|<18.5\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ and ${\geq}40\mathsf{kg}/$ $\mathsf{m}^{2}$ , respectively), with very few events particularly at the higher range of obesity, limiting power to detect treatment differences across BMI or BW. Our study expands on these previous reports by pooling individual patient data across all 4 trials, exploring results at the extremes of BMI and BW with patient-level risk adjustment across the range of body mass while addressing between-trial heterogeneity using a stratified model. By leveraging the greater sample size and number of events, we were able to examine heterogeneity of treatment effect with greater power and granularity, particularly at the extremes. Reconciling the findings from these previous trial-specific analyses, we found general consistency for the treatment effect of SD NOACs for both stroke/SEE and ischemic stroke/SEE, whereas the reduction in major bleeding with SD NOACs was attenuated for patients at greater obesity severity despite a robust reduction in ICH across the full spectrum of BMI and BW.  

We observed heterogeneity of treatment effect for the NCO composite across BMI and BW, a finding that was driven primarily by an increase in death in those with class III obesity randomized to SD NOACs versus warfarin, coupled with an attenuation of the reduction in major bleeding with SD NOACs relative to warfarin in this high BMI group. It is important to note that the differences in death did not appear to be driven by ischemic events or fatal bleeding, which are the 2 outcomes most modifiable by anticoagulation. Moreover, the findings for the SD NOAC versus LD NOAC comparison do not support the hypothesis that the adverse signals observed with SD NOACs in class III obesity were a result of reduced intensity of anticoagulation in these patients. Hence, these findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly given the low number of events among patients with class III obesity and the multiple subgroups/outcomes tested.  

It is also plausible that these findings may be driven by an alternative mechanism that is independent of ischemic and bleeding risk altogether. For example, warfarin may suppress levels of vitamin- $\cdot\mathsf{K}$ dependent proteins such as gas6 (growth-arrest-specific 6), which is elevated in heart failure27,28 and linked to the development of obesity.29 Thus, warfarin may exert other protective roles in those at a higher BMI or BW independent of its anticoagulant effects on ischemic or bleeding events.27  

# Clinical Implications  

Our analyses have important implications for clinical practice. Contemporary guidelines and consensus statements remain reserved in their recommendation of NOAC use in patients who are underweight or have greater obesity severity. The 2021 International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis guidance recommends the use of vitamin- $\cdot\mathsf{K}$ antagonists over NOACs in patients with a BMI $\geq40\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ or $\mathsf{BW}\geq120\mathsf{kg.}^{\sharp}$ The 2021 European Heart Rhythm Association practical guidance recommends caution with use of NOACs in patients with class III obesity, noting a lack of robust data for those at a $\mathsf{BMI}\geq40\mathsf{kg/m^{2}}$ , and recommends measuring NOAC plasma levels in patients with BMI ${\geq}50~\mathsf{kg}/\mathsf{m}^{2}$ if used instead of a vitamin- $\cdot\mathsf{K}$ antagonist.9 Moreover, the European Heart Rhythm Association guidance further notes that special care is needed with anticoagulation in patients with very low weight $(<50$ kg), and that measurement of trough NOAC levels may be considered in this population to assess for accumulation of drug.9  

The expected “normal” range of drug levels for each NOAC, derived primarily from descriptive post hoc reports of trial substudies, varies by dose and indication. Furthermore, data supporting the applicability of NOAC levels to guide dosing are lacking,30 with society guidelines noting that evidence-based recommendations cannot be given about dose reduction or escalation in response NOAC level measurements. Thus, our results offer clinicians a more comprehensive overview of the clinical efficacy and safety of NOACs in these patient populations to guide decision-making.  

# Prognostic Associations Between Body Mass and AF Outcomes  

Several previous studies have examined the prognostic associations between AF outcomes and BMI. Previous analyses from randomized trials of patients with AF have demonstrated an inverse relationship between BMI and the occurrence of stroke/SEE and major bleeding,5,31 whereas observational registries have demonstrated either an inverse or U-shaped relationship with AF outcomes across BMI.32,33 These associations appear paradoxical given the progressively greater burden of cardiometabolic risk factors with increasing BMI (eg, diabetes), which has been seen across each of these analyses, a finding that has been termed the “obesity paradox” in AF.5,31 Although still significant after multivariable adjustment, these inverse relationships may be manifestations of unmeasured differences in patient characteristics, which, by themselves, may be independently associated with greater risk.  

It is important to note that our findings speak to the complexity of BMI as a metric, which may be a surrogate for many patient characteristics beyond adiposity. For example, underweight patients not only had a lower BMI or BW but were more than a decade older, with a markedly greater prevalence of previous stroke compared with those with class III obesity $26\%$ versus $11\%$ ). Therefore, the prognostic associations between BMI and AF outcomes not only are reflective of the pharmacokinetic implications of renal clearance and volume of distribution for a specific anticoagulant but also encapsulate the many characteristics that affect a patient’s baseline risk for AF outcomes. Multiple studies have shown previous stroke and older age to be the most important predictors of stroke risk in patients with AF.34–36 In addition, older age is also independently associated with an increased risk of bleeding, perhaps driven by an accumulation of comorbidities and treatments that may predispose to bleeding (eg, peptic ulcer disease, nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drug use, need for concomitant antiplatelet therapy, or chronic kidney disease).37 Furthermore, it is also increasingly appreciated that BMI is an imperfect metric of obesity, as 2 patients with the same BMI may have marked differences in visceral and peripheral adiposity compared with lean mass, in addition to cardiometabolic risk factors and pre-existing disease.  

Although we performed risk adjustment for multiple potential confounding factors including age, many factors (eg, sarcopenia, frailty, cardiac cachexia, or occult malignancy) that are difficult to capture systematically in large, randomized trials, and hence might be unaccounted for, may explain the differences resulting in this paradoxical relationship.  

# Limitations  

Patients enrolled in each of the 4 trials reflect trial-specific entry criteria. Therefore, results may not be generalizable to an unselected group of patients with AF encountered in clinical practice. Most patients enrolled across the individual trials were of White race, and as such, we are unable to examine consistency of these findings across other races. In addition, although we used a stratified Cox model to account for between-trial heterogeneity, the pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics of each NOAC (eg, hepatic metabolism, renal excretion, or volume of distribution) may differ between drugs and further across BMI and BW; thus, NOAC-specific outcomes across BMI and BW may differ by the specific NOAC used. The findings particularly in underweight patients reflect appropriate dose reduction for 3 of the 4 NOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban), as based on commercial labeling. Moreover, despite having ${\tt>}2900$ patients with class III obesity in the SD NOAC versus warfarin comparison in this pooled meta-analysis, this group had the lowest event rate for clinical outcomes, thereby limiting the power to detect differences in this BMI and BW group. Furthermore, we are unable to account for differences in postrandomization treatments that may have influenced outcomes. Finally, we did not adjust for multiple hypothesis testing, which may increase the likelihood of type I error.  

# CONCLUSIONS  

In a pooled individual patient-level meta-analysis of the 4 pivotal trials of NOACs versus warfarin in 58 464 patients with AF, we observed a generally consistent treatment effect of SD NOACs for stroke/SEE across the spectrum of BMI and BW compared with warfarin, with a reduction in major bleeding primarily among patients with lower BMI or BW. Moreover, we found a marked and consistent reduction in ICH across the full spectrum of BMI and BW with SD NOACs versus warfarin, including the group of underweight patients traditionally felt to be at increased risk of bleeding. However, there remains uncertainty about the treatment effect of SD NOACs versus warfarin for the NCO and all-cause death among patients with greater obesity severity. Collectively, these new analyses help inform selection of anticoagulants in patients with AF across the spectrum of BMI and BW.
