Introduction: Golden standard of acute stroke treatment is recanalisation therapy. However, opening the occluded blood vessel sometimes does not show the expected clinical result or leads to haemorrhagic complications. As neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity play an important role in the pathophysiology of stroke, neuroprotective agents might preserve brain tissue after futile recanalisation.  

Patients and methods: After recanalisation therapy and not later than $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after symptoms onset, patients with initial NIHSS of $\geq8$ were assigned to the investigational and control group. The investigational group received intravenous Cerebrolysin as add-on therapy. The primary objective was to assess the clinical efficacy of Cere­ brolysin. The secondary objective was to investigate its effect on haemorrhagic transition and to confirm its safety profile.  

Results: Baseline characteristics of patients showed no significant differences between the two groups. No dif­ ference could be detected between the two groups in the mRS scale though the Cerebrolysin group showed descriptive superiority over the control group. We found a statistically significant difference considering hae­ morrhagic transition and mortality rate in favour of the Cerebrolysin group.  

Discussion: The multimodal neurotrophic agent Cerebrolysin holds promise to impact on the late consequences of a reperfusion syndrome. Its influence on reducing neuroinflammation, promoting neuronal cell viability and neurogenesis as well as the stabilising effect on the blood-brain barrier suggests a protective effect on the neurovascular unit even when no recanalisation occurs. We confirmed the excellent safety profile of Cerebrolysin.  

Conclusion: Cerebrolysin as add-on therapy might be beneficial and safe for patients with acute stroke in terms of lowering risk for haemorrhagic complications after recanalisation therapy.  



# 1. Introduction  

Recanalisation therapy is the golden treatment standard for acute ischaemic stroke. Since approval, intravenous thrombolysis with re­ combinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) is a routine systemic therapy for selected cases and for the last five years, it shares the first choice therapy together with thrombectomy for large vessel occlusions of the anterior circulation [1,2]. Still, in spite of good recanalisation rates, some of the patients do not benefit from either method alone or in combination. Patients treated with rtPA still show significantly more symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage in the first $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after treatment compared to patients who were not thrombolysed [3] and even suc­ cessful recanalisation of occluded vessels does not result in effective reperfusion in nearly half of the cases [4,5]. Even more, early recanal­ isation, a precondition for good outcome, can still result in futile reperfusion or reperfusion injury. Even after positive results from clin­ ical studies in endovascular recanalisation procedures, recanalisation fails in at least $20\%$ of patients and futile recanalisation was present in nearly $60\%$ of patients [6]. Most of those patients do not clinically improve [7,8]. Attempts to achieve neuroprotection for those patients are without significant clinical success until now [9,10]. As neuro­ inflammation plays an important role in ischaemic stroke leading to neurotoxicity and blood-brain barrier disruption which results in a higher risk for haemorrhagic transition in ischaemic tissue especially after treatment with rtPA, a drug which could influence these mecha­ nisms could be beneficial in patients with acute stroke after futile recanalisation [9–11]. Cerebrolysin, a neuropeptide preparation with proven in-vitro neurotrophic and neuroprotective effects, has still to be confirmed in the clinical setting of acute stroke. While several studies have shown a positive clinical effect and benefit-risk ratio [12], a meta-analysis of seven randomised clinical trials indicated with moderate-quality evidence, that there is little or no beneficial effect of Cerebrolysin on preventing all-cause death in acute ischemic stroke and a potential increase in non-fatal serious adverse events while the total number of serious adverse events was similar to placebo [13]. However, the first clinical trial investigating a combination of Cerebrolysin with thrombolytic therapy using rt-PA showed a good safety profile of the drug as well as a favourable response in neurological outcome (measured with National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale) as compared to rt-PA alone during the treatment period [14]. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis of nine ischemic stroke trials demonstrated a statistically significant effect of Cerebrolysin in improving functional outcome measured with the mRS at day 90 post stroke in a subgroup of patients with moderate to severe stroke [12]. In our prospective study, we compared the clinical outcome and the rate of haemorrhagic compli­ cations in a group of acute stroke patients after futile recanalisation therapy who were treated with Cerebrolysin in comparison to a group of matched stroke patients treated with standard therapy alone in order to extend the previous findings.  



# 2. Material and methods  

In this prospective, open-label study we included acute stroke pa­ tients $\geq18$ years of age, with a diagnosis of acute ischaemic stroke and an initial National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) of $\geq8$ suitable for recanalisation therapy. Recanalisation therapy was per­ formed according to international and national guidelines. All patients received intravenous thrombolytic therapy (Alteplase $0.9\mathrm{mg/kg}$ maximal dosage $90\mathrm{mg}$ , $10\%$ of the drug given in bolus and the rest in 60 min of intravenous injection) if they were suitable for it, followed by thrombectomy if large vessel occlusion has been confirmed by angiog­ raphy. All patients were treated in the Neurological Intensive Care Unit of the University Hospital of Zagreb by dedicated stroke intervention­ alists and stroke neurologists. Patients were recruited from January 2018 until January 2019 and followed-up for at least 12 months. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Zagreb prior to the start of the recruitment and every patient or his legal representative signed an informed consent for the study. After recanal­ isation therapy and not later than $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after symptoms onset, patients were assigned to the investigational and control group. Patients were included successively in a 1:1 ratio. Inclusion criteria were diagnosis of acute ischaemic stroke with initial $\mathrm{NIHSS}\geq8$ and indication for thrombolytic therapy, thrombectomy or both, as well as futile early recanalisation. Futile recanalisation was defined by either neuroimaging criteria or clinical worsening. Neuroimaging criteria for no recanalisa­ tion were TICI 2a or less on control angiography or an infarct zone corresponding to the occluded artery on follow-up computed tomogra­ phy. Clinical criteria for futile recanalisation were no improvement or worsening of the NIHSS by $\geq2$ points in the first $^{12\mathrm{~h~}}$ after recanali­ sation therapy. The investigational group received intravenous Cere­ brolysin $\mathrm{30ml}$ i.v. during $60\mathrm{min/day})$ for a minimum of 14 and maximum of 21 days, starting no later than $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after symptoms onset. Exclusion criteria were premorbid modified Rankin scale (mRS) of $\geq2$ renal or hepatic insufficiency, acute infections, pregnancy, known al­ lergy against the investigational drug or participation in another clinical trial. Treatment and care for the patients in the study was according to standard procedures for stroke care. Patients age, sex, stroke severity, recanalisation treatment modality and stroke etiology were analysed and compared. Efficacy of Cerebrolysin treatment was assessed using the NIHSS on Day 7 after stroke onset and the mRS at discharge, as well as 3 and 12 months after stroke onset. The assessment was performed by an independent rater trained in stroke assessment scales. Safety was assessed by close monitoring of clinical status, adverse events and routine laboratory tests. Neuroimaging follow-up included a noncontrast computed tomography of the brain for all patients, no later than $24\mathrm{~h~}$ after recanalisation treatment, brain CT or MRI on day 7 after admission, and a control brain CT or MRI 6–12 months after stroke.  

The primary objective of the trial was to assess the clinical efficacy of Cerebrolysin as an add-on therapy in patients with moderate or severe stroke after futile recanalisation.  

As a secondary objective of our study we looked at the rate of hae­ morrhagic transformation after recanalisation therapy which were defined as either symptomatic intracerebral haematoma (sICH), or asymptomatic intracerebral haematoma (aICH) presented as haemor­ rhagic transformation demonstrated by neuroimaging (native computed tomography of the brain) performed after $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and at 7th day after symptoms onset.  



# 2.1. Statistical analysis  

Results of both groups were compared and analysed using appro­ priate descriptive statistics. Statistical analysis was performed with the IBM SPSS Statistics 25. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine if the data had a normal distribution. Differences in the dis­ tribution of categorical variables were determined with the $\chi2$ test. For the analysis of the differences in the quantitative variables, parametric (independent t-test) and non-parametric (Mann-Whitney test) statistical methods were used. The level of statistical significance was set at $\mathsf{p}<$ 0.05, two-sided.  



# 3. Results  

Two groups of patients were included and compared in the study. Group 1 (investigational group) was treated with intravenous Cere­ brolysin in addition to standard care after recanalisation therapy, and group 2 (control group) received standard care after recanalisation therapy alone. The Cerebrolysin group consisted of 23 patients (mean age $76.0\pm9.8$ , 9 females), and the control group consisted of 21 patients (mean age $72.7\pm10.6$ , 11 females). There was no statistically signifi­ cant difference in sex and age between the two groups $(\mathtt{p}=0.378$ and p $=0.296$ , respectively).  

Baseline characteristics showed no significant differences between the two groups. Likewise, treatment modalities were identical with 1 patient in each group receiving thrombectomy alone, 15 patients in the Cerebrolysin and 16 patients in the control group receiving thrombolysis alone and 7 versus 4 patients receiving thrombolysis followed by thrombectomy. All patients in the study had an anterior circulation stroke. In addition, the specific stroke etiology listed in the Table 1 was examined for all patients in the study. Also. there was no statistically significant difference between groups in the recanalisation rate measured by the Thrombolysis In Cerebral Infarction score (TICI score).  

Group comparison of the median value of NIHSS scale at three time points – baseline NIHSS, as well as NIHSS $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and seven days after stroke is presented in Table 2. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in the initial NIHSS or NIHSS scale at any time point although the Cerebrolysin group showed a trend for a better NIHSS on day 7 with a median of 6 compared to 9 in the control group.  

Etiology of stroke slightly differed between the groups, with a higher number of cardioembolic strokes in the Cerebrolysin group, while the control group accounted for more strokes due to large artery athero­ sclerosis as well as a higher number of small artery occlusion strokes (lacunar strokes). This difference, however, was not statistically significant.

# 4. Discussion  



# 4.1. Efficacy of Cerebrolysin  

In our study, we observed a clear trend for a better long-term outcome at month 12 as evidenced by the mRS score with $70\%$ of pa­ tients having a favourable outcome in the Cerebrolysin group versus only $48\%$ in the control group. This is certainly a remarkable finding given the relatively small number of patients in our study and is in concordance with most previously published studies and reviews on Cerebrolysin [12–16]. However, functional outcome of our patients expressed in mRS, did not show any statistically significant difference between the groups at 90 days. This fact might suggest also that some other factors influenced final outcome in a long-term follow up or may be attributed to the relatively small number of patients in our study. Furthermore, in our study we included moderate to moderately severe patients [17,18] which may also have contributed to non-significant results as previous studies have suggested that treatment effects in stroke patients are more clearly detectable in more severe patients $({\mathrm{NIHSS}}>12)$ ) [12,14].  

Importantly, long-term follow-up demonstrated a significant survival benefit for patients treated with Cerebrolysin. This finding is in concordance with a meta-analyses of nine studies with Cerebrolysin in acute stroke [12] but contrasts the results of another meta-analyses of seven studies with Cerebrolysin which looked specifically at all-cause death within 90 days after stroke [13]. Data from our study on all-cause death over a time period of one year suggests just the opposite. Data from our study on all-cause death over a time period of one year suggests just the opposite. Given the differences in stroke etiology between the groups, we can, however, not exclude that comorbidities associated with specific types of stroke (which may cause death not connected with stroke itself) may have had an influence on the favourable mortality outcome in our study. Therefore, inspite the fact that the difference in stroke etiology did not reach statistical significance between our groups of patients, heterogeneity of the groups in future studies should be avoided to the extend possible.  

The late and long-term positive effect of Cerebrolysin in our patient cohort may be attributed to its neurotrophic and neuroplastic effects such as the stimulation of neurogenesis and oligodendrogenesis which might potentially trigger enhancement of long-term functional recovery [19].  

Haemorrhagic transformation and reperfusion injury after recanali­ sation can represent one of the worst complications after successful recanalisation, with a direct influence on patient outcome. Symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage occurs in about $4\mathrm{-}6\%$ of patients after any recanalisation therapy, and fatal clinical outcome is reported in more than $40\%$ of those cases [20,21].  

Even asymptomatic intracranial haemorrhage, often discovered only by later control neuroimaging has been reported to worsen long-term outcomes. Some recent studies did point to the negative impact of even asymptomatic ICH after thrombectomy on the final outcome [20]. As we have clearly shown in our study, Cerebrolysin treated patients had a significantly lower rate of haemorrhagic complications. The occur­ rence of symptomatic intracerebral haematoma was identical $(4.3\%$ vs $4.8\%)$ in both groups and occurred few hours after thrombolytic ther­ apy, before the first dosage of Cerebrolysin was given. Asymptomatic ICH on the contrary, occurred more often in the control group ( $38\%$ vs $13\%$ in the Cerebrolysin group). The reduction of the number of aICH might also explain the positive trend in the 12-month functional outcome (mRS) and the statistically significant reduction of mortality in the Cerebrolysin group. Finally, we can also speculate that the Cere­ brolysin group had less haemorrhagic transformation and better outcome despite of having higher risk of haemorrhagic transformation and worse outcome due to a higher rate of patients with cardioembolic stroke [22].  

It remains to be clarified whether acute haemorrhagic complications of thrombolytic therapy and delayed haemorrhagic transition due to a reperfusion syndrome share the same pathophysiological mechanism. In all likelihood they do have at least some pathophysiological mechanisms in common, such as increased blood brain barrier permeability, presence of post-ischaemic necrotic blood vessel walls as well as prolonged ischaemia [20]. Thus, multimodal neuroprotective / neurotrophic agents such as Cerebrolysin may reduce the consequences of a reperfu­ sion syndrome. Their influence on reducing neuroinflammation, pro­ moting neuronal cell viability and neurogenesis as well as the stabilising effect on the blood-brain barrier suggest that such agents also have a neuroprotective effect when no recanalisation occurs.  



# 4.2. Safety of Cerebrolysin  

Finally, one of the goals of our study was to confirm the safety of Cerebrolysin administered to patients with moderate and severe stroke. In our study group we did not experience any adverse event which we related to the investigational drug Cerebrolysin. This is in line with previously published results, including the meta analysis of nine rand­ omised studies from Bornstein et al., a published safety profile analysis from Thome and Doppler as well as a published review from Ziganshina [12,13,23,24].  



# 5. Strengths and weaknesses  

Our study certainly has several weaknesses. It was designed as an unblinded pilot study to provide preliminary data for a hypothesis generation. Moreover, our study is underpowered for the primary effi­ cacy outcome due to the small number of patients. Also, in our analysis we were not able to control for risk factors for the development of ICH after stroke, such as uncontrolled hypertension, poor collaterals and/or presence of microbleeds [18,19], due to the small patient numbers.  

Considering strengths, to our knowledge, this is the first prospective study which evaluated the effects of a neuroprotective compound in patients with moderate and severe acute stroke after futile recanalisa­ tion based on clinical evaluation and the rate of haemorrhagic trans­ formation after recanalisation therapy over 12 months. It provides preliminary evidence that neuroprotective add-on treatment with Cer­ ebrolysin may contribute to a better long-term clinical outcome and a reduction of mortality.  



# 6. Conclusion  

The idea of neuroprotection has been investigated for many years and a very extensive list of in-vitro neuroprotective agents have been studied. However, as clear clinical success failed, neuroprotection still remains a clinical challenge. Cerebrolysin as a multimodal neuro­ protective and neurorestorative agent has already shown some prom­ ising results in stroke patients with respect to functional outcome. In our study, Cerebrolysin as an add-on therapy for patients with moderate and severe stroke proved to be beneficial in preventing haemorrhagic transformation after futile recanalisation and showed clear reduction in mortality in the investigational group of patients. Finally, we can also argue whether those two effects are in causal connection, which is one of the remaining questions for future investigations.
