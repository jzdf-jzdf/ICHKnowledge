Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is defined by the rupture of a cerebral blood vessel and the entry of blood into the brain parenchyma. With a global incidence of around 3.5 million, ICH accounts for almost $30\%$ of all new strokes worldwide. It is also the deadliest form of acute stroke and survivors are at risk of poor functional outcome. The pathophysiology of ICH is a dynamic process with key stages occurring at successive times: vessel rupture and initial bleeding; hematoma expansion, mechanical mass effect and secondary brain injury (perihematomal edema). While deep perforating vasculopathy and cerebral amyloid angiopathy are responsible for $80~\%$ of ICH, a prompt diagnostic work-up, including advanced imaging is require to exclude a treatable cause. # 1. Introduction  

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is defined by the rupture of a cerebral blood vessel and the entry of blood into the brain parenchyma. Although ICH represents $20–30~\%$ of all acute strokes, the mortality and disability-adjusted life years lost for ICH is greater than that of ischemic stroke [1–3]. For decades, ICH remained devoided of specific treatment leading to a pessimistic mindset among clinicians, including early palliative care decision. Thanks to huge translational research efforts from bench to bedside, this view is now being chal­ lenged. This review summarizes the significant advances in our under­ standing of the pathophysiology, the causes, the prognosis and the management of ICH.  

# 2. Recent updates in the epidemiology of ICH  

In the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019, ICH constituted $27.9\%$ of all new strokes [3]. The worldwide global incidence of ICH was ${\sim}3.5$ million [2,3]. ICH has massive social and economic impacts. In 2019, an estimated ${\sim}70$ million DALYs lost (combination of years of life lost owing to premature mortality and years lived with disability) were due to ICH [3]. ICH is also the deadliest form of acute stroke. Between 1990 and 2019, ICH rose from the 9th to 4th leading cause of premature death, with an estimated 3 million individuals dying from ICH in 2019. Incidence and mortality disproportionately affect low-income countries (probably be due to a lack of public awareness of preventive measures and access to health care) [3]. Although risk of ICH increases with age [4], ICH can also affect young individuals with an annual incidence of ICH ranging from 2 to 5 per 100,000 individuals $<50$ years old [5]. All age-group combined, ICH might be more prevalent in men than in women, possibly due to a complex sex-based interaction between age, ethnicity and underlying risk factors [6,7]. Regarding ethnicity, the incidence of ICH among Asian individuals is roughly double than among Black or White individuals. Of note, Black and Hispanic individuals who suffered from an ICH, were 10 years younger than White individuals [1,8,9].  

# 3. Emerging concept in the pathophysiology: ICH, a multifaceted disease  

The pathophysiology of ICH is a complex and dynamic process with key stages occurring at successive times, but sharing a common mech­ anistic overlap: vessel rupture and initial bleeding; hematoma expan­ sion, mechanical mass effect and secondary brain injury (perihematomal edema).  

Regardless of the underlying cause, ICH begins with blood vessel rupture and extravasation of blood into the brain parenchyma, directly responsible for mechanical mass effect. In the following hours, one out of five patients will experience subsequent hematoma expansion which worsens the prognosis: a $3\mathrm{ml}$ increase in ICH volume triples the risk of death or disability [10,11]. The two main predictors of hematoma expansion are the use of antithrombotic drugs before ICH onset and a shorter delay between ICH onset and first brain imaging: the risk of expansion is highest during the first $^{3\mathrm{~h~}}$ and plateaus after 6 h [10]. The mechanisms underlying this expansion are based on the Fisher’s avalanche theory [12]. In this theory, the mass effect of the initial he­ matoma may tear surrounding arterioles by shearing, precipitating further bleeding and hematoma expansion [13,14]. After a few hours following ICH onset, the cellular component within the hematoma core starts to organize and assemble a clot responsible for a mechanical mass effect and larger hematomas will lead to raised intracranial pressure and herniation.  

Peri-hematomal edema (PHO) is a key contributor to ICH patho­ physiology and is used as a radiological biomarker of inflammation and secondary brain injury. It develops in the ensuing days after ICH onset and its growth is mainly mediated by neuroinflammation, including the activation of immune resident brain cells, leakage of the blood-brain barrier and recruitment of circulating systemic immune cells. This in­ flammatory response is triggered by initial bleeding and perpetuated by the blood products toxicity. In ICH, neuroinflammation exerts both deleterious (secondary brain tissue damages) and protective effects (hematoma resorption and tissue repair) [15,16]. However, these pro­ cesses are unbalanced with an exacerbated deleterious effect ultimately worsening the outcome: for every $1~\mathrm{ml}$ increase in absolute volume of PHO, the odds of a poor functional outcome increase by $3\%$ [17]. In a latter phase of the disease, scarification and fibrosis of the injured area can contribute to remodelling of brain tissue and the formation of chronic hydrocephalus [18].  

# 4. Better knowledge of prognosis, outcome and quality of life after ICH  

ICH is a dramatic condition. Less than a half of patients with ICH survive 1 year, and less than a third survive 5 years [1]. Most of deaths occur within the first 30 days and, among survivors, the cumulative survival rate at 10 years reaches $40\%$ [19]. Similar findings are observed in young ICH patients (18–55 years), with long-term mortality rate reaching $35~\%$ after a median follow-up of 8 years [20]. ICH sur­ vivors are exposed to a global functional decline that affect their quality of life. Among them, roughly half of them are dependent at 1 year after ICH [21]. Even in patients who initially have relatively preserved au­ tonomy $(\mathrm{mRS}0{-}3\$ at 6 months), functional decline continues over time (transition to mRS 4–5) in $25\%$ of them over the following decade [22]. Survivors are also at risk of cognitive impairments with various severity, including new onset dementia for one third of them [23]. This cognitive impairment affects several cognitive domains, such as language, mem­ ory, executive functions, processing speed and visuo-spatial abilities [24]. Neuropsychiatric symptoms, especially apathy and hyperactivity are also prominent, affecting more than half of 6-month ICH survivors [25]. More than $10\%$ of survivors will also develop epilepsy in the years and decades following ICH which affects their quality of life [26,27].  

Survivors of ICH face a high global vascular risk reaching a $\approx5\%$ risk of vascular (hemorragic and ischemic) events per year [28]. The risk of  

ICH recurrence ranged from $1.3~\%$ to $7.4~\%$ per year. This risk varies according to the underlying vessel disease: deep locations of ICH are predominantly caused by deep perforating vasculopathy (arterio­ losclerosis), while lobar ICH are mainly caused by cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). The risk of CAA related ICH is superior to the risk of deep perforating vasculopathy related ICH [21]. There is also a signifi­ cant burden of thromboembolic events. Ten percent of ICH survivors will experience an ischemic stroke five years after ICH [29]. This ischemic risk goes beyond the brain, with a high risk of extra-cerebral events, especially in patient with atrial fibrillation, that probably ex­ ceeds the overall hemorrhagic risk in the long term [29–31]. Impor­ tantly, the nature of incident events differs according to the presumed underlying vessel disease. Rate of ischemic events at five years is six times higher than the rate of hemorrhagic events in patients with deep perforating vasculopathy related ICH, suggesting deep perforating vas­ culopathy being part of a dynamic multisystemic disorder affecting the small vessels beyond the brain [32].  

# 4.1. ICH etiologies  

ICH is a heterogeneous condition with several underlying causes sharing distinct management, prognosis and secondary prevention strategies. In ${\sim}80~\%$ of cases [33], ICH involves a small arteriole that is affected by chronic cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) of two main origins: deep perforating vasculopathy (also termed hypertensive arte­ riopathy or arteriolosclerosis) or cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). In deep perforating vasculopathy related ICH, vessel wall fragility is related to lipohyalinosis changes and fibrinoid necrosis, which often occurs at bifurcation points in the lenticulostriate arterioles that become prone to rupture [34,35]. Bleeding generally occurs in deep structures (including basal ganglia, thalamus, deep white matter, and pons) but can also arise from lobar regions [36,37]. The main vascular risk factors for ICH caused by deep perforating vasculopathy are increasing age, hypertension, and excessive alcohol consumption [4]. In CAA patients, a weakening of cortical and leptomeningeal vessel walls by the deposition of $\upbeta$ -amyloid (mostlyAß40) leads to lobar hemorrhage. Although he­ reditary and iatrogenic forms of CAA exist [38,39], most cases are sporadic with growing prevalence in elderly individuals [40]. Two thirds of spontaneous ICH in patients ${>}70$ years are related to CAA [41]. Three main factors increase the risk of ICH in CAA: hypertension, a genetic predisposition (especially Apolipoproteinε2/4 status) and antithrombotic use [40]. Other clinical manifestations of CAA are cognitive impairment and transient focal neurological episodes but can also remain asymptomatic for years [42]. New diagnostic criteria for CAA have recently been proposed: the 2.0 Boston criteria. Probable CAA is defined as either the presence of two strictly lobar hemorrhagic lesions (ICH, microbleeds, or cortical superficial siderosis), or the presence of one strictly lobar hemorrhagic lesion plus at least one white matter characteristic (white matter hyperintensities in a specific multispot pattern and/or severely enlarged perivascular spaces in the centrum semiovale). These criteria apply to patients ${\geq}50$ years who present with intracerebral hemorrhage, transient focal neurological episodes or cognitive impairment (and in absence of any deep hemorrhagic lesions and other causes of hemorrhagic lesions). For a diagnosis of probable CAA, the Boston 2.0 criteria showed a reasonable sensitivity $74.5~\%$ (65⋅4–82⋅4) and good specificity $95.0\%$ (83.1–99.4) in individuals with histological diagnosis as the goldstandard. Recently, the Edinburgh CT scan-based criteria have been developed to predict moderate or severe CAA as the cause of the ICH. They include three component predictors: subarachnoid extension of the ICH, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, and the presence of bleeding elongated projections arising from the ICH [36].  

Other causes of ICH can be a macrovascular or neoplastic cause, and are usually referred to as “secondary” ICH: brain arteriovenous mal­ formation, intracranial aneurysm, dural arteriovenous fistula, cavernous malformation, intracranial venous thrombosis, hemorrhagic transformation of cerebral infarction, severe clotting factor deficiency such as hemophilia, brain tumor (both primary or metastasis), vasculitis, infective endocarditis, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome.  

Some of these causes of ICH are potentially treatable, so their iden­ tification should be as prompt as possible. To do so, an effective and timely etiological diagnostic work-up is required and attempts have been made for a classification system according to anatomical location or presumed mechanisms [43–47].  

# 5. Neuroimaging in ICH: From diagnostic work-up to prognostication  

Neuroimaging (non-contract CT (NCCT) or magnetic resonance im­ aging (MRI) is a critical first step to identify ICH. It has also a key role in the etiological work-up. Although $85~\%$ of cases are due to chronic ce­ rebral SVD, “secondary” causes may require urgent curative care. As soon as possible, those causes should be ruled out using either CTangiography (CTA) or MR-angiography (MRA). In lobar ICH, espe­ cially in the young, cerebral venous thrombosis should be excluded by CT or MR venography. Some advanced sequences, such as arterial spinlabeling MR imaging can also be useful to screen for arteriovenous shunt in arteriovenous malformations. When the clinico-radiological appear­ ance is suggestive of an underlying macrovascular malformation, a catheter digital subtraction angiogram (DSA) is indicated or should be discussed in selected patients with (a) lobar spontaneous ICH and age $<$ 70 years, (b) deep/posterior fossa ICH and age $<45$ years, or (c) deep/ posterior fossa and age 45 to 70 years without history of hypertension and negative non-invasive imaging $\mathrm{CTA\pm}$ venography and MRI/MRA) [48]. In patients with spontaneous ICH, MRI is useful to assess for bio­ markers of the underlying small vessel disease (cerebral amyloid angi­ ography and deep perforating vasculopathy). There are no guidelines on the need for repeat imaging after the acute phase. It should be carried out systematically in case of clinical worsening. Further delayed imaging (MRI if available) between 3 and 6 months is generally recommended to rule out ‘secondary’ causes of ICH (in particular vascular malformations and brain tumours).  

Neuroimaging contributes to better prognostication of outcome. In addition to the well-known features associated with poor outcome (e.g. ICH volume, posterior fossa location, cerebral herniation, intraventric­ ular contamination), other radiological markers can help predict func­ tional outcome. The “spot sign” is a radiological finding of contrast extravasation from a ruptured vessel, representing active hemorrhage, so patients at risk of hematoma expansion. The odds of death and disability triple with as little as $3\mathrm{ml}$ of hematoma expansion [11]. Perihematomal edema (PHO) is a key contributor to ICH pathophysiology and is used as a radiological biomarker of inflammation. It develops in the ensuing days after ICH onset and worsens functional outcome [17].  

In the most recent advances, new tools are being developed to better understand the pathophysiology of ICH, predict outcomes and, in the future, better select patients for therapeutic interventions. $\mathrm{\textperthousand}$ relax­ ometry and quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) are advanced MRI techniques that enable precise quantification of iron in intracere­ bral hemorrhage, a highly toxic blood product for the brain after ICH [49]. These techniques are particularly valuable in characterizing he­ matoma content and evolution, as well as neuroinflammation in the peri-hematomal area [50]. Dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic reso­ nance imaging (DCE MRI) is a sequence used to quantitatively measure the extent of blood-brain barrier disruption, a key marker of neuro­ inflammatory processes and PHO after ICH [50].  

Radiomics applied to CT Imaging offer a quantitative approach, extracting a multitude of features from CT images to objectively assess the texture, shape, and intensity of hematomas. This method enhances the precision of prognostic models by incorporating detailed image analysis that goes beyond traditional visual estimation [51,52].  

The coming years will be marked by the use of Artificial Intelligence (AI). The development of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) has significantly enhanced the performance of automated tools for detecting ICH on head CT scans. CNNs have achieved high accuracy in detecting and classifying various subtypes of ICH. For instance, a deep learning algorithm developed by Wang et al. demonstrated expert-level perfor­ mance with AUCs close to 0.99 across different ICH subtypes, making it a potential robust tool for clinical applications [53]. The implementa­ tion of such AI-based tools has shown promise in reducing the time to communicate urgent results. Seyam et al. reported that integrating an AI tool into clinical workflows reduced the average time needed to communicate critical findings, thus potentially improving patient out­ comes in emergency settings [54]. However, the integration of AI in clinical practice is not without challenges. Del Gaizo et al. highlighted that, while AI tools can expedite the identification of hemorrhages, they can also increase interpretation times in certain contexts, particularly when false positives are generated [55]. These false alarms can lead to additional workload for radiologists, potentially offsetting the benefits of AI by prolonging the time required to confirm or rule out the presence of ICH. This underscores the importance of carefully evaluating the implementation of AI tools to ensure that they truly enhance efficiency and effectiveness in clinical settings.  

# 6. Acute management: Reducing the nihilism around ICH  

There are three key stages in ICH pathophysiology occurring at successive timepoints that should be targeted with complementary innovative therapeutic approaches to: (i) fight against hemorrhage expansion; (ii) promote hematoma evacuation; (iii) and reduce perihematomal edema (PHO). All available data on ICH management showed that a single intervention (i.e. blood pressure lowering, hema­ toma expansion) is unlikely to solve the challenge of such a dramatic acute event.  

# 6.1. Global acute management: Toward a fast care bundle approach  

There is increasing agreement over the benefits of a fast care bundle approach combining different simple therapeutic measures and opti­ mizing the pre and in-hospital workflow [56]. The third Intensive Care Bundle with Blood Pressure Reduction in Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage Trial (INTERACT-3) was an international stepped wedge cluster RCT where hospitals were centrally randomly allocated into three sequences of implementation of a care bundle [57]. The study involved 7036 pa­ tients with spontaneous ICH who presented within $^{6\mathrm{h}}$ of symptom onset to one of 121 hospitals in nine low- and middle-income and one highincome countries. It demonstrated that a timely combination of simple interventions (early intensive lowering of systolic blood pressure, strict glucose control, treatment of fever, and rapid reversal of abnormal anticoagulation) was effective in increasing the chances of survival without major disability and in improving overall quality of life at 6 months. These results are gamechangers. It also lays down a challenge to physicians and global healthcare systems: hospitals need to organize their systems to implement this package and maximize care.  

Whenever possible, patients should be monitored in a stroke unit. A meta-analysis of 8 trials involving 2657 patients showed that stroke unit care was at least as beneficial for ICH patients as for ischemic stroke patients [58].At last, early do-not-resuscitate orders should be avoided in the majority of ICH patients because they can lead to early palliation, precluding stroke unit admission and independently increasing mortal­ ity even after adjusting for clinical stroke severity [59]. In a 2015 study, waiting until 5 days post-ICH to make do-not-resuscitate decisions improved outcomes [60].  

# 6.2. Fighting against hemorrhage expansion: Blood pressure lowering, reversion and hemostatic agents  

# 6.2.1. Blood pressure lowering  

To fight against hematoma expansion the mantra $\ll$ Time is brain $\gg$ applies. The fourth INTEnsive ambulance-delivered BP Reduction in hyper-ACute stroke Trial (INTERACT4) showed clear benefits of rapid blood pressure control within just a few hours of the onset of ICH [61]. In this randomized trial, individuals with probable stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic) were given a bolus of intravenous antihypertensive in the ambulance not equipped with imaging on the way to the hospital. The neutral primary outcome of the trial (no difference in 90-day mRS including all stroke) masked a key subgroup finding: for ICH, blood pressure lowering in the ambulance significantly improved 90-day functional outcome (common odds ratio, 0.75; $95~\%$ CI, 0.60 to 0.92). A pooled analysis of individual patient data from 16 RCTs in America, Asia, and Europe supports that achieving ultra-early lowering and maintaining SBP at $120–140~\mathrm{mmHg}$ is associated with better outcomes after acute ICH [62]. The study included 5761 patients with at least one blood pressure reading and 4159 patients with complete blood pressure reading data. About one third of patients achieved and maintained SBP $120–140\mathrm{mmHg}$ until $24\mathrm{{h}}$ . Death or dependency $(\mathrm{mRS}3–6)$ was used as the primary outcome. Earlier achievement and maintenance of SBP $120–140\mathrm{mmHg}$ was significantly associated with less risk of death or dependency (mRS 3–6: OR 0.98, $95~\%$ CI 0.97–0.99; mRS 4–6: OR 0.98, $95~\%$ CI 0.97–0.99) and less risk of HE at $24\mathrm{~h~}$ (OR 0.98, $95~\%$ CI 0.96–1.00) and was not significantly associated with neurological deterioration, or serious adverse events.  

Overall, these data suggest that for blood pressure management, the earlier is the better, and maintenance is important. Although the dura­ tion of maintenance is unknown, in INTERACT 3, all target concentra­ tions within the care bundle (including systolic $\mathbf{BP}<140\mathrm{mmHg}$ ) were to be maintained in patients for 7 days [57]. Some uncertainties persist, especially the optimal target for the sub-group populations of patients with very high blood pressure (systolic blood pressure $>220\mathrm{mmHg})$ , patients with large hematoma and patients undergoing surgery.  

# 6.2.2. Antithrombotic reversal  

Nearly $50\%$ of patients are taking anti-thrombotic treatment at ICH onset and $15~\%$ are using oral anticoagulants with a recent shift in practice from vitamin K antagonists (VKA) to direct oral anticoagulants (DOAC) use [30,63]. Anticoagulant-related ICH are severe with larger hematoma volume at baseline, higher hematoma expansion rate and higher mortality rate [64–66]. Therefore, reaching a normalization of coagulation is a primary treatment goal in these patients. As soon as possible, ongoing anticoagulation should be stopped followed by reversal of the anticoagulation effect [67–69]. To do so, several reversal agents exist. In patients treated with VKA, prothrombin complex con­ centrates $(25{-}50~\mathrm{U/kg~IV})$ and vitamin K should be administered to target an INR to normal value $(<1.3)$ [68,70]. In DOAC-related ICH the use of prothrombin complex concentrate is also recommended in guidelines [71] and, when available, specific reversal agents should be administered. Idarucizumab (to reverse Dabigatran) and Andexanet alfa (to reverse Xa inhibitor molecules) showed favorable pharmacological results [72,73] and benefit on limiting hematoma expansion [72]. However, further data are required to guide their application in routine practice considering the lack of data on functional outcome, the po­ tential thrombotic complications (5 to $10\%$ and the unsolved issue of cost-effectiveness [72,73]. In patients with ICH associated with anti­ platelet use, platelet transfusion may be harmful and should not be used [74].  

# 6.2.3. Hemostatic agents  

Although there are no definitive data to support the use of hemo­ static agents in daily practice, Tranexamic acid (TXA), an anti­ fibrinolytic agent hold promise also in ICH. The administration of TXA showed positive result in reducing hematoma expansion and early mortality but the effect on long-term functional outcome is still uncer­ tain, even in patients with active bleeding (radiological spot sign) [75–77]. The drug effect might be time sensitive and more effective with ultra-early intervention. However, the result of the Stopping  

Hemorrhage With TXA for Hyperacute Onset Presentation Including Mobile Stroke Units (STOP-MSU) trial challenge this assumption [78]. In this phase 2 RCT, including 201 patients (median age, 66 years; $41\%$ female), TXA was not useful for preventing hematoma growth when used within $2\mathtt{h}$ of stroke onset and given in the ambulance on the way to the hospital. Larger trials evaluating the benefit TXA on mortality and functional outcomes are in progress (NIHR129917, ISRCTN97695350, CHICTR1900027065). No proven benefit has been seen with other he­ mostatic therapies such as desmopressin for antiplatelet associated ICH or recombinant factor VIIa [79,80].  

# 6.3. Surgery for ICH, new horizons  

# 6.3.1. Hematoma evacuation, a step forward for minimally invasive surgery to improve lobar ICH prognosis  

Surgical evacuation in patients with ICH may seem intuitive: early evacuation of the blood clot from the brain should translate into sig­ nificant benefits for patients with ICH. However, conventional surgery (craniotomy plus drainage) indicated an increase in survival rates but did not demonstrate improvement in neurological function compared with medical management [81,82]. In the recent years, there was a paradigm shift toward less invasive approaches for ICH evacuation to mitigate damage to surrounding healthy brain. Multiple less-invasive techniques and devices have been developed. One of these approaches is Minimally Invasive Surgery with Thrombolysis in Intracerebral Hemorrhage Evacuation (MISTIE). The MISTIE III trial showed that minimally invasive surgery followed by thrombolytic irrigation of the catheterized ICH with alteplase in patients with supratentorial ICH was safe and resulted in survival benefit compared with standard manage­ ment [83]. However, the primary outcome (mRS score 0 to 3 at 1 year) was not met. Interestungly, the MISTIE III trial suggested that a threshold of ICH evacuation of $\geq70~\%$ hematoma reduction or end-oftreatment volume to $\leq15~\mathrm{ml}$ was associated with a higher probability of good outcome compared with medical management alone [84]. More recently, the Early MiNimally-invasive Removal of ICH (ENRICH) trial compared early clot evacuation with minimally invasive parafascicular surgery (MIPS) to medical management alone [85]. ENRICH was a randomized, multicenter, adaptive clinical trial conducted across 37 hospitals in the United States. Main inclusion criteria comprised of: age 18–80 years, imaging confirmed ICH (volume of $30{-}80~\mathrm{ml})$ , a Glasgow Coma Scale of 5 to 14, a pre-morbid mRS of 0 or 1. ENRICH enrolled 300 patients with spontaneous ICH (208 were lobar ICH) who were randomly assigned within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ since last known normal. The primary outcome was long-term functional outcome assessed by the utilityweighted modified Rankin Scale (UWmRS) scores at 180 days. After a preplanned interim efficacy analysis, enrollment of patients with deep anterior hematomas was stopped so that the final study population comprised ${\sim}70~\%$ of patients with lobar ICH. In the total population, MIPS showed a UWmRS of 0.458 compared to 0.374 for medical care with a $98.1~\%$ posterior probability of superiority, exceeding the 0.975 prespecified threshold superiority of surgery $95~\%$ Bayesian credible interval: 0.005–0.163). The surgery benefit was not equal across ICH locations. A net benefit was observed in lobar ICH only (mean betweengroup difference:0.127 $[95\%$ Bayesian credible interval, 0.035 to 0.219]) while surgery was futile for patients with deep ICH. MIPS also reduced mortality rates at 30 days $(9.3\%\mathrm{vs.18.1\%}$ and shortened both ICU and hospital stays compared to medical management. Within the surgery group, there was a mean percent reduction of 73.2 $(\pm37.8)$ in hematoma volume from baseline to $24\mathrm{~h~}$ , with a mean residual volume of 14.9 $(\pm21.7)\mathrm{ml}$ after surgery. Notably, nearly three-fourths $(72.7\%)$ of the patients in the surgery group had a volume of $15~\mathrm{ml}$ or less after the completion of surgery. This is a major breakthrough to support minimally invasive surgery for ICH. While the results are positive for lobar ICH, the benefit for deep ICH has not been established. Several trials evaluating different approaches to hematoma evacuation in different ICH subpopulations and at different timings are ongoing (NCT05539859, NCT03342664, NCT04434807, NCT05460793, NCT05681988, NCT04172376).  

# 6.3.2. Decompressive hemicraniectomy, a new strategy for deep ICH  

Beside hematoma evacuation, the use of decompressive hemi­ craniectomy without hematoma evacuation in large deep ICH (basal ganglia and thalamus) may be an option. The Swiss Trial Of Decom­ pressive Craniectomy Versus Best Medical Treatment Of Deep Supra­ tentorial Intracerebral Hemorrhage (SWITCH) trial was multicenter, multinational phase 3 RCT [86]. SWITCH enrolled 201 patients (18–75 years) with large deep ICH (NIHSS 10–30, ICH volume $30{-}100\mathrm{ml)}$ within $^{66\mathrm{~h~}}$ of last known normal. Patients were randomized to decompressive craniectomy (within $^{6\textrm{h}}$ of randomization) versus best medical treatment with severe disability and death at 6 months as pri­ mary outcome. A total of 201 of 300 planned participants were enrolled (median age of 61 years; $32\%$ women) in almost 10 years in 42 stroke centres in nine European countries. The recruitment phase was stopped due to funding issues. Decompressive surgery resulted in $44\%$ of par­ ticipants having an $\mathrm{mRS}~5{-}6$ compared to $58~\%$ in the group receiving only best medical treatment (adjusted risk difference [aRD] $-13~\%$ , 95 $\%$ CI −26 to 0 $,p=0{\cdot}057\$ . In other words, decompressive craniectomy seems to result in more survivors at the expense of more patients with severe disability (mRS of 4). The ordinal-shift analysis (pre-specified secondary outcome) showed lower mRS in favor of surgery (common odds ratio [OR] for a worse outcome 0⋅57, $95~\%$ CI 0⋅34 to 0⋅97, $\mathsf{p}=$ 0⋅039). The study might be underpowered and many questions remain regarding patient selection and timing of surgery. In analogy with ma­ lignant ischemic strokes, the decompressive surgery decision also raises the ethical questions of quality of life after stroke for the patients and caregivers and the decision will be individual-based.  

# 7. Novelties in the secondary prevention after ICH  

ICH survivors are at risk of major cardio-vascular events, including hemorrhagic but also ischemic events and this create a clinical dilemma when come the issue of prevention management.  

If lowering blood pressure levels post ICH is both safe and an effec­ tive way to reduce the risk of future vascular events, the riks/benefit balance of other secondary prevention treatments is less clear.  

# 7.1. Antithrombotic treatment after ICH  

High quality data on the risk of antithrombotic medication post-ICH is scant. Although antithrombotic agents reduce the risk of cardiovas­ cular, cerebrovascular, and thromboembolic events, initiation or resumption after ICH remains challenging as they potentially increase the risk of rebleeding. Current guidelines recommend an individual benefit/risk evaluation regarding whether or not to initiate (or restart) antithrombotic treatment after ICH [48].  

# 7.1.1. Antiplatelet agent  

RESTART was an open-label trial including 537 ICH patients (mean age 77 years vs 76 years and $35~\%$ vs $30\%$ females in the treatment group vs non-treatment group) with a history of vaso-occlusive disease, that compared the effects of restarting versus avoiding antiplatelet therapy after ICH. Patients allocated to resume antiplatelet therapy experienced fewer recurrent ICH (aHR 0.51 [95 $\%$ CI 0.25–1.03]), fewer major hemorrhagic events (aHR 0.71 $[95\%$ CI, 0.39–1.30]) and similar rate of major vaso-occlusive events (aHR 1.02 [0.65–1.60]) [87]. These results were consistent with a meta-analysis of 1916 ICH patients that reported no significant increase in risk of ICH recurrence and a decreased risk of thromboembolic and ischemic events with resumption of antiplatelet therapy [88]. Although the results from RESTART were statistically nonsignificant it seems safe to restart antiplatelet therapy after ICH for patients with a clear indication for antiplatelet therapy. Furthermore, an international phase 3 trial comparing antiplatelet agent versus no antiplatelet in patients with a history of ICH but no history of vaso-occlusive events (i.e. in a setting of primary prevention) is currently ongoing (ASPIRING trial, ISRCTN16705062).  

# 7.1.2. Anticoagulation  

Twenty percent of ICH survivors suffered from atrial fibrillation (AF) [30]. In this population, the benefit of anticoagulation is unclear. SoS­ TART and APACHE-AF were the first two RCTs to investigate the safety of oral anticoagulant drug (OAC) use after ICH in patients with AF [89,90]. Both trials reported starting OAC after ICH was not non-inferior to avoiding OAC. However, in SoSTART symptomatic major vascular events were numerically higher in patients without OAC treatment indicating that OAC therapy might be superior for preventing MACE [89], and APACHE-AF found annual event rates of the composite outcome of non-fatal stroke or vascular death was about $12\%$ in both treatment groups [90]. An individual patient data meta-analysis of 412 patients with ICH and AF found weak evidence that starting anti­ coagulation might reduce the risk of stroke and cardiovascular death [91]. Though, they found some evidence of a reduction of major ischemic adverse events when treated with OAC compared to no treat­ ment, they also found an increase in hemorrhagic major adverse events. The reduction of ischemic events may outweigh the increased risk of hemorrhagic events, but the net benefit of starting OAC remains uncertain.  

Even with a clear indication, the optimal timing of OAC after ICH remains uncertain [92]. A Swedish study suggested that starting anti­ coagulation 7–8 weeks after ICH in patients with AF had a net benefit of stroke risk reduction and bleeding risk minimization [93]. Direct OAC (DOAC) are preferred over vitamin K-antagonists (VKA) as they are equally effective in prevention of ischemic events but have lower rates of hemorrhage [94]. Currently left atrial appendage closure (LAAO) is being studied as a potential non-pharmacological alternative in patients with AF after ICH. A small Danish retrospective observational study reported lower cardiovascular mortality, all-cause mortality, hemor­ rhagic stroke risk, and MACE with LAAO compared with standard medical therapy [95]. This is currently being assessed in an ongoing French RCT (NCT03243175).  

# 7.2. Statins after ICH  

Statins use after ICH has been controversial as they may increase the risk of ICH [96], though recent data suggest that statin treatment does not increase the risk of rebleeding after ICH [97,98]. However, statin use is associated with a significant overall reduction in mortality and better functional outcomes due to the prevention of future ischemic events [97]. The SATURN trial (NCT03936361), a phase III RCT, is investi­ gating the benefit of continuation versus discontinuation of statins after spontaneous lobar ICH. Currently, after ICH there is no evidence to avoid statin when there is a clear benefit such as secondary prevention in atherosclerotic disease.  

# 8. Perspectives  

In recent years, significant progress has been made in the causes, diagnosis, classification, pathophysiological understanding, treatment and prevention of ICH. However, many aspects of acute management remain unclear, and research efforts are still needed to precisely estab­ lished therapeutic targets, their time-window and the best patient responder profile. Fig. 1 describes the current therapeutic interventions under the scope in ICH. Recent translational data have also taught us that a single intervention is unlikely to solve the problem of ICH prog­ nosis. Indeed, the various key stages in the pathophysiology of ICH, which occur at successive times, should be targeted by innovative and complementary therapeutic approaches. These include fighting against hematoma expansion, promoting hematoma evacuation, as well as reducing peri-hematoma edema, which could be the next frontier in ICH

Early phase trials combining hematoma evacuation and treatment of inflammation are currently underway (NCT05582707, NCT06087965). Although more evidence is needed to change our daily practice, we already need to get prepared and we to rethink the multidisciplinary organization of ICH manage­ ment. Care pathways will gradually incorporate new evidence-based strategies, and care systems will have to adapt accordingly. Each medical centre should be equipped with a bundle of care for ICH. Care structure will have to develop technical platforms for minimally invasive surgery and train teams in these techniques. On a territorial scale, and in analogy with ischemic stroke with large vessel occlusion, drip-and-ship and mother­ ship models will need to be compared in ICH to identify which provides optimal results in terms of mortality and functional status.  

Several fundamental questions regarding prevention require further evidence. In the near future, better phenotyping of ICH (including brain imaging augmented by deep learning algorithms and genetic) will enable more accurate identification of the cause, but also prognostic assessment of future vascular events and functional outcomes, leading to better tailored secondary prevention.  

The challenge of individualized and specialized care should not overshadow a greater one: the reduction of ICH burden worldwide. Consequently, public health measures should be strengthened in lowincome countries to facilitate access to knowledge and simple thera­ peutic measures for acute management (acute bundle of care with reduction of blood pressure, strict glycemic control, treatment of fever, etc.) but also for primary prevention.
