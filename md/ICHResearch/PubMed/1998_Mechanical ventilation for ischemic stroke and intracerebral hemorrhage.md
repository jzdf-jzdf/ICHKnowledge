# Mechanical ventilation for ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage  

Indications, timing, and outcome  

A.R. Gujar, DM; E. Deibert, MD; E.M. Manno, MD; S. Duff, MD; and M.N. Diringer, MD  

Article abstract--Objective: To compare the incidence, indication, and timing of intubation and outcome in patients with cerebral infarction (ISCH) and intracerebral hemorrhage (HEM) requiring mechanical ventilation (MV). Background: Poor outcomes have been reported for ISCH patients requiring MV. Because the target population, pathophysiology, and management of ISCH and HEM patients differ considerably, we compared the characteristics of patients with ISCH and HEM who required MV. Methods: A retrospective review of ISCH and HEM stroke patients who underwent MV at a tertiary care academic center from 1994 to 1997 was performed to determine age, sex, type, and location of stroke (anterior or posterior circulation); brainstem dysfunction at intubation (pupillary, corneal, and oculocephalic reflexes); indication for intubation (neurologic deterioration, cardiopulmonary deterioration, or elective intubation for surgery); timing of intubation (on presentation or later); comorbidities; and outcome (hospital disposition). Results: A total of 230 patients, 74 with ISCH and 156 with HEM (mean age, $61\pm16$ years; male-to-female ratio, 1.15:1), underwent MV. Intubation rates were $6\%$ for ISCH patients and $30\%$ for HEM patients. Two-thirds of the patients required intubation on presentation $84\%$ were intubated for neurologic deterioration) and 131 patients $(57\%)$ died (ISCH, $55\%$ ;HEM, $58\%$ 0. Signs of brainstem dysfunction predicted a higher mortality for both groups. Additionally, early intubation and older age predicted mortality for HEM, and male gender predicted mortality in ISCH. Stroke location and comorbidities did not influence outcome. Conclusions: MV in acute stroke is associated with high mortality. Mortality and outcome were similar for ISCH and HEM; however, the factors predictive of outcome may differ and influence decisions about the use of MV in such patients.  

NEUROLOGY 1998;51:447-451  

Mortality rates for stroke in the United States declined $1\%$ annually between 1915 and 1960. More recently, between 1973 and 1981, this fall in mortality rate has accelerated to as high as $7\%$ annually.1 Two factors appear to explain this phenomenon: improvements in acute stroke care and the ability to diagnose milder cases of stroke, using imaging technology, that previously went undetected.2 The development of specialized neurologic intensive care and stroke units, and the use of mechanical ventilation (MV) has probably contributed to this decline in mortality. Although outcome is reported to be better in patients cared for in dedicated stroke units,3,4 the outcome in stroke patients who require MV remains poor. Mortality rates are high, ranging from 57 to $90\%$ , and functional outcome is poor in survivors.5-13  

Various factors have been reported to influence outcome in stroke patients requiring MV, including age, level of consciousness, presence or absence of brainstem reflexes, apneic episodes, location of infarction, high intracranial pressure, reason for intubation, and additional interventions such as hemicraniectomy. The wide variations in outcome and differences in factors predictive of outcome in these studies have led to conflicting recommendations. Thus, the decision to apply costly and labor-intensive types of care, including MV, to patients with severely disabling stroke continues to be controversial. The ability of MV to improve survival and the possibility of converting what may be a dignified death into survival with severe disability remains unresolved.  

Different stroke syndromes may be associated with different rates of intubation and outcomes, by virtue of the differences in the underlying pathophysiology, location, severity, and degree of reversibility of neurologic insult. For example, Mayer et al.12 observed higher rates of intubation as well as higher mortality rates in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (HEM) compared with ischemic stroke. The mechanisms responsible for altered sensorium or brainstem dysfunction that may necessitate intubation in patients with cerebral infarction (ISCH) are either direct brainstem injury (as in vertebrobasilar territory infarction) or diencephalic or brainstem distortion from tissue shifts. Intracranial hemorrhage, in contrast, may cause an early increase in intracranial pressure independent of location, hydrocephalus, or direct injury to the brainstem or diencephalon. The primary therapies for ISCH and HEM also differ and may influence the outcomes by virtue of their relative efficacy.  

Recent literatures-13 has begun to explore the utility of MV in patients with acute stroke. Most studies have focused on ISCH, or included a few patients with HEM or subarachnoid hemorrhage. Because the target population, pathophysiology, and management of ISCH and HEM differ considerably, we sought to determine if the use of MV differed in these two varieties of stroke. We conducted this study to compare the incidence, indication, and timing of intubation and outcome among patients with ISCH and HEM requiring MV.  

Methods. We performed a retrospective review of patients with ISCH or HEM admitted to the Neurology/ Neurosurgery Intensive Care Unit (NNICU) of a tertiary care university hospital who received MV between May 1994 and April 1997. Most patients were admitted through the emergency department, although some were referred directly to the NNICU from outside hospitals. All patients were evaluated by the attending neurologist and neurointensivist, and were investigated with imaging (cranial CT, MRI, or MR angiography) and other procedures required to establish a diagnosis.  

All patients received standard care for unconscious MV patients. Neurologic criteria for intubation were no (or very brief) eye opening to noxious stimuli, failure to follow commands (Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS], approximately ${\le}8,$ , occasionally associated with a dilated pupil. Intubation was also performed when noninvasive means of ventilatory support were ineffective in assisting a patient in maintaining adequate ventilation or oxygenation. Patients were always ventilated using intermittent mandatory ventilation mode immediately after intubation. If they were felt clinically to be herniating they were not weaned and were administered mannitol. Otherwise, after the sedatives administered during intubation wore off, the primary mode of ventilation was switched rapidly to pressure support. Most patients were weaned quickly to minimal levels of pressure support (8 to $12\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{Hg},$ because they were intubated primarily for airway protection. Those in respiratory distress were weaned to the minimum amount of pressure support that resulted in a respiratory rate of less than 25 bpm. Patients were extubated after the pressure support had been weaned to $10\mathrm{mmHg}$ or less, oxygenation was adequate on $40\%$ inspired oxygen, secretions were not excessive, and the patient had adequate cough and gag reflexes. Some patients who did not meet these criteria were extubated at their family's request and administered opiates to prevent respiratory distress.  

All ISCH patients received aspirin, $38\%$ receivedIV heparin to maintain a partial thromboplastin time of 1.5 to 2 times control, and $18\%$ underwent intra-arterial thrombolysis with urokinase. Three patients with cerebellar infarction underwent posterior fossa decompression, and $12\%$ with hemispheric infarction underwent hemicraniectomy.Among thepatientswithHEM, $28\%$ underwentventriculostomy and $25\%$ underwent surgical evacuation of the hematoma. Seventeen percent of the entire cohort received mannitol.  

Charts were reviewed to determine the age, sex, type (ISCH or HEM), and location (carotid or vertebrobasilar circulation for ISCH; supratentorial, cerebellar, or brainstem for HEM) of stroke, signs of brainstem dysfunction (pupillary,corneal,and oculovestibular reflexes)at the time of intubation, indication for intubation (neurologic deterioration, cardiopulmonary deterioration, or elective for surgery), timing of intubation (on presentation or later in the course of illness), comorbidities (ischemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancer), duration of hospital stay, mortality, and hospital disposition (home, nursing facility, rehabilitation facility, or another hospital).  

Variables were compared between the ISCH and HEM groups using the chi-square test for categorical variables, andunpaired $t$ -testswereusedforcontinuousvariables. Factors predictive of hospital mortality were determined using univariate and multivariate logistic regression. Variables were included in the multivariate model if they had a $_p$ value of less than 0.1 in the univariate logistic model. Data are presented as mean $\pm$ SD.A $p$ value of less than 0.05 was required for statistical significance. The institution's Human Studies Committee approved the study.

Age, sex, and comorbidities were comparable between the two groups. As expected, signs of brainstem dysfunction (absent pupillary and oculovestibular reflexes) at intubation were more frequent in VB. The vast majority of both groups required intubation for neurologic deterioration. Discussion. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the use of MV in the context of acute, severe ischemic, and hemorrhagic stroke. We found a similar mortality and outcome in patients with ISCH and HEM requiring MV, but different intubation rates and factors predictive of mortality. Patients with HEM had an intubation rate that was five times higher than those with ISCH, were more likely to have signs of brainstem dysfunction, and more likely to be intubated on presentation. Absence of pupillary response predicted mortality in both groups. In HEM patients, however, mortality was also associated with absent corneal and oculocephalic reflexes, older age, and intubation on presentation for neurologic deterioration.  

Studies addressing patients with ISCH requiring $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{V}^{5-13}$ have identified factors such as poor level of consciousness, absence of brainstem reflexes, apneic episodes, and stroke progression to be associated with poor outcome. Few studies, however, have addressed the role of MV in a large cohort of patients with HEM. Higher mortality rates of 70 to $96\%$ have been reported in smaller cohorts (range, 18 to 39 patients).5,6,8,11,12 Similar to ISCH patients, decreased level of consciousness, brainstem dysfunction, and intubation for coma or neurologic deterioration have been associated with poor outcome in HEM patients 6.11.12 Intubation for primary neurologic deterioration was found to be a predictor of poor outcome for ISCH patients in this study and is consistent with the observations of Steiner et al.11 and CruzFlores et al.13 The need for intubation early during the ictus as a predictor of mortality, however, has not been reported previously.  

The intubation rates in the current study $(5.8\%$ for ISCH and $30\%$ for HEM) are comparable with or lower than other reports. For example, Mayer et al.12 reported rates of intubation of $5\%$ among patients with ISCH and $30\%$ among patients with HEM. Comparison of intubation rates across studies provides an index of the threshold for intubation at a particular center. For example a high rate of intubation might suggest that less severely affected patients are being intubated and thus a lower mortality rate would be expected. Since our intubation rates were similar to those in the literature, our having a lower threshold for intubation cannot account for any differences in mortality. The significantly higher rate of intubation, as well as of early intubation in patients with HEM is probably due to the common occurrence of diminished level of consciousness as part of the natural course of HEM.  

Mortality rates reported for patients with acute stroke requiring MV are varied, resulting in differing recommendations regarding the use of MV in these patients. Several factors may have contributed to this variability. Those reporting the highest mortality rates were highly selective in inclusion of patients,79,10 were part of a study not restricted to stroke patients, or studied patients who were not treated in an ICU setting.8 The three more recent patient series,l1-13 which are comparable with the current study, report mortality rates very similar to ours.  

From the observations in this study, high mortality may be expected in patients with acute ISCH with evidence of brainstem dysfunction, and in patients with HEM who, in addition, require intubation early in the course of stroke for neurologic deterioration. However, the predictive power of these factors is limited. Our models only predicted outcome correctly in $66\%$ of ISCH patients and in $76\%$ ofHEM patients. Thus, decisions to withhold intubation based on these factors may not be warranted. Further studies that provide more definitive predictors of functional outcome may be helpful in addressing this issue.  

It may be advisable to provide MV for patients with severe stroke with the understanding that it could be withdrawn if there was no neurologic improvement or further deterioration. Defining, in advance, a specific degree of neurologic improvement within a predetermined time frame may serve to establish the futility of the situation and may direct further care. On occasion, MV may be used in preparation for other, potentially useful but as of yet unproved interventions, such as thrombolytic therapy, craniectomy, or hematoma evacuation. Future studies are needed to explore the role of MV in cohorts with uniform subtypes of stroke receiving specific treatment regimens and to determine its impact on long-term functional outcome.
