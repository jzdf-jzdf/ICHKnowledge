# Tranexamic Acid for Adult Patients with Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage: A Systematic Review with Meta‑analysis  

Xing Wang1 $\bullet$ Lu Ma1 $\cdot$ Jinlei Song1 $\cdot$ Chao You1,2  

Accepted: 21 September 2021 / Published online: 18 October 2021   
$\circledcirc$ The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature Switzerland AG 2021  

# Abstract  

Background  The effects of tranexamic acid on spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage in reducing hematoma expansion and mortality as well as its role in thromboembolic complications and in the improvement of functional outcomes remain substantially uncertain.  

Objective  The objective of this systematic review was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of tranexamic acid in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage.  

Methods  Several databases were searched from inception up to 20 June, 2021. We included randomized controlled trials that compared tranexamic acid with placebo or no treatment for the management of intracerebral hemorrhage. The primary outcomes were hematoma expansion and 90-day mortality. The secondary outcomes were hemorrhagic volume change, thromboembolic complications, and functional outcomes.  

Results  Overall, six trials with 2800 patients were included in this meta-analysis. Tranexamic acid was associated with a reduced risk of hematoma expansion (relative risk 0.87, $95\%$ confidence interval [CI] 0.77–0.99, $p=0.03$ , $I^{2}=0\%$ , six trials with 2800 participants) and a lessening of hematoma volume change (mean difference − 1.28, $95\%\mathrm{CI}-2.44$ to − 0.12; $p=0.03$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ , four trials with 2626 participants), without a corresponding higher rate of major thromboembolic complications (relative risk 1.20, $95\%$ CI 0.85–1.69; $p=0.80$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ , five trials with 2759 participants). The present analysis also demonstrated that tranexamic acid had no effect on reducing 90-day mortality (relative risk 1.02, $95\%$ CI 0.88–1.19; $p=0.80$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ , five trials with 2770 participants).  

Conclusions  In adults with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, tranexamic acid reduced the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage growth compared with the control. The effects on 90-day mortality remained inconclusive. Further studies should report death within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and death due to bleeding whenever possible.  

# 1  Introduction  

Each year, as many as two million people around the world experience a spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), with few effective interventions and poor clinical outcomes [1]. Case fatalities are reported to be as high as $40\%$ within 1 month of onset, increasing to $54\%$ at 1 year [2, 3]. Previous studies have demonstrated that more than $30\%$ of hematomas expand within the first $^{24\mathrm{~h~}}$ after onset, and hematoma growth strongly correlates with mortality and negative functional outcome after ICH [4, 5]. Thus, avoiding hematoma growth is crucial for treating ICH [6].  

Tranexamic acid (TXA), a synthetic derivative of the amino acid lysine, exerts antifibrinolytic function by inactivating the lysine-binding site of plasminogen [7, 8]. Tranexamic acid improves survival after acute traumatic brain injury possibly owing to its role in preventing the secondary expansion of hematoma [9–11]. However, the effects of TXA on spontaneous ICH in terms of reducing hematoma expansion and mortality, as well as its safety in thromboembolic complications and improved functional outcome, remain substantially uncertain [12–14]. Although there is some evidence for the efficacy of TXA in patients with spontaneous ICH, the conclusions are not strong enough to make firm recommendations [5, 15, 16]. Therefore, it is necessary to perform an updated meta-analysis to pool the results of each trial.

Accordingly, to offer an updated summary of the current evidence, we conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate the effect of TXA vs placebo or no treatment on hemorrhagic expansion, mortality, functional outcomes, and thromboembolic complications in adult patients with spontaneous ICH.  

# 2  Materials and Methods  

# 2.1  Guidance and Protocol  

The methods of reporting the present meta-analysis complied with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) guidelines [17]. This study was registered with OSF platform (http://osf.io/ mk4pc).  

# 2.2 Selection Criteria  

Eligible studies were selected on the basis of the following criteria. Patients: adult patients (defined as $\mathrm{age}>18$ years) with ICH regardless of severity. Interventions: administration of TXA ${\mathrm{~1~g~}}$ intravenously over $10\mathrm{min}$ as a loading dose, followed by a maintenance dose of ${\mathrm{~1~g~}}$ in a $0.9\%$ sodium chloride infusion over $^\textrm{\scriptsize8h}$ . The drug was administered as soon as possible after randomization $(\leq24\mathrm{~h~})$ . The controls employed a matching placebo or $0.9\%$ saline according to the same administration schedule. Outcomes: the primary outcome was intracerebral hematoma expansion within $^{24\textrm{h}}$ , and mortality within 90 days. We defined intracerebral hematoma expansion as any form of extended spatial profile of the initial hemorrhage that contributed to an absolute increase of more than $6\mathrm{mL}$ or a relative growth of greater than $33\%$ as assessed by brain imaging [18]. Secondary outcomes were defined as a change in hematoma volume by $^{24\mathrm{h}}$ , thromboembolic complications (defined as myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, pulmonary embolism, and deep vein thrombosis), major thromboembolic events (including myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and pulmonary embolism), modified Rankin Scale at day 90 [19], health-related quality of life evaluated by EuroQoL-5 Dimension: HUS (EQ-5D HUS) [20], and EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS) [21], disability assessed by the Barthel Index [22], mood evaluated by the Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale [23], and neurological function assessed by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [24]. These functional and quality assessment scales were used to evaluate different aspects of patients. Study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs).  

Additionally, we excluded trials that (1) evaluated effects of TXA in traumatic intracranial bleeding, (2) compared TXA with other blood-clotting agents, and (3) only evaluated effects of TXA in terms of coagulation function.  

# 2.3 Search Strategy  

The search strategy is presented in Table A1 of the ESM Appendix. Several keywords and their free combinations such as “tranexamic acid”, “intracerebral hemorrhage”, and “randomized controlled trial” were used. We searched Ovid EMBASE, PUBMED, Ovid Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Ovid MEDLINE, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) from inception to 20 June, 2021. We also performed a comprehensive search of references from the selected trials and published systematic reviews on the same topic to discover any additional trials.  

# 2.4 Selection Process and Data Extraction  

After deleting duplicates, we excluded studies that were not eligible based on the titles or abstracts. Full-text articles were then either excluded or included based on the aforementioned criteria in the meta-analysis. Data associated with the following items were subsequently extracted onto a standardized form: (1) study characteristics: name of primary author, year of publication, location, centers where trials were conducted, and duration of follow-up; (2) treatment characteristics: dose, interval, duration, and route of drugs; (3) patient characteristics: mean age, proportion of male individuals, and severity of illness based on the Glasgow  

Coma Scale. Two reviewers independently completed this procedure together using a predesigned tabular form. In the case of unextractable or incomplete data, we would contact authors for clarification.  

# 2.5 Evaluation of Risk of Bias and Quality of Evidence  

Risk of bias for all RCTs was evaluated by the Cochrane Collaboration risk of bias tool according to the seven following domains: blinding of study participants, blinding of outcome assessment, allocation concealment, random sequence generation, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias [25]. Each domain was judged as low risk of bias, lacking clarity, or high risk of bias. In addition, we contacted the original study investigators in cases where more information was needed.  

The quality of evidence for outcomes was judged using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) working group tool for rating each estimate quality [26]. The following five domains were assessed: inconsistency, imprecision, limitations in design, publication bias, and indirectness. The overall quality of evidence for each estimate was rated as “high”, “moderate”, “low”, or “very low”.  

# 2.6 Statistical Analysis  

We calculated mean differences using a $95\%$ confidence interval (CIs) for continuous outcomes, and a $95\%$ CI for dichotomous outcomes with relative risks (RRs). A metaanalysis was performed using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed by the $I^{2}$ test, with values greater than $50\%$ regarded as having substantial heterogeneity [27]. Potential publication bias was evaluated by the funnel plot. We evaluated the asymmetry of the funnel plot using Egger’s regression test, and Begg’s test if ten or more trials were analyzed [28]. The level of statistical significance was defined using a two-sided $p$ value of less than 0.05. All analyses were conducted in RevMan software (5.4.1; The Cochrane Collaboration).  

# 2.7 Subgroup Analysis and Sensitivity Analysis  

Subgroup analyses were conducted for hematoma expansion on (1) onset to treatment time ( $\leq3\mathrm{~h~}$ and $>3\mathrm{~h~}$ ); (2) baseline ICH volume $\leq30\mathrm{mL}$ and $>30\mathrm{mL}$ ); (3) ICH location (lobar and deep); and (4) intraventricular hemorrhage (yes and no). We conducted sensitivity analyses for the primary outcomes by (1) excluding trials with ten or fewer events in total; (2) excluding trials with $<100$ patients in total; (3) using the odds ratios method; and (4) using a fixed-effect model.  

# 2.8  Post‑hoc Analyses  

We used the trial sequential analysis (TSA) to detect whether the cumulative data would be of sufficiently high power to evaluate primary outcomes and draw firm conclusions. The TSA is a statistical method that evaluates the risk of random errors due to multiple testing, as well as calculates the required information size in the meta-analysis by the incorporation of effect and information size. [29]  

We used the following variables to calculate the trial sequence monitoring boundaries: a statistical significance level $(\alpha)$ of $5\%$ for primary outcomes, a $\beta$ of $20\%$ (power of $80\%$ ), RR reductions or RR increases of $20\%$ , and the pooled event rates calculated across the TSA trials [30, 31]. We used a $\alpha$ of $5\%$ for the conventional boundary. Trial sequential analysis-adjusted CIs and naïve $95\%$ CIs for primary outcomes were reported according to the preceding method. The trial sequential analysis was conducted using the trial sequential analysis software, version 0.9.5.10 Beta (Copenhagen Trial Unit, Centre for Clinical Intervention Research, Copenhagen, Denmark).  

# 3  Results  

# 3.1 Eligible Studies and Study Characteristics  

On searching several databases, 1075 articles were identified. After removing duplicates and excluding studies based on titles and abstracts, 39 studies were read in full text. Among them, 33 studies were excluded because of inappropriate PICOS criteria. The reasons for excluding trials and the publication screening process are provided in Fig. 1. From these analyses, six controlled trials were regarded as eligible and included in the meta-analysis [5, 15, 16, 32–34]. Table 1 summarizes the study characteristics. The studies were published from 2014 to 2021. Population sizes ranged from 24 to 2325 patients.  

# 3.2  Risk of Bias Judgment  

Four trials were judged as having an overall low risk of bias. The other two trials were judged as having an overall high risk of bias. All trials were performed using random-sequence generation and allocation concealment. One trial was judged as having a high risk of bias due to performance bias. The other trial was judged as having an indeterminate risk of bias due to performance bias and detection bias. The full details of the risk of bias judgment for each trial are provided in Figs. A1 and A2 of the ESM Appendix.

# 3.3  Primary Outcomes  

# 3.3.1  Hematoma Expansion  

All six trials, which included 2800 patients, provided data on hematoma expansion. The pooled estimate of the RR suggested that compared to placebo, TXA was associated with a reduced risk of hematoma expansion (RR 0.87, $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI0.77-0.99}$ , $p=0.03$ , $I^{2}=0\%$ , Fig. 2a) despite a risk of bias. According to the TSA for hematoma expansion, the required information size was exceeded. The TSA-adjusted CI remained the same at 0.77–0.99 (Fig. A4 of the ESM Appendix). The quality of evidence was rated as high using the GRADE analysis (Table 2).  

The sensitivity analysis indicated that our results were robust when using (1) exclusion trials having fewer than ten events in total; (2) exclusion trials with $<100$ patients in total; (3) the odds ratios method; and (4) a fixed-effects model (Table A2 of the ESM Appendix). A subgroup analysis stratified by demographic and clinical characteristics did not show any interaction (Fig. 3).  

# 3.3.2 90-Day Mortality  

Five trials involving 2770 patients reported 90-day mortality. Overall, the pooled estimate of RR suggested that compared with placebo, TXA was not associated with a reduced risk of 90-day mortality (RR 1.01, $95\%$ CI 0.87–1.18; $p=0.86$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ , Fig. 2b). The TSA demonstrated that more evidence was needed to draw firm conclusions (Fig. A6 of the ESM Appendix). The quality was rated to be moderate because of imprecision using the GRADE tool (Table 2). A sensitivity analysis showed that our results were stable after excluding specific trials (Table A2 of the ESM Appendix).  

# 3.4 Secondary Outcomes  

# 3.4.1 Change in Hematoma Volume  

Four trials totaling 2626 patients provided data on the change in hematoma volume. The present analysis demonstrated that TXA was associated with a decreased change in hematoma volume compared with placebo (mean difference − 1.28, $95\%$ CI − 2.44 to − 0.12; $p=0.03$ ; $I^{2}=0\%$ , Fig. A7 of the ESM Appendix).  

# 3.4.2 Thromboembolic Events  

Five randomized trials, which included 2759 patients, provided data on major thromboembolic events. Overall, the pooled RR indicated that TXA treatment was not associated with a higher risk of major thromboembolic events compared with the controls (RR 1.20, $95\%$ CI 0.85–1.69; $p=0.30;I^{2}=0\%$ , Fig. A8 of the ESM Appendix). According to GRADE approach, the quality of the evidence was rated as moderate because of imprecision (Table 2). We further pooled trials that compared pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, myocardial infarction, or stroke of TXA treatment with placebo. The results did not show any difference in any of the outcomes (Fig. A9 of the ESM Appendix).

# 4  Discussion  

In the present systematic review and meta-analysis of six trials with 2800 patients, we aimed at evaluating the effect of TXA on the clinical outcomes of adult patients with nontraumatic ICH. We used the TSA to identify the required information size and GRADE approach for assessing the overall certainty of each estimate. We did not downgrade the primary outcomes from high-to-moderate quality of evidence because the weight of the point estimate was primarily attributed to trials adjudicated as having a low bias risk. In the present meta-analysis, high-quality evidence showed that TXA reduced hematoma expansion in patients with ICH. The TSA indicated that the required information size for detecting a $20\%$ relative difference in hematoma expansion had been incorporated, which indicated firm evidence. We are therefore confident that the true effect is approximated by the estimate of the effect. We did not find any association based on the subgroup analysis. However, some points should be noted. Tranexamic acid treatment within a window of 3 hours following ICH showed a trend toward a better outcome. Ongoing large RCTs may be helpful in addressing this issue.  

However, TXA, while effective in reducing hematoma expansion failed to significantly reduce 90-day mortality. The effect on 90-day mortality was inconclusive, as neither the required information size was reached nor were the trial sequential monitoring boundaries exceeded. However, TXA has a short half-life, and its effect is eliminated after only 1 day of application. As an antifibrinolytic agent, moreover, TXA is unable to prevent infections or edema, which can negatively impact patient survival. Therefore, a more appropriate outcome measure would be death within $^{241}$ or death due to bleeding [35, 36]. Considering that no studies have reported 24-h mortality or cause-specific mortality, our study’s data on 90-day mortality could potentially dilute the therapeutic effect of TXA. Perhaps this explains why TXA exerted no effect on 90-day mortality or 90-day functional outcomes. Future studies should report these outcomes whenever possible.

We used various functional and quality scales to assess different aspects of the patients. The GRADE quality of most of the functional outcomes was downgraded because of imprecision. It should be noted that most outcomes were assessed at day 90 from the start of drug administration. Only the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale was assessed at day 7 by two trials or at discharge by one trial. The quality of evidence was therefore downgraded to “very low”.  

# 4.1 Comparation with Other Studies  

To our knowledge, there are several meta-analyses that have evaluated the effectiveness of TXA in the treatment of cerebral hemorrhage, but all have combined spontaneous ICH with subarachnoid hemorrhage and traumatic intracranial bleeding. As a result, their conclusions might not be reliable because there was significant clinical heterogeneity among the population used in the analyses. In 2020, Gao et al. [14] reported TXA led to a significant reduction in HE and HV change in spontaneous and traumatic intracranial hemorrhage on the basis of data pooled from 3102 patients and seven RCTs. In 2019, Hu et al. [12] conducted a metaanalysis of 14 trials that included 4733 patients and found that TXA was associated with a decreased rate of hematoma expansion compared with placebo. However, their conclusion could not be repeated in patients with spontaneous  

ICH. Another study published in 2018 by Huang et al. [37] concluded that TXA was associated with a significantly decreased growth of hematoma. However, the three pooled trials were designed to evaluate the effectiveness of TXA for on traumatic brain injury.  

The methodology and data of this study differ from those of previous meta-analysis. First, we have only included patients with spontaneous ICH. Second, we have included two recent trials on this topic. Third, we conducted a trial sequence analysis of all primary outcomes to assess whether the information size was adequate.  

# 4.2 Study Implications  

To date, the clinical use of TXA is mainly based on a consensus among experts and on the experience of clinicians. Current guidelines from the American Heart Association/ American Stroke Association do not give recommendations for the administration of TXA in managing patients with spontaneous ICH [38]. Moreover, guidelines from the European Stroke Organization do not draw conclusive recommendations for TXA use in spontaneous ICH because of insufficient RCTs [39]. In this analysis, we have found highquality evidence that TXA treatment decreases hematoma expansion in patients with spontaneous ICH without adverse effects. The present meta-analysis should thus prompt reconsideration of this recommendation.  

The present analysis assessed TXA in adult patients with spontaneous ICH. However, children undergo a slightly different pattern of ICH than adults because of the variability in the anatomic, physiological, and developmental age spectrum that encompasses pediatric care [40]. Data regarding TXA use in children are mostly limited to those undergoing certain elective surgeries, notably cardiac surgery [41]. The determination of its dosage regimen is empirical, with an intravenous loading dose range from 10 to $1000~\mathrm{mg}$ and a maintenance infusion rate range from 1 to $100~\mathrm{{mg/kg/h}}$ [42]. While the evidence for TXA in pediatric ICH is not yet available, young children with hemodynamic instability and a potential risk for hemorrhage might benefit from TXA [43].  

# 4.3 Strength and Limitations  

Given the limited evidence analyzed by previous studies, the present meta-analysis is significant for its thorough search strategy, clear inclusion/exclusion criteria, reasonable use of the GRADE approach to rate overall quality of evidence, and precise application of the TSA to calculate the required information size for the primary outcomes.  

The present meta-analysis has some limitations. First, as the cumulative meta-analysis $Z$ curve did not cross the trial sequential monitoring boundary, the results of 90-day mortality may be inconclusive. However, all-cause mortality might not be an optimal outcome measure because of the effect of dilution from deaths unaffected or reduced by TXA. Future studies should report death within $^{24\textrm{h}}$ and death due to bleeding whenever possible. Second, although we included two recent trials (STOP-AUST and TRAIGE) on this topic, the weight of the point estimate was mostly attributed to the TICH-2 trial. Several ongoing trials including TICH-NOAC (NCT02866838) may be helpful. Third, publication bias was not detected in the present analysis because we only included six eligible trials. However, we believe that publication bias may be insignificant because most trials reported negative conclusions. Fourth, as most of the trials excluded patients with serious conditions (Glasgow Coma Scale $\leq8$ ), our conclusions may not be applicable to those patients.  

# 5  Conclusions  

In adults with spontaneous ICH, TXA reduced the risk of hematoma expansion and decreased hematoma volume compared with placebo. Its effect on 90-day mortality was inclusive as we were not able to reject a relative change of $20\%$ of 90-day mortality in the trial sequential analysis. However, it may not be an optimal outcome measure because of a dilution effect arising from deaths unaffected or reduced by TXA. Further studies should report death within $24\mathrm{~h~}$ and death due to bleeding whenever possible.
