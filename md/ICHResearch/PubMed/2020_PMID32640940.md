# Impact of Goal-Directed Therapy on Delayed Ischemia After Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage  

Randomized Controlled Trial  

Aida Anetsberger, MD; Jens Gempt , MD; Manfred Blobner, MD; Florian Ringel, MD; Ralf Bogdanski, MD; Markus Heim, MD; Gerhard Schneider, MD; Bernhard Meyer, MD; Sebastian Schmid, MD; Yu-Mi Ryang, MD; Maria Wostrack, MD; Jürgen Schneider, MD; Jan Martin, MD; Maximilian Ehrhardt; Bettina Jungwirth, MD  

BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) is the most important cause for a poor clinical outcome after a subarachnoid hemorrhage. The aim of this study was to assess whether goal-directed hemodynamic therapy (GDHT), as compared to standard clinical care, reduces the rate of DCI after subarachnoid hemorrhage.  

METHODS: We conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial. Patients ${>}18$ years of age with an aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage were enrolled and randomly assigned to standard therapy or GDHT. Advanced hemodynamic monitoring and predefined GDHT algorithms were applied in the GDHT group. The primary end point was the occurrence of DCI. Functional outcome was assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) 3 months after discharge.  

RESULTS: In total, 108 patients were randomized to the control $\mathtt{(n=54}$ ) or GDHT group $\mathtt{\Gamma}(\mathtt{n}{=}54\$ ). The primary outcome (DCI) occurred in $13\%$ of the GDHT group and in $32\%$ of the control group patients (odds ratio, 0.324 $[95\%$ CI, $0.11{-}0.86]$ ; $\widetilde{Pmathrm{=}0.021}$ ). Even after adjustment for confounding parameters, GDHT was found to be superior to standard therapy (hazard ratio, 2.84 $[95\%$ CI, 1.18–6.86]; $P=0.02.$ ). The GOS was assessed 3 months after discharge in 107 patients; it showed more patients with a low disability (GOS 5, minor or no deficits) than patients with higher deficits (GOS 1–4) in the GDHT group compared with the control group (GOS 5, $66\%$ versus $44\%$ ; GOS 1–4, $34\%$ versus $56\%$ ; $_{P=0.025}$ ). There was no significant difference in mortality between the groups.  

CONCLUSIONS: GDHT reduced the rate of DCI after subarachnoid hemorrhage with a better functional outcome $\mathtt{\tiny{GOS=5}}$ ) 3 months after discharge.

When evident vasospasm occurs, Triple-H therapy including hemodynamic augmentation methods in form of hypervolemia, hemodilution, and hypertension has been widely used, even though the evidence for single components is still missing.13–15 Overall, there are only few data from randomized controlled trials to guide strategies for reducing the risk of DCI, and ideal hemodynamic targets still remain unclear.11,16–20  

To address this uncertainty, we performed this trial to test the hypothesis that a goal-directed hemodynamic therapy (GDHT), as compared with standard clinical care, would reduce DCI after SAH. Second, the purpose was to study whether the described intervention could help to reduce further complications after SAH and thus improve the outcome.  



# METHODS  

We performed a prospective, randomized, controlled, parallel-group, single-center clinical trial at a university hospital in Germany (Klinikum rechts der Isar, Technical University Munich). The study was approved by the local ethics committee (ID: 5425-12) and was registered at the registration site of the US National Institutes of Health (https://www.clinicaltrials. gov; unique identifier: NCT01832389). The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.  



# Participants  

Patients ${>}18$ years of age with an aneurysmal SAH diagnosed by CT and confirmed by angiography were eligible for enrollment. Exclusion criteria were defined as follows: traumatic SAH, congestive heart failure, severe diseases of aorta or aortic valve, pregnancy, and calcium antagonist intolerance. A research team member informed the patients or their legal representative, respectively, about the course and potential risks of the study. After obtaining written consent, patients were enrolled in the study and randomized using a computer-generated list into either the GDHT group or the control group. Physicians and nurses were aware of the group assignment.  



# Intensive Care Unit Management in Both Groups  

Both groups were treated according to current guidelines.11 In both groups, the treatment goals included enteral nimodipine, normothermia, avoidance of hypo- or hyperglycemia, electrolyte balance, and adequate ventilation.12  

Until the aneurysm was secured, the goal was to keep systolic blood pressure below $160~\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ . Subsequently, maintenance of normovolemia was pursued (goals: mean arterial pressure [MAP] ${>}70\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ , cerebral perfusion pressure ${>}60$ $\mathsf{mmHg}$ , and central venous pressure $>4\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ ). In the presence of vasospasm, MAP was increased to 100 to $120\mathrm{mm}\mathsf{Hg}$ . If a vasopressor was needed, norepinephrine was favored.1 Balanced crystalloid infusions were administered for fluid therapy in both groups.  

In case of neurological impairment, increased intracranial pressure, or dilated pupils, a CT scan or further diagnostic evaluation was performed. All other aspects of perioperative care were carried out in accordance with the center’s usual practice in both groups. In both groups, transcranial Doppler was scheduled daily. All patients were monitored with a transpulmonary thermodilution monitor (PiCCO2; Pulsion Medical Systems SE, Feldkirchen, Germany), which was calibrated first after the insertion of catheters and then at least twice daily at the intensive care unit (ICU). In the absence of contraindications, an $8.5\mathsf{F}$ central venous catheter was placed in the internal jugular vein and a 5F thermistor-tipped catheter in the femoral artery. Transpulmonary thermodilution data were collected in a blinded manner within the control group and did not affect hemodynamic treatment.  

If DCI occurred in the territory of a major cerebral artery in spasm and showed no improvement with induced hypertension, then endovascular therapy and intra-arterial vasodilator treatment were considered in an interdisciplinary approach.12  



# Goal-Directed Therapy  

In the goal-directed group, advanced hemodynamic monitoring including transpulmonary thermodilution was performed to direct the volume therapy. The algorithms used are shown in Figure I in the Data Supplement. The hemodynamic protocol was initiated after the insertion of the probe and continued for 14 days or, if patients were transmitted to a regular ward earlier, until discharge from the ICU. In the absence of vasospasm, fluid management focused on maintenance of normovolemia. Next to close monitoring of MAP, the cardiac index, global enddiastolic index (GEDI), and extravascular lung water index were used to optimize volume, vasopressor, and inotropes therapy. Physicians could discontinue adherence to designated values in the case of nonresponders or adverse events but had to document the reason for the contravention.  



# Outcomes  

The primary outcome was the occurrence of DCI. DCI was defined as a new focal neurological deficit or a cerebral infarction in the presence of vasospasm revealed by radiological imaging or both.1 A $^{\ge2}$ -point decrease in the Glasgow  

Coma Scale or a 2-point increase in the score of the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale lasting for at least 8 hours was used for detecting DCI.21 Other causes of neurological aggravation such as hydrocephalus, rebleeding, seizures, or metabolic impairment, as well as procedure-related infarctions, had to be excluded.10,22  

Several secondary outcomes were defined, including vasospasm (diagnosed by transcranial Doppler: peak value increase by ${>}50~{\mathsf{cm}}/{\mathsf{s}}$ per 24 hours compared with the previous result or a mean value ${>}120~\mathrm{cm/s}$ in one of the main supply branches) and other neurological events23; the occurrence of pulmonary, cardiovascular, metabolic, renal/hepatic events; sepsis; the duration of stay at the ICU or the hospital; mortality; the need for vasopressors; and the amount of fluid administered. A detailed description of the secondary outcome measures is given in Table I in the Data Supplement. A neurosurgeon blinded to group assignment evaluated the in-hospital outcomes.  

Functional outcome was assessed 3 months after discharge using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS).24 The participants were usually seen in the neurosurgical outpatient department by a neurosurgical consultant unaware of group assignment; otherwise they were contacted by telephone.  



# Statistical Analysis  

Sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome DCI. From literature, it is known that DCI after SAH occurs in about one-third of patients with standard clinical care.12 We postulated a relative reduction of DCI by $50\%$ in the GDHT group compared with the control group.20 Given an error probability of $5\%$ , power of $80\%,$ and a sampling ratio of 1 (1:1 randomization), 108 subjects were needed to demonstrate a statistically significant difference.  

Analysis was performed on an intention-to-treat basis. Variables are presented as means and SDs or as medians and interquartile ranges, if distributions were skewed. Data obtained by thermodilution were compared between the groups in different phases using the median of each parameter: early phase representing the period before the vasospasm onset, the phase of vasospasm onset, and a late phase after vasospasm. Additionally, goal achievement rates defined as the ratio of samples within the target range of the algorithm were calculated for the different phases and compared between the groups. To assess differences in volume management over time, fluid balance and fluid input were compared during the active study period (days 1–3, days 4–7, and a late phase on days 8–14).  

To assess differences between the groups, categorical variables were compared using a $\chi^{2}$ test or a log-rank test for time-dependent variables. Differences between continuous variables were obtained using a 2-sided Student t test or a Mann-Whitney $U$ test for nonparametric variables.  

Cox regression analysis was performed to estimate the hazard ratio with $95\%$ CI for occurrence of DCI in the control group compared with the GDHT group. Potential confounders were selected based on published data to adjust the primary effect of the study.4 A Cox regression analysis was performed, adjusted for age, sex, surgical procedure, Hunt-Hess grade, World Federation of Neurological Surgeons grade, and modified Fisher scale, taking into consideration the amount of blood in the initial CT scan.5–7 All statistical tests were performed using the software package SPSS statistics 25. $R{<}0.05$ was considered statistically significant.  



# RESULTS  

Patients were screened for eligibility between March 2013 and December 2015. A total of 117 patients consented to take part in the study, of whom 108 underwent randomization (Figure  1). The baseline characteristics were similar in both groups (Table 1).  

The median age was 55 (47–69) years, and $76\%$ were women. Both groups were comparable considering the Hunt-Hess Grade, World Federation of Neurological Surgeons Grade, the severity of SAH on the initial CT scan evaluated by the modified Fisher scale, and American Society of Anesthesiologists physiological status. There were more patients with arterial hypertension in the GDHT group, whereas other medical conditions were comparable in both groups. In addition, $55\%$ of patients underwent coiling, and $45\%$ of aneurysms were clipped with no significant differences between the groups (Table 2).  



# GDHT and Hemodynamic Profiles  

No serious adverse events were reported due to GDHT intervention. One patient in the GDHT group was reported not to reach the designated GEDI values despite several fluid challenges.  

In 4 cases in the GDHT group and 6 cases in the control group, it was not possible to read the digital data, and GDHT data were not documented in the ICU chart. Therefore, data obtained by thermodilution were compared between 50 patients in the GDHT group and 48 patients in the control group in different phases and are presented in Table 2. There was no significant difference between the groups considering MAP, GEDI, and extravascular lung water index in none of the phases. At the late vasospasm phase, cardiac index was slightly higher in the control group compared with the GDHT group (median [interquartile range], 3.6 [3.3–4.2] versus 3.2 [3–3.4]; $P{=}0.009,$ ). Goal- achievement rates indicating the ratio of values within the designated range were higher in the GDHT group compared with the control group for MAP $(92\%$ versus $82\%$ ; $P\mathrm{<}0.001$ ) and GEDI $83\%$ versus $72\%$ ; $_{P=0.003}$ ) in patients without vasospasm or before onset of vasospasm. In the late vasospasm phase, goal achievement rates for GEDI were lower in the GDHT group compared with control $(65\%$ versus $76\%$ ; $\scriptstyle{\mathsf{P=0.033}}$ ) with comparable goal achievement values for MAP, cardiac index, and extravascular lung water index. Similar to overall daily fluid input shown in Table 3, the comparison of daily fluid input over time showed no differences between the GDHT group and control (Figure 2). The comparison of daily fluid balance over time demonstrated a difference in the early phase (days 1–3) between GDHT and control group though (median [interquartile range], $-286~\mathsf{mL}$ [−830 to 205] versus $179\mathrm{mL}$ [−527 to 734]; $_{P=0.017}$ ). There was no difference in daily fluid balance on days 4 to 7 and 8 to 14 between the GDHT group and control (Figure 2).

Analysis of secondary outcomes revealed no differences in the rates of vasospasm between the groups (Table 3). Vasospasm occurred on day 4 (3–6) and was present in all 24 patients developing DCI. The rates of cerebral edema ( $15\%$ versus $30\%$ ; ${\ttP=}0.064.$ ) and infarctions or hypodensities that were not clearly attributable to DCI were reduced in the GDHT group $2\%$ versus $11\%$ ; $P_{=0.051}$ ) without reaching statistical significance though. # DISCUSSION  

The major finding of this study is that the use of GDHT significantly reduces the rate of DCI after SAH. The association was present even after adjustment for confounding variables. There were significantly more patients with favorable functional outcome $(\mathsf{GOS}{=}5)$ ) 3 months after discharge in the GDHT group compared with the control group.  

Hemodynamic optimization has been an object of great interest in neurosurgical and perioperative research, and diverse case studies have already explored the benefit of optimal management after an SAH.15,25–27 Although avoidance of hypovolemia and hyponatremia is recommended in therapy to treat an SAH, there are no instructions regarding how these goals could be achieved to date.1 Despite several studies reporting the utility of advanced hemodynamic monitoring in relation to an SAH, there are only few data from randomized controlled trials to guide strategies in hemodynamic optimization.15,27–29 Although central venous pressure was found to be unreliable in indicating volume status, no specific technology has been determined to be superior in hemodynamic management.30  

Our findings are in line with other studies that show favorable outcomes for patients receiving GDHT after an SAH.18,20 Our approach differs from published data, as our algorithm does not involve hyperdynamic therapy or volume expansion using hydroxyethyl starch.20 Based on current guidelines and widely used clinical practice, induced hypertension was included in our algorithm.31 The evidence for this approach is still lacking, however, as the only randomized controlled trial exploring this issue was terminated due to slow recruitment and a higher rate of adverse events.15 Nevertheless, no cases of myocardial ischemia or increased risk for cardiac dysfunction were observed in this study.  

Similar to other studies showing favorable outcome of GDHT, we found no differences in amounts of the overall fluid intake or in the number of patients who received vasopressors or inotropes between the 2 strategies.32,33 Besides, there were no significant differences considering MAP, GEDI, or extravascular lung water index between the groups with even higher cardiac index levels in the control group in some phases. Interestingly, the approach seems to differ with and without an algorithm though. The comparison of the amount of norepinephrine administered revealed higher rates in the control group compared with the GDHT group. Therefore, it is possible that the therapy in the control group was rather vasopressor driven. Further, it is conceivable that the beneficial effect of GDHT could be due to the right timing of fluid administration ensuring euvolemia and optimizing oxygen delivery even before an evident vasospasm. The comparison of the 2 strategies revealed higher daily fluid balance rates in the early ICU phase (days 1–3) in the control group compared with GDHT. This observation is comparable to other trials indicating a potential association of the fluid assessment in the early ICU phase and DCI.34 Finally, the observation of higher MAP and GEDI achievement rates in the GDHT group in the phases without vasospasm combined with a significantly lower fluid balance compared with control in the early ICU phase suggests that without an algorithm, volume therapy is undirected.

Further, recent evidence indicates different vascular and neural changes that lead to DCI, even in the absence of vasospasm.35 In our study, the overall infarction rate is also noticeably higher in the control group—independent of vessel narrowing. This finding supports the scientific approach that considers a complex pathophysiology behind DCI involving microcirculatory dysfunction and impaired autoregulation.31 In addition to the optimization of macrocirculatory parameters, GDHT optimizes volume status and might consequently lead to an improved microcirculation.36 Anyway, vasospasm doubtlessly remains crucial in the development of DCI. In contrast to previously published data, however, we cannot confirm a reduction of vasospasm by GDHT.20 Further, the lengths of ICU and hospital stays were similar between the groups. This might be explained by several organizational and logistic factors that influence data regarding length of stay.

Finally, our study suggests an algorithm based on the best available evidence that focuses on normovolemia and induced hypertension. Although a shift from the paradigm of fixed hemodynamic goals to a personalized GDHT has been reported in recent research, the enclosed algorithm helped optimizing volume therapy in our study.36 Further pragmatic trials are needed to confirm the significance of GDHT in relation to an SAH.

After adjustment for relevant risk factors in a forward (likelihood ratio) stepwise manner, GDHT was found to be superior compared with standard care (hazard ratio, 2.8 $[95\%$ CI, $1.18\substack{-6.86}$ ; $P=0.02,$ ). Age, sex, surgical procedure, Hunt-Hess grade, World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade, and modified Fisher Scale were included as potential confounders. DCI indicates delayed cerebral ischemia.  

The main limitation of our trial was the inability to keep physicians, nurses, and investigators unaware of the group assignment. However, the use of objective end points, such as infarction and functional outcome 3 months after discharge, reduced detection bias. Moreover, our trial is a single-center study with the potential limitation that it is performed by a skilled team that does not test the adoption of the protocol into daily clinical practice in other clinical centers. Due to the nonblinded treatment, potential adverse events related to the study were monitored by the ICUs; an independent data and safety monitoring board was not involved. Further, this study does not investigate the mechanisms underlying the development of DCI, nor does it assess microcirculation parameters. Finally, discharge criteria were not predefined, which might influence the interpretation of the length-of-stay parameters.  



# CONCLUSIONS  

GDHT reduces the rate of DCI and improves the functional outcome after an SAH. This is the first study suggesting a suitable algorithm focusing on normovolemia and, in the presence of vasospasm, induced hypertension. Further research is needed in this field to confirm the protocol in different clinical settings and to develop personalized GDHT after an SAH.
