# Endovascular treatment versus standard medical treatment for vertebrobasilar artery occlusion (BEST): an open-label, randomised controlled trial

# Summary  

Background Previous randomised trials have shown an overwhelming benefit of mechanical thrombectomy for treating patients with stroke caused by large vessel occlusion of the anterior circulation. Whether endovascular treatment is beneficial for vertebrobasilar artery occlusion remains unknown. In this study, we aimed to investigate the safety and efficacy of endovascular treatment of acute strokes due to vertebrobasilar artery occlusion.  

Methods We did a multicentre, randomised, open-label trial, with blinded outcome assessment of thrombectomy in patients presenting within $^{81}$ of vertebrobasilar occlusion at 28 centres in China. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to endovascular therapy plus standard medical therapy (intervention group) or standard medical therapy alone (control group). The randomisation sequence was computer-generated and stratified by participating centres. Allocation concealment was implemented by use of sealed envelopes. The primary outcome was a modified Rankin scale (mRS) score of 3 or lower (indicating ability to walk unassisted) at 90 days, assessed on an intention-to-treat basis. The primary safety outcome was mortality at 90 days. Secondary safety endpoints included the rates of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage, device-related complications, and other severe adverse events. The BEST trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02441556.  

Findings Between April 27, 2015, and Sept 27, 2017, we assessed 288 patients for eligibility. The trial was terminated early after 131 patients had been randomly assigned (66 patients to the intervention group and 65 to the control group) because of high crossover rate and poor recruitment. In the intention-to-treat analysis, there was no evidence of a difference in the proportion of participants with mRS 0–3 at 90 days according to treatment (28 $[42\%]$ of 66 patients in the intervention group vs 21 $[32\%]$ of 65 in the control group; adjusted odds ratio [OR] 1·74, $95\%$ CI $\mathbf{0\cdot81-3\cdot74})$ . Secondary prespecified analyses of the primary outcome, done to assess the effect of crossovers, showed higher rates of mRS 0–3 at 90 days in patients who actually received the intervention compared with those who received standard medical therapy alone in both per-protocol $(28[44\%]$ of 63 patients with intervention vs 13 $[25\%]$ of 51 with standard therapy; adjusted OR 2·90, $95\%$ CI $\mathbf{1}{\cdot}2\mathbf{0}{-}7{\cdot}03$ ) and as-treated $(36[47\%]$ of 77 patients with intervention vs 13 $[24\%]$ of 54 with standard therapy; 3·02, 1·31–7·00) populations. The 90-day mortality was similar between groups (22 $[33\%]$ of 66 patients in the intervention vs 25 $[38\%]$ of 65 in the control group; $\mathbf{p=}0\cdot54!$ ) despite a numerically higher prevalence of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage in the intervention group.  

Interpretation There was no evidence of a difference in favourable outcomes of patients receiving endovascular therapy compared with those receiving standard medical therapy alone. Results might have been confounded by loss of equipoise over the course of the trial, resulting in poor adherence to the assigned study treatment and a reduced sample size due to the early termination of the study.

# Introduction

Whether endovascular treatment is beneficial for acute vertebrobasilar artery occlusion remains unknown. # Research in context  

# Evidence before the study  

We searched PubMed with no language restrictions for papers published from Jan 1, 2010, to June 30, 2019, using the search terms “endovascular therapy”, “intra-arterial treatment”, or “mechanical thrombectomy” crossed with “basilar artery occlusion” or “vertebrobasilar artery occlusion”, and “randomized controlled trial”. We identified only one previous randomised trial for vertebrobasilar occlusion. However, this trial used an obsolete treatment method (urokinase infusion) and ended prematurely because of poor recruitment, with only 16 participants. A few non-randomised prospective studies of intra-arterial treatment for vertebrobasilar occlusion have been reported. Although some of these studies have included controls, they had many methodological limitations and were unable to provide any definite evidence regarding the specific benefit of endovascular therapy for patients with acute vertebrobasilar occlusions. We did not find any randomised controlled trials of modern thrombectomy devices for the treatment of acute vertebrobasilar occlusion.  

# Added value of this study  

To our knowledge, our study is the first randomised controlled trial to assess the effect of contemporary endovascular therapy, including stent-retriever-based mechanical thrombectomy, in the treatment of acute vertebrobasilar occlusion. The early termination of the study due to drop in valid recruitment and high crossover rate is consistent with the expected loss of equipoise in face of the several trials showing an overwhelming benefit of thrombectomy in anterior circulation strokes, as well as the known poor outcomes of patients with untreated vertebrobasilar occlusions with severe presentation. Despite not meeting its prespecified primary endpoint on intention-to-treat analysis, the secondary prespecified per-protocol and as-treated analyses of our trial provided some evidence to show that patients undergoing endovascular therapy might have better outcomes than those managed with standard medical treatment alone.  

# Implications of all the available evidence  

The overall evidence points to a benefit of endovascular reperfusion of large vessel occlusion strokes. Our data confirm the poor outcomes of untreated patients presenting with acute vertebrobasilar occlusion and severe clinical findings, and support the overall safety and potential efficacy of early endovascular treatment in this patient population. As seen in our trial, equipoise in this context has been lost in most clinical settings where mechanical thrombectomy can be safely done.

In the Basilar Artery Occlusion Endovascular Intervention versus Standard Medical Treatment (BEST) trial, we aimed to compare endovascular treatment plus standard medical therapy with standard medical therapy alone for the treatment of patients with acute ischae­mic stroke due to vertebrobasilar occlusion within $^{\mathrm{~8~h~}}$ of estimated occlusion time.  

# Methods  

# Study design and participants  

The BEST trial was a multicentre, prospective, open-label, randomised controlled trial with blinded assessment of endpoints, done at 28 stroke centres in China. The trial protocol was approved by the institutional review board of each participating site. The trial was designed and conducted by a steering committee, which was composed of inde­ pendent academic investigators and statisticians. The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are available in the appendix (pp 1–111).  

Patients were eligible for inclusion in the trial if they had an acute ischaemic stroke consistent with acute occlusion of the basilar artery, could be randomly assigned within $^{8\mathrm{~h~}}$ of estimated occlusion time, and had occlusion of the basilar artery confirmed by CT angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, or digital subtraction angiography. Patients with occlusion of the distal intracranial vertebral artery (V4 segment) resulting in no flow to the basilar artery (eg, functional basilar artery occlusion) were also included in the trial. The estimated occlusion time was defined as the sudden onset of stroke symptoms consist­ ent with acute occlusion of the basilar artery (eg, not considering any preceding minor prodromal symptoms), as adjudicated by two local experienced neurologists. Other inclusion criteria were age 18 years or older, a prestroke score of 0–2 on the modified Rankin scale (mRS; range 0–6, with a score of 0 indicating no disability and higher scores indicating more severe disability), and no evidence of intracranial haemorrhage, significant cerebellar mass effect, acute hydrocephalus, or extensive bilateral brainstem ischaemia on CT or MRI. Patients either did not meet the usual criteria for treat­ment with intravenous alteplase or received treatment with intra­ venous alteplase and had persistent basilar occlusion at the time that they were eligible for enrolment in the trial. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the appendix (p 122). Information on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, interventions, and assessments has been published previously.10 Written informed consent was obtained from enrolled patients or their surrogates.  

# Randomisation and masking  

Participants were allocated (1:1 ratio) to receive either endo­ vascular treatment plus standard medical therapy (intervention group) or standard medical therapy alone (control group). The randomisation sequence was computergenerated (SAS Statistical Package, version 9.3) and stratified by participating centres; this was done by a statistician who did not directly participate in trial recruitment and enrolment. Allocation concealment was implemented by use of sealed envelopes. Each participat­ ing centre was required to do at least one roll-in patient (ie, a patient who underwent the treatment assessed by the trial) before the randomisation phase, and each neuro­ interventionist had to have completed at least five mechanical thrombectomy procedures with stent retriever devices in the preceding year.  

# Procedures  

In the intervention and control groups, patients received intravenous alteplase if they met the criteria for intra­ venous thrombolysis within $4{\cdot}5\mathrm{~h~}$ of stroke symptom onset (defined as the time the patient was last seen well [eg, by a family member or co-worker in case the event was not witnessed], not the estimated time of acute vertebro­ basilar artery occlusion), as per existing guidelines. In the intervention group, endovascular preparation was initiated simultaneously with or soon after intravenous alteplase administration was started. The endovascular procedure consisted of mechanical thrombectomy with stent retriever (the preferred method) or thrombo-aspiration devices. Patients in both groups were admitted to stroke units or intensive care units and received standard medical therapy, which followed the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines.11  

The neurointerventionist attempted to recanalise the occlusive vessel until successful reperfusion (modified Thrombolysis In Cerebral Infarction [mTICI] 2b $[50\%$ to $99\%$ reperfusion] or 3 [complete reperfusion])12 was achieved or until the maximum number of passes recom­ mended by the manufacturer’s instructions for use had been done. Intra-arterial thrombolysis, balloon angio­ plasty, or stenting were used as rescue therapy at the discretion of the neurointerventionist. If recanalisation was not achieved within $10\mathrm{~h~}$ after stroke onset, term­ ination of the procedure was recommended. Patients underwent a clinical examination including assessment of the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score $24\textrm{h}$ after randomisation. Patients also underwent CT angiography or magnetic resonance angiography to evaluate the recanalisation rate and CT or MRI to assess the change of posterior circulation Acute Stroke Prognosis Early CT score (pc-ASPECTS) and the presence of intracerebral haemorrhage. At 5–7 days (or at discharge if earlier), NIHSS score, GCS score, and adverse events were evalu­ ated. At day 90, mRS scale scores were computed along with any adverse events.  

# Outcomes  

The primary efficacy outcome was the proportion of patients with mRS score of 0–3 (with 0 meaning no symptoms, 1 able to do all usual activities, 2 able to look after own affairs without assistance but unable to do all previous activities, and 3 requires some help but able to walk unassisted) at 90 days. The score was assessed in person, or by telephone if an in-person visit was not possible, by a certified rater who was not aware of the trial group assignments.

# Statistical analysis  

The primary data analysis was done on the intention-totreat population. To assess the effect of crossovers between treatment groups, we did secondary prespecified analyses in the per-protocol population (patients treated according to the randomisation assignment [excluding crossovers] and did not violate the inclusion or exclusion criteria or had significant protocol deviations) and the as-treated population (based on the actual treatment received, regardless of the randomisation assignment). We calculated that a sample size of 344 patients (172 patients in each group) would detect a $15\%$ difference $45\%$ for the intervention and $30\%$ for the control group) in the proportion of patients achieving $\mathrm{mRS}$ score of 0–3, with a power of $80\%$ and two-sided significance levels of 0·05 for the analysis of the primary outcome. For the primary outcome variable, we compared the dichotomised mRS outcome $\mathrm{(mRS\leq3~\nus~mRS~}{>}3\mathrm{)}$ using the $\chi^{2}$ test between the intervention group and control group. We used the Student’s $t$ test or the Mann-Whitney $U$ test to compare the NIHSS and GCS score changes between both groups. Furthermore, we used a prespecified multivariable reg­ ression model to adjust for any potential imbalances in prognostic factors across the two groups, including age, baseline NIHSS score, and any variables with $\scriptstyle{\mathrm{p}}<0\cdot10$ on univariable analysis. The primary and secondary safety outcomes were compared between the two groups by use of the $\chi^{2}$ test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All analyses were done with the SPSS software package, version 22. Two-sided p values of $<0\cdot05$ were considered statistically significant. Decisions related to safety and trial discontinuation were made at the recommendation of an independent data and safety monitoring board. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02441556.

# Results  

Between April 27, 2015, and Sept 27, 2017, we assessed 288 patients for trial eligibility. Of these, 131 $(45\%)$ were enrolled and randomly assigned to the intervention group (66 patients) or control group (65 patients; figure 1). The study was terminated prematurely by the steering com­ mittee according to the recommendation of the data and safety monitoring board because of excessive crossovers and progressive drop in the average rate of valid percentre recruitment (appendix pp 120–21). In the control group, 14 $(22\%)$ of 65 patients ended up receiving endovascular treatment because patients’ families did not accept only standard medical therapy after randomisation despite providing informed consent. In the intervention group, three $(5\%)$ of 66 patients received only standard medical therapy (figure 1). No patients were lost to follow-up throughout the 90-day evaluation period. In the intention-to-treat population, baseline characteristics between the two groups were well balanced (table 1). Baseline characteristics were generally balanced in the per-protocol and as-treated populations, except for age in the as-treated population (median 70 years in the control group vs 62 years in the intervention group; appendix pp 124–25).

For the primary endpoint analysis in the intention-totreat population, we found no evidence of a difference in the proportion of patients with a favourable neurological outcome $(\mathrm{mRS~}0{-}3)$ at 90 days ( $28[42\%]$ of 66 patients in the intervention group vs 21 $[32\%]$ of 65 in the control group, $\mathrm{p}{=}0\cdot23$ ; adjusted odds ratio [OR] 1·74, $95\%$ CI 0·81–3·74; table 2, table 3). We observed a significant interaction between treatment and site of occlusion, with patients with vertebral artery occlusion faring better with standard medical treatment alone than with intervention, and patients with basilar artery occlusion faring better with intervention treatment than with standard medical treatment alone (appendix p 133). This finding might have been the result of a type I error, given the small number of vertebral occlusions. We found no evidence of differences in the effect of thrombectomy in any other of the prespecified subgroup analyses (appendix p 133). However, the small sample size resulting from the early termination of the trial and the high rate of crossovers reduced the power of these analyses.  

Regarding technical results, 77 patients received endo­ vascular treatment. Stent retriever thrombectomy was done in 64 patients $(83\%)$ , four patients $(5\%)$ were treated the treatment of basilar artery occlusion within $^\textrm{\scriptsize8h}$ of estimated occlusion time. However, our results might have been confounded by the fact that equipoise was lost over the course of the study, as shown by the high crossover rates and progressive drop in the average rate of valid per-centre recruitment, which eventually led to the early termination of the trial. This limited our final sample size to only 131 $(38\%)$ of our planned target of 344 patients, resulting in an underpowered analysis. Moreover, as sug­ gested by the per-protocol and as-treated analyses, our high rate of crossovers (17 $[13\%]$ of 131 patients) might have lowered the treatment effect size. This culminated at a $10\%$ difference in the rate of $\mathrm{mRS}0{-}3$ at 90 days, which was lower than our initial assumption of a $15\%$ dif­ ference. Finally, the fact that a third of the qualifying patients declined trial participation might have introduced selection biases that also contributed to our negative findings.

Our findings should be considered in the context of the best evidence that is available. Despite that the first study showing improved outcomes in basilar artery occlusion after intra-arterial recanalisation is from 30 years ago,13 a striking paucity of high-quality data on the natural history and treatment outcomes on basilar artery occlusion still exists. The only other available randomised clinical trial of intra-arterial treatment for basilar artery occlusion used an outdated treatment method (urokinase infusion) and was terminated prematurely with only 16 patients.7 The Basilar Artery International Cooperation Study (BASICS) was a prospective registry that enrolled 619 patients over the course of 5 years.5 Treatment methods included antithrombotic treatment alone (antiplatelet or systemic anticoagulation; $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n=1}83$ ), primary intravenous throm­ bolysis (including any subsequent intra-arterial therapy; $\scriptstyle\mathrm{n=1}21\mathrm{!}$ ), or intra-arterial therapy alone $ (\mathrm{n}{=}288)$ , with 245 patients presenting with mild-to-moderate deficits and 347 with severe clinical deficits. No statistically signifi­ cant superiority was found for any treatment strategy in avoiding unfavourable outcomes (1-month mRS 4–6). The BASICS study did not support superiority of intra-arterial approaches over intravenous thrombolysis; however, an adjusted analysis showed that patients with a severe deficit had significantly worse outcomes with anti­ thrombotics alone than with intravenous or intra-arterial reperfusion methods (relative risk 0·88, $95\%$ CI 0·81–0·96).5 Notably, the treatment effect size of intraarterial treatment versus antithrombotics alone was significantly higher in patients presenting with severe deficits than in those with mild-to-moderate deficits. This result becomes relevant to our trial, because patients enrolled in BEST generally had high clinically severity on presentation (median baseline NIHSS 32, IQR 18-38, in the intervention group vs 26, 13–37, in the control group). Studies enrolling patients with lower symptom severity will probably require larger sample sizes.

# Discussion  

On the basis of its prespecified intention-to-treat analysis, the BEST trial did not show a benefit of mechanical thrombectomy over standard medical therapy alone for  

The BASICS registry highlights the poor outcomes of basilar artery occlusion, including an existent, but limited, response to intravenous thrombolysis. However, as the study was concluded 10 years ago and, there­ fore, considerably before stent-retrievers and large-bore thromboaspiration systems became available, its findings are not applicable to contemporary clinical practice. For example, initial randomised trials of endovascular therapy in the anterior circulation using intra-arterial lytics and first-generation mechanical devices did not show a benefit of endovascular therapy over medical treatment alone;14–16 however, subsequent studies, largely based on stentretriever technology, showed unequivocal superiority.1–4 Indeed, meta-analyses, published in 2016, of stentretriever studies for the treatment of basilar artery occlus­ ion showed a proportion of $42\%$ of patients achieving functional independence and $30\%$ mortality at 90 days.8,9 In the Trevo Registry, a prospective, open-label, multi­ centre, international registry concluded in 2017 that included 2008 patients undergoing stent-retriever thromb­ ectomy as first-line therapy, 121 patients had vertebrobasilar occlusions. $34\cdot7\%$ of these patients received intravenous alteplase and $44\cdot6\%$ started treatment at least $^{8\mathrm{~h~}}$ after stroke onset. The proportion of patients achieving 90-day functional independence was $50\cdot4\%$ , and the 90-day mortality was $21\cdot5\%$ .17 These results are substantially better than the $17\cdot4\%$ proportion of functional inde­ pendence at 30 days observed with intra-arterial therapy in BASICS. A study published in 2018 suggested that outcomes in basilar artery occlusion might be similar or better with the first-line use of direct aspiration than with stent-retrievers.18 Our interventional outcomes after the exclusion of crossovers (secondary analyses) are within a similar range of what was reported on these stent-retriever meta-analyses.  

Conversely, in the absence of reperfusion treatment, outcomes in patients with basilar artery occlusion pre­ senting with severe deficits are known to be extremely poor. In BASICS, 26 $(96\%)$ of 27 patients presenting with coma or tetraplegia and not receiving antithrombotic or reperfusion therapy were dead at 1 month, with the remaining patient having a mRS of 5.5 In another study, 24 $(83\%)$ of 29 patients with severe presentation (median NIHSS 36, IQR 27–38) who were treated conservatively died before 90 days and only two $(7\%)$ had a mRS of 0–3 at 90 days.19 As seen in our trial, this striking contrast between the accumulating evidence of favourable outcomes with modern endovascular treatment and the notoriously poor natural history of basilar artery occlusion with severe clinical presentation has made treatment randomisation in this patient population increasingly challenging.  

Basilar artery occlusion has a pleomorphic presentation, ranging from mild symptoms such as isolated cranial nerve deficits, dysarthria, or vertigo, all the way to quadri­ plegia, locked-in syndrome, coma, and death. Outcomes are influenced by several factors including age, clinical severity and imaging extent of ischaemia on presentation, collateral flow, duration of ischaemia, and thrombus burden and location.6,20 Despite our overall negative results, secondary analyses of the patients who were actually treated versus those not treated with thrombectomy showed significantly better outcomes with endovascular therapy, even after adjustment for age and baseline stroke severity. However, although incomplete adherence to study regimen and treatment crossovers might result in an effective intervention appearing to be ineffective, the use of as-treated or per-protocol populations is widely known to introduce selection bias because patients who do not adhere to the treatment protocol and those who crossover to the other treatment strategy might have a different prognosis that is unrelated to actual treatment. This obviously hinders any definite conclusions regarding treatment efficacy that are not solely based on the intentionto-treat principle.21 Our results are of unique interest to the medical community, given the scarcity of prospective randomised controlled data regarding the treatment of basilar artery occlusion and the growing loss of equipoise in face of the overwhelming benefit of thrombectomy in patients with anterior circulation strokes, coupled with the well-established poor outcomes in patients with basilar artery occlusion with severe clinical presentation.  

Two additional randomised trials in patients with basilar artery occlusion are currently ongoing. The Dutchinitiated Basilar Artery International Cooperation Study (BASICS, NCT01717755) aims to assess the benefit of endovascular intervention within $6\mathrm{~h~}$ of estimated time of basilar artery occlusion.22 By contrast with BEST, where the time window for intravenous thrombolysis was kept at $4{\cdot}5\mathrm{~h~}$ from time last seen well, BASICS has opted to extend the intravenous thrombolysis window to $4{\cdot}5\mathrm{~h~}$ from the estimated time of basilar artery occlusion. The trial started in 2011 and, as of Aug 6, 2019, has enrolled 280 patients across seven countries. The overall slow recruitment rate of BASICS further highlights the existent ethical challenges surrounding randomisation of patients to non-endovascular treatment in this population. Moreover, the fact that BASICS has not yet ended, despite multiple interim analyses, raises the possibility that their more inclusive criteria for treatment with intra­ venous thrombolysis might have led to better outcomes in patients in their control group compared with those in the control group of our trial, which used the traditional and more conservative intravenous thrombolysis approach. The Basilar Artery Occlusion Chinese Endovascular Trial (NCT02737189) focuses on patients presenting within $6{-}24\mathrm{h}$ of symptoms onset. As of Aug 6, 2019, 125 patients have been randomly assigned, of a total planned sample size of 318 patients to show a treatment difference of $20\%$ . We hope that these studies will shed additional light on this crucial issue and will be able to address the considerable  logistical  compromises in  trial  conduct caused by crossovers.  

In conclusion, our study did not provide evidence of a difference in favourable neurological outcomes in patients treated with endovascular therapy plus stand­ ard medical therapy compared with those treated with standard medical therapy alone. Our results might have been confounded by its reduced sample size and the poor adherence to the assigned study treatment, because patients with basilar artery occlusion who were treated with thrombectomy had better outcomes than those who received standard medical therapy alone.
