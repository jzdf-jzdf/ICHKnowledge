# Non‑Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulants for the Treatment of Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis: a Retrospective, Matched Cohort Analysis  

James A. Giles $1^{*}\oplus$ Vamshi K. S. Balasetti2 and Allyson R. Zazulia1,3  

$\circledcirc$ 2021 Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature and Neurocritical Care Society  

# Abstract  

Background:  The management of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVT) is a common problem facing vascular neurologists. American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines suggest the use of heparin followed by vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) for anticoagulation in CVT. In recent years, the evidence base has solidified for the use of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) in lower extremity deep vein thrombosis. Because data supporting their use in CVT are limited, with the strongest evidence comprising one randomized controlled trial of dabigatran, we sought to review our experience with NOACs in the treatment of CVT at a tertiary care center to address efficacy and safety.  

Methods:  We retrospectively reviewed charts of all patients with CVT treated with an NOAC at our tertiary care facility in the years 2011–2019. We collected data on demographics, risk factors for CVT, clinical features at presentation, imaging results, anticoagulation regimen, bleeding complications, and disability at follow-up. We compared disability at follow-up and major hemorrhagic events with age-matched and sex-matched controls treated with VKAs over the same time period and with historical controls.  

Results:  We identified 29 patients with CVT treated with an NOAC, 27 of whom had follow-up within our system. NOACs that were used for treatment included apixaban (20 patients), rivaroxaban (6 patients) and dabigatran (1 patient). NOAC use was associated with stabilization of a clot or partial recanalization in $55.6\%$ of patients and complete recanalization in $14.8\%$ at a median follow-up time of 6 months. The median modified Rankin Score (mRS) at follow-up was 0, with one death. Three patients $(11.1\%)$ had major bleeding complications, including two with symptomatic worsening of intracranial hemorrhage. Comparisons of 27 age-matched and sex-matched controls treated with VKAs showed no significant differences in terms of partial recanalization $55.6\%$ vs. $63.0\%,$ $p{=}0.29,$ ), complete recanalization ( $14.8\%$ vs. $25.9\%,p=0.73$ ), mRS at follow-up (median 0 vs. 0, $p{=}0.23.$ ), or major bleeding ( $11.1\%$ vs. $11.1\%,p>0.99;$ . Comparisons with the historical International Study on Cerebral Vein and Dural Sinus Thrombosis cohort showed similar functional outcomes: $92.6\%$ of patients treated with NOACs and $88.9\%$ of patients treated with VKAs at the Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, as well as $86.2\%$ of patients treated with VKAs in the historical study cohort, had mRS of 0–2 at follow-up $(p=0.60)$ . Rates of major bleeding compared with this cohort were also similar ( $11.1\%$ vs. $11.1\%$ vs. $14.5\%,p=0.80)$ .  

Conclusions:  The safety and efficacy results of NOAC use for CVT were similar to those for age-matched and sexmatched controls treated with VKAs, as well as historical published controls. Assessment of NOAC efficacy and safety in CVT in multicenter cohort studies and randomized controlled trials is warranted.  

Keywords:  Cerebrovascular disorders, Sinus thrombosis (intracranial), Anticoagulants, Factor Xa inhibitors  

# Introduction  

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVT) is a relatively rare form of thrombotic disease, with an incidence of 3–15 per million person-years [1–4]. After diagnosis, current guidelines recommend acute treatment with parenteral heparin followed by transition to oral anticoagulation with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) for 3–12  months, depending on the clinical context [5, 6]. Despite regulatory approval for and the widespread use of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) for systemic venous thromboembolism (VTE) based on clinical trial evidence, data for their use in CVT are lacking. Therefore, NOACs are currently not recommended for the treatment of CVT. A recent randomized controlled trial compared dabigatran to dose-adjusted warfarin and found equivalently low risks of recurrent VTE in both groups, as well as similar bleeding rates [7]. This enhanced the evidence base for the use of dabigatran in small case series, which also showed comparable clinical and radiographic outcomes between dabigatran and VKAs [8, 9]. Evidence for the use of rivaroxaban [10– 12] and apixaban [12, 13] remains limited to small case series, which, again, showed comparable rates of recanalization between these NOACs and VKAs. Here, we report a single-center retrospective cohort of patients with CVT treated with NOACs (the largest of such series to use apixaban) compared with age-matched and sexmatched control patients treated with VKAs and historical controls from the literature treated with VKAs.

We then identified age-matched and sexmatched controls treated with VKAs in a 1:1 ratio from the remaining patients in the data set to form a VKAtreated control group. We collected data on demographics, risk factors, clinical features at presentation, radiologic features, anticoagulation regimen, clinical outcomes, and adverse events (including major and minor bleeding and recurrent VTE). Because of the limitations of the electronic record, we were not able to consistently monitor adherence to anticoagulation regimens. Major bleeding was defined as nontraumatic symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage or bleeding resulting in the need for transfusion of 2 or more units of blood, the need for hospitalization or prolongation of an existing hospitalization, or death. Patients were observed from index CVT (date of radiographic diagnosis) until 12  months. In terms of outcomes, the modified Rankin Score (mRS) was based on documentation and clinical databases at 3–12 months’ follow-up. Follow-up angiography was reviewed to assess for venous recanalization. Assessors were not blinded to treatment group, as references to treatment could not be avoided via the chart review approach. Radiology reports were used to determine recanalization status, and images were reviewed by one member of the research team (JAG).  

# Methods  

We conducted a single-center retrospective, matched cohort analysis of patients treated for CVT at our institution. The radiology database was searched at the Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis (WUSTL) for magnetic resonance or computed tomographic imaging reports with a finding of CVT for the period January 1, 2011–December 31, 2019, inclusive. All patients identified were screened for anticoagulation with an NOAC following their incident scan. We further screened patients for CVT etiology and adequacy of follow-up data. We excluded patients if they had major traumatic, septic, or postoperative CVT, and if they had no follow-up beyond their initial hospitalization in our record system. Univariate comparisons were made for baseline characteristics and outcomes data between the NOAC-treated group, age-matched and sex-matched controls treated with VKAs, and historical published controls from the International Study on Cerebral Vein and Dural Sinus Thrombosis (ISCVT) [14]. Comparisons for paired data were made with paired Student’s $t$ tests (age), paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (time to anticoagulation, mRS as the ordinal variable), or exact McNemar tests. Unpaired severity variables (intracerebral hemorrhage [ICH] volume, ICH score, Glasgow Coma Scale, and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale) were compared via unpaired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Comparisons between the WUSTL and ISCVT cohorts were made using $\chi^{2}$ test.  

A prespecified protocol for the study, including planned data collection, was approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board (Ref. 201905143).

We matched 27 control patients treated with VKAs within the same time period. # Discussion  

Our data are the largest published series to date of CVT treated with NOACs that include apixaban. We compared clinical and radiologic outcomes with those of a cohort of age-matched and sex-matched controls treated with VKAs. Overall, radiographic recanalization and clinical outcomes were excellent in both groups, with no differences identified between the treatment options. Further, there was a comparable safety profile. This reflects the experience reported in prior case series of NOAC treatment for CVT and the single randomized controlled trial of dabigatran [7–13].  

We made further comparisons of our two treatment groups with clinical outcomes from the ISCVT cohort of 421 patients and did not identify any major differences in safety or functional neurologic outcomes [14]. This was a prospective multicenter observational study that aimed to determine both the long-term prognosis of CVT and the best acute parenteral anticoagulant, unfractionated or low-molecular-weight heparin. The study does not report on what oral anticoagulants were used, but the cohort predates the introduction of NOACs.  

Our institutional preference for apixaban is based on its favorable risk profile with respect to ICH (apparent in clinical trials for treatment of VTE) and its prevention of cardioembolic stroke in atrial fibrillation [13, 15]. The baseline difference between the groups in the use of low-molecular-weight heparin is explained by its common use as a bridging agent for warfarin. Generally, at our institution, a 6-month course of treatment was planned with imaging repeated at 6  months, reflected in the median time to follow-up of 6  months in both groups. In most cases, if residual thrombus was seen on the 6-month scan, anticoagulation was continued for a total of 12  months (unless there was an indication for lifelong anticoagulation). This adheres to CVT management guidelines [5].  

Our study is retrospective and, given the low incidence of CVT, has a small sample size. Therefore, it may lack the statistical power to detect smaller differences in outcomes. For example, we noted that the rate of complete recanalization in our VKA group was nearly twice that of the NOAC group, which, although not statistically significant, may be meaningful and may have been significant had the study been larger. On the other hand, recanalization after CVT is not associated with early or late clinical outcomes [16, 17]. Patients treated with VKAs did suffer more hemorrhagic events, which, again, although not statistically significant, may be a meaningful difference between NOACs and VKAs (especially given the lower hemorrhage rate with apixaban noted in trials for atrial fibrillation) [15]. It should also be noted that our observed adverse event rate did not differ significantly from that of the larger ISCVT cohort. Further, we were able to demonstrate similar functional outcomes between age-matched and sex-matched controls treated with VKAs at the same institution. Further limitations include, first, our inability to measure adherence to prescribed anticoagulation regimens as a possible etiology for recurrent VTE; second, our retrospective approach limited what outcome measures we could reliably use. Given the favorable prognosis of CVT, the mRS may be a relatively insensitive outcome measure for important clinical differences, although it did likely capture serious neurologic deterioration.  

This cohort, in combination with the clinical trial of dabigatran and other published series, has not identified any significant safety concerns regarding the use of NOACs for CVT treatment. Our increasing institutional use may represent a more general secular trend in CVT management, arising from the preference for NOACs above VKAs for treatment of lower extremity deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism and the comfort among vascular neurologists, given their indication for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation [13, 15, 18, 19]. Multicenter cohort studies using a similar approach with larger numbers may have greater power to examine possible differences between NOACs and VKAs in this context. Prospective studies are needed to more fully evaluate the efficacy and safety profiles of the different NOACs for the treatment of CVT. Two further clinical trials are currently registered on ClinicalTrials.gov to prospectively compare rivaroxaban and dabigatran to VKAs, and a further study is seeking to establish optimal duration of treatment with NOACs for CVT.  

# Conclusions  

The safety and efficacy results of NOAC use for CVT were similar to those for age-matched and sex-matched controls treated with VKAs as well as historical published controls. NOAC therapy may be a reasonable therapeutic option in eligible patients with this condition. Although the evidence for dabigatran is strongest, further multicenter cohort studies or clinical trials of the efficacy and safety of other NOACs in the treatment of CVT are warranted.
